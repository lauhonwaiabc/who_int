12.0.1.1 Genetic tumour syndromes associated with eye and orbit malignancies: Introduction

There are numerous genetic tumour predisposition syndromes that may manifest as tumours of the eye (#35867Table 13.01). In this chapter, several of these syndromes have been selected for more in-depth discussion owing to their prominent clinical considerations in the practice of eye pathology. An additional 10 syndromes are listed only in #35867Table 13.01, as they also can have eye tumour findings but are well described in other WHO Classification of Tumours volumes. It is important for ophthalmologists and pathologists to recognize these syndromes as they can be associated with tumours elsewhere in the body, requiring monitoring and early intervention for optimal outcomes.

Retinoblastoma syndrome typically results from inheritance of a single copy pathogenic sequence variant in the tumour suppressor RB1 gene, which makes all cells in the body susceptible to cancer development through acquiring a somatic second-hit alteration {20301625}. The most common secondary cancer types are leiomyosarcoma in the field of radiation if the retinoblastoma was irradiated and osteosarcoma outside the field of radiation {30031154}. Retinoblastoma treatment is now so successful in developed countries that secondary cancers more commonly cause death rather than the primary retinoblastoma {30031154}.

The two-hit scenario for tumour formation occurs in additional syndromes that predispose to eye and other neoplasms: BAP1 alterations are associated with uveal melanoma in BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome {27748099}; VHL mutations predispose to retinal haemangioblastoma in von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {20301636}; and NF1 mutation in neurofibromatosis type 1 results in optic nerve glioma and Lisch nodules {20301288}.

Sturge–Weber syndrome (encephalofacial haemangiomatosis) arises owing to somatic activating mutations of GNAQ, or more rarely GNA11 and GNB2, that induce increased blood vessel growth {36263782;36405075}. This syndrome may include naevus flammeus of the eyelid, episcleral tortuous vessels, glaucoma, diffuse haemangioma of the choroid, retinal vascular changes, heterochromia, and pigmentary changes of the retinal pigment epithelium.

Goldenhar syndrome (craniofacial microsomia) is thought to be multifactorial with multiple genetic candidates; most recently, haploinsufficiency of SF3B2 was found in about 3% of sporadic and about 25% of familial cases {34344887}. It probably involves abnormal development of the first and second branchial arches resulting in structural eye anomalies.

Muir–Torre syndrome is a subtype of Lynch syndrome and the dominant form arises from microsatellite instability induced by mutations in the mismatch repair genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2 {20301390}. Sebaceous neoplasms of the ocular adnexa may rarely occur in people with Muir–Torre syndrome.

Diagnosticians must be familiar with these syndromes so that they are recognized (often after presentation of patients with the corresponding tumour) and managed appropriately.
