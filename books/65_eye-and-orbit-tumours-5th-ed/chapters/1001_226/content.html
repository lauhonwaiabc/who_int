10.0.1 Soft tissue and bone tumours: Introduction

A variety of soft tissue tumours arise in the eye and ocular adnexal tissues (eyelids, conjunctiva, and orbit). The most significant change from the fourth edition of the classification is to combine all soft tissues of the eye and ocular adnexa in a single chapter. The neoplasms that are most commonly encountered or have a characteristic presentation are discussed in detail. For others not specifically discussed here, the fifth edition of the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]] provides a more detailed account of each soft tissue tumour. This chapter provides the key etiological, pathogenetic, diagnostic, and prognostic information on each neoplasm with an emphasis on site-specific characteristics. Mesenchymal neoplasms of the eye and ocular adnexa are uncommon. Soft tissue and bone tumours make up 20–30% and 1–3% of all orbital tumours, respectively (see #33118Table 11.01 and #36425Table 11.02) {15121380;15692775;27152568} [[Heegaard S, Grossniklaus H, editors. Eye Pathology: An Illustrated Guide. Heidelberg (Germany): Springer; 2014.]]. Soft tissue tumours account for 2–12% of all eyelid tumours and 3–4% of all conjunctival tumours (see #36426Table 11.03 and #36430Table 11.04) {26028084;19384066;34937206;30450316;27725148;32066308}.

Collectively, vascular lesions predominate in all age groups, followed by tumours of peripheral nerve sheath and fibrocytic lineage, and rhabdomyosarcoma in children {11825802;32959608;3703507;30932383;17410962;27725148;26028084;19384066;30450316}. However, the collated data on the frequency of soft tissue and bone tumours in the eye and ocular adnexa are limited by the usage of old terminology and the uneven application of more modern immunohistochemical and molecular diagnostic methods for definitive characterization. This is particularly relevant in the categorization of fibroblastic lesions (e.g. solitary fibrous tumour), adipocytic lesions (e.g. herniated subconjunctival orbital fat vs spindle cell / pleomorphic lipoma), and sarcomas [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. In addition to their diagnostic value, cytogenetic and molecular techniques are increasingly important in guiding targeted therapies for various soft tissue and bone tumours {32800827;29755264}.

This volume follows the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification of vascular anomalies with separation into tumours and malformations [[International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies [Internet]. Milwaukee (WI): International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies; 2018 [Cited 2022 Apr 18]. ISSVA Classification of Vascular Anomalies. Available from: https://www.issva.org/classification.]]. Cavernous vascular malformations, venous malformations, and arteriovenous malformations are discussed in section Tumours of the orbit, where they occur most commonly. Occasionally malformations may involve the retina, conjunctiva, or eyelid as part of a larger malformation that also involves the orbit – these lesions are not discussed further in this chapter. Retinal and choroidal haemangiomas have not been classified by ISSVA and it is uncertain at this time if they are neoplasms or malformations; these lesions are summarized in #35618Table 11.05.

High-resolution MRI with and without gadolinium is recommended for evaluation of most soft tissue orbital tumours. CT, using 0.6–1 mm slices after contrast, is the imaging modality of choice for evaluating calcifications and osseous orbital lesions. Ultrasound and optical coherence tomography are valuable diagnostic imaging modalities for ocular soft tissue tumours {26518678}. Although cytology-based techniques are employed with increasing frequency in the diagnosis of intraocular and orbital tumours, incisional and excisional biopsies remain standard methods for pathological diagnosis of soft tissue tumours {27018233;19628495}. Cytology-based diagnosis is particularly challenging for vascular malformations, cystic lesions, fibrous bone lesions, and lesions localized to the orbital apex {27018233;19628495;23457033}. Currently, the grading system of the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) Sarcoma Group is the preferred system for grading of sarcomas of the orbit {6693192}. The ophthalmic site-specific staging system developed by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) is the main staging system used for orbital sarcomas {32105303}.

In this chapter, rare benign and malignant fibroblastic and myofibroblastic tumours of the ocular adnexa are summarized in #33118Table 11.01. Some of these lesions have predilection for the eyelid, such as fibrous hamartoma of infancy, while others, such as myxofibrosarcoma, primarily involve deep orbital tissues. The epidemiology, microscopic features, and biological behaviour of these tumours are generally similar to the non-ocular counterparts [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. Some tumours, such as dermatofibroma of the eyelid, may demonstrate site-specific morphological differences {30183445}. Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans is the most common of these rare entities and is described in greater detail.

Cavernous haemangioma of the retina (RCH)

RCH is typically unilateral and asymptomatic, discovered on routine examination

Some lesions may be associated with vision loss due to macular subretinal fluid or haemorrhage, vitreous haemorrhage with secondary vitreoretinal traction, and hyphaema with secondary glaucoma {27820777;24849703}

RCH is composed of grape-like clusters of dilated thin-walled interconnected vascular spaces lined by a continuous non-fenestrated endothelial lining {5553009;6538410;27820777}

Occasional areas of partially organized thrombus may be present

The adjacent retina may exhibit gliosis

RCH can be sporadic or coexist with familial cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM) and cutaneous vascular malformations

It is seen primarily in the White population with an even sex distribution; although RCH is believed to be a congenital lesion, the median age at presentation is 21 years {27820777}

5% of patients with familial CCM also have RCH {16769843}

Circumscribed choroidal haemangioma (CCH)

CCH typically presents in the fourth to sixth decades of life with loss of vision, metamorphopsia, and visual field defects

CCH in patients < 20 years of age is associated with worse visual acuity {31686962}

Subretinal fluid accumulation is seen in 64–80% of cases

Cystoid macular oedema and retinal haemorrhage can occur {31755430}

On ultrasonography, CCH demonstrates high internal reflectivity and acoustic solidity

CCH shows hyperfluorescence in all phases on intravenous fluorescein angiography and early hyperfluorescence with the washout phenomenon on indocyanine green angiography

MRI demonstrates a choroidal mass that is hyperintense to vitreous on T1-weighted images and hyperisointense on T2-weighted images with contrast enhancement {31755430}

Tumours are unilateral, orange-red, round, and well circumscribed, with a predilection for the superotemporal quadrant close to the macula

The average basal diameter is 7.2 mm and the average height is 2.4 mm {30602446}

Choroidal haemangioma is composed of various proportions of dilated (cavernous) or capillary-sized thin-walled vascular channels lined by flat endothelial cells with scant intervening stroma {820013}

CCH has sharply demarcated pushing margins that compress adjacent normal choroid {820013}

Associated histopathological features include dilated draining vortex veins, retinal pigment epithelial hyperplasia, chorioretinal adhesions, and serous (exudative) retinal detachment

In CCH, GNAQ mutations occur in Q209, similar to those seen in intraocular melanocytic naevi and melanomas {31336681}

Choroidal capillary malformation (CM) / diffuse choroidal haemangioma (DCH)

 

(See also <<12.0.1.14>>Sturge–Weber syndrome)

DCH occurs in the setting of Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) in 20–70% of cases {32233696;15763200;1287171}, usually ipsilateral to the facial CM (naevus flammeus) and presents as a diffuse, poorly defined red-orange mass involving > 50% of the choroid {34847393}

DCH remains asymptomatic early in childhood with a later increase in choroidal thickness with ageing

Vision loss occurs from refractive error (hyperopic shift), overlying retinal changes, exudative retinal detachment, and secondary glaucoma {32233696}

DCH appears as an unencapsulated collection of thin-walled, variably sized vascular channels

Secondary changes include retinal pigment epithelial alteration, subretinal fibrosis, serous (exudative) retinal detachment, and sequelae of paediatric glaucoma (buphthalmos) {34847393;820013}

In DCH, GNAQ mutations involve the R183 codon, similarly to other Sturge–Weber syndrome–associated capillary malformations {23656586}
