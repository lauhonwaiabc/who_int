5.0.0.1 Tumours of the optic disc and optic nerve: Introduction

The optic nerve arises from the embryonal optic stalk and contains axons extending from the retinal ganglion cells to the brain. These axons exit the globe through the lamina cribrosa, a connective tissue meshwork continuous with the sclera. Within the globe, the axons are unmyelinated; posterior to the lamina cribrosa, the axons are myelinated by oligodendrocytes, with additional support provided by astrocytes and microglia. The optic nerve is surrounded by the same layers as the rest of the CNS (the dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater), with invaginations of the pia dividing bundles of axons into individual fascicles. This section focuses on prechiasmal lesions.

Consistent with the derivation of the optic nerve from diencephalic extensions, tumours in the optic nerve make up a subset of those seen in the CNS and its coverings. Choristomas containing heterotopic smooth muscle and adipose tissue can also be found around the optic nerve and optic disc {29755281}. Astrocytomas most commonly arise in children, are of low-grade, and have the microscopic features of pilocytic astrocytoma {25057976;23702730}. They are often associated with neurofibromatosis type 1, and they can be bilateral in such cases {18344915}. High-grade astrocytomas and gangliogliomas can also be encountered in the optic nerve, but only rarely {28208187;25043166}. High-grade B-cell lymphoma and medulloepithelioma can also involve the optic nerve. Interestingly, IDH-mutant gliomas, including diffuse infiltrating astrocytoma and oligodendrogliomas of various grades, are almost never found in the optic nerve. Meningioma, solitary fibrous tumour / haemangiopericytoma, melanocytoma, melanoma, and other neoplasms of the CNS coverings can also arise in the optic nerve.

In this, the fifth edition, tumours metastatic to the optic nerve are covered in a separate chapter on section metastases to the eye and surrounding structures.
