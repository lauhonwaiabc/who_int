10.2.5.1 Aneurysmal bone cyst

Definition

Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a destructive, expansile neoplasm of bone composed of multiloculated blood-filled cystic spaces and with a potential for local recurrence.

ICD-O coding

9260/0 Aneurysmal bone cyst

ICD-11 coding

FB80.6 Aneurysmal bone cyst

Related terminology

Not recommended: giant cell reparative granuloma of small bone; giant cell lesion of small bones.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ABC can affect any bone, but usually arises in the metaphysis of long bones, especially the femur, tibia, and humerus, and the posterior elements of vertebral bodies. Rarely, the bones surrounding the orbit may be involved {35246319;22500206}.

Clinical features

The most common signs and symptoms are pain and swelling, which are rarely secondary to fracture. In the orbit, the common signs and symptoms include proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and exophthalmos. Radiographically, ABC presents as a lytic eccentric, expansile mass with well-defined margins. Most tumours contain a thin shell of subperiosteal, reactive bone. CT shows a cystic, expansile, and radiolucent tumour. MRI studies show internal septa and the characteristic fluid-fluid levels {7863874;1591685}.

Epidemiology

ABC affects all age groups but is most common during the first two decades of life (80%). Both sexes are equally affected {2835141;1591685}. The estimated annual incidence is 0.15 per 1 million {10379320}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

ABC most commonly shows the translocation t(16;17)(q22;p13) {10825009;15026324;10502326;1777412;11106078} leading to the fusion of CDH11 with USP6, with resultant upregulation of USP6 transcription {15026324;15735689}. USP6 rearrangements are found in approximately 70% of primary ABCs but not in secondary ABC {15509545}. While the most frequent USP6 fusion partner is CDH11 (30%), others include THRAP3 (TRAP150), CNBP (ZNF9), OMD, COL1A1, CTNNB1, STAT3, FOSL2, EIF1, SPARC, PAFAH1B1, USP9XI, and RUNX2 {15735689;28449806;30553465;27910166;30767316}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, ABC is well defined and multiloculated, and composed of blood-filled cystic spaces separated by tan-white, gritty septa. Solid areas can be seen.

Histopathology

ABC is well circumscribed and composed of blood-filled cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa, which are composed of a moderately dense cellular proliferation of bland fibroblasts, with scattered, multinucleated, osteoclast-like giant cells and reactive woven bone rimmed by osteoblasts. In about one third of cases, the bone is basophilic and has been termed “blue bone”, although its presence is not diagnostic. Mitoses are commonly present and can be numerous; however, atypical forms are absent. Necrosis is rare unless there has been a pathological fracture. The solid pattern of ABC has the same components and is very similar to giant cell lesion of small bones {8431852;6850506}. Primary ABC accounts for approximately 70% of all cases, while secondary ABCs may develop in association with other benign neoplasms, most commonly giant cell tumour of bone, osteoblastoma, chondroblastoma, and fibrous dysplasia {7863874;2835141;1591685}.

Differential diagnosis includes ABC-like areas consequent to haemorrhagic changes, associated with benign and, less frequently, malignant bone tumours (formerly termed “secondary aneurysmal bone cyst”) {7863874;2835141;1591685}. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma may mimic ABC on imaging and macroscopically, but microscopically malignant cells within fibrous septa are seen.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

USP6 gene rearrangement can be detected by FISH study using break-apart probes, or by next-generation sequencing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: radiology: multicystic lesion with fluid-fluid levels; histology: cyst wall composed of fibroblasts, osteoclast-like giant cells, haemosiderin pigment, and new bone formation; molecular: USP6 gene rearrangement.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

ABC is a potentially locally recurrent neoplasm. The recurrence rate after curettage is variable (20–70%). Spontaneous regression after incomplete removal is very unusual.
