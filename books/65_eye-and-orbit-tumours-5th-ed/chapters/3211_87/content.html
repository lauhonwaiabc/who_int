3.2.1.1 Choroidal and ciliary body naevi

Definition

Choroidal and ciliary body naevi are benign neoplastic proliferations of uveal melanocytes.

ICD-O coding

8720/0 Pigmented naevus

8723/0 Halo naevus

8761/1 Giant pigmented naevus

ICD-11 coding

2F36.0 & XH4L78 Benign neoplasm of choroid & Pigmented naevus, NOS

2F36.2 & XH4L78 Benign neoplasm of ciliary body & Pigmented naevus, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: common naevus; melanocytic choroidal tumour of indeterminate malignancy; suspicious naevus.

Not recommended: mole; benign melanoma.

Subtype(s)

Common naevus; pigmented naevus of indeterminate malignancy; giant pigmented naevus; halo naevus

Localization

Most choroidal naevi are postequatorial and evenly distributed throughout both eyes {19039331}. Ciliary body naevi are rarely found because this part of the uvea is hidden behind the iris, unless they extend to the iris root or anterior chamber {17041573}.

Clinical features

Choroidal naevi are usually asymptomatic. Most are < 5 mm in diameter and < 1 mm thick {18408179}. They usually have a greyish surface which is featureless or shows drusen, which indicate chronicity, as does atrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium and intraretinal oedema {848534}. Some are amelanotic and others have amelanotic margins (i.e. halo naevi) {20625046}. Melanocytic choroidal tumours of indeterminate malignancy show some features suggestive of uveal melanoma, which include orange lipofuscin (found in 6–10% of choroidal naevi), subretinal fluid (found in 9–15% of choroidal naevi), and large size {18067966}. Choroidal naevi usually appear and enlarge in the first two or three decades of life after which time any growth is slow (i.e. ~1% of their diameter or 0.04–0.06 mm per year) {34666992;20801518}. Retinal invasion is rare {31559248}. Ciliary body naevi can cause sectoral cataract and can have dilated episcleral feeder blood vessels {17041573}.

Imaging

On optical coherence tomography, the naevus may be flat or dome shaped. The tumour obscures the choroidal vasculature and spares the choriocapillaris unless the naevus is thick. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) over thick naevi shows irregularity, atrophy or detachment, drusen, and sometimes orange lipofuscin deposits. The neurosensory retina is usually normal over flat naevi, but develops so-called shaggy receptors, outer retinal atrophy, serous detachment, or intraretinal cystoid oedema over larger naevi. These aberrations are more frequently seen in choroidal melanocytic tumours of indeterminate malignancy and choroidal melanomas {35200586}. Optical coherence tomography may allow measurement of the diameter and, if the anterior scleral surface is identified, thickness of the naevus. On fundus autofluorescence imaging, any lipofuscin is brightly hyperautofluorescent, whereas RPE atrophy is hypoautofluorescent. On fluorescein angiography, which is rarely necessary, naevi are relatively hypofluorescent with any hyperfluorescence arising from RPE abnormalities and subretinal fluid. Indocyanine green angiography shows choroidal naevi as hypofluorescent relative to the surrounding choroid, and it may reveal vascular patterns predictive of tumour growth {12466160}. If the naevus is large enough, ultrasonography may allow measurement of tumour dimensions and assessment of internal acoustic reflectivity, which tends to be higher than that of choroidal melanomas. On MRI, choroidal naevi show enhancement after contrast administration, but most of them are too small to differentiate them from uveal melanoma using functional imaging {34718831}.

Differential diagnosis

This includes choroidal or ciliary body melanoma, melanocytoma, metastasis, bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation, congenital hypertrophy of the RPE, adenoma and adenocarcinoma of the RPE, and the so-called combined hamartoma of the retina and RPE.

Epidemiology

Choroidal naevi are most common in fair-skinned individuals, with the prevalence ranging from approximately 5.6% in White people to 0.6% in African-American people {26255109}. An autopsy study found ciliary body naevi in about 4% of 200 eyes {5838370}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Risk factors for choroidal naevi are fair skin and older age.

Pathogenesis

Choroidal and ciliary body naevi arise from uveal melanocytes derived from the neural crest, similarly to blue naevi. In the only known study, almost all choroidal naevi showed low-frequency, almost mutually exclusive GNAQ and GNA11 mutations in 15 of 16 eyes, suggesting a role in their development from normal uveal melanocytes {28809862}. Other aberrations included heterogeneous YAP1 expression and, in 1 case, a CYSLTR2 mutation {33588787}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Ciliary body and choroidal naevi are typically composed of variably pigmented plump polygonal naevus cells and slender spindle cells, both of which have bland oval nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli {5924936}. About 4% show balloon cells with a foamy cytoplasm [[Eagle RC. Eye Pathology: An Atlas and Text. 3rd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Wolters Kluwer; 2017. pp. 200â€“1.]]. Other possible findings include stromal thickening, obliteration of the overlying choriocapillaris, atrophy of the overlying RPE, intraretinal oedema, and photoreceptor disruption {5924936}. Choroidal naevi show intralesional vessels, distended stromal vessels at the naevus border, or both {8152768}. Halo naevus histopathology has not been reported.

Cytology

FNAB of melanocytic tumours of indeterminate malignancy may allow differentiation from melanoma by excluding cytological and genetic features of uveal melanoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Choroidal and ciliary body naevi are not usually subject to molecular investigation, although genetic aberrations may be detected when the tumour is sampled for histological or cytological diagnosis to exclude a melanoma. GNAQ, GNA11, and CYSTLR2 mutations have been detected in choroidal naevi, but these mutations do not differentiate them from uveal melanoma. A total of 8 naevi showed no loss of nuclear BAP1 immunostaining {28809862;33588787;19078957;21083380}. SF3B1 and EIF1AX mutations have not been assessed in choroidal and ciliary body naevi.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: choroidal naevi (diameter < 5 mm; thickness < 1 mm); flat or shallow dome shape; presence of drusen and/or a halo; minimal or no subretinal fluid; minimal or no orange lipofuscin; minimal or no growth in later life; absence of cytological features of malignancy on histology (if resected).

Staging

No staging exists for choroidal and ciliary body naevi.

The Shields original TFSOM (To Find Small Ocular Melanoma) and the later TFSOM-DIM (Doing IMaging) mnemonics estimate the risk of tumour growth according to Thickness > 2 mm, Fluid under the retina, Symptoms, Orange pigment, Melanoma touching the optic disk or later Melanoma hollow on ultrasonography, and DIaMeter > 5 mm, with the risk increasing with the number of indicators present {15327099;30608349}.

Damato devised the MOLES acronym to estimate the likelihood of malignancy according to Mushroom shape, Orange pigment, Large size, Enlargement, and Subretinal fluid. Each of these is scored 0, 1, or 2, with tumours categorized as common naevus, low-risk naevus, high-risk naevus, and probable melanoma, according to whether the total score is 0, 1, 2, or > 2, respectively {34604203;32455720;35764877}.

It has been proposed that the clinician should consider the possibility of a small choroidal melanoma when the observed growth rate of the tumour exceeds the 90th percentile reported for growing benign choroidal naevi, i.e. 0.12 mm per year or 2.6% per year {34666992}.

Prognosis and prediction

When a melanocytic choroidal tumour with clinical features of a naevus grows, it is not known how often this is because of normal evolution {20801518} or malignant transformation, or because the lesion is a small melanoma that lacks the typical clinical signs of malignancy, which are not yet seen in incipient melanomas {34666992}.

Assuming all choroidal melanomas arise from naevi, the risk of malignant transformation is estimated to be 1 per 8845 per year {16154197}; however, this average greatly overestimates the risk in younger individuals and underestimates the risk in older individuals. The lifetime risk of malignant transformation is estimated to be approximately 0.2% {16650691}.
