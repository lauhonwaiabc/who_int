9.5.0.2 Langerhans cell tumours

Definition

Langerhans cell neoplasms are clonal proliferations of myeloid dendritic cells expressing a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a and CD207 expression). Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) usually follows a benign clinical course. Langerhans cell sarcoma (LCS) is an aggressive malignant neoplasm of Langerhans cell immunophenotype with high-grade cytological features.

Subtype(s)

Langerhans cell histiocytosis

None

Langerhans cell sarcoma

Primary malignant histiocytoses of Langerhans subtype; secondary malignant histiocytoses (after or associated with another haematological neoplasm) of Langerhans subtype

Localization

LCH can affect many organ systems (see #34878Table 10.05), but most frequently presents in bone as osteolytic lesions with or without soft tissue extension {32106306;27273725}. Ophthalmic presentation can be solitary or part of a multisystem presentation {33157073}. Orbital LCH usually manifests as a unilateral, extraconal lesion, affecting the roof and lateral orbital wall with or without intracranial extension {33157073} and eyelid involvement. Less-common ophthalmic sites include the extraocular muscles, lacrimal gland, and periocular skin without orbital bone involvement {26359698;33315841}. Iris, conjunctival, and choroid involvement have also been reported {33315841;34637661}. Presentation of LCS in periocular tissues is extremely rare {29755270}.

Clinical features

Common symptoms of ophthalmic LCH include eyelid swelling, exophthalmos, palpable orbital mass, pain, limited eye movement, visual dysfunction, uveitis, and retinal vasculitis {31856763;31762762;34637661}. LCS generally presents with rapid growth and local invasion, and symptoms/signs vary with the site involved.

Imaging

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is advised to investigate histiocytosis lesions in the orbit {29720485}. Fundus fluorescein angiography and optical coherence tomography imaging can be used to assess choroidal infiltration and intraocular disease {28153505}.

Epidemiology

LCH incidence ranges from 2.6 to 8.9 cases per 1 million children under 15 years of age with an M:F ratio of 1.2:1 {16652350;18260117;19060008}. Incidence in adults is 1–2 cases per 1 million {14556926;26461147}. Orbital involvement occurs in 8% of children with skull lesions {33157073}.

LCS is very rare with a reported incidence of 2 per 100 million {27980300;31005630}. Almost all cases present in adults, with a median age of 50 years {25805533}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

LCHs are clonal neoplastic disorders of haematopoietic origin {24638167;28512190;32092141;32750121} and are marked by Langerhans cell–like histiocytes frequently expressing MAPK pathway–activating somatic mutations {24638167;29526544;28566492;28512190;32750121;30157397;31899802;34620874;33901419} evidenced by near universal phosphorylated ERK expression. BRAF p.V600E is the most prevalent molecular alteration, followed by hotspot mutations affecting MAP2K1 or exon 12 of BRAF and more rarely ARAF, ERBB3, NRAS, and KRAS {29526544;30157397;36083130;31806714}.

LCS is a clonal disease with unknown cell of origin. A subset shows MAPK pathway mutation, including KRAS and infrequently BRAF p.V600E {29194093;33904632;26468227}, and a few others including deletions in CDKN2A and TP53, and PTEN mutation {33904632;29194093}. Secondary LCS has been associated with follicular lymphoma {23759932;30255584}, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia {21666687;29194093;23923114}, and T-lymphoblastic leukaemia {32852896}, and shares clonality / molecular aberration with the original haematological neoplasm, suggesting transdifferentiation {16778410;28614815;35004363}. Progression is not well documented in primary (de novo) LCS {19642834;26966089}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance varies with site of involvement from bony to fibrotic to soft haemorrhagic lesions.

Histopathology

LCH shows aggregates of large oval or epithelioid histiocytes (10–25 microns) with folded or grooved nuclei and abundant eosinophilic (pale-pink) cytoplasm, fine chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli. Nuclear features are usually bland; mitotic figures can be brisk. LCH lesions typically display an inflammatory infiltrate composed of eosinophils, lymphocytes, and other myeloid cells, including monocytes, myeloid dendritic cells, neutrophils, and osteoclastic-type multinucleated giant cells {29526544;32750121;32187362} also found in non-osseous lesions {15753204}. No emperipolesis is noted {32187362}. The proliferation index with Ki-67 is usually < 10%. Birbeck granules with a tennis-racket shape can be demonstrated on ultrastructural examination {12121233}.

LCS is characterized by overtly malignant pleomorphic cells with a high N:C ratio, clumped chromatin, conspicuous nucleoli, and occasional nuclear grooves. A high mitotic count and atypical mitoses are classic features.

Immunohistochemistry

LCH histiocytes display membranous or perinuclear CD1a staining and a granular/cytoplasmic pattern with CD207 (langerin) staining. S100 reactivity is also seen. Both S100 and CD207 patterns are often discordant with CD1a {31899802}. A variable number of LCH cells display cytoplasmic phosphorylated ERK and nuclear cyclin D1{33444078}. Some cells may display staining of CD68 {32750121}, but not of other macrophage markers (CD163, factor XIIIa, or fascin).

LCS cells are positive for CD1a, CD207 (langerin), and S100, which might be focal. Reactivity for vimentin, CD68, fascin, factor XIIIa, cyclin D1, and HLA-DR has been reported {27980300;32644218}. An assessment of staining should be made on high-grade atypical cells.

Differential diagnosis

See #34878Table 10.05.

For LCH, this includes other histiocytic lesions involving orbital, periocular, or intraocular sites such as Rosai–Dorfman disease, Erdheim–Chester disease, and juvenile/adult-onset xanthogranuloma {16622091;17135338;19961259;34637661}.

For LCS, differential diagnosis includes malignant histiocytosis {29720485;33710041;34637661}. The histological features often overlap with those of other histiocytic malignancies, including those of the histiocytic, interdigitating, and indeterminate cell types. Some cases may have an atypical immunophenotype, with overlapping dendritic and macrophage phenotypes, defying strict classification of either LCS or histiocytic sarcoma {26966089}.

Cytology

Definitive diagnosis on cytological specimens is difficult {31017324;33939500}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Properly validated immunohistochemistry (VE1 antibody specific for BRAF p.V600E) may be used in conjunction with nucleic acid–based assays detecting the BRAF c.1799T>A mutation encoding BRAF p.V600E {22859608;30923995;28219109;32750121} and other molecular alterations of the MAPK pathway.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Essential: large round to oval histiocytes with grooved to convoluted nuclei and Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a+, CD207+/−) in the correct histological/anatomical context.

Desirable: mutation analysis in the MAPK pathway, with sensitive testing for low-frequency allele detection.

Langerhans cell sarcoma

Essential: pleomorphic histiocytes with high-grade cytology, increased mitoses, and immunoreactivity for CD1a, S100, and CD207, any of which can be focal.

Desirable: clinical evidence of rapid tumour progression; molecular alteration, preferably in the MAPK pathway.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Clinical evaluation and therapy decisions are based on the number and type of affected tissues assessed by PET-CT or MRI imaging of the entire body {32187362;32106306;34781268;23109216}.

LCH, except for a disseminated presentation affecting, among others, the spleen, liver, and/or bone marrow (MS-RO+, i.e. multisystem, risk organ involvement), has excellent survival rates. BRAF inhibitors have been shown to induce durable responses in MS-RO+ patients testing positive for refractory BRAF p.V600E {31513482;32092141;32985015;32972045;35065150}. Patients co-presenting with haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis have significantly poorer survival {30521100;34133378}.

The prognosis for LCS depends on clinical presentation with a high (50%) mortality risk for patients with disseminated disease; the median survival is 19 to 27 months {12121233;31005630;25805533}. Multimodality treatment (surgery combined with chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy) seems more effective {27502715;26468227}. Bone marrow transplantation is an additional option for patients with disseminated disease {25805533}. More localized disease confers a survival advantage if complete surgical excision can be achieved {25805533;33565241}.
