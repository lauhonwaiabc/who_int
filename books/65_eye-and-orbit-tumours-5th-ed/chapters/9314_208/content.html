9.3.1.4 Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma

Definition

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) is a mature small B monomorphic cell neoplasm coexpressing CD5 and CD23. A B-cell (monoclonal) count of ≥ 5 × 109/L cells with characteristic CLL morphology and phenotype in the peripheral blood should be found. SLL applies to cases with a circulating CLL cell count of < 5 × 109/L and proven nodal, splenic, or other extramedullary involvement, such as ophthalmic sites.

Related terminology

Acceptable: B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

For ophthalmic locations, lesions are usually in the posterior pole of the eye (choroid, retina, optic nerve, sclera) and orbital tissues {32906630;32407752}.

Clinical features

Clinical presentations are divided into asymptomatic, symptomatic, and Richter transformation. Clinical features depend on the site of infiltrations but are most frequently bilateral. The main symptoms are visual acuity disturbance, ocular pain, and ophthalmoplegia. Swelling of the lacrimal glands and/or eyelids may be seen in less than half of the patients. Ophthalmic symptoms are usually isolated; < 10% of patients with ophthalmic involvement present with systemic symptoms such as weight loss. On examination, the retina and vitreous may have perivascular infiltrates, haemorrhage, aneurysms, and tortuosity of vessels with neovascularization. Creamy infiltrates in the choroid with secondary retinal pigment epithelium changes and retinal serous detachments may present if the choroid is involved. Posterior scleritis–type presentation may be seen in posterior scleral / episcleral involvement. Ocular involvement in CLL patients is diverse: secondary to CLL infiltration (more than half), followed by lymphoma, and least common by infection, and others. CLL infiltration is mostly bilateral, whereas lymphoma is usually unilateral. Optic neuropathy is always secondary to CLL infiltration {33612166;32407752}.

Epidemiology

CLL/SLL is the most common leukaemia in fair-skinned people and is more frequent in men than women. Ocular involvement is rare, has a median patient age of 65 years, and is most often seen after patients have a CLL diagnosis rather than as a first symptom of CLL. Usually, ophthalmic symptoms appear after 3 years of CLL follow-up. CLL in these patients is mostly stable, followed by progressive disease, with Richter transformation the least common {32407752}.

Etiology

The etiology is essentially unknown; family members of patients with CLL have a 5- to 8-fold increased risk of CLL {31395603}.

Pathogenesis

The cellular origin of CLL/SLL remains unclear; however, transcriptomic and epigenetic analyses suggest that CLL/SLL cells most closely resemble antigen-experienced, memory-like B cells {33604581;26780610}. CLL/SLL could derive from normal B cells differentiating via follicular or extrafollicular maturation pathways, reflected by the presence or absence of somatic hypermutation, respectively {34174979;33326765}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearance is dependent on the location of the lesions.

Histopathology

CLL infiltrates are usually diffuse in ophthalmic locations because of the absence of normal lymphoid tissue. Typically, CLL is composed of small lymphocytes with clumped chromatin, occasional small nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm, and rarely plasmacytoid cells. Mitotic count is extremely low.

Immunohistochemistry

CLL/SLL cells express pan–B-cell markers (CD19, CD20, CD79a, and PAX5), CD5, and CD23, and are negative for CD10 and other germinal centre markers. As many as 95% of cases express LEF1, which is rarely expressed in other small B-cell lymphomas {28395058;25713417;21685909}. Usually, cyclin D1 and SOX11 are negative.

Differential diagnosis

Differential diagnosis includes chronic non-granulomatous uveitis and inflammations, and other small cell non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphomas.

Cytology

Cytology is often performed on vitreous specimens with opacities to exclude reactive inflammation versus lymphoma. Cytology of vitreous aspirates/washings or from FNA of a uveal lesion may disclose the presence of CLL/SLL with or without large B-cell (Richter syndrome) transformation. Cytological features may show abundant cellularity of small slightly atypical lymphocytes that are CD20+ and CD5+. In reactive chronic non-granulomatous vitritis, the infiltrate is usually scant and predominantly CD3+/CD20−. In cases of transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, these cells are seen as the predominant component in patients with a history of CLL/SLL. Vitrectomy specimens containing haemorrhage in patients with CLL may contain the neoplastic lymphocytes but it is not possible to exclude tissue involvement versus circulating CLL cells in the blood from the haemorrhage {23744124}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The most commonly detected cytogenetic aberrations are deletion of 13q and deletion of 11q, followed by deletion of 17p/TP53, and less frequently trisomy 12. Absence of recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities such as deletions of 6q and rearrangements of BCL2 or BCL3 with IGH may be seen {35207569}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: classic morphology of CLL cells and absolute B-cell count > 5 × 109/L; flow cytometry (on peripheral blood and/or bone marrow aspirate samples) showing expression of CD19, CD5, CD20, and CD23 (variable) and weakly expressed monotypic light chain; histopathology/immunohistochemistry (on bone marrow cell clot/core biopsy, and biopsies of lymph node or other tissue samples) demonstrating CD20+/weak+, CD5+/weak+, CD23 (variable) expression, and no expression of cyclin D1 (weak positivity in a subset of cells in proliferation centres is allowed).

Desirable: flow cytometry is positive for CD200, ROR1, and CD43 with absence of FMC7, CD79b (can be weakly positive), CD10, and CD81 expression; histopathology/immunohistochemistry is positive for CD23, LEF1, CD43, and IRF4 (MUM1) (proliferation centres) with absence of CD10 and SOX11 expression.

Staging

For CLL, the Rai and Binet systems are used [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. The stage of SLL is determined with the Lugano modifications to the Ann Arbor system {25113753}.

Prognosis and prediction

In general, for systemic involvement of CLL there are two clinical (Rai and Binet) prognostic systems based on physical examination findings and cell blood counts. Molecular genetics prognosis is based on the following: deletion of 13q is associated with indolent disease, trisomy 12 and absence of recurrent cytogenetic abnormalities are associated with an intermediate prognosis {11136261;31364186}, while deletion of 11q and deletion of 17p/TP53 and rearrangements of BCL3 and/or IGH are associated with a more aggressive course and reduced overall survival {30575108}. A comprehensive, international prognostic score (CLL International Prognostic Index) integrates genetic, biological, and clinical variables to identify distinct risk groups of CLL patients {27185642;35207569}. Furthermore, IGHV-mutated CLL/SLLs (50–70%) and unmutated CLL/SLLs (30–50%) show different kinetics with respect to proliferation and, therefore, have different time-to-therapy and time-to-relapse profiles with time-limited chemotherapy-based regimens {10477712;10477713}.

In a systematic review of 123 cases of patients with ophthalmic involvement associated with CLL, the patients with ocular involvement had an overall poor prognosis, with a third of the patients having died from the disease within 3 years, and a quarter having partial or total visual loss. Eye infections were associated with the poorest prognosis (almost half died from the disease), with a 6-month median survival {32407752}.
