2.4.1.1 Cutaneous melanoma in situ

Definition

Melanoma in situ is a preinvasive form of melanoma. The atypical melanocytes proliferate intraepidermally and may extend down along hair follicle epithelium or eccrine ducts.

ICD-O coding

8742/2 Lentigo maligna

8743/2 Low-CSD melanoma in situ

ICD-11 coding

2E63.0 & XH08X7 Melanoma in situ of skin & Superficial spreading melanoma

2E63.0 & XH9NL4 Melanoma in situ of skin & Lentigo maligna melanoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: Hutchinson melanocytic freckle.

Subtype(s)

Lentigo maligna (LM); low cumulative sun damage (CSD) melanoma (superficial spreading melanoma [SSM] in situ)

Localization

LM, a precursor of lentigo maligna melanoma (LMM), develops on high-CSD skin. The head and neck region, including the eyelid and periocular skin, is most commonly affected. In contrast, melanoma in situ leading to low-CSD melanoma (SSM) is related to sunburn in childhood and intermittent sun exposure, so eyelids and periocular skin are less-common locations {30578807;33520295}.

Clinical features

LM typically occurs on the face, scalp, and neck of elderly patients with severe chronic sun damage. The lesions may be large, are poorly defined, and often merge with other cutaneous changes of chronic sun damage {19322612}. Low-CSD melanoma / SSM in situ occurs in middle-aged patients who are mostly lighter skinned (Fitzpatrick skin types I–III), and it is usually smaller and well circumscribed. Owing to their radial growth phase, both melanoma types are usually flat. They are asymmetrical in shape and structure and may have more than one colour.

Epidemiology

See section Invasive eyelid melanoma.

Etiology

In situ melanocytic tumours may occur in chronically sun-damaged skin (LM), or in a low-CSD setting (SSM in situ).

Pathogenesis

See section Invasive eyelid melanoma.

Macroscopic appearance

Lesions appear as pigmented, flat, asymmetrical lesions that are multicoloured, light brown, dark brown, blue-black, or black, with irregular or circumscribed borders.

Histopathology

LM occurs in chronic actinic damaged skin with elastoid degeneration and epidermal atrophy. There is proliferation of atypical melanocytes involving the (supra)basal epidermis. Hair follicle epithelium or eccrine ducts may be involved. Nuclei are atypical, enlarged or small with perinuclear halo, hyperchromatic, and pleomorphic. Scattered mitosis may be present {18532874}. Two major growth patterns exist: classic LM and dysplastic naevus–like LM {15953373}. The classic-pattern type has epidermal atrophy, loss of rete ridges, and few irregularly distributed nests, which hang down from the junction in a droplet-like pattern; in contrast, the dysplastic LM type has a stronger tendency of nest formation, and some nests bridge elongated rete ridges. The distinction from junctional dysplastic naevus can be difficult, particularly in partial biopsy specimens. Owing to an extensive longstanding radial growth phase, LM may be large and spread on the conjunctiva.

In low-CSD melanoma in situ (SSM in situ), usually there is a proliferation of atypical melanocytes at many levels of a normal or even hyperplastic epidermis. There are variably sized nests along the dermoepidermal junction. A confluence of intraepidermal nests and prominent buckshot pagetoid spread is seen. The tumour cells may extend down hair follicle epithelium or eccrine ducts. The atypical melanocytes have large nuclei, irregular clumped or dense chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and eosinophilic or pigmented cytoplasm. In contrast to melanoma, LM actinic damage is not a dominating feature.

Immunohistochemistry

Atypical melanocytes can be highlighted with SOX10 immunohistochemistry. Caution is advised with melan-A or HMB45 staining, which can have a nonspecific reaction in keratinocytes of heavily pigmented actinic keratosis {18494818}. Positive immunostaining for PRAME or loss of p16 can support melanoma in situ diagnosis. Since lentigo solaris can also be positive, caution is also required here {30045064}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

This is not clinically relevant for in situ lesions.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a proliferation of atypical melanocytes with a lentiginous or pagetoid spread growth pattern; absence of dermal invasion; elastoid degeneration in LMM.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Long-term survival approaches 100%. However, LM often recurs: 30–50% of cases will progress to LMM if left untreated, with a latency period varying from 10 to 50 years. The estimated risk of progression to LMM is 3.5% per year {26082796;31095041;33502077}.

Surgical excision with evaluation of margins on permanent and delayed reconstruction can lead to excellent local control rates for melanomas of the periocular region {25053759}.
