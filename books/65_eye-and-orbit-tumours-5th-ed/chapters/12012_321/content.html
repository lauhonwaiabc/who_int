12.0.1.2 Retinoblastoma syndrome

Definition

Retinoblastoma syndrome is a heritable cancer predisposition disorder associated with a germline heterozygous pathogenic RB1 variant with development of retinoblastoma and other extraocular malignancies.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

180200 Retinoblastoma; RB1

ICD-11 coding

2D02.2 Retinoblastoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: trilateral retinoblastoma; heritable retinoblastoma, familial retinoblastoma, hereditary cancer syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Heritable unilateral and bilateral retinoblastoma; trilateral retinoblastoma – associated with primary intracranial tumour (pineal or suprasellar tumour); second primary non-ocular malignancies

Localization

Retinoblastoma develops in the retina of one or both eyes. In retinoblastoma syndrome, bilateral tumours most frequently occur, although 15% of patients will have unilateral disease. In 5% of affected children, synchronous or metachronous malignant intracranial tumours (pineal or suprasellar) develop. Pineoblastomas occur in retina-like tissue in the pineal gland of the brain. Concurrence of pineoblastomas or primitive neuroectodermal tumours and retinoblastoma is referred to as trilateral retinoblastoma. Pineoblastoma is rare (< 5% of patients with retinoblastoma syndrome) and usually fatal, unlike retinoblastoma of the eye, which is generally curable when diagnosed before extraocular spread {24589388}. Later in life, patients with retinoblastoma syndrome may develop second primary malignancies (SPM), most frequently in soft tissues (rhabdomyosarcoma), bone (osteosarcoma), endometrium, lung, or skin (melanomas) {25185089;31622129}. The overall risk of a second tumour is 0.5–1% per year of life {9727521}.

Clinical features

MRI of the orbits and brain is performed during the initial diagnosis of intraocular retinoblastoma to exclude extraocular extension (extrascleral and/or optic nerve) and brain involvement with a pineoblastoma. If possible, radiation (including X-ray, CT, and external beam radiation [EBRT]) should be avoided in individuals with heritable retinoblastoma to minimize the lifetime risk of developing late-onset second cancers.

Synchronous trilateral retinoblastoma initial therapy should address both ocular and intracranial disease (see section Retinoblastoma). For pineal tumours, a maximal safe surgical resection is encouraged at diagnosis followed by intensive chemotherapy, often requiring autologous haematopoietic cell rescue. Irradiation can be done using focal radiotherapy on the pineal region (proton beam or stereotactic radiotherapy) associated or not with craniospinal irradiation {32187454}.

Survivors of heritable retinoblastoma may develop SPM. They should receive counselling and enrolment in long-term survivor programmes that have access to specific screening, follow-up, and treatment.

Epidemiology

The incidence, localization, and type of SPM is retinoblastoma treatment–type dependent. EBRT is especially associated with a 36% cumulative incidence of sarcoma in the irradiation field {15800318;30031154}. Chemotherapy plus EBRT further increases the risk of SPMs {25185089}.

Etiology

Heritable retinoblastoma is caused by a germline pathogenic RB1 mutation (first hit) followed by a somatic alteration (second hit), and originates from developing retinal cells, probably cone progenitors {25252974;30213853}. Although this event is required for initiation, it may not be independently sufficient; presumed benign retinal lesions (see section Retinocytoma) also have biallelic RB1 mutation {18211953}. Germline pathogenic RB1 variants are heterogeneous. Low- and regular-penetrance alterations show variation of phenotypic expression for retinoblastoma and for SPM in families {32434110}. Patients with somatic mutational mosaicism more often develop a milder phenotype and unilateral retinoblastoma compared with patients with non-mosaic variants {19280657}. Large deletions on the q-arm of chromosome 13 including RB1 and neighbouring genes cause a rare genetic disorder referred to as “13q deletion syndrome”. Depending on the localization and the size of the deletion, these patients may show a neurodevelopmental delay, characteristic facial features, organ malformation, and retinoblastoma {21505449;24858910}.

The origin of intracranial (pineal) retinoblastoma, somatic or germline associated, is probably different from other pineal tumours based on divergent DNA methylation profiles, as well as finding common characteristics specifically between intracranial and ocular retinoblastoma (e.g. chromosome 16 loss and 1q gain) {31820118;31768671}.

Pathogenesis

See section Retinoblastoma.

Macroscopic appearance

Intracranial tumours are poorly demarcated, soft, and friable. Adequacy of surgical resection of intracranial tumours may be defined based on postoperative imaging studies, and the surgical specimen is representative of the amount of tumour resected (see the section on pineoblastoma in the Central nervous system tumours volume of this series {34185076}).

Histopathology

The histological features of the intracranial tumour in trilateral retinoblastoma are identical to those of retinoblastoma (see section Retinoblastoma). Pineoblastoma is a high-grade embryonal tumour (WHO grade 4) (see the section on pineoblastoma in the Central nervous system tumours volume {34185076}).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of the specific RB1 gene variant and genetic counselling should be considered for any patient with retinoblastoma, regardless of laterality or trilateral disease. Inactivation of RB1 can result from small or large DNA alterations, complex rearrangements such as chromothripsis, loss of heterozygosity, and promoter methylation {20301625;24509483;33670346;12541220}. Increasingly, next-generation sequencing is used to analyse the exons of RB1 and determine complex rearrangements {27155049;33670346}. RB1 RNA analysis may identify pathogenic splice variants caused by deep intronic mutations {24688104}. Methods such as next-generation sequencing and PCR-based assays can detect mosaic RB1 variants or promoter methylation {19280657;27155049;29851531}. In addition, capture-based targeted sequencing and relative haplotype dosage analysis of maternal plasma have been used to evaluate fetal RB1 status for paternal or de novo variants; in a small study, the prenatal diagnosis was 100% concordant with the postdiagnostic result {33143217}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: confirmation of diagnosis from ocular tumour(s); degree of differentiation for intracranial tumours; association with meningeal involvement should be included.

Desirable: demonstration of presence of germline pathogenic variant in RB1.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) has developed a clinical and pathological TNM, and new in the eighth edition is the hereditary classification system (cTMNH) for retinoblastoma. The hereditary category includes HX (unknown or insufficient evidence of a germline pathogenic RB1 gene variant), H0 (normal RB1 alleles in blood tested), and H1 (bilateral retinoblastoma, trilateral retinoblastoma, patient with family history of retinoblastoma, or molecular definition of germline pathogenic RB1 gene variant).

Prognosis and prediction

Trilateral retinoblastoma has a poor prognosis. Prognosis is related to age at presentation with children < 6 years of age having the worse outcomes (~6% survival) {32187454}. The mortality of SPM in patients with heritable retinoblastoma syndrome remains about 50% {31904159;19351917}.

Current screening recommendations include annual history and physical examination with a focus on bony structures and radiotherapy field, and regular skin examination {32422154}. Each tumour type presents at different ages – osteosarcomas (first and second decades of life), soft tissue sarcomas (10–50 years after retinoblastoma diagnosis), and epithelial tumours and melanoma (after the second decade of life). Screening should continue through the life of the patient as they may develop more than one SPM.

Essential:

Intraocular small blue round cell tumour with typical histopathology and demonstrated germline alteration of the RB1 gene

OR

Trilateral occurrence of intraocular small blue round cell tumours in both eyes and in the pineal region
