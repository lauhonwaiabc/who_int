8.1.2.5B Orbital venous malformation

Definition

Orbital venous malformations (VMs) are defined as a vein-to-vein diversion of blood in the absence of an interceding arterial bed.

ICD-O coding

9122/0 Orbital venous malformation

ICD-11 coding

LA90.2Z Peripheral venous malformations, unspecified

Related terminology

Not recommended: orbital varix; venous haemangioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Owing to developmental defects, VMs can affect any tissue and organ. Involvement of the skin and subcutis is frequently seen as well as deeper tissues within the orbit. They can be complex, crossing into associated anatomical regions including the intracranial space, the infratemporal fossa, and the temporalis fossa. Intraosseous components are not uncommon {34166070;26055853}. They are usually unifocal and infrequently multifocal.

Clinical features

VMs can present as bluish subdermal lesions when superficial and, though they can be noted at birth, typically present later in life. Subcutaneous lesions are typically soft and can be emptied by digital pressure. If found deeper in the orbit, Valsalva manoeuvre may lead to expansion of the lesion, manifesting as proptosis, strabismus, and globe dystopia. As they are venous in nature, there is typically no thrill, bruit, or pulsation. Symptomatology relates to repeated cycles of filling and emptying, leading to Valsalva-induced pain and globe dystopia during filling and enophthalmos when emptying owing to chronic tissue atrophy. Acute intralesional thrombosis may occur and this can be painful, with associated signs of orbital compression including optic neuropathy and strabismus {20888460;32049943;26077629;33777625;12789590;34798656;20870869}.

Epidemiology

VMs affect as much as 1.5% of the population. They are the most common slow-flow vascular malformations and a common orbital disease {20870869;35047613}.

Etiology

Venous malformations arise during embryogenesis owing to mosaic mutations or, more rarely, germline mutations leading to developmental errors in the vascular system {34166070;31950476;26839221}. VMs are mainly sporadic and rarely inherited, and can be associated with lymphatic or other tissue in complex malformations (lymphatic-venous malformation, capillary lymphatic-venous malformations, etc.) {20870869}.

VMs are associated with cutaneomucosal venous malformation (distensible and non-distensible; autosomal dominant inheritance), blue rubber bleb naevus (Bean syndrome; sporadic), and glomuvenous malformation (sporadic or autosomal dominant inheritance) {20870869;15313813}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic systemic cases show activating somatic mosaic mutations predominantly in TEK. Inherited malformations harbour a combination of a germline mutation with a postzygotic/somatic second hit leading also to overexpression and autophosphorylation of the corresponding endothelial receptor tyrosine kinase TIE2. TIE2 activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway. In a few sporadic cases PIK3CA is an alternatively affected gene. Glomuvenous malformations harbour inactivating mutations in GLMN {34166070;26055853;33069286}. Orbital lesions have not been studied molecularly.

Macroscopic appearance

These lesions show a vascular/haemorrhagic circumscribed or diffuse spongy lesion with variable fibrous tissue, although they often collapse after removal. The cavities are communicating, separated by septa. Thrombi (often calcified) may be observed.

Histopathology

The lesions comprise an abnormal collection of enlarged veins that are superficial or deep, diffuse or localized, and solitary or multiple. Veins show a variable diameter and a variably thick smooth muscle layer, absence of an internal elastic lamina, and inconspicuous endothelium. Small venules and capillaries may also be present. Thrombi and phleboliths with signs of recanalization are relatively common. The presence of Masson lesions (intravascular papillary endothelial hyperplasia) can be confused with malignancy {31950476;26055853;26839221}.

In glomuvenous malformations the media of the veins contain cuboidal glomus cells with relatively monomorphic round nuclei. The glomus cells can also be seen in the wall of arteries and capillaries. They are highlighted by SMA immunohistochemistry {26055853}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: slow-flow vascular lesion with characteristics described in Clinical features; histological features of a dilated venous channel.

Desirable: Valsalva-augmented CT can be useful in diagnosis and characterization; phleboliths common by imaging; MRI may be additionally helpful in cases of combined lymphatic-venous malformation.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Treatment is indicated for improvement of quality of life and to prevent or treat complications. Embolization, sclerotherapy, and targeted treatment (e.g. rapamycin) with or without surgery can be performed {10366093;24614549}. Recanalization and recurrence is common with intravascular therapy alone {20870869;34166070;34798656;33777625}.
