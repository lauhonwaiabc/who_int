10.1.6.5 Haemangioblastoma

Definition

Haemangioblastoma of the retina is a vascular tumour defined by unique stromal cells that have characteristic histological, immunohistochemical, and molecular features (e.g. VHL alterations).

ICD-O coding

9161/1 Haemangioblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y & XH6810 Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa & Haemangioblastoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: von Hippel’s tumour; retinal capillary haemangioma; angiomatosis retinae; angioblastoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Haemangioblastomas usually occur in the retina, where they are most common in the temporal quadrants {12359597}. Lesions can be located in association with the optic disc (juxtapapillary) or within the retina elsewhere (extrapapillary) {31259811}. Haemangioblastomas can also occur in the optic nerve head, retrobulbar optic nerve {17057815;18395800;12359597}, and rarely in the orbit {18209654}.

Clinical features

Small retinal haemangioblastomas appear as flat or slightly raised clumps of capillary-sized vessels. Juxtapapillary lesions have a similar appearance, but they often have an indistinct margin where they abut the optic nerve, and they are often associated with serous retinal detachment. Larger haemangioblastomas are elevated, and lipid exudate surrounds the lesion because of vascular leakage. The dense capillary plexus acts as a shunt, causing the feeding artery and draining vein to become dilated, resulting in a very characteristic clinical appearance. Larger haemangioblastomas often also have a fibrotic so-called glial component, and the overlying vitreous may become adherent to the lesion. Vascular leakage may progress to the point of exudative retinal detachment, with the vitreous adhesion providing a tractional component to the detachment. A small proportion of patients develop retinal neovascularization {17057815}. In the most extreme cases, progressive exudative retinal detachment can lead to phthisis bulbi.

Epidemiology

Retinal haemangioblastomas can occur sporadically or as part of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome (VHL) along with CNS haemangioblastomas (see section Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome). Sporadic tumours are generally solitary and unilateral, whereas haemangioblastomas occurring in VHL are frequently multiple with bilateral involvement. The median patient age at diagnosis of sporadic cases has been reported to be about 40 years, and older than those with VHL, which typically arises within the second decade of life {12359597;28972023}. There is equal sex distribution {17057815;12359597}. Approximately 50% of patients with VHL mutations develop retinal haemangioblastoma {17057815}.

Etiology

Retinal haemangioblastomas occur in 45% of patients with partial deletions, simple missense mutations, or nonsense mutations of the VHL gene at chromosome 3p25.3; they are much less common in patients with complete deletion of the VHL gene, occurring in only 9% {17057815}. In VHL, there is a germline defect in one copy of the gene; random loss of the normal allele (loss of heterozygosity) predisposes patients to developing haemangioblastomas (see section Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome). In sporadic cases, presumably there is somatic loss of both copies of the VHL gene, resulting in a similar but solitary tumour {17057815;17846357}.

Pathogenesis

VHL is a tumour suppressor gene, encoding VHL protein, which has many different functions (see section Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome). VHL protein is lost in the stromal cells of the tumour {10326959}, leading to upregulation of HIF1 and angiogenic factors, including VEGF. Tumorigenesis in VHL has been suggested to parallel haemangioblast formation and differentiation in the embryo {18836991;9435292;16618738}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours may appear as well-circumscribed reddish-orange masses sometimes associated with a dilated, tortuous feeding artery {31097435;11578646}.

Histopathology

Haemangioblastomas demonstrate two components: stromal cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and abundant pink to clear foamy cytoplasm with fine vacuoles; and capillary-sized vascular channels. Small clusters of angiomesenchymal cells, called tumourlet cells, have also been described {17218907}. Some haemangioblastomas may exhibit extramedullary haematopoiesis, but this feature is more common in extraocular tumours. The stromal cells and tumourlet cells may express CD133, a marker present on haematopoietic, endothelial, and neural progenitor cells. Only the stromal cells harbour alterations in the VHL gene; these alterations are not present in the vascular or glial components {10326959}. This observation suggests that only the stromal cells are truly neoplastic cells {17218907}. The neoplastic stromal cells frequently express inhibin {12883249;19847606}, which has been considered as one of the key diagnostic features. In addition stromal cells may express aquaporin and cytoplasmic brachyury, and stain positive with D2-40 {20179436;22446946}. Negative staining for PAX2, PAX8, RCCm, and EMA helps exclude metastatic renal cell carcinoma {21263247}. Proliferation markers typically show very low indices.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of a VHL germline sequence variant is desirable to confirm VHL.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a non-infiltrative tumour composed of large multivacuolated and lipidized stromal cells in a rich capillary network; AND one of the following: focal or strong immunopositivity for inhibin in stromal cells OR loss or inactivation of the VHL gene OR clinical diagnosis of VHL disease.

Staging

No currently accepted staging system is recognized.

Prognosis and prediction

The disease phenotype depends on the type of germline mutation present in the VHL gene (see section Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome). Treatment has been reported to correlate with tumour size, location, degree of exudation, presence of retinal detachment, and associated fibrotic or haemorrhagic changes {31259811}. The prognosis for vision in patients with VHL is good: the majority of patients retain 20/20 (or better) vision in at least one eye {17057815;31259811}. Laser therapy and cryotherapy are effective treatments for small to medium-sized intraocular haemangioblastomas that do not involve the optic nerve. Surgical resection of unresponsive and large retinal haemangioblastomas is becoming increasingly common, with good visual outcomes {17846357;31259811}. Treatment of haemangioblastomas involving the optic nerve remains challenging. In terms of systemic morbidity and overall survival in VHL, the prognosis depends on the treatment of the other associated pathologies such as cerebellar haemangioblastoma and renal cell carcinoma.
