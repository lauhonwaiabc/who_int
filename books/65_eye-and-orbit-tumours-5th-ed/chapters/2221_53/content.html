2.2.2.1 Basal cell carcinoma of the eyelid

Definition

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a family of carcinomas that arises from epithelial cells of either the epidermis or hair follicle stem cells. It is composed of nests of basaloid cells, variably arranged.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Localization

BCC is predominantly found in sun-exposed skin. Periocular BCC arises in the lower eyelid (50%), medial canthus (30%), upper eyelid (15%), and lateral canthus (5%) {32395200;28507440}.

Clinical features

BCCs located near the eyelid margin can cause madarosis. Orbital and/or perineural intracranial invasion, seen in 2–4% of cases, presents with diplopia or globe displacement {28831360;15808267}. Definite BCC typing can only be performed on an excision specimen, rather than an incisional biopsy {25794023}.

Epidemiology

BCC is the most common eyelid malignancy in the White population. Owing to chronic sun exposure, climatic changes, and individual and social conditions, its incidence is increasing worldwide. Incidence rates are inversely related to geographical latitude and are higher in lighter-skinned populations. The risk of BCC steadily increases with age {32751327}. Exposure to indoor tanning is a risk factor for early onset {34885049}.

Etiology

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure is the most important risk factor for BCC carcinogenesis. Ionizing radiation and systemic arsenic exposure are also known environmental insults, and immunosuppressed patients develop BCC at a rate as high as 10 times that of the general population {28220485;34427560}.

Naevoid BCC syndrome (Gorlin–Goltz syndrome) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by multiple BCCs, odontogenic keratocysts of the jaws, tarsal cysts, palmar and/or plantar pits, and skeletal abnormalities {23725561}. Other genetic abnormalities that predispose individuals to BCC include xeroderma pigmentosum, Bazex–Dupré–Christol syndrome, and Rombo syndrome {22391625}.

Pathogenesis

BCC has one of the highest prevalence rates of somatic mutations of all cancers. Loss of heterozygosity or mutations of PTCH1 are found in 58–69% of sporadic BCCs, as well as in all BCCs in patients with naevoid BCC syndrome. TP53 mutations are frequent (44–65%) and MC1R variants are also implicated in BCC pathogenesis {18067130;23774526;25766766}.

Macroscopic appearance

Pentagonal (eyelid margin location) or oval excision specimens show a well-circumscribed nodular tumour, often with central ulceration and rolled-up edges.

Histopathology

All types of BCC are characterized by basaloid tumour cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Apoptotic cells are common within the nests. Stromal changes include fibromyxoid change, retraction of tumour nests from stroma, calcification, and amyloid deposition. Tumour nests may be colonized by melanocytes and contain melanin pigment. Mature keratinization is most common in nodular BCC.

Superficial BCC: This shows lobules of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading extending from the base of the epidermis, and occasionally the adnexal epithelium, into the papillary dermis {23627639;30741348}. Lesions may appear multifocal but are connected in three dimensions {3631446}.

Nodular BCC: Well-circumscribed, variably sized nodules of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading and retraction spaces are seen; disorganized nuclear arrangement with apoptosis is frequently observed in the centre of the nodules. The tumour is usually connected with the epidermis. Keratinizing BCC tumour nests show central mature keratinization. Nodulocystic or cystic BCC shows cystic degeneration within tumour islands.

Micronodular BCC: This is composed predominantly of rounded, ovoid, or slender groups of basaloid cells often scattered throughout the dermis and subcutis {34931722;35885545}. These may appear as separate satellites outlined by a thin rim of stroma and separated by normal dermis. The tumours have an irregular, tentacular, and infiltrative deep or peripheral edge composed predominantly (i.e. > 50%) of rounded small discrete groups of cells (< 0.15 mm in diameter). Single nodules may be seen distant from the bulk of the main tumour. The tumours can reach a large size and may demonstrate perineural involvement.

Infiltrating BCC: Variably sized, sometimes jagged nests of basaloid tumour cells infiltrate the normal dermal collagen. The tumour has an irregular permeating pattern of invasion at the deep tumour edge {27311865}. Perineural invasion is more frequent in this subtype {16112353}.

Sclerosing/morphoeic BCC: Narrow cords of tumour (1–5 cells thick) are compressed by induced sclerotic collagenous stroma, disrupting the normal dermal architecture. Peripheral palisading and retraction artefacts are uncommon. These tumours penetrate deeply and show an irregular/tentacular, deeply infiltrative border with the surrounding stroma. There is often overlap with infiltrating BCC, which lacks the prominent induced collagenous stroma.

Basosquamous BCC: Lesions show histologically and immunophenotypically distinct zones of BCC intermixed with squamous cell carcinoma, in varying proportions. There may be a zone of transition between the two growth patterns, or they may be closely intertwined. The BCC areas show the typical basaloid cell growth and are often of infiltrative histology; SCC zones show histological atypia with keratinization.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes basaloid follicular neoplasms (trichoepithelioma, trichoblastoma, basaloid follicular hamartoma), basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, sebaceous carcinoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma. BCC shows strong expression of p40/p63 and CK5/6, diffuse BCL2 and CD10 positivity, and focal AR expression, and is negative for EMA, CEA, and CK20. The basaloid follicular neoplasms show colonizing CK20-positive Merkel cells, stromal but not tumoural CD10 expression, and peripheral BCL2 positivity. Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma is positive for EMA and negative with BerEP4, and sebaceous carcinoma is strongly positive for EMA, AR, and adipophilin, but negative with BerEP4. Merkel cell carcinoma is diffusely CK20-positive with perinuclear dot-like positivity, and diffusely expresses neuroendocrine markers {29072946}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology may be performed if an inherited genetic background is suspected or if sonic hedgehog–targeting therapy is considered.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nests, nodules, and/or infiltrative cords of basaloid cells with hyperchromatic-staining nuclei and scant eosinophilic cytoplasm; peripheral palisading and retraction artefact to often mucinous stroma.

Desirable: in most cases, numerous mitotic and apoptotic figures.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system for non-melanoma eyelid carcinoma applies.

Prognosis and prediction

In order to avoid local recurrence, wide excision is crucial {32395200}. Tumours in the medial canthus and sclerosing/morphoeic BCC tend to be incompletely excised resulting in a higher risk of recurrence {23154501}. Locally invasive / unresectable tumours can be treated by hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib) {35003569;31077664} before resection. BCC metastasis is extremely rare; most commonly, regional lymph nodes are involved {23313228}.
