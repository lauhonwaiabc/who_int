1.2.2.2 Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is an invasive malignant epithelial neoplasm, arising from conjunctival epithelium that shows squamous differentiation.

Localization

SCC is most commonly located in the interpalpebral conjunctiva and limbus (particularly the nasal limbus), where it frequently extends into the cornea. Less frequently, caruncular, tarsal, or forniceal conjunctiva can be involved. Tarsal and forniceal involvement by SCC is seen in immunosuppression or immunocompromised states, or in advanced, recurrent conjunctival SCC. Rarely, patients can present with glaucoma induced by intraocular invasion of SCC through the limbus. This presentation is typically seen in a setting of prior surgery {17972091}.

Clinical features

Conjunctival SCC most commonly appears as a unilateral, elevated, well-demarcated pearly pink to grey nodule, which can be leukoplakic, gelatinous, or papilliform. The lesions are often associated with feeder blood vessels and intrinsic vasculature. Pigmentation is variable, depending on the Fitzpatrick skin type. High-resolution optical coherence tomography, angiography, and confocal microscopy might be helpful in clinical assessment {32344147;31163136}. However, differentiating SCC from squamous intraepithelial neoplasia is clinically challenging, and excisional biopsy is required for definitive histopathological diagnosis. Ultrasound biomicroscopy and MRI help to assess for intraocular and orbital invasion.

High-resolution MRI allows the identification of most eyelid structures and assesses local extension of an SCC of the conjunctiva inside the eyelid {32178233}. It is also important to evaluate whether the nearby structures are invaded, in particular if there is orbital invasion. Furthermore, MRI aids in the differential diagnosis of eyelid masses; in particular, diffusion- and perfusion-weighted imaging help in differentiating an SCC of the conjunctiva from other benign eyelid masses.

Epidemiology

Conjunctival SCC is a very common malignancy showing an increasing incidence in recent decades. Worldwide, the mean age-standardized incidence rate is 0.18 cases per 100 000 person-years among males and 0.08 cases per 100 000 person-years among females {24237784;8676629;9037556;9429933}. Two broad patterns of disease incidence are recognized: in immunocompetent populations in temperate countries, the incidence is higher in men and increases with age, sunlight exposure, and fairer skin {22654961;9037556;31949098}, whereas on the African continent the disease mainly affects women, at a younger age, and is strongly associated with HIV infection and variably with HPV infection {24237784;31255960}. The incidence rate decreases by 49% with each 10° increase in latitude {8676629}. Worldwide, a high incidence rate has been demonstrated among individuals with HIV {12177799;20066157;23613511} and in other immunosuppressed populations {17617273;17698951}.

Etiology

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is the strongest environmental etiological factor {8676629}. Impaired immunosurveillance also plays a role and links risk factors such as UV radiation (which suppresses cell-mediated immunity) and immunosuppression (e.g. due to HIV infection or immunosuppressive therapy after organ transplantation). Reduced immunity potentiates the effect of oncogenic viruses such as HPV {25447808}.

The prevalence of HPV infection in conjunctival SCC in immunocompetent populations has been controversial {22134594;31141610}. Although no strong causal association between HPV infection and conjunctival SCC was found when the available data were evaluated by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 2007 {18354839}, other evidence, including a recent systematic review, suggests that the association may have been underestimated {26735858;33191633}. In Africa, a confluence of low latitude and high prevalence of HIV and HPV infection probably explains the high incidence of conjunctival SCC. EBV has also been associated with increased risk of conjunctival neoplasia, mainly in individuals infected with HIV {32525572}. Atopy is also linked to conjunctival SCC and may predispose to HPV infection {31485010}. There is an association with ocular cicatricial pemphigoid and xeroderma pigmentosum {35094225;28509683}.

Pathogenesis

The frequent limbal location suggests a vulnerability of limbal stem cells to oncogenic factors, although conjunctival SCC can also arise at other sites. It has been hypothesized that limbal stem cells are particularly vulnerable to the oncogenic effects of UV radiation and viral infection {25447808}. UV radiation–induced mutations in TP53 and the TERT promoter have been identified in conjunctival SCC and its precursors {29562930;26472403;25447808}. EGFR mutations have also been reported {32833992}. Copy-number analysis of conjunctival SCC has identified amplification of the 8p11.22 locus in some cases {25491297}. Gene expression analysis and more targeted studies suggest that overexpression of matrix metalloproteinases may also play a role in the pathogenesis of this disease {26916071;23838020}. Chronic immunosuppression and antigenic stimulation are the proposed mechanisms for SCC associated with pemphigoid {28509683}.

Macroscopic appearance

Non-keratinizing tumours are gelatinous greyish nodular lesions, whereas keratinizing tumours are white/greyish with verrucous or papilliform architecture. Invasion of the cornea, sclera, intraocular contents, and orbit soft tissue may be apparent on gross examination, depending on the specimen type.

Histopathology

Invasive conjunctival SCC breaches the basement membrane to invade the underlying substantia propria. When the invasion is subtle and just beyond the basement membrane, the term “early stromal invasion” is permissible. The tumour usually arises from conjunctival squamous carcinoma in situ (see section Conjunctival squamous intraepithelial neoplasia). Invasive tumours can have infiltrative or pushing/expansive borders {7660300;24227983}. The tumour triggers a desmoplastic response. Perineural, vascular, or lymphatic invasion can be identified.

Morphological patterns

SCC patterns include conventional, basaloid, spindle, squamous carcinoma with mucinous differentiation, pigmented, and acantholytic {7660300;24227983}.

Conventional SCC shows various degrees of keratinization. Well-differentiated tumours show cells with eosinophilic polyhedral cytoplasm, intercellular prickles, single cell keratinization, and squamous pearls. The nuclei are hyperchromatic or have open chromatin with eosinophilic nucleoli. There is minimal pleomorphism. Surface keratin and parakeratin may be seen. Mitotic figures are sparse. Moderately differentiated tumours show focal keratinization with more prominent nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic activity. Poorly differentiated tumours show very little or no keratinization, higher N:C ratios, and higher degrees of nuclear pleomorphism and increased mitoses {7660300;24227983}.

Basaloid SCC is poorly differentiated, with scant basophilic cytoplasm, a high N:C ratio, and poorly defined cell boundaries, with oval to spindle hyperchromatic nuclei without prominent nucleoli, and shows apoptosis and tumour necrosis {7660300;24227983}. Cases associated with high-risk HPV are usually basaloid {20062602;31485010;31649051}.

Spindle SCC is poorly differentiated, comprising pleomorphic spindle-shaped cells with tapering eosinophilic cytoplasm, with poorly defined cell boundaries, arranged as solid sheets or in a storiform manner. Mitotic figures are conspicuous. Areas of conventional SCC and dysplastic epithelium may be present {7660300;24227983;27380985}.

SCC with mucinous differentiation (formerly mucoepidermoid carcinoma) shows conventional SCC with focal intracytoplasmic mucin, resembling signet-ring cells highlighted by mucin stains. There is no true gland formation. SCC in situ or conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia with mucinous differentiation is usually present (see section Conjunctival squamous intraepithelial neoplasia and section Adenosquamous carcinoma).

Pigmented invasive SCC is found in patients with Fitzpatrick type IV–VI skin types and shows conventional SCC with hyperplastic melanocytes and melanophages among the tumour cells, and melanosomes in some SCC cells {974995;6677848}.

Acantholytic SCC is characterized by acantholytic pseudolumina that contain acantholytic and dyskeratotic cells with debris {9505827}.

Immunohistochemistry

This is only required for diagnosis in poorly differentiated tumours. SCC is positive for broad-spectrum and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins {1695722;25103648;20062602}, EMA {25103648}, and p63 {16021499;16328355}, and negative for AR {25103648}. In spindle cell carcinoma, the cytokeratin positivity can be focal, with other spindle cells exhibiting SMA and calponin positivity {1695722;27380985}. Block-like p16 positivity can be predictive of high-risk HPV infection {31932683;31649051} but has poor specificity for a diagnosis of SCC {31649051;34985048}.

Differential diagnosis

For basaloid SCC the main differentials are poorly differentiated sebaceous carcinoma, and cutaneous basal cell carcinoma invading the conjunctiva. For spindle cell carcinoma, the main differentials are spindle cell melanoma and atypical fibroxanthoma. For SCC with mucinous differentiation, the main differential is adenosquamous carcinoma (see section Adenosquamous carcinoma).

Cytology

Features suggestive of invasive SCC on exfoliative {6990906;835082;7660300} and impression cytology {11241267;16299150;7867466;11159477;18362650;11423469;21498806;7660300} include atypical nuclei, tadpole and spindle cells, and hyperkeratinization with a background of tumour diathesis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant. While routine HPV-specific testing is not recommended in conjunctival SCC, it can occasionally be helpful for diagnostic purposes.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant tumour arising in the conjunctiva with squamous differentiation that invades the substantia propria.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry for spindle SCC and basaloid SCC to exclude morphologically similar entities.

Staging

SCC is staged using the most recent American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification system for conjunctival carcinomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Conjunctival SCC has a relatively high rate of local recurrence (~10–40%) and the recurrence is often associated with positive surgical margins and larger tumour size {9429933;11815342;22189448;29863543;34985048;35094225}. The reported incidence of lymph node metastasis is up to 1–2% {22189448;34985048;35094225}. The prognosis is generally favourable; the overall risk of tumour-related death is nearly 2% {34985048;35094225}. In a series of 38 cases of T3/4 conjunctival SCC, the reported rates of metastasis and death due to tumour were 13% and 5%, respectively {24858526}. The significance of high-risk HPV infection for worse prognosis or higher risk of recurrence has been controversial {22942158;31141610;31649051;34985048}. Spindle cell carcinoma is thought to have aggressive biological behaviour, but its rarity has not permitted accurate prognostication {23960894}.
