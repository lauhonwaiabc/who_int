8.1.3.2 Teratoma

Definition

Teratomas are a family of embryonic tumours comprising tissues derived from all three germ cell layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm.

ICD-O coding

9080/0 Mature teratoma

9080/3 Immature teratoma

9080/3 Malignant teratoma

ICD-11 coding

2F36.3 & XH3GV5 Teratoma of orbit & Teratoma, benign

2F36.3 & XH7YZ9 Teratoma of orbit & Teratoma, malignant, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mature teratoma; immature teratoma; adult teratoma; infantile (congenital) teratoma

Localization

Lesions are found in the orbit and occasionally within the eye.

Clinical features

Congenital (infantile) orbital teratomas present in healthy newborns as rapidly growing, tense, and fluctuating masses causing massive, unilateral proptosis with chemosis, exposure keratopathy, and eyelid distension with elongation of the palpebral fissure {34963454;28275597;2581161}. Intracranial extension, although rare, has been documented and may be seen at presentation or occur later {2581161;27526235;20438911}.

Adult orbital teratomas are mostly unilateral and present as mobile, non-tender, slow-growing masses that are easily dissectible from the surrounding tissue {31238481}.

At imaging, teratomas are well circumscribed and cystic, revealing hyperintense T1- and T2-weighted sequences due to the sebaceous content on MRI {31238481}. Fat attenuation in a cyst is diagnostic of mature cystic teratoma. Cartilage, calcification, and fibrosis result in echogenic foci.

Epidemiology

True congenital (infantile) orbital teratomas represent 0.8–1.3% of childhood orbital tumours {34963454}. Adult orbital teratomas are extremely rare compared with those in infants; they mostly involve the lacrimal gland {31238481;23895389}. No definitive sex bias has been observed.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Origin from a primordial germ cell is the most likely possibility. The exact stimuli for differentiation and mechanisms of development are not yet elucidated.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours are soft to firm, and frequently nodular. The cut sections show heterogeneity due to the presence of several tissue types and usually appear solid and cystic. Solid areas are firm to soft and grey-white to tan. Cysts are filled with serous, mucoid, or keratinous material. Rokitansky tubercles, from which hair typically arises, may be seen in the wall. Cartilage, bone, and tooth may be identified grossly.

Histopathology

Ectodermal derivatives include squamous epithelium, cutaneous adnexal structures, hair, and tooth enamel; neuroectodermal tissues are glia, ependyma, and cerebellum. Mesodermal derivatives include adipose, bone, cartilage, and smooth muscle. Endodermal derivatives include gastrointestinal and respiratory/bronchial epithelium, thyroid, pancreas, and salivary glands. Stroma may show fibroblastic, myofibroblastic, smooth muscle, and even skeletal muscle differentiation. Fat necrosis and foreign body reaction to keratin may be present.

Immature teratomas contain variable amounts of immature tissues, mostly neuroectodermal tubules and rosettes. Immature orbital teratomas have not been described in adults.

Cytology

Not reported

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: tissue derived from at least two of the three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, or endoderm); the tissue types may range from mature and completely differentiated to immature and undifferentiated.

Staging

Pure extragonadal teratomas are benign and are not staged.

Prognosis and prediction

Since most orbital teratomas are benign, complete surgical excision is the treatment of choice. Given that infantile teratomas may extend intracranially or recur or undergo malignant transformation, close follow-up is recommended {22826728;28275597;23619505;15110666;14685907;2581161;27526235;20438911}.
