12.0.1.14 Sturge-Weber syndrome

Definition

Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) is a congenital neurocutaneous condition due to a somatic mosaic activating mutation, most often in GNAQ, affecting small blood vessels and capillaries. There is no clear evidence that it is hereditary.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

185300 Sturge–Weber syndrome; SWS

ICD-11 coding

LD23 Syndromes with vascular anomalies as a major feature

Related terminology

Acceptable: encephalofacial angiomatosis; encephalotrigeminal angiomatosis; craniofacial angiomatosis.

Subtype(s)

Sturge–Weber syndrome types 1, 2, 3, 4 (see <<34822>>Table 13.04)

Localization

The vascular anomaly (capillary malformation [CM]; see #34822Table 13.04) that characterizes SWS can impact as many as three organ systems, including the eye (choroid, episclera, conjunctiva, anterior segment) {27257371;31313748}, skin {15295918;33175124}, and brain (leptomeninges) {23907585;16505293;21712663}.

Clinical features

Choroid CM (diffuse choroidal haemangioma) occurs in 20–70% of SWS patients {15763200;1287171}. Patients may experience blurry, distorted, or declining vision related to choroidal thickening and secondary retinal and retinal pigment epithelium changes from exudation {24658736;29403070;12689885}.

Conjunctival and episcleral CMs appear as red, dilated, tortuous, and elevated vessels {27257371;15763200;1287171}.

Anterior segment abnormalities of eyes with SWS include iris heterochromia and malformations in the anterior chamber angle {7523256}. Anterior chamber angle malformations, increased episcleral venous pressure related to CM, and accelerated ageing of the trabecular meshwork are some of the proposed mechanisms for pathogenesis of glaucoma in SWS {27257371;34847393}.

Cutaneous CM appears as discolouration of the skin, ranging from pink to red to deep purple. It can appear flat, but with time may develop vascular ectasias producing a nodular appearance and superficial blebbing {33175124}. The characteristic SWS cutaneous lesion, facial CM (port-wine stain), is usually unilateral and follows the distribution of the first branch of the trigeminal nerve and the embryonic vasculature in this area. SWS-associated CMs less frequently involve the trunk and extremities.

Leptomeningeal CM most commonly involves the parietal and occipital lobes and is best diagnosed by MRI {23907585}. Venous stasis within the malformation may lead to hypoxia and ischaemia marked by dystrophic calcification and cortical necrosis. Neurological manifestations are variable and include headaches, hemiparesis/hemiplegia, visual field deficits (if involving the occipital cortex), recurrent stroke-like episodes / transient ischaemic attacks, and seizures of varying degrees of severity {21712663;3598114;7769179}. Cognitive effects are highly variable ranging from unaffected to mild learning disabilities to severe cognitive impairments. Behavioural problems have been reported including attention problems and mood issues {23000106}.

SWS is associated with endocrine dysfunction, including central hypothyroidism and an increased risk of growth hormone deficiency {16551788}. This presents as childhood growth below the fifth percentile or height significantly below the mean parental height.

Epidemiology

The incidence of SWS is estimated at 1 in 20 000–50 000 births. There is no sex predilection and individuals of any ethnicity or race are affected equally.

Etiology

The congenital vascular anomalies characterizing SWS are due to a gain-of-function somatic mosaic mutation in GNAQ at chromosome 9q21.2. Approximately 90% of SWS patients have a specific GNAQ variant, NM_002072.5:c.548G>A, predicted to result in p.R183Q, which has been found in affected tissues at a mosaicism level of 1–18% {23656586}. This leads to activation of downstream MAPK signalling, resulting in vascular anomalies {23656586}. The p.R183Q alteration has also been detected in skin tissue from patients with non-syndromic port-wine stains. Recently, a mosaic activating variant, NM_002067.5:c.547C>T p.(R183C), in GNA11, a gene located at chromosome 19p13.3 that is highly homologous to GNAQ, was identified in a limited number of SWS cases negative for GNAQ p.R183Q; the GNA11-mutated cases may have an atypical clinical presentation {32771470;31838126;35715928;31726051}.

Pathogenesis

SWS is thought to result from aberrant vessel growth and malformations in the skin, eye, and brain due to the effects of somatic mosaic GNAQ mutation on the cephalic neural crest during the first trimester {23656586}. It is hypothesized that GNAQ mutation may affect the G-protein–coupled receptor endothelin, which has important roles in vasculogenesis {23656586}. The embryonic stage of development during which mutation occurs may govern whether the malformations are syndromic or not {23656586}; non-syndromic port-wine stains may represent a late origin of somatic GNAQ mutation in vascular endothelial cells, while mutation in SWS may occur earlier in development in progenitor cells that are precursors to a larger variety of cell types and tissues resulting in a syndromic phenotype.

Macroscopic appearance

Choroid CM appears as a diffusely thickened choroid with variably sized blood-filled vascular channels separated by thin septa, associated with dilated draining vortex veins.

Histopathology

CMs in the choroid, episclera, and conjunctiva appear as unencapsulated collections of thin-walled, variably sized vascular channels. Secondary changes include retinal pigment epithelial alteration, subretinal fibrosis, serous (exudative) retinal detachment, and sequelae of paediatric glaucoma (buphthalmos) {34847393;820013}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

GNAQ evaluation is performed using the affected tissue to identify a somatic heterozygous pathogenic sequence variant consistent with the diagnosis of SWS (although not specific for SWS) {23656586}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: two of three diagnostic criteria: cutaneous facial CM, increased intraocular pressure, and leptomeningeal CM; individuals with leptomeningeal angiomatosis only are considered to have type 3 SWS.

Desirable: demonstration of pathogenic somatic variant in GNAQ or related gene in affected tissue.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis and life expectancy for SWS is markedly variable and depends on the location of lesions and their functional impact. The extent of facial cutaneous CM may be predictive of neurological disability {28711177;30853154;33677229}. Bilateral brain involvement and early onset of intractable seizures before 1 year of life are clinical features associated with worse outcomes. Intellectual disability occurs in approximately half of individuals with SWS and is reportedly severe in a third of patients {8216540;7645596}. Screening and earlier detection of lesions may be beneficial to patients. Published guidelines for surveillance are available {33175124;30020536;29803545}.
