2.2.1.5 Actinic keratosis

Definition

Actinic keratosis (AK) is a very common neoplastic intraepidermal squamous proliferative lesion associated with ultraviolet (UV) light exposure and characterized by squamous dysplasia. AK may remain stable, regress spontaneously, or progress to invasive squamous cell carcinoma.

ICD-O coding

8070/0 Actinic keratosis

8070/0 Proliferative actinic keratosis

8070/0 Hypertrophic actinic keratosis

ICD-11 coding

EK90.0 Actinic keratosis

Related terminology

Acceptable: solar keratosis.

Not recommended: keratinocytic intraepidermal neoplasia; bowenoid solar keratosis; senile keratosis.

Subtype(s)

Proliferative actinic keratosis; hypertrophic actinic keratosis; lichenoid actinic keratosis; pigmented actinic keratosis

Localization

AK arises on sun-exposed skin, such as periocular skin, including the lid margin.

Clinical features

AKs are erythematous scaly patches and plaques, often with irregular fuzzy margins, in patients with sun-damaged skin.

Epidemiology

AK is diagnosed at older ages (> 60 years) and represents the third most often diagnosed skin disease {33820677}. There is no known sex predisposition.

Etiology

UV radiation exposure is the main risk factor. Other predisposing factors are fair skin (Fitzpatrick type I and type II), immunosuppression, and genetic diseases such as xeroderma pigmentosum with autosomal recessive inherited impaired DNA repairing mechanisms {32012240}.

Pathogenesis

AKs have a UV radiation–induced high mutation burden (43.5/Mb) and UV radiation signatures (cytosine to thymidine transition at dipyrimidine sites). Copy-number alterations, mutation signatures, and patterns of driver gene mutations are similar to those of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma. The most common alterations are TP53, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, and FAT1 mutations, and a loss of heterozygosity of p16 {29405222;33482222;7923098}.

Macroscopic appearance

AKs appear as erythematous, scaly patches and plaques, often with irregular fuzzy margins.

Histopathology

Skin with atrophic or hyperplastic epidermis, hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis, dyskeratosis, apoptosis, and dermal elastoid degeneration is seen. There is neoplastic keratinocyte proliferation with enlarged, pleomorphic, hyperchromatic nuclei and increased mitosis. Dysplastic cells replacing the mid to upper epidermal layers and mitosis throughout the epidermis represent high-grade dysplasia in an AK and should be mentioned. Atrophic, bowenoid, and acantholytic patterns may be recognized. Moderate atypical melanocytes may be increased.

Immunohistochemistry

p53 and Ki-67 immunohistochemistry is useful proof of AK, or it can be used to assist in the grading of dysplasia, if this is difficult. p40 and SOX10 staining is recommended to avoid an incorrect diagnosis of lentigo maligna in pigmented AK.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, seborrhoeic keratosis {32012240}, lentigo maligna, inflammatory skin disease such as discoid lupus erythematosus, and psoriasis {15837871}. Squamous cell carcinoma in situ is characterized by full-thickness dysplasia (see the relevant section in the Skin tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Skin Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]]).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraepidermal squamous dysplasia that is not full thickness.

Staging

Prognosis and prediction

AK is a precursor of squamous cell carcinoma {27500794}. At least two thirds of squamous cell carcinomas arise from AK. However, the estimated progression rate is < 0.075% per lesion per year and about 0.53% if the patient already had squamous cell carcinoma elsewhere. At the same time, single lesions have frequently been found to regress (15–53% per year), but also to recur (long-term recurrence rates > 50%) {23647091}.
