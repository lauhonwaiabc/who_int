1.3.2.2 Conjunctival melanoma

Definition

Conjunctival melanoma is an invasive malignant melanocytic neoplasm arising in the conjunctiva.

ICD-O coding

8720/3 Conjunctival melanoma

ICD-11 coding

2D00.0 Melanoma of conjunctiva

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Conjunctival melanoma can affect any part of the conjunctiva (i.e. bulbar, forniceal, palpebral, caruncular/plical). Most often it presents on the bulbar surface, near the limbus. Invasion of corneal tissue, eyelid margin, sclera, or orbit may occur in advanced tumours {20723990}.

Clinical features

Lesions vary in size, configuration, and colour. Nodular lesions may be well circumscribed, while flat lesions with irregular margins can be difficult to delineate, particularly in cases with surrounding primary acquired melanosis / conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesion. The colour of lesions may range from amelanotic (pale or pink) to darker shades of brown or black, and can be patchy or mixed within one lesion {29777046}. No pigmentation, or mixed tumour pigmentation, is found in 40% of cases. Lack of cysts (observed in conjunctival naevi), tumoural haemorrhage, large tumour base, tortuous feeder vessels, adherence to the sclera away from the limbus, corneal invasion, and multifocal lesions favour melanoma over conjunctival naevus {28125759}.

High-resolution MRI allows the identification of most eyelid structures. Therefore, MRI has a crucial role in the assessment of the local extension of a melanoma of the conjunctiva inside the eyelid. It is also important to evaluate whether the nearby structures are invaded, in particular if there is orbital invasion. Furthermore, MRI aids in the differential diagnosis with other eyelid masses; in particular, diffusion- and perfusion-weighted imaging help in differentiating a melanoma of the conjunctiva from benign eyelid masses. Finally, in the case of a melanotic melanoma, high signal on T1-weighted images and low signal on T2-weighted images are expected, also helping in distinguishing a melanoma of the conjunctiva from other eyelid masses {32178233}.

Epidemiology

Conjunctival melanoma represents 5% of all ocular melanomas and is the second most prevalent malignancy of the conjunctiva {28399347}. The incidence ranges from 0.3 to 0.8 cases per 1 million person-years. The incidence is highest in (northern) Europe and North America; people of Asian or African descent are less commonly affected {12407149;31079055;32215588;18191091}. The incidence of conjunctival melanoma has risen over the last five decades {16033743;27009410}. Conjunctival melanoma mainly affects patients from the fifth or sixth decade of life onwards and is rare in children. There is no clear sex predilection {16033743;12407149;27009410}.

Etiology

Several studies revealed C>T transitions suggestive of ultraviolet (UV) light exposure {33383577;31647501;33431815;26657935}, consistent with more frequent development in the interpalpebral fissure, which is exposed to the sun. Since conjunctival melanoma can develop in non-exposed sites, other (unknown) mechanisms are debated {34015548}.

Pathogenesis

Conjunctival melanoma arises in 60–70% of cases from conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesions (C-MIN/PAM with atypia), while a smaller proportion develop from pre-existing naevi or are de novo {26425792;20723990}.

Pan-genomic studies demonstrated a high tumour mutation burden {33383577;31647501;33431815;26657935}. Driver mutations include NF1 (33–50%), BRAF (29–46%), and NRAS (11–26%) mutations {33383577;27009410;24170549;29559732;34359736;34071371}. An ATRX mutation (25%) often co-occurs with an NF1 mutation {35085662}. TERT promoter mutations, identified in as many as 54% of cases, and NRAS mutations have been associated with metastatic disease {34071371;35085662}. Other oncogenic mutations occur in CTNNB1 (17%) {34359736}; ACSS3 {33383577}; and (rarely) RET, TP53, KIT, TET2, CDKN2A, MAPK21, RAC1, MET, SF3B1, GNAQ, and GNA11 {33383577;34359736;35085662}. Gain of 6p is the most common aneuploidy, and 10q loss has been associated with metastatic disease {30672666}. Many other copy-number alterations have been described {30672666;33383577}. Most tumours show cytogenetic rearrangements involving multiple chromosomes {30672666;33383577}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lesions appear as flat to nodular conjunctival masses with variable pigmentation.

Histopathology

The histological features are similar to those of invasive melanoma in the skin or mucosa {21128776}. The intraepithelial/radial component can be nested, pagetoid, lentiginous, or (rarely) absent. It can extend well beyond the invasive component and involve the skin, minor lacrimal glands, and/or cornea. The subepithelial/vertical component shows stromal invasion by nests or sheets of atypical melanocytes. Melanoma cells can show the entire spectrum of cytomorphology encountered in melanoma elsewhere, ranging from small naevoid cells to highly atypical pleomorphic melanocytes with spindle or epithelioid cytomorphology. Nuclei can be hyperchromatic with inconspicuous nucleoli or vesicular with prominent nucleoli, and sometimes giant, pleomorphic nuclei can be found. Ulceration, mitotic activity, angiotropism, satellite and in-transit metastases, and neurotropism may be seen. A residual naevus may be present. Invasive growth can be associated with melanophages as well as a predominantly lymphocytic infiltrate. Increased mitotic activity (> 5.5 mitoses/mm2) has been associated with nodal metastasis, whereas ulceration and greater tumour thickness have been associated with increased mortality {31425167}. Conjunctival melanomas express melan-A (MART1), S100, SOX10, HMB45, and MITF. PDL1 may be expressed {33266349}.

Conjunctival melanoma in situ and C-MIN/PAM with atypia can extend into pseudoglands of Henle, and this should be distinguished from truly invasive melanoma. The distinction from an atypical conjunctival naevus can also sometimes be challenging, especially at a young age, notably in inflamed juvenile naevus. Conjunctival naevi can also show lateral extension beyond the subepithelial component, large junctional nests, and pagetoid spread. Locally invasive behaviour, necrosis, and mitotic activity in the deep stromal portion of the tumour are features favouring malignancy. Immunohistochemistry can be helpful: loss of p16 nuclear expression, 5-hydroxymethylcytosine loss, PRAME nuclear expression, and pS6 activation have been described in conjunctival melanoma {34198758;34268800;31247083}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Aneuploidy, assessed by FISH (6p gains, 11q gains, and/or 6q loss), has been used to distinguish conjunctival melanoma from atypical conjunctival naevus {23137666;34240653}. Nuclear expression of PRAME and loss of p16 on immunohistochemistry is more common in conjunctival melanoma than in naevi {34268800;31247083}.

As some tumours have actionable mutations, molecular investigation of lesions is often indicated, particularly if metastatic. Targeted therapy aimed at reported mutations (see Pathogenesis, above) is currently under investigation {34015548}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: stromal invasion; severe cytological and architectural atypia exceeding that expected in a naevus.

Desirable: demonstration of melanocytic markers by immunohistochemistry; pagetoid growth; mitotic activity; invasion into cornea or skin; ulceration; tumour necrosis.

Staging

Tumours are clinically staged by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system, which has been validated for development of metastasis and survival {31169891;35245602}.

While sentinel lymph node biopsy appears promising, large comparative studies are lacking {31440020}.

Prognosis and prediction

Conjunctival melanoma may extend locally into the eye or eyelid, invade the orbit, disseminate through the lacrimal drainage system, or spread through the lymphatics {11713086}.

Local recurrences occur in 29–45% in 5 years {20723990;12407149;29122819}. Factors associated with local recurrence include tumour thickness ≥ 2 mm, non-bulbar location, epithelioid cell type, lack of tumour pigmentation, and lymphatic invasion {20723990;12407149;35245602;29122819}.

The 10-year disease-specific mortality rate is 25–35% {8199108;20723990}. Tumour-related death has been associated with de novo origin and location in the non-bulbar conjunctiva, as well as nodular growth, multifocal location, and sentinel lymph node positivity {28399347;11815341;8199108;20723990;31425167}. Negative microscopic features include mitoses {23818735} and lymphatic invasion {20723990;12407149;35245602}, as well as angiotropic satellite and in-transit metastases {27020389}. Lymphocytic infiltration may be favourable, but the findings are conflicting {34015548}.

Metastatic spread may occur to regional lymph nodes, or be more general, most often involving the lungs, liver, and brain {11713086;29122819}. Factors predictive of metastasis include larger size, increased tumour thickness, and orbital invasion. Incisional biopsy and excision without adjuvant therapy have also been correlated with poor outcome {26425792;29122819}.

Conjunctival melanomas may respond to targeted therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors, or immunotherapy {31965542;34015548}. PDL1 is expressed in conjunctival melanoma and relates to the presence of distant metastases and worse survival {33266349;28903377}. Checkpoint inhibitor therapy has been reported as successful in several cases and is currently under investigation {34015548}.
