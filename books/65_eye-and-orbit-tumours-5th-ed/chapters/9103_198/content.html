9.1.0.3 Primary vitreoretinal large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Primary vitreoretinal large B-cell lymphoma (PVR-LBCL) is the most common aggressive lymphoma that arises in the eye and is frequently accompanied by involvement of the CNS.

ICD-O coding

9680/3 Vitreoretinal lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2A81 & XH86B5 Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma & Vitreoretinal lymphoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: primary vitreoretinal diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Not recommended: primary intraocular lymphoma; vitreoretinal lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

PVR-LCBLs are typically located in the subretinal space (between the photoreceptors and the retinal pigment epithelium overlying the Bruch membrane). Tumour cells are also found around vessels in the retina as well as scattered in the vitreous. With advanced disease, the tumour infiltrates the aqueous, forming deposits on the cornea’s posterior surface (keratic precipitates). PVR-LCBL may involve the vitreous without retinal invasion in 15% of cases {34439078}. Optic nerve involvement is infrequent {18954321;34036310;35210172}.

Clinical features

PVR-LBCLs develop insidiously, typically mimicking other conditions, and thus causing delays in diagnosis. These lymphomas are bilateral in 60–80% of cases and may occur independently or concurrently. Patients present with blurred or decreased vision, floaters, chronic intermediate or posterior uveitis (masquerade syndrome), retinal haemorrhage, and/or serous retinal detachment. Vitreous haze is the most common clinical finding and is characterized by larger vitreous cells that are less dense than those seen in vitritis and which may be arranged in sheets or aligned along vitreous fibrils in an aurora borealis (or string-of-pearls) pattern. A creamy yellow coloured subretinal pigment epithelial accumulation of lymphoma cells is characteristic of PVR-LBCL and may facilitate earlier diagnosis. The cells in the subretinal pigment epithelial space may resolve spontaneously, with their disappearance leaving a retinal pigment epithelium defect, producing a characteristic image on fluorescein angiography. Less commonly, PVR-LBCL may cause ischaemic retinopathy {18954321;29760948;34036310;34439078;35210172}.

Patients with concurrent involvement may have focal neurological deficits (50–80%) as well as headache, nausea, and cranial neuropathies; neuropsychiatric symptoms are common {21180644;30445539}.

Imaging

Ophthalmic imaging studies, although not specific for the diagnosis, usually provide initial clues regarding the diagnosis of PVR-LBCL. The localized spotty hypofluorescence and hyperfluorescence (a leopard skin–like appearance) on fundus fluorescein angiography or indocyanine green angiography, and focal hyperreflections on optical coherence tomography resulting from the subretinal infiltration can all enable a clinical diagnosis of suspected lymphoma to be made {19243734;34036310;34439078;34843686}. Fundus autofluorescence imaging showing hyperautofluorescence has been useful for monitoring of recurrences in known PVR-LBCL cases {34439078;35210172}.

Epidemiology

PVR-LCBLs represent 1% of all ocular malignancies, with an incidence of 0.47 cases per 100 000 population. The incidence has increased over the last two decades. PVR-LCBL occurs in patients in the fifth to seventh decades of life, with a median age of 58 years. Globally, there is a slight female predominance (M:F ratio 2:3), although this varies between studies {18954321;22661396;23307208;29760948;34439078}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Like those of other LBCLs arising in immune-privileged sites (i.e. primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [DLBCL] of the CNS and primary testicular LBCL; see the Haematolymphoid tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]), PVR-LBCL cells demonstrate features corresponding to germinal-centre B cells that have undergone a prolonged germinal-centre reaction with evidence of ongoing somatic hypermutation in their rearranged IG genes {16186327;32267931;34448823}.

PVR-LBCL preferentially rearranges the IGHV4-34 gene segment, which is implicated in autoimmune diseases, in 55% of cases {16186327;32267931}. Like other immune-privileged LBCLs, these tumours possess common genetic alterations that enable immune escape and downregulation of specific immune reactions {11071656;12203788;12471623;16675561;34448823}.

PVR-LBCLs demonstrate concordant MYD88 p.L265P and CD79B mutations {25768255;25900979;30027272;30390359;33054063;34448823}, which are probably acquired at an early stage of lymphomagenesis {29713087}. The genomic signature resembles the C5/MCD/MYD88 signature found in DLBCL-NOS {25900979;29713087;32187361;32289277;34448823}.

Other mutations include those in PIM1 (71%), IGLL5 (52%), TBL1XR1 (48%), and ETV6 (45%) {34448823}.

At the chromosome level, translocations tend to involve the IGH and BCL6 genes {33054063}. Genetic imbalances are frequent, particularly 9p21/CDKN2A deletions (75%) {34448823;34576156;33054063}.

Macroscopic appearance

Enucleation samples are occasionally received for PVR-LBCL; these are only seen in advanced cases where no definitive diagnosis could be reached via vitrectomy or aqueous tap, and the patients have complete painful vision loss. Such specimens usually demonstrate a detached, necrotic, and haemorrhagic retina with dense vitreous strands.

Histopathology

Microscopic examination of chorioretinal biopsy specimens and enucleated eyes reveals atypical lymphocytes in PVR-LBCL in the vitreous and neural retina, and/or beneath the retinal pigment epithelium. The highly characteristic subretinal pigment epithelial infiltrates (of variable thickness) consist of neoplastic B cells accumulating anterior to the Bruch membrane, which acts as a barrier to their infiltration into the choroid. There is perivascular accumulation of atypical lymphocytes in PVR-LBCL cases with the appearance of ischaemic retinopathy, often with occlusion of retinal vessels.

Immunophenotyping

This can be undertaken by flow cytometry, immunocytology on cytospins of fluid samples, or immunohistology of cell blocks {9439362;1417546;27133587;34439078}. The neoplastic B cells of PVR-LBCL are positive for CD79a, CD20, PAX5, BCL2, BCL6, IRF4 (MUM1), and IgM, and have a high Ki-67 index {16249468;27133587}. Staining for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is negative.

Although vitrectomy specimens are often paucicellular, they can be sufficient for the detection of light chain monotypia by flow cytometry immunophenotyping {9439362;1417546}.

Differential diagnosis

PVR-LBCL can mimic both infectious (e.g. cytomegalovirus retinitis and acute retinal necrosis) and non-infectious uveitides. Ocular disorders that disrupt the retinal pigment epithelium (e.g. adult vitelliform dystrophy, drusenoid pigment epithelium detachment, and confluent drusen) and diseases infiltrating the optic nerve (e.g. sarcoidosis and leukaemia) should also be excluded. Vitreoretinal lymphoma secondary to systemic DLBCL {23744124}, or T/NK-cell lymphoma {26585973;26763364} [[Jones N, Gibb A, Irion L, et al. Gamma-Delta T-Cell Lymphoma of Skin, Eye and Brain Presenting with Visual Loss. BMJ Case Rep. 2017 Jun;2017]] may also occur.

Finally, extremely rare PVR lymphomas of T-cell lineage have been reported {15806372;34198843;27002464;30321569} and these must be considered in the differential diagnosis of vitrectomy specimens with T-cell–rich infiltrates that may show atypia.

Cytology

Vitreous cytological examination is commonly used to diagnose PVR-LBCL. The atypical lymphoid cells are generally medium- to large-sized cells with a condensed chromatin and prominent nucleoli. The nuclei are often irregular in shape, with finger-like projections. The neoplastic lymphocytes are usually admixed with reactive inflammatory cells. In some cases, small, mature T lymphocytes predominate and can mask the presence of neoplastic B cells. PVR-LBCL cells are typically friable, with numerous apoptotic bodies; therefore, vitreous samples often contain abundant lytic cell remnants, often within macrophages; together their presence is highly suggestive of PVR-LBCL {1106372;19653716;28558176}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clonality analysis of IG genes enables the differentiation of LBCL from reactive inflammatory lesions. In rare cases where a PVR T-cell lymphoma is suspected, clonality analysis of TR genes is also of value.

MYD88 and CD79B hotspot mutations are highly recurrent in PVR-LBCL and offer diagnostic value especially when the number of cells is limited {25900979;30027272;32056332;33505989;33793360;34448823;35142151}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: LBCL primarily confined to the vitreous or retina at presentation; exclusion of secondary involvement by systemic DLBCL.

Desirable: post–germinal-centre B-cell phenotype (CD20+, CD79a+, IRF4+, BCL6+, CD10−); demonstration of a high Ki-67 growth fraction; demonstration of B-cell clonality; detection of mutant MYD88 and/or CD79B.

Staging

This follows the Lugano lymphoma staging system {25113753}; however, it is key that examination of the contralateral eye and brain imaging should be undertaken {27133587}.

Prognosis and prediction

The treatment and prognosis for PVR-LBCL are very much dependent on disease extent – i.e. ocular only (unilateral or bilateral), with or without involvement of the brain. Generally, patient survival is very poor {27133587}. Treatment protocols vary, and usually include intravitreal injections of methotrexate and/or rituximab, rituximab-based systemic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation {31358925;32845738;35210172}.
