8.1.2.5A Cavernous vascular malformation

Definition

Cavernous vascular malformation (CVM) is an encapsulated mass composed of numerous dilated vein-like structures.

ICD-O coding

9121/0 Cavernous vascular malformation

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y & XH1GU2 Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa & Cavernous haemangioma

Related terminology

Acceptable: cavernous haemangioma; cavernous venous malformation.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

CVMs are typically (75%) located at least partially in the intraconal space, mostly in the mid and posterior orbit {24627253;28131871}.

Clinical features

The presence of an orbital mass with development of progressive axial proptosis is the most common sign. Pain can be noted in as many as a third of patients {24627253}. Visual impairment is typically due to optic neuropathy or hyperopic shift. Diplopia is uncommon {28131871}. With imaging (ultrasound, CT, or MRI), CVMs tend to present as well-circumscribed T2-hyperintense ovoid or lobular lesions and demonstrate progressive enhancement with contrast {24627253}.

Epidemiology

CVM is the most common benign orbital lesion of adults and the third most common diagnosis after lymphoid tumours and orbital inflammatory syndromes. Lesions typically become symptomatic in the fourth and fifth decades of life. About 60% occur in women {28131871}.

Etiology

Vascular malformations arise during embryogenesis owing to mosaic mutations or, more rarely, germline mutations leading to developmental errors in the vascular system {31950476;26839221;34166070}.

CVM may be associated with blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome (predominantly sporadic and rarely hereditary [autosomal dominant]) (MIM number 112200).

CVM associated with Maffucci syndrome (sporadic) has a relationship with spindle cell haemangioma, another vascular malformation {28131871} (MIM number 614569).

Pathogenesis

CVMs have been postulated to undergo growth owing to repeated cycles of thrombosis and recanalization leading to slow enlargement of the mass {24627253}. In other venous malformations, TEK is commonly affected by somatic or germline mutations, but this has not been studied in CVM. CVMs in Maffucci syndrome usually harbour IDH1 or IDH2 hotspot mutations {28131871}.

Recently, two studies have shown mutation of GJA4 in a high proportion of these tumours {35902510}. GJA4 encodes a transmembrane protein involved in gap junctions and hemichannels in vessels, and the mutation is found within the endothelial cell component of these tumours. GJA4 mutation is also seen within cutaneous cavernous malformations and hepatic haemangiomas {33912852}.

Macroscopic appearance

CVMs form nodular encapsulated spongy lesions filled with blood. The size distribution is not documented, but most are 10–25 mm in diameter.

Histopathology

CVM is usually a circumscribed encapsulated lesion characterized by clustered cavernous dilated veins. The veins are lined by normal-appearing endothelial cells {28131871}. Focal areas of microvascular proliferation adjacent to the larger vascular spaces and organizing thrombi may be present {24627253}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: typical clinical and imaging features; circumscribed mass composed of dilated, cavernous venous channels.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

CVMs generally progress slowly. Treatment is determined by progression and symptomatology. Surgery, typically direct orbital approaches, is the mainstay of treatment; however, some have described endoscopic-assisted and purely radiological approaches {22410657}. Recurrences are rare after complete excision. Sclerotherapy can be used in challenging lesions {28131871}.
