1.2.1.1 Conjunctival squamous papilloma

Definition

Squamous papilloma is a benign epithelial proliferation composed of squamous to columnar cells, including some goblet cells, surrounding fibrovascular cores.

ICD-O coding

8052/0 Squamous cell papilloma

8052/0 Exophytic conjunctival squamous papilloma

8053/0 Inverted (endophytic) conjunctival squamous papilloma

8052/0 Mixed conjunctival squamous papilloma

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y & XH50T2 Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa & Squamous cell papilloma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: conjunctival papilloma; caruncular papilloma; squamous cell papilloma.

Not recommended: mucoepidermoid papilloma.

Subtype(s)

Exophytic conjunctival squamous papilloma; inverted (endophytic) conjunctival squamous papilloma; mixed conjunctival squamous papilloma

Localization

Papillomas can arise singly or as multiple lesions on either the bulbar or the palpebral conjunctiva, but they most commonly occur on the non-limbal bulbar conjunctiva {25527693}. The caruncle is often involved {11167228}, and occasional corneal extension occurs.

Clinical features

Tumours may display a pedunculated or sessile architecture, are often filiform, and show a variegated or cerebriform surface. They are vascular with a tan-pink to red coloration {14711437}.

Epidemiology

Conjunctival papillomas are most common in men, typically aged between 21 and 40 years. Children and adolescents are also commonly affected and may have multiple lesions with extensive forniceal involvement {23538642;14711437;25527693;11167228}.

Etiology

Most conjunctival papillomas are driven by low-risk HPV subtypes, in particular HPV6 and HPV11 {25527693;18572051}. Occasional lesions are positive for high-risk HPV, i.e. HPV16 {8567996;11423448;33901465;30997398}. Reported rates of HPV detection vary significantly, from 44% to 92% {31236424}.

Pathogenesis

HPV infection is considered to be the main stimulus of these conjunctival squamous proliferations; however, in HPV-negative lesions, the pathogenesis is unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Exophytic papilloma shows a frond-like non-keratinizing squamous epithelial hyperplasia surrounding central vascular cores and containing varying numbers of goblet cells. Small intraepithelial mucous cysts or neutrophilic microabscesses may be present {34284422}. Surface keratinization or low-grade dysplasia / koilocytic changes may occur {25527693;11167228}.

Inverted papilloma appears as a well-circumscribed, endophytic squamous proliferation pushing into the substantia propria that may architecturally mimic squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) on scanning magnification {25818286;31361247;30997398;25890626}.

Mixed growth patterns (i.e. both exophytic and endophytic) can also occur.

Immunohistochemistry

This is usually not required for diagnosis; however, these lesions are positive for cytokeratins (e.g. AE1/AE3, CK10/13, CK14, and CK19); Ki-67 highlights proliferating cells. Block-like expression of p16 is only seen in cases associated with high-risk HPV.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnosis is SCC, particularly in inverted papilloma. An absence of cytological atypia favours inverted papilloma over SCC (see section Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma). Sessile papilloma with higher-grade dysplasia is more likely to be papillary conjunctival squamous intraepithelial neoplasia / carcinoma in situ (see section Conjunctival squamous intraepithelial neoplasia).

Cytology

FNAB / impression cytology is not typically used to diagnose these lesions.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HPV detection methods often reveal a viral etiology but are not necessary for diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a benign, verrucous, or endophytic squamous proliferation with a variable number of goblet cells surrounding fibrovascular cores; absence of high-grade dysplasia / SCC in situ and/or invasive growth.

Desirable: demonstration of low-risk HPV infection.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Younger patients (aged ≤ 20 years) may have larger and more numerous lesions that are more likely to recur {23538642}. Corneal involvement and surgical excision alone without adjuvant therapy are also documented risk factors for recurrence {29675344}. Progression to SCC has been described but is rare {19692355}. Isolated reports suggest an increased risk of dysplasia or carcinoma in inverted (endophytic) conjunctival papillomas {33901465;28237169}.
