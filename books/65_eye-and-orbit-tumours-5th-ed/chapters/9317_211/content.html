9.3.1.7 Plasmacytoma

Definition

Plasmacytoma of the bone or soft tissues of the orbit is an isolated neoplasm of clonal plasma cells without evidence of plasma cell (multiple) myeloma (PCM/MM).

ICD-O coding

9731/3 Plasmacytoma of bone

9734/3 Extramedullary plasmacytoma

9731/3 Solitary plasmacytoma of bone

ICD-11 coding

2A83.2 Solitary plasmacytoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: extramedullary plasmacytoma; plasmacytoma of bone.

Not recommended: osseous plasmacytoma; plasma cell tumour; primary extramedullary plasmacytoma; solitary extramedullary plasmacytoma.

Subtype(s)

Extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP); solitary plasmacytoma of bone

Localization

Isolated or primary plasmacytoma of the orbit/adnexa is very rare. It can present as a soft tissue mass or as an osseous lesion. Superotemporal orbit involvement is the commonest {19709708;14685909}, but it can also present as a lacrimal gland mass or as isolated rectus muscle involvement {31289159}. It may rarely involve the conjunctiva and eyelid {32435472;20435269}.

Clinical features

Plasmacytoma usually presents in elderly people, with a male preponderance; however, the reported age range is 18–81 years. Symptoms and signs include orbital mass, proptosis, eyelid swelling, conjunctival mass, diplopia, pain, ptosis, and motility restrictions {11037925}.

Imaging

Orbital imaging (CT and MRI) features a homogeneous to heterogeneous unencapsulated extraconal mass of altered signal intensity, commonly with bony changes including bone remodelling and hyperostosis. It may involve the lacrimal gland and mould around the orbital structures. It may be associated with involvement of orbital/skull/frontal bones along with intracranial extension {29567915;26226234;32435472}.

Epidemiology

Primary plasmacytoma of the orbit/adnexa is extremely rare; however, secondary involvement from a pre-existing or subsequently diagnosed multiple myeloma or secondary invasion from an upper respiratory tract / sinonasal EMP or from osseous plasmacytoma of facial bones is relatively common {32435472;19229750;11037925;28582371}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown, although in 10% of EMP cases, EBV has been detected in immunocompetent patients {7675768;25556356;16308165}, and some reports of plasmacytoma show association with ongoing inflammation, trauma, or chronic hepatitis C infection {19229750}.

Pathogenesis

Solitary EMP and PCM/MM are closely related {18326524;23000906}. However, studies show that solitary EMPs are cytogenetically less identical when compared with extramedullary disease (EMD) in PCM/MM {23368088;32321465;15586381} and have comparatively fewer cytogenetic aberrations.

Macroscopic appearance

These are unencapsulated, greyish-pink masses that are soft, fleshy, to gelatinous in appearance.

Histopathology

The tumour consists of sheets of plasma cells with varying differentiation. The neoplastic plasma cells might appear mature with the presence of binucleated and multinucleated forms, Russell bodies, and Dutcher bodies. Immature plasma cells (plasmablastic or anaplastic features) exhibit a higher N:C ratio, dispersed chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. Poorly differentiated lesions with a predominant population of plasmablastic cells are infrequent in solitary plasmacytoma as compared with EMD in PCM/MM {32321465}.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumour cells are positive for CD138, CD38, and IRF4 (MUM1) with restriction of κ or λ light chains. EBV may be rarely positive in plasmacytoma {25556356;7894217}. Unlike PCM/MM and multiple myeloma with EMD, solitary plasmacytoma universally lacks cyclin D1, and may rarely express CD56 {32321465;15586381}. The proliferation index is usually low, but may show marked variation (1–90%).

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnosis includes B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma with extensive plasma cell differentiation, lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, and plasmablastic lymphoma, which may require immunohistochemistry, immunophenotyping, and clonality determination. Melanoma and poorly differentiated carcinoma might need to be excluded in cases of plasmacytoma with anaplastic features. Clonality determination helps to exclude orbital inflammatory diseases with plasma cell predominance.

In EBV-positive plasmacytoma, it is mandatory to exclude plasmablastic lymphoma, which has a high Ki-67 proliferative index (often > 60%) {25193957} and is usually associated with an immunocompromised state. When a clear distinction may be difficult, a descriptive diagnosis such as plasmablastic neoplasm indeterminate between plasmablastic lymphoma and anaplastic plasmacytoma is allowed.

The differential diagnosis from multiple myeloma depends on bone marrow sampling: < 10% clonal plasma cells is required to ascertain the diagnosis of plasmacytoma.

Cytology

On cytological preparations, small bland plasma cells or plasma cells with atypia are seen. Cases of isolated EMP tend to be more differentiated than cases of PCM/MM with EMD {33125778}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

EMPs are closely related to PCM/MM on cytogenetic evaluation. However, the distribution of IGH translocation partners might be different with notable absence of t(11;14) {18326524}. Clonal immunoglobulin gene rearrangement may assist in cases with ambiguous clonality by light chain immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or flow cytometry.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: biopsy-proven clonal plasma cell neoplasm of bone/soft tissue; no clonal B-cell proliferation; no physical or radiological evidence of any other plasma cell tumour; no evidence of end-organ damage (hyperCalcaemia, Renal insufficiency, Anaemia or Bone lesions [CRAB]) due to plasma cell neoplasm; < 10% clonal plasma cells on non-targeted bone marrow sampling; plasmacytomas with no marrow involvement must be distinguished from those with minimal (< 10%) marrow involvement.

Staging

Extensive evaluation is required to exclude PCM/MM or the presence of plasmacytoma elsewhere in the body.

There is no staging system for plasmacytoma; however, patients should be evaluated for PCM/MM with a complete blood count, serum creatinine and calcium, serum and urine protein electrophoresis, serum free light chains, radiological evaluation {25439696;29338789}, and bone marrow aspiration/biopsy with flow cytometry and/or immunohistochemistry.

Prognosis and prediction

EMP is usually indolent, in contrast to PCM/MM with EMD. When compared with EMP, solitary plasmacytoma of bone has a worse prognosis with increased rates of progression to PCM/MM, although there might not be a significant difference in overall survival. Minimal bone marrow plasmacytosis (< 10%), abnormal serum free light chain ratio, and persistent serum monoclonal protein after radiation therapy might serve as poor prognostic indicators {29338789}.
