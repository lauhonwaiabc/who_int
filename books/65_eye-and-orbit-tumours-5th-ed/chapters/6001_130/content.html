6.0.0.1 Tumours of the lacrimal gland: Introduction

The lacrimal gland is composed of lobules of glands with acini that drain via interlobular ducts into excretory ducts. The acini are lined internally by acinar epithelium and externally by myoepithelium. The ducts are lined internally by low-cuboidal epithelium and externally by myoepithelium. The most common causes of lacrimal gland enlargement are inflammatory processes, but neoplasms can also arise within the gland. There are few changes in this edition, though some new molecular and other advances in knowledge are included in relevant entries.

Lacrimal gland tumours are rare and constitute a wide spectrum of different entities ranging from benign epithelial and lymphoid lesions to high-grade carcinomas, lymphomas, and sarcomas with large differences in prognosis and clinical management {28399336;27273588}. Epithelial neoplasms of the lacrimal gland are classified along a continuum on the basis of their origin in the gland. At one end of the continuum are acinic cell tumours, which arise in the inner epithelial layer of the acinus; at the other end are monomorphic adenomas, which arise from the inner layer of the duct. Pleomorphic adenomas contain two components: an epithelial component (probably arising from the inner layer of the duct) and a myoepithelial (stromal) component. Carcinomas, such as squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and poorly differentiated carcinoma, can arise from the inner epithelial lining of the duct in pleomorphic adenoma (carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma). Lymphoid aggregates in the gland may give rise to lymphomas; these are most commonly extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT lymphoma), although other forms of lymphoma may also arise in the gland.

There are many similarities between the neoplasms that occur in the salivary gland and in the lacrimal gland, but there are also some differences in terms of the frequency of the different subtypes and treatment considerations between lacrimal gland and salivary gland tumours. Salivary gland carcinomas comprise several morphologically and clinically heterogeneous subtypes, of which adenoid cystic carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and salivary duct carcinoma are the most commonly encountered, representing 70% of cases (see the Head and neck tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Head and Neck Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 9). https://publications.iarc.who.int/629.]]). In the lacrimal gland, adenoid cystic carcinoma is the most common carcinoma accounting for about 70% of cases, with carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma being the next most frequently encountered carcinoma. Mucoepidermoid carcinomas occur very rarely in the lacrimal gland.

For disease coding, and also in the published literature, there is some lumping of lacrimal drainage apparatus (lacrimal sac/duct) tumours with lacrimal gland tumours. We believe this to be inappropriate, and potentially confusing, as the most common tumour types, the anatomical structures involved, and thus the functional outcomes and treatments required differ substantially between lacrimal gland tumours and lacrimal drainage (sac/duct) tumours. For classification of tumours of the lacrimal drainage pathway, see the separate section chapter in this volume.

Several lacrimal gland neoplasms harbour specific genetic alterations that have been implicated in tumour pathogenesis. Gene fusions in MYB or MYBL1 with NFIB have been reported in lacrimal gland adenoid cystic carcinoma. Recent studies of whole exome and genome performed in salivary gland carcinomas suggest that adenoid cystic carcinomas of salivary gland tend to generally fall into two molecular categories: the ones that have the canonical fusion genes and those that harbour the NOTCH mutation, the latter being of more aggressive biological behaviour and having a more solid histological (basaloid) appearance {34011559}.

Requirements for resection

In general, when pleomorphic adenoma is the clinical diagnosis, it is advisable to excise the tumour in toto, including with an intact capsule. Rupture of the capsule can spread the tumour, and it may then recur and give rise to carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma, or to benign multifocal recurrence that is difficult to treat. Tumours that appear nodular on imaging (CT or MRI) and do not mould to the adjacent bone are probably epithelial (pleomorphic adenoma), whereas tumours that mould to the adjacent bone are probably lymphoid (e.g. lymphoma). Adenoid cystic carcinoma, the most common carcinoma of the lacrimal gland, usually presents as a more rapidly growing mass compared with pleomorphic adenoma, and on imaging studies such as MRI it extends posteriorly and laterally along the lateral wall of the orbit towards the superior orbital fissure. In locally advanced cases of adenoid cystic carcinoma, bony erosion and dural and CNS extension as well as extension into the temporal fossa are seen. The second most common carcinoma of the lacrimal gland is carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma, which as mentioned above may be an adenocarcinoma (of varying grade) or squamous carcinoma. Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland has varying clinical and radiological presentation and can have a slow growth pattern if low-grade, and be more rapid in presentation and degree of tissue expansion and bony destruction when it is of high grade {32282452;33825717}.

It is important for the surgeon to recognize the possibility of a pleomorphic adenoma as the diagnosis when evaluating a patient with a lacrimal gland mass based on imaging. Incisional biopsies of lacrimal gland pleomorphic adenoma should be avoided for fear of seeding and increased risk of local recurrence and potential malignant transformation. In contrast, an incisional biopsy is the most appropriate first step for lacrimal gland lesions suspected to be carcinoma based on imaging findings and clinical presentation. Definitive surgical treatment for lacrimal gland carcinoma can then be carried out after diagnosis is established and is usually followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy {32680839;32282452}. FNAB is commonly used to assess salivary gland tumours, but it has not been widely used in clinical practice for lacrimal gland tumours.

Lymphomas

See section Haematolymphoid tumours.

For lacrimal gland lesions suspected to be lymphoid, a biopsy to establish the diagnosis and the exact histological type of lymphoma is the appropriate first step; FNAB has been successful for lymphoid lesions of the lacrimal gland, but again with limited application in clinical practice since open biopsies of most lymphoid lesions are easy to do, have limited morbidity, and provide larger amounts of tissue for ancillary studies. Ultra-low-dose radiation therapy (only 4 Gy total dose) {30990235} has been successfully used for low-grade lymphomas of the orbit and lacrimal gland, whereas chemotherapy and monoclonal antibody therapy (rituximab) are appropriate for higher-grade lymphomas such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma or mantle cell lymphoma of the lacrimal gland {32574779}.
