10.2.5.2 Giant cell tumour of bone

Definition

Giant cell tumour of bone is a locally aggressive and rarely metastasizing neoplasm composed of neoplastic mononuclear stromal cells with a monotonous appearance admixed with macrophages and osteoclast-like giant cells.

ICD-O coding

9250/1 Giant cell tumour of bone

9250/1 Conventional giant cell tumour of bone

9250/3 Malignant giant cell tumour of bone

9250/3 Dedifferentiated giant cell tumour of bone

ICD-11 coding

2F7B & XH4TC2 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of bone or articular cartilage & Giant cell tumour of bone, NOS

2B5Y & XH0492 Other specified malignant mesenchymal neoplasms & Giant cell tumour of bone, malignant

Related terminology

Not recommended: osteoclastoma; benign fibrous histiocytoma.

Subtype(s)

Conventional giant cell tumour of bone; malignant giant cell tumour of bone; dedifferentiated giant cell tumour of bone

Localization

Most lesions occur predominantly in the bones around the knee, sited in the epiphysis. Orbital tumours usually arise from the frontal bone {8492864;22123486;28210962;30034918;30449636} and may rarely arise in the temporal bone {9747621}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms are not specific for giant cell tumour but are similar to any space-occupying lesion in the orbit and include a visible mass, proptosis, eyelid oedema, diplopia, eyelid erythema, and neurological sequelae such as hearing loss, changes in vision acuity, and facial changes due to nerve compression.

Imaging

Imaging does not exhibit specific diagnostic features for a giant cell tumour. It is an osteolytic lesion without a sclerotic well-defined rim. The surrounding cortical bone is thinned.

Epidemiology

Giant cell tumour of orbital bone is rare {9747621;30034918}. In the most common sites, the peak incidence is between the ages of 20 and 45 years, with approximately 10% of cases occurring in the second decade of life {27526293}. There are several reports in the old literature of giant cell tumour in children of 10 years or younger but these may represent other pathologies.

Etiology

Giant cell tumour, defined by the presence of a histone (H3-3A) gene sequence variant, is a sporadic disease the cause of which is unknown, other than in phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma and giant cell tumour syndrome, which is brought about by an early postzygotic event {26700204;28476232}. Giant cell tumour–like tumours that occur associated with pagetic bone disease do not harbour the H3-3A SNV but are associated with specific germline alterations, namely in ZNF687 {26849110} and PFN1 {33599011}.

Pathogenesis

There is limited literature with respect to the presence of an H3-3A mutation in giant cell tumour of orbital bone {9747621;30034918}. The pathogenic changes are driven by the oncohistone H3-3A gene by altering the epigenome and transcriptome and disrupting the physiological differentiation of the osteoprogenitor {32967858;33067396;33110075}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of giant cell tumour of the orbit is not reported but probably shows features typical of giant cell tumour elsewhere in the body (see the fifth-edition Soft tissue and bone tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]).

Histopathology

A conventional giant cell tumour is characterized by abundant large osteoclasts containing as many as 100 nuclei set amid the neoplastic monotonous mononuclear cell population harbouring the H3-3A mutation. Mitotic figures may be copious but atypical forms are not present. Ischaemic necrosis may be present. Scattered enlarged mononuclear cells may be present and should not be interpreted as malignant.

Denosumab treatment of giant cell tumour shows significantly reduced numbers of osteoclasts and bone formation {26338802;22711702}. The features may raise concern of malignancy {26414220}.

Malignancy in giant cell tumour arises simultaneously or subsequent to a conventional benign giant cell tumour with an H3-3A mutation and may occur subsequent to radiotherapy. It can show a variety of histological features. The conventional malignant tumour is an osteoclast-rich tumour but the neoplastic tumour exhibits highly atypical cells and commonly atypical mitotic figures.

The dedifferentiated malignant giant cell tumour is defined, like dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma and liposarcoma, by the presence of a biphasic tumour including a conventional benign component of a giant cell tumour abruptly delineated from a non-osteoclast–rich tumour exhibiting a spindle cell sarcoma NOS or osteosarcomatous features. Generally, this component is high-grade but may have low-grade morphology {28505000;32866294}. Malignancy in giant cell tumour may be associated with loss of the H3-3A mutation {31285528;32866294}.

Immunohistochemistry

H3.3 p.G35W (G34W) immunoreactivity is a reliable indicator of the presence of the mutation {28505000;29241742}. Antibodies are not employed clinically for other H3.3 p.G35 SNVs.

Differential diagnosis

Other osteoclast-rich tumours include primary and secondary aneurysmal bone cyst, giant cell reparative granuloma {23511994}, chondroblastoma, and the recently identified giant cell tumour–like lesion with MGA2::NCOR2 fusion, although not as yet reported in the orbit {35181586}, as well as germline lesions including cherubism and neurofibromatosis–Noonan syndrome, and brown tumour of hyperparathyroidism {35246319}. The malignant subtype can mimic a telangiectatic osteosarcoma. Giant cell tumour of orbital bone must be differentiated from giant cell tumour of soft tissue, which also arises rarely in the orbit and has been characterized recently by a recurrent HMGA2::NCOR2 fusion gene and immunoreactivity for AE1/AE3 {4952464;33553846;33090178;33742141}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A driver gene mutation is detected in the neoplastic stromal cell population in as many as 96% of giant cell tumours {28505000;24162739;28545165;26457357;28059095;29241742}, 90% of which are represented by the H3.3 p.G35W (G34W) sequence variant. Other H3.3 p.G35 variants are uncommon {24162739;28545165;27499898}. Detection of the mutation does not distinguish between conventional and malignant giant cell tumours.

Loss of the H3-3A variant in an undifferentiated or osteosarcomatous component of a conventional giant cell tumour which harbours an H3-3A mutation represents a dedifferentiated giant cell tumour {32866294;28505000}. Failure to detect H3.3 p.G35 mutation should prompt testing for alterations characteristic of giant cell tumour mimics.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour compatible with imaging; unusually large osteoclasts and a mononuclear cell population without atypia in the conventional subtype.

Desirable: skeletally mature individuals; detection of H3.3 p.G35 (G34) mutated cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

A multidisciplinary approach to surgical treatment is recommended in view of the potential complications including ocular morbidity, orbital compartmental syndrome, and facial disfigurement.

Surgical resection is the recommended treatment. Recurrence rates vary depending on whether a wide resection (7%) or more limited surgery (60%) is undertaken. Radiotherapy is generally restricted to inoperable tumours {30877429}. Denosumab can be used for reducing tumour size thereby facilitating surgical treatment; it also reduces pain {31704134;30877429}.
