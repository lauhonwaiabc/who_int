11.0.0.1 Metastases to the eye and surrounding structures: Introduction

Orbital metastases are estimated to occur in < 5% of patients with cancer, although this may be an underestimation given that in patients with end-stage cancer the presence of orbital metastasis maybe underrecognized {17700235;33587417}. The incidence of orbital metastases has increased in the past few decades given that patients with cancer are living longer as newer treatments have led to improved survival {27662198}. Most often the diagnosis of orbital metastases is made in a patient with a previous known history of cancer, but in a minority of cases, orbital symptoms are the first sign of widely metastatic cancer, and in an even smaller number of cases, the orbit is the only identifiable anatomical site of metastasis. An orbital biopsy may be indicated to confirm the diagnosis and give access to tissue for analysis in case potential targeted therapy or immunotherapy is contemplated, helping treatment selection.

In a cohort of 118 patients with orbital metastasis treated at a tertiary cancer centre in a recent period, the most common types of cancer that metastasized to the orbit were breast carcinoma followed by cutaneous melanoma and lung carcinoma.

The usual presenting signs and symptoms of orbital metastasis are proptosis, diplopia, droopy eyelid, orbital pain, and optic nerve compression. Multidisciplinary management of orbital metastasis using a combination of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, palliative radiation therapy, and surgery can help improve quality of life {33587417;27662198}.
