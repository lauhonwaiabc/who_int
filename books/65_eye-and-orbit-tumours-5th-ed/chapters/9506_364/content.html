9.5.0.6 Adult orbital xanthogranulomatous disease

Definition

Adult orbital xanthogranulomatous disease (AOXGD) is a group of rare orbital and ocular adnexal disorders within the spectrum of non–Langerhans cell histiocytoses.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Acceptable: non–Langerhans cell histiocytosis; xanthogranuloma; adult-onset xanthogranuloma.

Not recommended: histiocytomas.

Subtype(s)

Based on clinical features and systemic associations, four subtypes are identified: adult-onset xanthogranuloma (AOX); adult-onset asthma and periocular xanthogranuloma (AAPOX); necrobiotic xanthogranuloma (NBX); and Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD).

Localization

AOXGD involves the subconjunctiva, eyelids, and subcutaneous periocular regions, and the orbit. Very occasionally, the corneal limbus can be affected {26794218}.

Clinical features

These vary considerably according to the subtype of AOXGD.

AOX may present as a solitary xanthogranulomatous eyelid or orbital lesion {25612656;16622091;36526862}.

AAPOX presents with lesions like those of AOX but is associated with late-onset asthma. In addition, it may be associated with IgG4-related disease and some cases may show reactive lymphadenopathy or lymphoma {31392499;27519554;26512617;31418752;33869889;34493168;36526862}.

NBX is one of the most frequently reported subtypes of AOXGD and is characterized by the presence of necrobiosis in adnexal and frequently accompanying skin lesions {16622091}. Systemic findings include monoclonal gammopathy and associated haematological malignancies {16622091;21658660;29568990;36526862}.

ECD is a lymphohistiocytic disorder of the orbit, often with systemic infiltration, affecting the long bones and visceral organs such as the lungs and heart. The orbital lesions in ECD tend to have a more diffuse involvement than the other subtypes of AOXGD. Because of the extensive involvement and the progressive and therapy-resistant lesions, ECD is the most devastating subtype (see section Erdheim–Chester disease).

Epidemiology

AOXG tends to affect patients ranging from 30 to 85 years of age with no significant sex prevalence {36526862}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis is unclear: it is debated whether histiocytosis is of a reactive or neoplastic nature. Multiple mutations including the BRAF p.V600E mutations and those in the MAP2K1 (MEK1), MAP2K2 (MEK2), ARAF, KRAS, and NRAS genes in the MAPK pathway have been found in patients with histiocytic and related disorders including ECD, AAPOX, and juvenile xanthogranuloma {22879539;29192649;35594918}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

All AOXGD subtypes share common histopathological features characterized by sheets of mononuclear foamy histiocytes (xanthoma cells) infiltrating the orbicularis muscles and the orbital tissue, accompanied by variable numbers of dispersed and/or aggregates of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and Touton giant cells. These infiltrating xanthoma cells often have small round nuclei and abundant clear or vacuolated cytoplasm. The cellular population usually abuts the surface epidermis or conjunctival epithelium, but a grenz zone may be present {26143425;36526862}.

In AAPOX, scattered lymphoid follicles with variable reactive germinal centres can be observed {26143425}.

In NBX, the lesions show necrobiosis, which can vary in extent.

For ECD, see section Erdheim–Chester disease.

Immunohistochemistry

The foamy histiocytes typically express CD68, CD163, and factor XIIIa, but lack CD21, CD35, S100, CD1a, and Birbeck granules {33869889;19961259}.

Differential diagnosis

The AOXGDs must be differentiated from each other because these diseases share similar histological patterns but differ in their systemic associations and overall prognosis.

Other entities such as Langerhans cell histiocytosis, Rosai–Dorfman disease, and crystal-storing histiocytosis as well as non-Hodgkin lymphoma and IgG4+ disease must be excluded (see related sections).

Infectious diseases must be also ruled out.

Cytology

A definitive diagnosis of any of the AOXGD subtypes is difficult to achieve with cytological smears.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are relevant in some ECDs, AAPOX with BRAF mutations having been described {22879539;29192649;35594918}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: presence of lipid-laden histiocytes, giant cells, and chronic inflammatory cells; for AAPOX: a setting of late-onset asthma; for NBX: foci of necrobiosis are required; for ECD: see section Erdheim–Chester disease.

Desirable: immunohistochemical profile of normal histiocytes and appropriate clinical setting to rule out other histiocytic disorders; BRAF mutation analysis or immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Since the prognosis and associated systemic diseases vary between the AOXGD subtypes, a thorough diagnostic workup is warranted. Staging is particularly relevant in those AOXGDs where an additional underlying non-Hodgkin lymphoma or monoclonal gammopathy may be present, the NBX may be multifocal, and systemic IgG4+ is suspected; ECD may or may not be present.

Prognosis and prediction

The AOXGDs differ significantly in their prognoses. Owing to their rarity, optimal treatment guidelines have not been established. Surgical debulking and steroids might be sufficient therapy for patients with AOX. Patients with AAPOX, NBX, and ECD need systemic therapy, with ECD being the most devastating subtype of non–Langerhans cell histiocytosis and having the poorest prognosis. Treatment targeted against BRAF or MAPK mutations have been shown effective in various histiocytic disorders and might indicate a possible role for targeted therapy in the treatment of AOXGD.
