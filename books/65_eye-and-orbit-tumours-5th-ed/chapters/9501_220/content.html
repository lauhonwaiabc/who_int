9.5.0.1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Definition

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a clonal expansion of non–Langerhans cell histiocytes in the skin or mucosa that share a dermal macrophage phenotype with a favourable outcome, except in rare cases with systemic dissemination.

ICD-O coding

9749/1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

ICD-11 coding

2B31.0 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Related terminology

Acceptable: disseminated juvenile xanthogranuloma.

Not recommended: nevoxanthoendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

JXGs are generally confined to the skin, with a predilection for the head and neck, upper trunk, and proximal extremities {12717244;15613853;32468628;26744443;26189188;8609257;29488239}. Ocular sites are the commonest extracutaneous sites for JXG compared with the lungs, brain, and spleen {8765620;17520744}. JXG can affect the eyelids, orbit, iris, conjunctiva/corneal sclera, choroid, retina, and optic nerve {4955207;6819503;34637661;7974193;13746162;15486764;15477475;19617803;18580271;20305521}. The most affected ocular sites are the iris, eyelid, and conjunctiva {4955207;26189188;34727265}.

Clinical features

Iris lesions are usually nodular and can often be missed clinically until the child presents with spontaneous hyphaema, often associated with increased intraocular pressure. Complications include corneal blood staining, recurrent bleeding, and secondary glaucoma {29450403;26189188}. Eyelid lesions can present as solitary or multiple yellowish painless nodules or dome-shaped papules; they can be complicated by fibrosis leading to eyelid retraction and lagophthalmos {34727265}. Choroidal JXG presents as a mass lesion, may result in vitreous opacities, and can be confused clinically with a malignant tumour {13746162;26189188}. Orbital JXG can present either as a primary soft tissue tumour or as secondary extension {2123087}. Conjunctival JXG presents as a pinkish mass with or without redness. Giant JXGs have been described {28300446}. Less than 5% of JXGs are systemic {12717244;15613853;32468628}.

Epidemiology

JXG is rare, with a frequency of about 1 per 1 million children {12717244;32468628}. More than 70% of JXGs arise in the first year of life (20–35% congenital) and show a male predilection (M:F ratio 1.4:1) {15613853;34620874;16416419}. JXG is very rare in adults.

Ocular lesions occur in about 0.3–10% of children with cutaneous JXG {8609257;9027680}. Most of the non-congenital JXGs (40–70%) manifest during infancy {9091465} with a median age of onset of 5 months {12717244}. Median age at presentation of ophthalmic JXG varies depending on the location but ranges from 0.5 to 2.5 years {26189188}. In a recent series, a higher median age (~6 years) was reported {34727265}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. JXG may occur in patients affected by neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) {15078345}; whether there is a higher prevalence of juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia in NF1 patients affected by JXG is debated {7632061;28189268;8765620;24065046;32468628}. JXG is also reported in Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome {23152840}. Occurrence of JXG in monozygotic twins may suggest genetic transmission {21091658;31768065}.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenetic mechanisms in JXG are poorly understood. Expression of fascin and factor XIIIa supports a histogenetic link with dermal macrophages {34620874}. MAPK/ERK pathway alterations play a pathogenetic role in JXG {29526544}. Activating mutations of NRAS, KRAS, ARAF, MAP2K1, and CSF1R, along with RNF11::BRAF fusion and NTRK1 fusions have been reported {28752840;31768065;26566875;28504206;28512266;31685033}. PIK3CD mutations, identified in a few cases, suggest a pathogenetic role of the PI3K pathway in some JXGs {25202140;26637772}.

Macroscopic appearance

Excised soft tissue masses are usually firm and greyish to brownish in colour, with a smooth outer surface and yellowish cut surface {34414559}.

Histopathology

Cutaneous and mucosal JXGs are unencapsulated, circumscribed lesions composed of histiocytes with a dermal macrophage phenotype, which typically spare the epidermis/epithelium {17786242;34620874}. Their histopathological appearance is related to the duration of inflammation. In recently formed lesions, histiocytes are intermixed with lymphocytes, plasma cells, and to a lesser extent eosinophils, and they may show elongated morphology {8765620}. In mature, well-developed lesions, the histiocytes are typically foamy with characteristic Touton giant cells (showing a ring of nuclei surrounded by foamy cytoplasm) observed in 85% of cases {8765620;9091465;12717244;29450403}. Touton giant cells are less prevalent in intraocular JXG compared with in cutaneous lesions {9514494;29450403}. Fibroblasts and fibrosis are dominant features in late-stage JXG lesions {9091465;29450403}.

Immunohistochemistry

JXG-associated histiocytes are positive for CD163, CD68, CD14, lysozyme, factor XIIIa, fascin, vimentin, and CD4, but lack positivity for CD1a and CD207 (langerin) {33901419;34620874}. S100 staining should be negative or weak {12717244;32187362;34727265}. Staining for ALK is negative.

Differential diagnosis

These include Langerhans cell histiocytosis, ALK-positive histiocytosis, epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma, and Erdheim–Chester disease {9338021;22859608;31685033;32187362;34727172}. Orbital inflammation in adolescents and adults can be due to IgG4-related xanthogranulomatous disease {24911364;24144223}. Necrobiotic xanthogranulomas also present as eyelid or anterior orbital lesions {24144223;34637661}.

Cytology

Cytological features in JXG can be difficult to differentiate from other histiocytic lesions that share the presence of foamy histiocytes and Touton giant cells, but immunocytochemistry may help {18833826;22090701}, together with molecular analysis and the exclusion of typical clinical features associated with Erdheim–Chester disease. In choroidal JXG, FNAB may be useful to establish the diagnosis and rule out malignant tumours {13746162;26189188}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant in typical cases

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: circumscribed lesion comprising histiocytes (commonly foamy) lacking significant nuclear pleomorphism, with immunophenotype of dermal macrophage (CD68 / CD163 / factor XIIIa); background comprising admixed diverse inflammatory cells and fibroblasts; negativity of histiocytes for CD1a, CD207, and ALK.

Desirable: Touton giant cells; low number of IgG4+ plasma cells; clinical exclusion of Erdheim–Chester disease.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

JXG has an excellent prognosis: cutaneous and mucosal lesions often resolve spontaneously, possibly leaving an atrophic scar. Surgical excision is indicated in eyelid and orbital JXG in cases of functional limitation {33901419} and should be curative {3932492;26637774;16416419;16814185}. Conjunctival lesions can be treated by different modalities including the use of steroids {18580271;26189188}. Ocular JXG may be complicated by glaucoma, uveitis, or visual impairment {32383820} [[Ceppi F, Abla O. Juvenile Xanthogranuloma and Related Non-LCH Disorders. In: Abla O, Janka G, editors. A comprehensive clinical assessment should be undertaken in patients with JXG who are suspected to have systemic involvement. Ophthalmic or intracranial JXG can be part of systemic JXG presentation.

Treatment with clofarabine, cladribine, and combined cytarabine with cladribine has been used in patients with systemic JXG with variable response {24106153;34620874}. Treatment with targeted kinase inhibition is also a newer option for those for whom conventional treatment does not work {32609843;34620874;31685033}.
