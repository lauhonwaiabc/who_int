2.0.0.1 Tumours of the eyelid: Introduction

The eyelid comprises five layers: skin, subcutis, orbicularis muscle, tarsal plate (containing the Meibomian glands), and tarsal conjunctiva. The grey line that represents the most superficial fibres of orbicularis divides the eyelid into an anterior lamella (skin and orbicularis) and a posterior lamella (tarsal plate and conjunctiva). The eyelid is replete with glandular tissue: the eccrine glands, the glands of Moll (apocrine glands), the accessory lacrimal glands of Wolfring, and the sebaceous glands (Meibomian glands and glands of Zeis).

Eyelid skin gives rise to a rich variety of epithelial and non-epithelial benign and malignant tumours. Many of these lesions are covered in this chapter with attention to site-specific characteristics. Most eyelid-skin tumours are benign, with the commonest entities being squamous papilloma, seborrhoeic keratosis, melanocytic naevus, and hidrocystoma {19384066}. Malignant tumours account for 5–10% of all skin cancers. There is considerable geographical, racial, and age-related variation in the incidence of various eyelid tumours. Among White people, basal cell carcinoma accounts for most cases of malignant eyelid tumours in the Western Hemisphere {19384066;26028084}, whereas in Asia, sebaceous carcinoma is the commonest eyelid malignancy {9590811;22754148;23732436}.

Given the concentration of various glands in the eyelid tissues, there are a number of rare adnexal tumours that have a particular proclivity for the eyelid. These include sebaceous neoplasms (adenoma, sebaceoma, and carcinoma), endocrine mucin-producing sweat gland carcinoma {16160476;33515627}, mucinous carcinoma {32890173;32452870}, eccrine signet-ring cell / histiocytoid carcinoma {21317710}, microcystic adnexal carcinoma {26974419}, and apocrine neoplasms (adenoma and adenocarcinoma). These tumours are encountered more frequently by ophthalmic pathologists than general pathologists and dermatopathologists in routine practice.

There are also certain lesions that are unique to the eyelid. These include intratarsal keratinous cyst {19875094}, a lesion that is derived from the Meibomian sebaceous gland duct, and phakomatous choristoma {5542122;34750316}, a congenital lesion of lenticular anlage that usually presents in the subcutis of the medial lower eyelid.

Looking to the future, an increasing knowledge of the molecular pathogenesis of eyelid and skin tumours will refine histological diagnosis, permit accurate prognostication, and open the way for more conservative molecular therapies rather than resorting to routine surgical intervention {23275038;32358805;33617508;33515627;34804623;31857679;30420449;34023105}.

For tumours of the eyelids and periorbital soft tissues, cross-sectional imaging is complementary to the physical examination as it can demonstrate the full extent of the tumour. Thin-section CT can be used to show the tumour matrix and is best suited to detect bony involvement. High-resolution MRI allows the identification of most eyelid structures and provides an assessment of soft tissue disease, perineural tumour spread, and intracranial extension {32178233}. Diffusion- and perfusion-weighted imaging will aid in the differentiation between the benign or malignant etiology of a lesion. PET-CT evaluates the tumour metabolic activity and detects local and distant metastases.

The ophthalmic site-specific staging system developed by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) is the main staging system used for eyelid carcinomas {28350693;32567368;30869769}.
