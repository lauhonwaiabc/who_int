4.1.2.2 Retinoblastoma

Definition

Retinoblastoma is a malignant neoplasm derived from the developing retina that usually develops in early childhood.

ICD-O coding

9510/3 Retinoblastoma

9510/3 Unilateral retinoblastoma

9510/3 Bilateral retinoblastoma

9510/3 Trilateral retinoblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2D02.2 Retinoblastoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: glioma retinae.

Subtype(s)

Clinical subtypes: unilateral retinoblastoma (non-heritable or heritable); bilateral retinoblastoma (heritable); trilateral retinoblastoma (heritable; associated with primary intracranial tumour [pineoblastoma or suprasellar tumour])

For histological and genetic subtyping, see #35638Table 5.01.

Localization

Retinoblastoma is a tumour of the neurosensory retina which can also involve the vitreous cavity. Tumour progression leads to involvement of the anterior segment, ocular coats, optic nerve, and periocular tissues.

Clinical features

Leukocoria, a white pupillary reflex, secondary to the retinal tumour, is the hallmark clinical feature of retinoblastoma identified in 63% of cases {32105305}. Leukocoria is often detected by parents, who bring it to the attention of their health care provider {9544909}. Identification of leukocoria is dependent upon ambient lighting and the direction of the child’s gaze. Larger pupils in dimmer lighting may facilitate detection of leukocoria while tumours arising outside the centre of vision may only be apparent in extreme positions of gaze {26019774}. Differential diagnosis of childhood disease that cause leukocoria include congenital and acquired lesions. Strabismus is the second most common presenting sign (10% of cases) {32105305}, while the combination of leukocoria and strabismus is seen less frequently (4% of cases) {34592118}. Clinical features associated with advanced disease at presentation include iris heterochromia (neovascularization of iris), glaucoma with buphthalmos, pseudohypopyon, uveitis, hyphaema, and aseptic orbital cellulitis {26023180}. In advanced cases, extraocular disease extension may produce proptosis or a cauliflower-like mass arising from the front of the eye. Proptosis, a symptom of advanced disease, is reported in 7% of cases worldwide and is more common in low-income countries than in high-income countries, owing to delayed diagnosis {32105305}.

In most cases, the diagnosis of retinoblastoma can be confirmed in the clinic by dilated eye examination. Ocular ultrasound is a non-invasive diagnostic imaging modality to detect the mass and the characteristic dystrophic calcifications within the tumour. However, an examination under anaesthesia is needed for a detailed evaluation of the tumour and the affected eye, to evaluate the contralateral eye, and to stage the tumour. After examination of eyelids and adnexa, the diameter and clarity of the cornea and the intraocular pressure are recorded. The anterior segment is evaluated for the presence of tumour cells, iris neovascularization, and lens opacification. The number and location of the tumours in each eye and associated serous fluid along with vitreous and subretinal seeding are detailed in a fundus drawing and/or documented by fundus photography.

On clinical examination, retinoblastoma appears as a white mass. Exophytic tumours arise from the outer layers of the retina. As the tumour increases in size, serous fluid accumulates detaching the retina. Total serous retinal detachments that can touch the crystalline lens may occur. Exophytic tumours often seed the subretinal space, resulting in new tumour loci that may invade below the retinal pigment epithelium and eventually the choroid. Endophytic tumours grow into the vitreous cavity producing small pieces of tumour that detach from the main mass (seeds) that can appear as dust, spheres, or clouds {27030103}. The type of seed is associated with diverse treatment response {18962920;32047663;33348043}. Vitreous seeding has the worst prognosis for eye preservation. Diffuse infiltrative retinoblastoma, the rarest growth pattern (2% of cases) is found typically in older children (mean age: 4 years), can masquerade as uveitis or hyphaema or keratic precipitates, and is associated with extensive vitreous seeding. However, a well-circumscribed mass and calcification can be absent {18962920;33348043}.

Imaging

MRI of the orbits and brain is performed {21850471} to confirm the clinical diagnosis, to exclude differential diagnoses {33266342}, and to evaluate extraocular extension into the orbital soft tissues and/or overt tumour invasion into the optic nerve. Brain images are reviewed to exclude trilateral retinoblastoma (pineoblastoma or suprasellar mass), which is associated with germline (constitutional) RB1 gene pathogenic variants {32061409}. Although MRI continues to improve as a diagnostic modality, histopathology remains the gold standard for assessing high-risk features associated with metastasis.

Epidemiology

Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignant tumour in infants and young children and is thought to be the most common primary malignant intraocular neoplasm {19704035}. It accounts for approximately 3% of all cancers occurring in young children. There is no race or sex predilection; however, some report a preponderance of boys over girls, which may reflect culture-based medical care bias {31173880}.

The incidence of retinoblastoma is reported as 1 case in 16 000–18 000 live births {24100372;18621794;19704035}. About 80% to 95% of cases are diagnosed before the age of 3 and 5 years, respectively {18621794;18838413}. About 7980 retinoblastomas were predicted to have been diagnosed in 2015 worldwide {31173880}. Predictions for Africa, representing 16% of the world population, are 2293, or 29% of the total. Almost double the number (4258 or 53%) is predicted for Asia, with 60% of the world population. Latin America, North America, and Europe, with 8%, 3%, and 6% of retinoblastomas, also have fewer incident cases relative to their percentage of the world population of 9%, 5%, and 10%, respectively. These discrepancies can be explained by differences in birth rates that range from 33.6 per 1000 population in Africa to 10.5 per 1000 population in Europe {31173880}. Incidence of retinoblastoma must always consider birth rate and infant mortality {27189421}, since median age at diagnosis is 24 months for unilateral disease, and 12 months for bilateral disease. Retinoblastoma cases rarely occur in older children and adults. The Global Retinoblastoma Outcome Study, a cross-sectional international study, showed in the analysis of retinoblastoma at the time of diagnosis important differences in presentation among patients from different countries, depending on their national income level. Patients with retinoblastoma from high-income countries present with early disease and are, therefore, likely to survive {27189421;32105305;32933936;35839812}. In contrast, patients from lower-income settings present with late disease, many with extraocular extension and some already with metastasis, and their prognosis is poorer, with a 3-year survival rate of 57% (52–63%), while those in high-income countries have a 3-year survival rate of 99.5% (98.8–100%) {35839812}. The incidence of trilateral retinoblastoma, which is retinoblastoma combined with an intracranial midline tumour in the pineal gland or around the sella turca, is 3.8% in patients with bilateral disease and 3.5% among all patients with hereditary retinoblastoma {26374932}.

Etiology

Retinoblastoma originates from developing retinal cells, most probably from cone progenitors {25252974;30213853}. In most cases (> ~98%; RB1−/− tumours), carcinogenesis is initiated by the inactivation of the RB1 tumour suppressor gene {23498719}. Inactivation of the RB1 gene may also be caused by promoter methylation or chromothripsis, the latter being a mutational phenomenon characterized by massive, clustered genomic rearrangements {24509483;33670346}.

Pathogenesis

Heritable retinoblastoma is caused by a germline mutation (first hit) followed by a somatic alteration (second hit), while non-heritable retinoblastoma can be initiated when two alterations arise in the same vulnerable cell. Although this event is required for initiation, it is probably not sufficient, because benign retinal lesions (retinocytoma) also have biallelic RB1 mutations {18211953}. Several chromosomal regions have been found to be recurrently altered in retinoblastoma tumours; these include large gains of regions at 1q, 2p, and 6p, and losses of regions at 16q {17437278;27115612}. The most likely candidate oncogene for 2p is MYCN (2p24.3), while the other candidate oncogenes are the MDM4 gene (1q32), coding for a p53 regulator, and the E2F3 and DEK genes (6p22), coding for proteins involved in transcription. Candidate tumour suppressor genes at 16q are the RB1-related gene RBL2 (16q12) and the CDH11 gene (16q22) belonging to the cadherin superfamily. In line with other paediatric tumours, the frequency of gene mutations is low. Besides RB1, the only gene found to be recurrently mutated is the BCOR gene, a chromatin remodelling–related gene {22237022;27126562;34552068}.

Two retinoblastoma subtypes have recently been proposed and characterized at the clinical, histopathological, and molecular levels {34552068}. Subtype 1 corresponds to a later cone differentiation, presents significantly fewer genomic alterations, and has an early onset. Subtype 2 contains all MYCN-amplified and BCOR-mutated tumours, presents a less-differentiated cone state, expresses ganglion/neuronal genes, and has been suggested to be more aggressive {34552068}. Subtype 2 has more intratumoural and intertumoural heterogeneity and the MYCN-amplified tumours display fewer genomic changes than other tumours belonging to this subtype {34552068}. Another study suggested that the small group (< 2% of unilateral tumours) of RB1-wildtype and MYCN-amplified tumours (28–121 MYCN copies) might be initiated by overexpression of MYCN. These primary tumours seem to have fewer overall genomic copy-number changes in genes characteristic of retinoblastoma than the RB1–/– tumours, although the number of tumours analysed of this type is still limited {23498719}.

Macroscopic appearance

Retinoblastoma arises from and destroys the neurosensory retina. Macroscopically, the tumour has a white encephaloid appearance that typically contains more intensely white flecks of calcification. Several growth patterns are recognized. Endophytic retinoblastomas arise from the retina, which remains attached, and invade the vitreous. Endophytic tumours may be confused with inflammation. Exophytic retinoblastomas arise from the outer layers of the retina and cause retinal detachment, which often is high and bullous and may cause anterior displacement of the lens iris diaphragm and secondary angle closure. In more-advanced cases, the growth pattern may be indeterminate or a combined endophytic/exophytic pattern. Diffuse infiltrating retinoblastoma diffusely thickens the retina and typically does not form a discrete tumour. Calcification typically is absent in such cases and the tumour often blankets the ciliary body and infiltrates the zonule. Many cases present with tumour clumps or a pseudohypopyon of tumour cells in the anterior chamber. Diffuse infiltrating retinoblastomas typically arise in older children (mean age: 6 years) and may be misdiagnosed clinically. Extensively necrotic tumours found in patients who present with aseptic orbital cellulitis are often bloodstained or orange in colour and may have additional signs of necrosis such as pigment dispersion and lens dislocation. Posterior uveal invasion manifests as thickening by white tumour tissue. Seeds of tumour may be found in the vitreous or subretinal space. They include diffuse vitreous infiltrates called dust or clouds and larger clumps or spherules of tumour cells. The latter may have a targetoid appearance reflecting a central core of necrotic tumour cells. Dust comprises scattered tumour cells alternating with macrophages, while clouds are composed largely of necrotic cells. Multiple tumours may occur in the same eye of patients who harbour germline mutations in RB1. However, it often is difficult to distinguish multiple primary retinoblastomas from tumour dispersion if the eye is full of tumour.

To assess high-risk features, adequate handling of the enucleated eye is key. A block of the optic nerve margin should be obtained before opening the eye, to avoid contamination by tumour. After fixation, the eye should be oriented and cut into three pieces in a posterior to anterior manner. The goal is to obtain a central pupil–optic nerve section including the centre of the eye, lens, pupillary region, and entire thickness of the optic nerve (avoiding cuts through the nerve). The resulting two pieces, the caps (calottes), are further sectioned (anterior to posterior with a bread-slicing approach) to increase the choroidal surface examined. As a result, the entire eye is processed in four cassettes: (1) the central pupil–optic nerve section, (2) and (3) the calottes, and (4) the optic nerve margin (blocked so that the surgical margin is cut first). Histological sectioning of the central pupil–optic nerve should include early sections of the surface cut grossly and then sections that show the centre of the optic nerve (optic nerve head in contact with retina/tumour) to evaluate invasion. Incomplete sections may hinder interpretation, possibly impacting adequate adjuvant treatment of the patients {19653709}.

Histopathology

Retinoblastoma is composed of poorly differentiated neuroblastic cells (retinoblasts) that have hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Apoptosis and mitotic figures are common. Viable retinoblastoma appears intensely basophilic. The tumour has a propensity to outgrow its blood supply and undergo necrosis. This typically occurs approximately 90–110 µm away from a vessel and is responsible for the characteristic appearance of basophilic perivascular cuffs and sleeves of viable cells that are surrounded by sheets of necrotic cells that have lost their nuclear DNA and appear eosinophilic. Foci of dystrophic calcification, which are a helpful clinical diagnostic feature, often occur within the necrotic tumour. Endophytic retinoblastoma may have vitreous seeding (dust, sphere, or cloud types) {28528011}.

Varying degrees of retinal differentiation may be evident as rosettes or photoreceptor differentiation. Homer Wright rosettes contain a central tangle of neurofilamentary neuropil ringed by nuclei. They represent neuroblastic differentiation and are a typical finding in neuroblastoma, and are also seen in other neuroendocrine tumours. Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes represent early retinal differentiation. They have a central lumen filled with mucopolysaccharide that corresponds to the subretinal space. The apices of the surrounding cells are joined by intercellular connections analogous to the external limiting membrane. Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes are a characteristic finding in retinoblastoma but also occur in ocular medulloepithelioma. Rosettes are more common in retinoblastomas that are enucleated from very young patients {19653710}. Retinoblastoma tends to become less well differentiated with increasing age. Foci of retinal differentiation are found in areas of viable tumour and appear relatively eosinophilic compared with retinoblastoma. These areas of well-differentiated retinal tumour are consistent with foci of precursor retinocytoma and often contain photoreceptor differentiation evident as fleurettes. The latter comprise bouquet-like aggregates of photoreceptor inner segments with a fleur de lis–like aspect aligned along a segment of neoplastic external limiting membrane. The nuclei are bland and apoptosis and mitotic figures are absent. Some areas of well-differentiated tumour lack fleurettes and are composed of cells with bland round or oval nuclei in a glial matrix (neuroglial differentiation). Rarely, retinoblastomas may undergo true spontaneous regression. Such cases typically are phthisical eyes with evidence of widespread necrosis that contain characteristic aggregates of calcified retinoblastoma cells. It is thought that these findings are the result of vascular occlusion secondary probably to severe neovascular glaucoma. This may be an end point in patients that originally had an orbital cellulitis presentation and went unrecognized. In some cases, viable tumour may persist in the orbital optic nerve. High-risk histopathological features for metastasis include extraocular extension, massive posterior uveal invasion defined as > 3 mm in diameter and/or full-thickness choroidal involvement, and retrolaminar optic nerve invasion. In a recent large prospective study, patients who had a combination of massive (> 3 mm) peripapillary choroidal invasion and 1.5 mm of retrolaminar optic nerve invasion had the poorest prognosis for CNS recurrence and death from the disease {31539297}. Focal detachments of the retinal pigment epithelium by invasion of retinoblastoma cells are observed frequently but do not represent uveal invasion, because the tumour cells are situated internal to the Bruch membrane.

Anaplasia has been reported to be an additional risk factor for metastasis and death in the absence of other high-risk features {30036517}. Anaplastic features include the presence of cells with large, extremely pleomorphic hyperchromatic nuclei that are angular, rhomboid, or fusiform in shape, as well as cell wrapping and numerous mitotic figures.

About 1.5% of unilateral retinoblastomas lack mutations in the RB1 gene and are thought to be caused by MYCN amplification. These RB1+/+ tumours have an early onset, behave aggressively, and lack Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes. Characteristically the tumours are poorly differentiated and contain cells with large oval nuclei with nucleoli. They also exhibit nuclear immunoreactivity for RB1 protein, which is absent in retinoblastomas with RB1 mutation {23498719}.

Tumours are graded by the growth pattern of the tumour in relationship with intraocular tissues and by specific characteristics of the tumour. These patterns translate to clinical presentations and associate with high-risk features (see Histopathology and #35638Table 5.01). Histological grades are defined by the degree of differentiation (College of American Pathologists [CAP] protocol [[Milman T, Grossniklaus HE, Eagle RC Jr, et al. Protocol for the Examination of Specimens from Patients with Retinoblastoma. Version: Retinoblastoma 4.1.0.0. Northfield (IL): College of American Pathologists; 2021 Jun. Available from: https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cancer-reporting-tools/cancer-protocol-templates.]]). Grades 1–4 represent very well differentiated to poorly differentiated tumours. Histological features suggesting MYCN amplification include unilateral retinoblastoma with a loose cellular pattern, round nuclei, and prominent nucleoli.

The pathology report in enucleated eyes with retinoblastoma must include confirmation of diagnosis, with the presence of small blue round cell tumour, composed of primitive embryonal cells with a high N:C ratio, basophilic nuclei, and scant cytoplasm, with increased mitosis and apoptosis. Tumour invasion status is given, in the choroid (and if present the amount in millimetres and if it reaches the sclera) and in the optic nerve (location and depth of invasion in millimetres measured either from the inner limiting membrane of the optic nerve or from the Bruch membrane to the deepest point of invasion at the centre of the optic nerve), along with status of the optic nerve margin, whether the tumour in the optic nerve encroaches on the meninges, and whether there is an extraocular extension (including the site). Also to be mentioned are the degree of differentiation (Homer Wright rosettes, Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes, and fleurettes), presence or absence of calcification, percentage of tumour necrosis, and other findings such as neovascularization of the iris, extensive necrosis of intraocular structures, or treatment effect {19653709}.

Cytology

Retinoblastoma should not be biopsied routinely as it may induce extraocular recurrence and metastasis. Cytological samples often have pitfalls (sampling of retinal neurons, scant material, and others) that may mislead. Diagnostic features of malignancy such as necrosis, mitosis, and nuclear atypia should be present when diagnosing retinoblastoma by cytology. Consultation with a trained ophthalmic cytopathologist is recommended if a biopsy is considered.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genetic risk subtypes are divided by absence of a pathogenic germline RB1 mutation based on blood evaluation using a high-sensitivity assay (sporadic = H0), and presence of a mutation, family history of retinoblastoma, or bilateral or trilateral retinoblastoma (hereditary = H1). Heritable retinoblastoma syndrome is further detailed in section Retinoblastoma syndrome. About 1.5% of sporadic unilateral retinoblastoma tumours have high-level MYCN amplification with no detectable pathogenic mutation in RB1 (see #35638Table 5.01).

Patients with the hereditable form of retinoblastoma due to germline RB1 mutation may present with bilateral tumours, and a delay in the occurrence of tumour in the second eye, or with a unilateral tumour {27189421}. Knowledge of RB1 mutation status is very important for patient care, case management, and counselling of family members. In high-income countries, the assessment of RB1 mutation detection is therefore common clinical practice. To perform DNA diagnostics of the RB1 gene, blood of the retinoblastoma proband and if available retinoblastoma tumour is collected, ideally fresh (kept frozen until used), but formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue may be used. Inactivation of RB1 can result from small or large DNA alterations, complex rearrangements such as chromothripsis, loss of heterozygosity, and promoter methylation {12541220;20301625;24509483;33670346}. The methodology for RB1 DNA diagnostics may differ; however, it is important to use suitably accredited laboratories (e.g. ISO15189) that have the high sensitivity and range of testing necessary for a reliable result. Increasingly, sensitive next-generation sequencing is used to analyse the exons of the RB1 gene and is instrumental in determining complex rearrangements {27155049;33670346}. RB1 RNA analysis may identify pathogenic splice variants caused by deep intronic mutations {24688104}. Specific protocols, including next-generation sequencing, are used to detect mosaic RB1 mutations or RB1 promoter methylation {19280657;27155049;29851531}. MYCN amplification testing should also be considered in very young children with unilateral retinoblastomas that have wildtype RB1 to detect RB1+/+ MYCNA (MYCN-amplified) tumours. Immunohistochemistry to detect RB1 protein is not reliable as about 20% of RB1 biallelic mutation tumours are positive owing to the presence of a non-functional protein {23498719}. Double immunostaining using RB1 and MYCN antibodies is also available {27155049}.

The use of cell-free DNA analysis is increasing as biopsies of intraocular retinoblastoma are contraindicated owing to the risk of metastatic spread. Sources of cell-free DNA include aqueous and blood samples for detection of both RB1 variants and MYCN amplification {30745306;32799607}. Aqueous enriched cell-free DNA, and to a lesser extent plasma analysis, may be useful to identify copy-number alterations for tumour prognosis based on relative chromosomal instability level {34283049}. It has also been proposed that blood cell-free DNA may aid in detection of second primary malignancies {35382685}. Single nucleotide variation or relative haplotype dosage may be determined in maternal plasma to test the RB1 status of the fetus. In a small study, the prenatal diagnosis was 100% concordant with the postdiagnostic result {33143217}. Capture-based targeted sequencing and relative haplotype dosage analysis of maternal plasma can evaluate fetal RB1 status for paternal or de novo variants; in a small study, the prenatal diagnosis was 100% concordant with the postdiagnostic result {33143217}.

Genetic counselling of the family is warranted by an expert clinical geneticist / genetic counsellor. For the H1 proband, counselling includes the increased risk for developing secondary primary malignancies. In addition, risk stratification based on proband testing directs screening of children at elevated risk of development of retinoblastoma {29056300}. Wildtype RB1 with amplified MYCN (RB1+/+ MYCNA) is not a heritable form of retinoblastoma; thus, no extensive genetic counselling is required.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: patient age < 5 years; intraocular tumour, unilateral or bilateral; histology – small blue round cell tumour with frequent mitoses, rosettes, and necrosis.

Desirable: germline alteration of RB1; assessment of MYCN mutation in RB1-wildtype tumours.

Staging

Classification schemes for staging stratify prognosis for ocular salvage and guide treatment. The current tumour grouping is based on tumour response to primary systemic chemotherapy. The two systems initially proposed were the International Intraocular Retinoblastoma Classification (IIRC) {15763190} and the International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB) {16996605}. Both group eyes based on tumour size, location, the extent of serous fluid, and the presence of vitreous or subretinal seeding, with tumour size the most significant difference. More recently, the American Joint Commission on Cancer (AJCC) developed a clinical Tumour Node Metastasis classification system (cTNM) for retinoblastoma with broad international input and data [[Mallipatna A, Gallie BL, ChÃ©vez-Barrios P, et al. Retinoblastoma. In: Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC Cancer Staging Manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. This incorporates additional prognostic features such as cellulitis, extraocular extension (clinical or radiographic evidence), and proptosis.

Staging is according to the AJCC and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) eighth-edition TNMH classification [[Mallipatna A, Gallie BL, ChÃ©vez-Barrios P, et al. Retinoblastoma. In: Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC Cancer Staging Manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. This comprehensive system includes both clinical and pathological findings, and incorporates intraocular, extraocular, and systemic disease aspects within the same system. The AJCC staging system serves to standardize data reporting, prognostication (predicting globe salvage, metastatic risk, and patient survival), and selection of the most suitable treatments. Retinoblastoma staging is also the first to acknowledge the role of genetic predisposition by including heritable trait (H) in the staging system {32512116;32526306}. Primary retinoblastoma (T) is divided into four groups, with several subgroups and additional descriptors. The stages from cT1a to cT3b closely reflect the five stages of the IIRC and ICRB. Stage cT3 (IIRC/ICRB Group E) indicates a high risk for tumour extension outside the eye and the need for urgent removal of the eye. Stages pT2 and pT3 confirm local invasion of tumour. Stages cT4, pT4, N, and M involve disease dissemination beyond the eye. When both eyes are affected, each eye is staged independently. The stage for a bilaterally affected child is based on the worse-affected eye, as an indicator of risk to the child’s life.

Prognosis and prediction

Priorities for treatment of retinoblastoma are first the life of the child, second vision, and third globe salvage. The natural history of retinoblastoma without therapy is 100% mortality {34944860}. Currently, therapies to salvage an eye have been greatly optimized to achieve 98% survival. Patients presenting with clinical high-risk features, including any suggestion of optic nerve enlargement or enhancement on MRI, are offered enucleation {31835688}. For advanced unilateral retinoblastoma in eyes with limited visual potential, primary enucleation provides the hope of complete curative resection with no need for additional therapy {34944860}. The extent of intraocular disease predicts the likelihood to successfully treat an eye with retinoblastoma. Eye salvage treatments historically relied on external beam radiation (EBRT), an option that is now replaced by chemotherapy, sometimes with focal treatment, to minimize radiation-induced secondary malignancies {26446627}, radiation retinopathy, and dry eyes. Proton beam therapy delivers an adequate radiation dose to the tumour, with minimal dose to the adjacent tissue and bone, but still carries increased risk of a second primary malignancy in H1 patients.

Chemotherapy is the basis of saving an eye with retinoblastoma. It is delivered by different routes including systemic {15747743;22084213}, intra-arterial {18342944;18854698;19734768}, intravitreal {22694968;26630319;25321846}, periocular (for locally advanced or recurrent retinoblastoma) {25014750}, and more recently intracameral {29208450;33120616}. In cases of tumour seeding, it is important to adequately treat the tumour source of the seeding. The presence and type of vitreous seeds {28528011} can impact the response to chemotherapy {25321846;28655537}. Systemic chemotherapy is paired with focal consolidating treatments to the eye that include cryotherapy, laser photocoagulation, and episcleral plaque brachytherapy {8437830}. Among some new therapies being assessed within clinical trials are tylectomy (“tulos”, Greek for “lump”, surgical resection of retinoblastoma) after standard chemotherapy using chemotherapy infusion during the procedure, and chemoplaque for local chemotherapy control {3483101}. Treatment choices depend on the size, number, and location of the tumour deposits within the eye. Regimens vary but most achieve similar efficacies.

The treatment of extraocular retinoblastoma requires the use of systemic chemotherapy, and radiation to the involved orbit and regional lymph nodes. Metastatic retinoblastoma is best treated with bone morrow transplantation {12799253;21602901}. However, the prognosis for CNS metastases remains poor.

The prognosis for retinoblastoma is excellent, with survival rates reaching 98% in high-income countries {33007338;32105305}. This can be attributed to radical improvements in understanding of the molecular biology, diagnostics, and therapeutic interventions of the disease. The life salvage rates worldwide vary between 90% and 99%, owing to the differences in age at presentation {31444587}, health care facilities within the reach of an affected child {33921566}, national income level and health care facilities in the country {33007338;32105305}, stage of disease at presentation {35093202}, and socioeconomic factors {20733021} across different geographical regions.

Globe salvage rates parallel life salvage trends with failure to salvage the globe in eyes which do not undergo primary enucleation ranging from 25% to 30% {33007338}. A paradigm shift from EBRT to combination therapy with focal treatment, chemotherapy (intravenous, intra-arterial, intravitreal, periocular), and plaque brachytherapy has improved the scope of globe salvage, especially in group D retinoblastoma {33447011}. After resolution of the tumour, periodic surveillance is warranted for early detection of tumour recurrence, metastasis, and development of second primary malignancies {33611762;16828510;32703017;32737729;33481394}. Multifocal tumours and the presence of retinal detachment are risk factors for tumour recurrence with most occurring within 5 years of completion of primary treatment {33611762}. Extraocular extension is an important risk factor for metastatic retinoblastoma {16828510}. However, metastasis from intraocular retinoblastoma can also occur. Sites of metastatic deposits include the CNS, lymph nodes, viscera, skull, and long bones {32703017}. Children with metastatic disease sparing the CNS fare better than those with CNS involvement {16828510}. Retinoblastoma survivors also run a lifetime risk of development of various second primary cancers such as sarcoma, leukaemia, and carcinomas of the breast and genitourinary system. The risk is greater for those with bilateral tumours, germline mutations, and a history of radiotherapy {32737729;33481394}. Lifelong surveillance is thus warranted for retinoblastoma survivors.
