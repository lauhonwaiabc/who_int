10.2.3.1 Fibrous dysplasia

Definition

Fibrous dysplasia is a benign bone neoplasm characterized by the proliferation of clonal fibrous tissue of osteoprogenitor cell origin capable of producing only woven bone. It may involve one or multiple bones.

ICD-O coding

8818/0 Fibrous dysplasia

8818/0 Monostotic fibrous dysplasia

8818/0 Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia

ICD-11 coding

FB80.0 Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Related terminology

Acceptable: craniofacial fibrous dysplasia (extending across more than one bone).

Not recommended: liposclerosing myxofibrous tumour; fibrocartilaginous dysplasia.

Subtype(s)

Monostotic fibrous dysplasia; polyostotic fibrous dysplasia

Localization

Fibrous dysplasia can involve any bone. The more frequent locations are the craniofacial bones, femurs, and ribs. In 631 Mayo Clinic cases, 297 (47%) involved craniofacial bones; excluding 66 (11%) mandibular lesions, the remaining 231 (37%) cases were located in other craniofacial bones {13996066}. The percentage of lesions originating in orbital bones is probably unknown.

Clinical features

Fibrous dysplasia usually produces radiolucent images, except cranial and mandibular lesions, which generate radiopaque images because of a more sclerotic bone composition {27492469}. Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia is frequently unilateral, and can arise in the orbit. It may be associated with McCune–Albright syndrome (multiple endocrine dysfunctions, precocious puberty, and café-au-lait spots). In the craniofacial skeleton, fibrous dysplasia usually involves the mandible, but can also involve the orbital, nasal, and/or intracranial regions, as well as the paranasal sinuses. When it arises in the orbital region, patients typically present with pain, proptosis, and altered ocular motility.

Epidemiology

Fibrous dysplasia accounts for 7% of bone tumours. Monostotic fibrous dysplasia represents about 75–80% of all cases and affects adolescents and young adults {16789622;19182771}. Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia makes up about 20–25% of cases, usually affects young children, and has a female predilection. Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia is associated with McCune–Albright syndrome in approximately 25% of cases and with Mazabraud syndrome in < 1% {9403710;23276185;27492469}. Fibrous dysplasia remains quiescent in adults.

Etiology

Fibrous dysplasia is caused by activating missense mutations in the GNAS gene; mutations arise in postzygotic somatic cells {17103447;9060842;11272890;27492469}. Normally, activation of G protein stimulates WNT/β-catenin signalling, which inhibits the terminal maturation/differentiation of osteoprogenitor cells {29158412}. The presence of karyotypic abnormalities and clonality for AR (HUMARA) confirms that fibrous dysplasia is neoplastic in nature {14576938}. However, the actual factor(s) triggering these changes remain(s) unknown.

Pathogenesis

Riminucci et al. {9403710} have shown that spindle cells are actually preosteogenic cells, rather than fibroblasts. Kahima et al. {18799196} have confirmed the expression of periostin, a marker of intramembranous ossification, in the fibrous component. Fibrous dysplasia is the result of the maturation arrest of osteoprogenitor cells that are capable of forming woven bone {13973557}. Cells in fibrous dysplasia may also secrete FGF23 leading to hypophosphataemia. An excess of growth hormone is associated with the expansion of craniofacial fibrous dysplasia. Moreover, lesions may grow during pregnancy {27492469}.

Fibrous dysplasia is caused by postzygotic activating missense mutations in the GNAS gene (20q13.32), encoding the alpha subunit of the stimulatory G protein (Gsα). GNAS activating mutations are detected in 50–70% of cases; p.R201H and p.R201C are the most common {22245114}.

Histopathology

Most cases show varying extents of fibrous and osseous components. Cases involving the craniofacial bones are often more osteogenic and may show extensive areas of psammomatous or cementum-like bone {8442673}. The fibrous component consists of variable cellular tissue with banal spindled fibroblastic-like cells. Mitoses are very rare. The pattern of bone deposition is irregular, with curvilinear trabeculae of woven (or rarely lamellar) bone, lacking obvious osteoblastic rimming. Pagetoid type ossification (interconnecting thickened trabeculae and scant intervening fibrous tissue), foci of hyaline cartilage, hyalinization, haemorrhage, cystic degeneration, aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes, osteoclastic resorptions, and xanthomatous change may be present {23276185}.

Differential diagnosis

This includes osteofibrous dysplasia {11272890}, non-ossifying fibroma, aneurysmal bone cyst, and low-grade central osteosarcoma.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in most cases, though GNAS mutation analysis may be helpful occasionally – especially when fibrous dysplasia may be confused histologically with low-grade central osteosarcoma.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; osseous component composed of irregular, curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone without conspicuous osteoblastic rimming; fibrous component composed of bland fibroblastic cells.

Desirable (in select cases): detection of activating missense mutation in GNAS.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Surgical excision or debulking is the treatment of choice. An orbital location habitually precludes complete resection. Recurrence depends on tumour size and the structures involved, such as the optic nerve or CNS elements. The incidence of recurrence rates for orbital tumours is not available. Metastases do not occur, although rare cases can undergo sarcomatous transformation, mostly to osteogenic sarcoma (70%), fibrosarcoma (20%), or undifferentiated sarcoma {23276185}.
