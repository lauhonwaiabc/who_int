4.1.1.3 Nodular and massive retinal gliosis

Definition

Nodular and massive retinal gliosis encompasses a spectrum of retinal glial proliferations with a vascular component, hence the clinical term “vasoproliferative tumour of the retina”, ranging from focal nodules to massive lesions that can fill the eye.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa

Related terminology

Not recommended: vasoproliferative tumour; peripheral retinal angioma; angioma-like mass; retinal angiomatous mass; neovascular fundus abnormalities; peripheral nodular retinal telangiectasis; presumed acquired retinal haemangioma; reactive retinal glioangiosis; focal nodular gliosis; reactive retinal astrocytic tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The lesions are most commonly located in the inferotemporal pre-equatorial retina.

Clinical features

Lesions may be asymptomatic or may present with blurring of vision, primarily from macular exudation or exudative retinal detachment. Clinically, they are yellow-orange to pink-red masses with a vascular appearance (hence the name “vasoproliferative tumour”). Although commonly unilateral and solitary, they can be bilateral {19653718}, multiple, or diffuse {23494037}. Retinal pigment epithelial hyperplasia is often seen at the base of the lesions. Sight-threatening complications include cystoid macular oedema, epimacular membrane, and vitreous haemorrhage. On fluorescein angiography, these lesions are hyperfluorescent and leak. Ultrasonographically, nodular gliosis appears solid and dome shaped {23494037;7748132}.

Based on size, these lesions can be categorized as {26703887} nodular retinal gliosis, which occupies < 25% of the vitreous cavity; submassive retinal gliosis, which occupies 25–50%; and massive retinal gliosis, which occupies > 50%.

Epidemiology

These lesions are non-familial with no sex predilection, and they occur in adults about 40 years of age.

Etiology

In one series, 80% of cases were primary/idiopathic and 20% were secondary {23494037}. Secondary lesions can occur in relation to neurofibromatosis {25317586}, retinitis pigmentosa, uveitis {31584561}, retinopathy of prematurity {19653718}, Coats disease, retinal detachment surgery, cryotherapy, trauma, infections (toxoplasmosis and toxocariasis), and idiopathic inflammatory retinal disease {23494037}. Massive retinal gliosis is commonly associated with phthisis bulbs and congenital diseases or malformations {26703887}.

Pathogenesis

These lesions are thought to be reactive in nature, arising from retinal astrocytes or Müller glial cells {3092790}. Findings that support this include clinical progression of nodular gliosis to the advanced stage of massive retinal gliosis {28275595}, polyclonality of massive retinal gliosis in 1 case {16021579}, the lack of KIAA1549::BRAF fusions and IDH1 p.R132H mutation {23219067}, and expression of genes associated with reactive astrocytes {24921169}. The production of angiogenic cytokines and angiogenesis are likely to be secondary events driven by glial proliferation {24921169}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lesions appear as discrete white-yellow retinal masses. An outer rim of calcification may be present.

Histopathology

Lesions are composed of elongated spindle-shaped fibrous astrocytes with filamentous eosinophilic cytoplasm. The astrocytic cells are positive for GFAP and S100. The nuclei are mostly bland and oval or fusiform in shape. Rosenthal fibres and cells with hair-like (piloid) processes can sometimes be observed. Within the lesion, there are often scattered PAS- positive hyalinized/sclerotic vascular channels. Glial cells may have a perivascular orientation {18835620}. Subretinal proteinaceous exudates may extend beyond its borders and contain cholesterol clefts or even a granulomatous response. Fibrous or osseous metaplasia and pseudoadenomatous hyperplasia of the retinal pigment epithelium are often present. Mitotic figures are absent or rare, and the Ki-67 proliferation index is generally < 5% {26703887;23219067}. Ultrastructural features include interweaving cells with intermediate cytoplasmic filaments corresponding to GFAP and small cellular processes that contain lysosomes.

Differential diagnosis includes pilocytic astrocytoma {29755278}, infiltrating astrocytoma, astrocytic hamartoma, and haemangioblastoma.

Cytology

Fine-needle biopsy shows cells with uniform nuclei, abundant fibrillary cytoplasm, and GFAP-positive elongated processes {29071275}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a proliferation of spindle-shaped astrocytes with scattered hyalinized vessels.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Although lesions are benign, patients can have poor visual outcomes and a long, protracted clinical course. Treatment may include laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, plaque brachytherapy, vitreoretinal surgery, and possible enucleation. While massive retinal gliosis generally remains confined to the eye, extrascleral extension with reactive microscopic features has been reported {28516476}.
