7.0.0.1 Tumours of the lacrimal drainage system: Introduction

The lacrimal drainage system (LDS) serves as a conduit for the exit of tears from the eye into the nasal cavity. Tears on the ocular surface enter the canaliculi through puncta on the medial aspects of the upper and lower eyelids. Both the puncta and canaliculi are lined by stratified, non-keratinized epithelium, which is generally 5–10 cells thick. The tears then drain into the lacrimal sac, which is lined by stratified columnar epithelium containing mucus-secreting goblet cells, and exit via the similarly lined nasolacrimal duct into the nasal cavity.

Disorders of the LDS are relatively common, accounting for approximately 3% of clinical visits in some series {21264538}. Most cases are associated with chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the lacrimal sac wall. Infectious organisms are identified in some cases of chronic dacryocystitis, but many cases represent nonspecific processes. A discrete dacryolith composed of variably mineralized inflammatory and shed epithelial cells, often containing bacteria or other infectious organisms, is sometimes removed and sent for microscopic evaluation.

Epithelial tumours

Although neoplasms of the LDS are relatively rare, they must be considered whenever patients present with obstructive lesions {32304675}. Epithelial neoplasms are the most common tumours of the LDS {20958177}. Papillomas can be covered with squamous or so-called transitional epithelium, and they display exophytic or inverted/endophytic growth patterns. Inverted papillomas are more likely to recur and progress to carcinoma {32501879}. Early clinical symptoms are nonspecific and may include epiphora, bloody tears, or symptoms of dacryocystitis. A progressive mass in the area of the lacrimal sac / nasolacrimal duct makes the diagnosis more obvious {27418476}, particularly if it is a firm mass. Extension above the medial canthal tendon or with extension into the paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity makes the diagnosis of a malignant tumour of the lacrimal sac more likely. In some instances the diagnosis is made intraoperatively during routine dacryocystorhinostomy when the surgeon notes a mass or other abnormal findings.

Squamous cell carcinoma is by far the most common epithelial malignant tumour of the lacrimal drainage apparatus. It can be divided into keratinizing and non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma. HPV infection has been identified in both benign/papillomatous and malignant (keratinizing or non-keratinizing squamous) lesions {26735858;17362365}. Less-common primary epithelial neoplasms at this anatomical site include adenocarcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, and lymphoepithelial carcinoma.

The treatment of primary lacrimal sac / nasolacrimal duct carcinomas is multidisciplinary and typically involves surgery followed by radiation therapy and/or chemotherapy {24195987;33555683}. In the majority of cases, eye-sparing treatments can be achieved and orbital exenteration is rarely needed.

Non-epithelial tumours

Those neoplasms originating in the LDS include malignant lymphoma and soft tissue tumours. Most arise from the lacrimal sac.

Haematolymphoid malignancies, including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), extranodal marginal zone lymphoma, and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma, occur at this site and must be differentiated from reactive lymphoid hyperplasia {20958177} (see section Haematolymphoid tumours). LDS lymphomas represent 2–11% of LDS tumours, with a patient age > 50 years and a female predilection, and are usually found incidentally during a dacryocystorhinostomy procedure {28399337;31267826;15715565}. Malignant lymphoma arises from LDS-associated lymphoid tissue, although DLBCL is known to arise de novo {31267826}. EBV has been associated with DLBCL and NK/T-cell lymphoma of the LDS {31267826}. DLBCL makes up one third of LDS lymphomas, followed by extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (20%), chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma (15%), and other low-grade B-cell lymphomas {31267826;16672329}. The morphological features of periocular lymphomas are described in section Haematolymphoid tumours. Periocular lymphoma–related deaths range from 2% to 12% for extranodal marginal zone lymphoma and from 38% to 48% for DLBCL {31267826;16672329}.

Soft tissue tumours have been reported {31267826} and represent 12–14% of LDS tumours, the most common being fibrous histiocytoma (FH) and solitary fibrous tumour (SFT) {7977578;28399337;31990895;27322416} (see section Soft tissue and bone tumours). The average age at diagnosis of FH is 34 years, and there is no sex predilection; for SFT, the average age is 58 years, with a female predominance {31267826;31990895;7977578;27322416}. Gene fusions involving PRKCB and PRKCD (encoding protein kinase isoforms) are described in a subset of cases of in FH {26121314}. SFT involves a NAB2::STAT6 fusion {23313954;25873501} (see section Solitary fibrous tumour for further details). FHs are composed of spindle to polymorphous cells in a storiform collagenous background and express vimentin, CD68, and factor XIIIa, and are negative for CD34 {24608257}. Wide and complete surgical excision is the management for FH and SFT. The prognosis is excellent for FH and SFT without histological features of aggressiveness {7977578;20958177;31990895}.

Metastatic tumours are only rarely seen in the LDS, and generally also involve orbital soft tissue or other surrounding structures {20958177}.

Melanocytic tumours of the LDS are very rare, and the lacrimal sac is the most common location.
