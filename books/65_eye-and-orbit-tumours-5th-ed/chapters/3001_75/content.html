3.0.0.1 Tumours of the uveal tract: Introduction

The uveal tract – the middle coat of the eye – is divided into the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. As regards the former two, this chapter deals with tumours arising from their stroma. Neuroepithelial tumours and cystic lesions of the iris and ciliary body are described in section Tumours of the retina and neuroepithelium.

The main cell types of the uvea are melanocytes, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle of the iris sphincter and ciliary muscles. Additionally, tumours can arise from choroidal blood vessels and nerves (see section Soft tissue and bone tumours) (the uvea does not have lymphatics). The uveal tract has resident lymphocytes and macrophages, and it can be infiltrated by neoplastic lymphocytes, plasma cells, leukocytes, and histiocytes (see section Haematolymphoid tumours). It is a common site for metastasis (see section Metastases to the eye and surrounding structures).

Congenital uveal tumours include ectopic lacrimal and, rarely, thyroid gland tissue. In addition, congenital ocular melanocytosis can predispose to uveal melanoma.

The most frequent benign and malignant uveal tumours are melanocytic. At least in White populations, uveal naevi are estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 20 individuals. A deeply pigmented magnocellular naevus – known as melanocytoma – is a characteristic subtype. Though uncommon for melanomas of the iris, approximately half of melanomas of the ciliary body and choroid result in metastasis. Schwannomas, neurofibromas, and mesectodermal leiomyomas are all rare uveal tumours (see section Soft tissue and bone tumours). Lisch nodules in the iris occur in the context of neurofibromatosis.

An intriguing entity is paraneoplastic bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation, which is most commonly associated with ovarian or uterine malignancies. We provide an update regarding the pathogenesis of this condition, which may involve autoantibodies to HGF.

Most tumours in the anterior uvea are resected. In case the eye is removed because of a larger tumour, a section should be cut that includes the centre of the pupil, the centre of the tumour, and the optic nerve (PO section). The calottes (caps) from either side can be breadloafed if they contain tumour.

FNAB of uveal tumours is now performed relatively often for prognostic molecular genetic purposes. Such biopsies must be evaluated together with histological or cytological examination to assign correct diagnosis. This chapter now includes descriptions of the cytology of benign and malignant melanocytic tumours of the uveal tract.

Note: Entities that can arise throughout the uveal tract are described in their most common or most prominent site.
