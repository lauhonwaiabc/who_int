3.2.2.1 Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas

Definition

Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas are primary malignant melanocytic tumours of the uvea.

Localization

Melanoma can arise anywhere in the choroid and ciliary body, and it can extend from one to the other, as well as to the iris, chamber angle, retina, vitreous, and optic nerve, and intrasclerally and extrasclerally {17498805;23116575}.

Clinical features

Approximately 25% of patients with a ciliary body or choroidal melanoma have no symptoms and are diagnosed during a routine eye examination {25704938}. Symptoms include photopsia (perceived flashes of light), floaters, and loss of vision {19606159}. Occasionally, large choroidal and ciliary body melanomas result in pain, redness of the eye, and secondary glaucoma {25704938}. Multifocal uveal melanomas have been reported {10406619}. Bilateral primary uveal melanoma occurs rarely {12855969;17952820}.

Ciliary body melanomas are usually circumscribed and nodular. Diagnosis is often late because the tumour initially is hidden behind the iris and is not apparent on routine examination. They often encroach on and distort the crystalline lens, producing lens dislocation, astigmatism, and ipsilateral cataract. The tumour can also lead to focal shallowing of the anterior chamber. Some ciliary body melanomas grow through the iris root, simulating a primary iris melanoma. Anterior chamber seeding and iris heterochromia can develop in such cases. Mechanisms of secondary glaucoma include anterior displacement of the lens iris diaphragm, invasion of a large segment of the iridocorneal angle, tumour seeding, deposition of melanophages, and iris neovascularization. Ring melanomas usually involve > 180° of the ciliary body and show no or disproportionately less anteroposterior growth to the choroid. The aqueous outflow pathways can be obstructed by a ring melanoma and lead to anterior extraocular extension {10396198;28159633;25550867}. Rare melanomalytic glaucoma develops when melanophages containing necrotic tumour debris obstruct the trabecular meshwork {26592671}. In contrast, some eyes become hypotonous when the tumour disrupts the ciliary epithelium, causing decreased production of aqueous humour. Hyphaema and vitreous haemorrhage occur rarely when the tumour disrupts blood vessels or undergoes necrosis. Ultrasonography, ultrasound biomicroscopy, anterior segment optical coherence tomography, and transillumination are helpful diagnostic techniques. External ocular signs that can suggest the diagnosis include dilated episcleral (sentinel) blood vessels overlying the tumour, and episcleral nodules from extrascleral extension through emissary canals in the anterior sclera.

Choroidal melanomas appear as a mass that is crescent dome shaped (in 75% of cases), mushroom shaped (in 20%), or diffuse (in 5%). They are pigmented in 55% of cases, non-pigmented in 15%, and of mixed colour in 30% {22466491}. Their mean size is 11.3 mm (range: 2–24 mm) in basal diameter by 5.5 mm (0.8–17 mm) in thickness {19667335}. The mass is deep to the retina, without feeder vessels, and often associated with an overlying exudative retinal detachment. Necrotic tumours cause painful uveitis, secondary glaucoma, or both. Extraocular extension may occur, anteriorly via aqueous outflow channels, equatorially via vortex veins, and posteriorly along emissary canals in the sclera around the optic disc. The tumour can also invade the optic nerve and its sheath or orbit, or both. Optic nerve invasion occurs in 5–6% of eyes {16877391;17223846} and especially frequently from retinoinvasive melanomas {9349160}.

Imaging

The most common way to localize a choroidal melanoma is by direct visualization with ophthalmoscopy. The tumour should be documented using fundus photography and measured using A-scan and B-scan ultrasonography. The latter is also helpful in excluding extraocular extension {8988193}. Optical coherence tomography is generally used to determine the effect of the tumour on the overlying and surrounding retina, and to evaluate subretinal fluid. Fundus autofluorescence imaging can reveal orange lipofuscin pigment overlying the tumour.

Multimodal imaging can be helpful in diagnosing small choroidal melanomas or distinguishing amelanotic melanomas from choroidal metastases. Optical coherence tomography can show subretinal fluid, subretinal lipofuscin pigment, and shaggy photoreceptors. Fluorescein angiography shows hyperfluorescent lesions with pinpoint leakage and intrinsic vasculature. Indocyanine green angiography may show a dual circulation.

On MRI the signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging correlates with pigmentation, and shows diffuse enhancement after contrast administration {34718831;10207481}. The mean apparent diffusion coefficient is 1.16 × 10−3 mm2/s and the tumour shows either a washout or plateau perfusion curve {34718831;23561057}. MRI is helpful in diagnosing ciliary body involvement, extrascleral extension, and optic nerve invasion, but it is limited in its ability to identify scleral invasion {34718831}.

Epidemiology

The worldwide incidence of uveal melanoma is uncertain, but it is estimated to be about 7000 per year [[KivelÃ¤ T. Incidence, Prevalence and Epidemiology of Ocular Melanoma. In: Murray TG, Boldt HC, editors. Ocular Melanoma: Advances in Diagnostic and Therapeutic Strategies. London (UK): Future Medicine; 2014. pp. 20â€“38.]], accounting for 5% of all primary melanomas {3279559}. Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas are most common in fair-skinned populations (in which 65% of all cases are reported, although only 12% of the world population); they are rare in individuals of Asian and African ancestry {3279559;18191091;21704381}. Among Asian people, the risk of developing uveal melanoma is 1.2 times the risk among African-American people; among Hispanic and non-Hispanic White groups, the risk is 5 times and 19 times, respectively, the risk among African-American people {18387420}. Worldwide, the incidence rate ranges between 2 and 8 cases per 1 million person-years, being lower at southern latitudes (1.3–2.9 cases per 1 million person-years) and increasing with latitude (to 6.1–8.6 cases per 1 million person-years) {17498805;23116575}. The age-adjusted incidence has been stable over a 25-year period {15763193;17498805}. The median age at diagnosis, which has increased because of rising life expectancy, is currently 60–64 years in fair-skinned populations and 45 years among Asian people {15132378;22557876;7107133;9645715}. These melanomas are rare before the age of 20 years, accounting for 0.4–0.8% of cases, and are still relatively rare before the age of 45 years {26854035;10900104;14578381}. They show no sex predilection {22079972} and are more common among lightly than darkly pigmented individuals {3279559;16226513;9627661;12750097}.

Etiology

Most choroidal and ciliary body melanomas appear to develop de novo. Others develop from melanocytes in uveal naevi including melanocytomas {11864894;16650691;16154197} or in congenital ocular or oculodermal melanocytosis (naevus of Ota), all with a lifetime risk of about 1 in 400 {9442799}. Dysplastic naevus syndrome {15763193} and BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome {22935333;9442799;21874003} are associated with a higher risk of uveal melanoma. Other risk factors include light skin pigmentation, self-described inability to tan, and light-coloured irises {16401785}. In addition, an association with a high number of common cutaneous naevi, atypical cutaneous naevi, cutaneous freckles, and iris naevi has been demonstrated {19167086}. The role of ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure in the development of choroidal and ciliary body melanoma is uncertain. Because of the rarity of the disease, quantitative meta-analytic reviews have provided the majority of information. A statistically significant association with arc welding (odds ratio: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.20–3.51) {16051363} and sunlamp use (odds ratio: 2.15, 95% CI: 1.27–3.64) was demonstrated in one series {33839068}, but other forms of intermittent and chronic UV radiation exposure were not found to be associated {16051363}.

Pathogenesis

Melanoma at this anatomical site is thought to arise from uveal melanocytes through well-defined driver mutations. Most cases develop de novo, but uveal naevi including melanocytomas can also transform into melanoma {11864894;16650691;16154197}.

Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas (and many naevi) have mutations in the Gqα signalling pathway, affecting the highly homologous Gα subunits Gqα and G11α encoded by the genes GNAQ and GNA11 in > 90% of cases, with the remaining tumours having mutations in the Gqα-coupled receptor CYSLTR2 or the downstream effector of Gqα and G11α, phospholipase Cβ4 (encoded by PLCB4) {27123562;21083380;26683228}. Pathogenic GNAQ and GNA11 variants affect codons Q209 and, less commonly, R183, and lead to constitutive activation and relay signals via PKC and the GTP-exchange factor RASGRP3, which is highly overexpressed in uveal melanoma, to the MAPK pathway {28486107}. The mutations in the Gqα pathway arise early during tumour progression and, in the absence of the additional mutations discussed below, also are found in benign neoplasms such as choroidal naevi, blue naevi, and naevi of Ota.

Transformation to uveal melanoma is caused by additional genetic alterations in one of three genes. Biallelic inactivation of the ubiquitin carboxy-terminal hydrolase BAP1 at chromosome 3p21, typically by a combination of an inactivating mutation and monosomy 3, is associated with the worst prognosis {31563544}. Hotspot mutations in the splice factor SF3B1, typically at codon 625 {23313955}, result in mis-splicing of genes including BRD9, a member of the non-canonical BAF complex involved in epigenetic regulation {31597964}. The third gene, EIF1AX, located at Xp22.1, encodes a translation initiation factor {23793026}, and mutations cluster near the 5′ part of the gene. Less frequently mutated genes of relevance include TP53 and RPL5, encoding an inhibitor of MDM2, which degrades p53 {32415113}. Only BAP1 mutations have been described to occur also in the germ line; the others are always somatic. Metastases often show additional pathogenic mutations involving the PI3K/AKT pathway, inactivation of CDKN2A, and activation of PRC2 {31253977}.

Loss of chromosome 3 (complete or partial monosomy 3 or isodisomy 3), in particular when associated with polysomy 8q, is associated with metastasis in choroidal melanoma {19078957}. BAP1, a gene located on chromosome 3 (at 3p21.1), carries inactivating mutations in about 80% of uveal melanomas with metastases {21051595}. Somatic or germline BAP1 loss-of-function mutations are observed in 30–50% of choroidal and ciliary body melanomas. BAP1 appears to be involved with chromatin remodelling {20436459} and cell-cycle control {21484256}. BAP1-deficient cells exhibit stem cell–like qualities {23915344}. BAP1 also appears to be involved in transendothelial migration and extravasation of cells to the systemic circulation {25506912}. Loss of BAP1 is associated with an increased numbers of tumour-infiltrating macrophages, which are mainly of the proangiogenic M2 type, and a higher microvascular density {8674036;10509639;20811059;31337000}. Once the neoplastic cells have entered the systemic circulation, a variety of factors, including receptor/ligand gradients (e.g. between c-Met and its ligand HGF) {18281525;18001467;17261188;9546344} and the stoichiometric properties of the liver {23392528;27476775}, appear to be involved in the propensity of uveal melanoma to metastasize to the liver {34999237}.

Mutations of codon 625 of SF3B1 on chromosome 2 are found in 10–23% of uveal melanomas {23313955;31563544}. SF3B1 is a splice factor involved in intron excision from pre-mRNA, and it may also be associated with chromatin remodelling {23313955}. BAP1, SF3B1, and EIF1AX pathogenic sequence variants appear to be mutually exclusive in the vast majority of uveal melanomas {27123562}.

Gene expression profiling has identified a set of differentially expressed genes in class 1A and 1B (low and intermediate metastatic risk, respectively) versus class 2 (high metastatic risk) uveal melanomas {15492234}. Class 1 melanomas retain a differentiated melanocytic phenotype and transcriptome, whereas class 2 melanomas have a dedifferentiated, cancer stem cell–like phenotype and transcriptome {18477893}. SF3B1 and EIF1AX mutations appear to be associated with a class 1 gene expression profile, while the hemizygous BAP1 mutation, when combined with monosomy 3, is associated with a class 2 gene expression profile {26933176}.

A recent study by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) categorized 80 uveal melanomas into four subsets: two associated with poor prognosis and monosomy 3 and two with better prognosis and disomy 3 {28810145}. These four different types (A–D) are characterized by distinct global DNA methylation patterns and distinct genomic, signalling, and immune profiles, which are associated with prognosis {30032793;31755428}. Types A and B correspond to class 1, and types C and D to class 2 gene expression profile.

In the liver, the melanoma forms relatively dormant micrometastases that have the potential to grow into clinical metastases {23392528;27476775}. These metastases may form nodules (the nodular pattern), infiltrate the hepatic parenchyma (the infiltrative pattern) {27476775}, or both. A variety of factors may contribute to the growth of micrometastases into clinical metastases, including likely abrogation of the host immune response (in particular, NK-cell function) {10520219;25683463} and an increased local VEGF:PEDF ratio {23793989}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, ciliary body melanomas are either dome-shaped or circumferential ring-shaped tumours of variable colour, ranging from amelanotic to dark brown and variegated. Choroidal melanomas may exhibit a flat to domed shape when confined by the Bruch membrane, or they may exhibit a characteristic collar-button or mushroom-like shape when they break through the Bruch membrane; the portion of the tumour that has broken through is choked off by the membrane, resulting in vascular congestion.

Histopathology

Microscopic examination is necessary to assess for invasion of ocular structures (e.g. the iridocorneal angle, iris, sclera, retina, and optic nerve) as well as for extrascleral extension.

Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas produce variable amounts of melanin pigment and thus appear as amelanotic to mildly, moderately, or heavily pigmented tumours; part of the pigment often resides in tumour-infiltrating melanophages. Their cytological description was traditionally based on the Callender system, which in its abbreviated form includes four morphological subtypes: spindle A (naevus-like) cell, spindle B cell, epithelioid-cell, and mixed-cell (spindle B and epithelioid) melanomas {6624832}. Spindle A cells are fusiform and cohesive, and they have poorly defined cell borders. Their spindle-shaped nucleus contains a central nuclear envelope fold (or groove). Spindle B cells are larger, fusiform, and cohesive, and they contain spindle- or cigar-shaped nuclei and prominent nucleoli; this is the most frequent cell type in most tumours. Epithelioid cells are non-cohesive and contain large, round nuclei and abundant, prominently eosinophilic cytoplasm; this cell type is associated with a high risk of metastasis. Small epithelioid cells are intermediate between spindle B and epithelioid melanoma cells. The tumour may undergo spontaneous necrosis of variable degree. Those that exhibit 90–100% necrosis are composed of so-called ghost cells; this phenomenon is probably due to a sequence of events that starts with vascular insufficiency within the tumour, followed by cytokine release, tumour necrosis, and secondary glaucoma {27175363}. Balloon cells are sometimes seen {6882713}. They have foamy, vacuolated cytoplasm because of faulty production of melanin or degradation of melanosomes, and their cytoplasm contains lipid. Balloon cells are often seen after plaque brachytherapy or proton beam irradiation {1323252}. Both dendritic- and scavenger-type tumour-infiltrating macrophages are ubiquitous in uveal melanomas, and they can constitute a substantial portion of the tumour mass {11381040}.

Although the cut-off points are arbitrary, spindle cell melanoma is defined as being composed of spindle B cells in ≥ 90% of the tumour, and epithelioid-cell melanoma is defined as being composed of epithelioid cells in ≥ 90% of the tumour; all other tumours are mixed-cell melanomas (the most common type in most series). Epithelioid-cell melanomas are associated with the worst prognosis, and spindle cell melanomas are associated with the best prognosis {15327098}.

Other patterns include clear cell melanoma {9230830}: the tumour cells have oval, centrally located nuclei and clear cytoplasm because of the dissolution of glycogen granules during fixation. Clear cell melanomas must be distinguished from renal cell carcinoma metastatic to the uvea. Cavitary melanoma is another uncommon pattern which develops cyst-like spaces that may be clinically detected on ultrasonography {16391628;9186143}.

A number of specific subtypes of choroidal and ciliary body melanomas may need to be considered.

Diffuse uveal melanoma is defined as being < 5 mm in thickness and occupying at least one quarter of the uveal tract {6966937}. This growth pattern may be associated with a high risk of extraocular growth and metastasis, probably related to its large basal dimension {8694731}.

Retinoinvasive uveal melanoma tends to evolve from a non-cohesive melanoma of the iris or ciliary body, then invades the vitreous, the retina, and through these tissues, sometimes, into the optic nerve {9349160;29344483}. These tumours express neurotrophin receptors, which may account for the trophism towards the retina {22343487}. Other mechanisms of optic nerve invasion include via the adjacent retina after retinal invasion, directly from the peripapillary choroid, and a combination of these mechanisms.

Ring melanoma is another subtype of ciliary body melanoma in which the tumour grows circumferentially up to 360° in the ciliary body typically without significant extension to the choroid {12476094}. A rare form of ring melanoma, anterior chamber angle melanoma, leads to secondary glaucoma with minimal involvement of the uveal tract {11530045;28159633}.

Immunohistochemistry

Choroidal melanomas are typically positive for immunostaining for HMB45, S100, melan-A, MITF, TYR, and SOX10 {3608565;1762354;17445277;12742347;11511840}. It is advisable to use at least two antibodies, such as those against HMB45 and S100, in case one is negative. Ki-67, a nuclear proliferation marker, may be used to assess proliferative activity. The Ki-67 index has been found to have some prognostic relevance {8664054;7573354}. Mitotic activity should be reported as the number of mitoses/mm2 {8551926}. Mitoses can alternatively be assessed using phosphohistone H3 immunohistochemistry {19900200}.

A number of additional prognostic features of choroidal and ciliary body melanomas may be considered for reporting to the clinician. Especially, a lack of nuclear immunostaining for the BAP1 protein is strongly associated with a high risk of metastasis {25058347;24633195;27015033}. Several extravascular matrix patterns have been identified, listed from least to most complex: normal, silent, straight, parallel, parallel with crosslinking, arcs, arcs with branching, closed vascular loops, and vascular networks – the last three patterns are associated with a higher risk of metastasis {8371929;10050870}. It has been proposed that the extravascular matrix patterns constitute vasculogenic mimicry {10666364;9135390}. Large tumour cell nucleoli {12766034;9160022} and a high microvascular density also are associated with shorter survival {12147581;8674036;10509639}. Prominent tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and macrophages are associated with an increased likelihood of metastasis, and they may reflect the presence of high-risk chromosomal abnormalities, in particular monosomy 3 or loss of expression of the BAP1 protein {18234992;20811059;22743317;28391358;11381040}.

Cytology

Transvitreal or transscleral FNAB may be performed to detect ciliary body or choroidal melanoma, although it is more commonly undertaken for genetic prognostication purposes {28596284;27324683;32273508;31306857}. The cytological specimen may contain cells with spindle-shaped cytoplasm or round cells with variable amounts of cytoplasm. Intracytoplasmic melanin pigment granules are often present. Nuclear atypia with margination of chromatin and prominent nucleoli may be present. FNAB can be used to obtain cells for molecular testing {26556006;32196967}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of mutations in the Gqα signalling pathway, affecting GNAQ and GNA11, can be helpful to distinguish uveal melanoma metastases from other melanomas {28228113}. Demonstration of the loss of chromosome 3 or BAP1, a gene located on chromosome 3, which has inactivating mutations in about 80% of uveal melanomas with metastases {21051595}, can be helpful to assess prognosis.

Gene expression profiling has identified a set of differentially expressed genes in class 1A and 1B (low and intermediate metastatic risk, respectively) versus class 2 (high metastatic risk) uveal melanomas {15492234} and can also be used to assess prognosis. The level of PRAME mRNA is an independent prognostic biomarker in uveal melanoma, which identifies increased metastatic risk in patients with class 1 or disomy 3 tumours {26933176}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: melanocytic tumour in the ciliary body or choroid; histological or cytological demonstration of spindle-shaped or epithelioid cells, or both, with round to oval nuclei and prominent nucleoli.

Desirable: if amelanotic, it is desirable to obtain an immunohistochemical confirmation using melanocytic markers; analysis of driver genes may be necessary to distinguish choroidal and ciliary body melanoma from cutaneous and mucous membrane melanoma metastatic to these sites.

Staging

Uveal melanomas are staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification, eighth edition, which has a separate staging for ciliary body and choroidal melanomas as distinct from iris melanomas.

According to the UICC/AJCC TNM classification system [[KivelÃ¤ T, Simpson ER, Grossniklaus HE, Jager MJ, Singh AD, Caminal JM, et al. Uveal Melanoma. In: Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York: Springer; 2017. pp. 805â€“17.]], choroidal and ciliary body melanomas are grouped by size as small (T1), medium (T2), large (T3), or very large (T4); and subgrouped by ciliary body involvement and extrascleral growth as choroid only (a), ciliary body involved (b), choroid and extrascleral < 5 mm (c), or ciliary body involved and extrascleral ≤ 5 mm (d), and extrascleral > 5 mm (e). They are staged (stages I–IV) according to size, ciliary body involvement, extraocular extension, and presence of metastasis {23816968}.

Staging systems also exist for newly diagnosed metastatic uveal melanoma {27296487;25780931;31234340;34999237}.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour size, ciliary body involvement, and extraocular extension of the melanoma are of independent prognostic significance (see Staging, above). The risk of metastasis increases with increasing tumour size, and from invasion of the ciliary body through extraocular invasion to invasion of both compartments {23816968;25555246}.

As many as 50% of patients with a ciliary body or choroidal melanoma ultimately die from haematogenous metastasis, most commonly (in 90% of cases) to the liver {11346394;14578381;34999237}. The prognosis for survival is worse for patients with melanomas of the ciliary body and anterior choroid {10922199}, and with larger tumours. Other common (often secondary) sites of metastasis are lung (affected in 24% of cases) and bone (in 16%) {11346394}. The median survival after detection of metastasis is 10–12 months, and the survival rate decreases from 52% at one year to 25% at 2 years, and 13% at 3 years {30664106;31150059}. In the past, when the liver was not imaged during surveillance, the reported median survival rates for patients with metastases were 15–20% at 6 months and 10% at 1 year {11346394}; in a recent study in which most patients underwent regular liver imaging, the reported rates were 75% at 6 months and 50% at 1 year {27296487}. Liver function tests and imaging, including MRI, CT, PET-CT, and ultrasound, can be used to detect and stage liver metastases {34999237}. Older age is a risk factor for shorter survival in patients with uveal melanoma {17365812}. Patients with metastasis confined to extrahepatic sites may have longer survival times than patients with hepatic metastases {11346394}.

The reported 5-year survival rates are 88% for the spindle-cell type and 60% for the epithelioid or mixed-cell type overall {11346394}, but melanomas that have monosomy 3, are of gene expression profile class 2, or show loss of nuclear BAP1 expression are associated with 5-year survival rates of only about 30% {25147369}. Some disomy 3 melanomas, in particular SF3B1-mutated uveal melanomas, also metastasize, leading to a bimodal metastatic profile with late-onset metastatic disease {30073324;26923342}. Gain of chromosome 8q is a risk factor for the development of metastases {2416949}: uveal melanomas with normal copy numbers of 8q are associated with a 5-year survival rate of 83%, while with 8q gain and a normal chromosome 3 status the rate is 67%; addition of more copies of 8q is associated with worse survival {25764247}. Additional adverse prognostic factors include larger tumour size {26596399;28159971;23816968;27123792}, extravascular matrix loops and networks {25827538;7777286}, high microvascular density {12147581;8674036;10509639}, high numbers of tumour-infiltrating macrophages {20811059;22743317;28391358;11381040}, and large tumour cell nucleoli {12766034;9160022}. Many of these prognostic factors are associated with the presence of monosomy 3 or loss of BAP1 expression {22743317;29316429}.

Combining genetic information with tumour size results in a more precise prognostication than either parameter alone {27123792;25537201;28159971;34793831}. This is being used in the Liverpool Uveal Melanoma Prognosticator Online (LUMPO) to calculate an individual’s risk of metastases {32334433;32273508}. While blue eyes are associated with an increased risk of developing ciliary body or choroidal melanoma, chromosome 3 and 8q aberrations have a greater impact on survival in patients with a light iris versus a dark iris colour {32998469;34780841}.
