10.1.5.1 Schwannoma

Definition

Schwannoma is a benign nerve sheath tumour composed entirely or nearly entirely of differentiated neoplastic Schwann cells.

ICD-O coding

9560/0 Schwannoma

9560/0 Cellular schwannoma

9560/0 Plexiform schwannoma

9560/0 Epithelioid schwannoma

9560/0 Microcystic/reticular schwannoma

ICD-11 coding

2A02.3 & XH98Z3 Benign neoplasm of cranial nerves & Schwannoma, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: neurilemoma.

Subtype(s)

Cellular schwannoma; plexiform schwannoma; epithelioid schwannoma; microcystic/reticular schwannoma

Localization

Common sites of origin are peripheral nerves in the skin and subcutaneous tissues of the head and neck. The most frequent location for schwannomas of the orbit is the superior or superomedial aspect of the orbit, where probably they originate from the supratrochlear or supraorbital branches of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve {17464300}. Other ocular and periocular locations are relatively rare but examples involving the conjunctiva {9109769}, caruncle {17688668}, choroid {22981398}, and ciliary body {6882714} have been recognized.

Clinical features

Schwannomas are slow-growing tumours, often appearing as asymptomatic masses or incidental findings on imaging studies. Painful schwannomas may be associated with schwannomatosis.

Epidemiology

More than 90% of schwannomas are solitary and sporadic, including those developing in the orbit, a minority of which are associated with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) {10026738}. Schwannomas affect people of all ages, but the peak incidence is in the fourth to sixth decades of life. There is no known sex or race predisposition.

Etiology

The etiology of most sporadic schwannomas is unknown, although there is an established increased incidence associated with prior irradiation {3173432;12381708}. There is an association with NF2 or schwannomatosis in some cases.

Pathogenesis

A causal relationship exists between schwannoma tumorigenesis and loss of expression of merlin (also called NF2 or schwannomin), the growth inhibitory protein product of the NF2 tumour suppressor gene located at 22q12.2 {9403715}. NF2-inactivating mutations have been detected in approximately 50–75% of sporadic cases {8889506;23921927;26360373;28409725}. Underlying genetic events are predominantly frameshift and nonsense mutations, with loss of the remaining wildtype allele on chromosome 22. Other common mutations involve the LATS1, LATS2, ARID1A, ARID1B, and DDR1 genes, whereas a recurrent in-frame SH3PXD2A::HTRA1 fusion is found in roughly 10% of cases {26360373;27723760}.

Multiple schwannomas are a feature of NF2 and schwannomatosis, both of which can also occur in mosaic or segmental forms {26851632;27921248}.

Macroscopic appearance

Schwannomas are mainly solitary and globoid, with a smooth surface. Fewer than half of all schwannomas have an evident attached nerve, which is most often small and draped over the tumour capsule. A capsule may be absent in tumours involving head and neck sites, including the orbit. Sectioned tumours reveal firm, light-tan, glistening tissue, interrupted by white/yellow areas and/or patches of haemorrhage.

Histopathology

Conventional schwannoma is usually an encapsulated spindle cell tumour composed nearly entirely of well-differentiated Schwann cells. Schwannomas have a broad morphological range. The large majority are biphasic tumours with compact areas (Antoni A tissue) showing occasional nuclear palisading (Verocay bodies), alternating with loosely arranged foci (Antoni B tissue). Cells of Antoni A tissue have eosinophilic cytoplasm, no discernible cell borders, and normochromic, elongated, tapered nuclei. Cytoplasmic nuclear inclusions, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic figures may be seen. Palisading (Verocay bodies) takes the form of parallel rows of Schwann cell nuclei separated by their aligned cell processes. Antoni B tissue commonly contains a cobweb-like network of tumour processes with collections of lipid-laden histiocytes and thick-walled, hyalinized blood vessels. Lymphoid aggregates are often present in a subcapsular distribution or at the periphery in unencapsulated tumours.

Diffuse staining for S100 in the cell nuclei and cytoplasm, which is more prominent in Antoni A areas than in Antoni B areas, is found in all tumours and subtypes. Similarly, SOX10 immunoreactivity is usually extensive {18636017;22495377}. Expression of GFAP is less frequent and more variable. Staining for neurofilament protein is helpful in identifying entrapped intratumoural axons, found in many sporadic schwannomas, albeit most often at their periphery {17721192}. EMA highlights perineurial cells in the capsule, if present.

Scattered atypical to bizarre-appearing nuclei, a feature that is often considered degenerative, have been designated ancient change. Benign hybrid nerve sheath tumours with schwannoma components have been reported in the orbit but they appear rare {33110748}.

Cellular schwannoma is composed exclusively or predominantly of Antoni A tissue and is devoid of Verocay bodies. The tumours most commonly involve large nerves and nerve plexuses at paravertebral sites and in the mediastinum, retroperitoneum, and pelvis {7337161;2400975;25189642}. Sinonasal schwannomas are often of the cellular subtype and may be locally aggressive with orbital extension {9267819}.

Plexiform schwannoma, which can be conventional or cellular, often arises in skin or subcutaneous tissue, growing as thinly encapsulated plexiform or multinodular tumours {18439936;3096870}. Less frequently, these tumours can occur in the deep soft tissues {16006798} and occasionally in the orbit {18439936;28235631}.

Epithelioid schwannoma and microcystic/reticular schwannoma are rare subtypes that typically do not involve the orbit.

Other patterns: Although most syndrome-associated schwannomas are not histologically distinguishable from their sporadic counterparts, several clinicopathological clues may indicate a setting of NF2 or schwannomatosis: young patient age, multiple tumours, extensive longitudinal involvement of a nerve, a discontinuous or multinodular growth pattern, and a mosaic SMARCB1 immunostaining pattern (an admixture of positive and negative nuclei) {18422762;28365909}. Some schwannomas feature predominantly small blue round cells, and may or may not have structures resembling Homer Wright rosettes or giant rosettes, which surround collagen fibres resembling those of low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma; these cases are often referred to as neuroblastoma-like, although they lack increased proliferative activity and show a typical schwannoma immunoprofile {30582192}. Another rare pitfall is a schwannoma with neuromelanin-like pigment accumulation that is positive on Fontana–Masson staining. Nevertheless, the histology is otherwise typical of schwannoma, and the tumour cells are negative for more-specific melanocytic markers, such as HMB45 {10792787}; therefore, these should not be equated with the more aggressive, Carney complex / PRKAR1A–associated malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumour (previously termed “melanotic schwannoma”).

Cytology

Aspirate smears of schwannoma typically yield cohesive syncytial fragments of spindle cells {25641870}. Within the fragments, variably wavy and bent tumour cell nuclei with tapered edges and elongate cytoplasm are seen. Nuclear pleomorphism or degenerative atypia and intranuclear inclusions may be seen. Schwannomas may be difficult to distinguish from other spindle cell neoplasms on cytological preparation alone, and their diagnosis requires correlation with core biopsy and/or immunohistochemical staining {30457183}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Loss of chromosome 22q and/or mutation of NF2 in schwannomas are frequent but nonspecific molecular alterations. Schwannomas exhibit a distinct DNA methylation pattern {29539639;26857854;25399693;27723760}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: histopathology of schwannoma, such as Antoni A or Antoni B areas.

Desirable: extensive S100 or SOX10 expression; Verocay bodies; subcapsular lymphocytes; hyalinized blood vessels; lack of a lattice-like pattern of CD34 staining; loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression (epithelioid schwannoma), or a mosaic pattern of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression (syndrome-associated schwannoma).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Schwannomas are benign and do not usually recur if treated by gross total resection. Cellular and plexiform examples are least amenable to total removal and sometimes can only be debulked. Malignant transformation of conventional schwannoma is extremely rare, and not a feature in orbital examples.
