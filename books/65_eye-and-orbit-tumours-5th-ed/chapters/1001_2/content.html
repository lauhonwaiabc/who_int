1.0.0.1 Tumours of the conjunctiva and caruncle: Introduction

The fifth edition of the classification of tumours of the conjunctiva and caruncle very much follows the fourth edition, with only minor organizational changes. The conjunctiva, together with the caruncle (its skin-like appendage located at the inner canthus of the eyelids), forms a protective layer that covers the anterior segment of the eye and the inner aspect of the eyelids. The conjunctiva is clinically divided into limbal, bulbar, forniceal, and palpebral parts. The epithelium is non-keratinizing and stratified columnar in type, and it harbours a population of basal melanocytes and superficial mucus-secreting goblet cells that are most abundant nasally and in crypt-like infoldings in the conjunctival fornices. The conjunctival stroma is fibrovascular and contains inflammatory cells, including lymphocytes that can form follicles and germinal centres in the fornices; it also has lymphatics and contains accessory lacrimal glands.

The chapter starts with a discussion of tumour-like lesions, including cysts, reactive epithelial lesions, and choristomas. Pseudoglandular and pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia are now grouped under reactive epithelial hyperplasia. Tumours more commonly found in the eyelids, such as phakomatous choristoma, keratoacanthoma, and sebaceous carcinoma, are now found under the chapter on section tumours of the eyelid, which is new to this volume. Non-neoplastic epithelial conjunctival masses include epithelial inclusion cysts, as well as degenerative or inflammatory lesions, such as pingueculae, pterygia, papillary and follicular conjunctivitis, reactive epithelial hyperplasia, and various choristomas (most commonly dermoids and dermolipoma). Epithelial tumours include benign squamous papillomas and hereditary benign intraepithelial dyskeratosis, as well as premalignant and malignant conjunctival squamous intraepithelial neoplasia, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and adenosquamous carcinoma. New additions to the fifth edition include the inclusion of spindle SCC and of SCC with mucinous differentiation as morphological patterns within the broader group of SCCs at this site.

Benign melanocytic conjunctival tumours include benign epithelial melanosis; junctional, compound, and subepithelial conjunctival naevi; inflamed juvenile conjunctival naevus; blue naevus of the conjunctiva; and combined naevus. Spitz naevus, exceedingly rare in the conjunctiva, has moved to the new chapter on section tumours of the eyelid. Premalignant and malignant melanocytic conjunctival tumours include conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesions, the nomenclature for which continues to evolve, and conjunctival melanoma. A new entity in the fifth-edition volume is WNT-activated deep penetrating / plexiform melanocytoma (naevus). Use of the term “melanocytoma” has caused some discussion, but its use in the Skin tumours volume to identify lesions genetically intermediate between naevus and melanoma was put forward in the fourth edition and is now established. WNT-activated deep penetrating / plexiform melanocytoma (naevus) almost always presents in the conjunctiva as a combined naevus. Finally, our understanding of conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesions as a spectrum from benign to preinvasive melanoma continues to advance, and the diagnostic matrix is tabulated to make this easier to use in diagnostic practice.

This chapter includes conjunctival oncocytoma, which generally arises in the accessory lacrimal glands of the caruncle. Lanugo hairs, sebaceous glands, sweat glands, and striated muscle are found only in the caruncle. Adnexal tumours, which can arise from these structures, are described primarily in the chapter on section eyelids. Similarly, haematolymphoid and soft tissue tumours, which can arise in the conjunctiva and caruncle, are covered in other chapters of this volume.
