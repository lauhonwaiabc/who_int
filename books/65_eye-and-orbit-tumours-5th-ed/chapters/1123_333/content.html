1.1.2.3 Pterygium and pinguecula

Definition

Pterygium and pinguecula are non-neoplastic, benign, degenerative, fibrovascular lesions of the bulbar conjunctiva, histologically characterized by actinic, elastotic degeneration of the substantia propria. Both arise within the interpalpebral zone, with pinguecula limited to the paralimbal conjunctiva, whereas pterygium involves the cornea as well.

Localization

Pinguecula is limited to the limbal bulbar conjunctiva within the interpalpebral zone. Pterygium arises within the interpalpebral zone and involves the bulbar and limbal conjunctiva and peripheral cornea, and it has a nasal predilection.

Clinical features

Pinguecula is usually located at the interpalpebral paralimbal conjunctiva, within the 3 o’clock to 9 o’clock meridian. It is often bilateral. A pinguecula is a discrete elevated nodule, sometimes wedge shaped, that has a greyish, white, or yellow mottled colour {32644391}. It stops abruptly at the limbal conjunctiva and is not fixed to the underlying sclera. It usually presents as a cosmetic complaint. If inflamed (pingueculitis), it can cause ocular irritation symptoms {32644391}.

Pterygium appears as a wing-like, white, fibrovascular, slightly raised plaque, extending from the conjunctiva to the cornea. A corneal Stocker iron line may be seen anterior to the leading edge. Clinical symptoms reflect the severity of the process and can include irritation, foreign body sensation, photophobia, and decreased vision due to the degradation of the refractive surface. Complications include astigmatism {34104871;26684048}, squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (the rate of which ranges from 1% to 11% depending on geography) {34206333}, and the development of melanocytic proliferations {16487360;33828861}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of pterygium varies according to the population studied, with a range of 1–33% {34104871;11735795;19965830;19570772;18600244;18043173}. A meta-analysis determined the pooled prevalence to be 10% {24253031}. It is most common in the tropics, within the so-called pterygium belt, where the incidence is as high as 30% {29849437}. In temperate climates, the estimated incidence is 2% {20544202}. Risk factors include older age {11735795;19965830}, male sex {16167080;10684840}, outdoor exposure {21183945;11735795}, rural settings {10684840}, darker skin complexion {11735795}, and smoking {19570772}.

Etiology

The following risk factors are associated with pingueculae and pterygia: ultraviolet A (UVA) and ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation {6712914;2803097}; wind, dust, and sand {8280691}; human papillomaviruses and herpesviruses {11423447;23856952}; genetic predisposition {24967209}; and immunological {6383051} and inflammatory conditions {2695353}. Numerous epidemiological studies have confirmed the strong association between UVA and UVB radiation and pterygium development {29849437}, especially data from the pterygium belt. Evidence for an association with HPV16 and HPV18 {22134594} and with the herpes simplex virus {34104871} is less robust.

Pathogenesis

The nasal proclivity for pterygium has been explained by the peripheral light focusing effect. This refers to UV light crossing through the temporal cornea, with damage to the stem cells in the nasal cornea {21670690}. Another hypothesis favours the temporal to nasal lacrimal flow, which refers to the concentration of dust and irritating particles from the ocular surface in the nasal aspect, locally triggering cytokine release, a causative factor for pterygium {32106293}. The precise molecular pathogenesis of pterygium remains uncertain, but the progression and the recurrence in pterygium seem to have epithelial–mesenchymal transition as a common ground. A variety of studies have reported different pathways and master regulators related to epithelial–mesenchymal transition, including NOTCH, p53, WNT/β-catenin, inflammatory mediators, TGF-β, and EGFR (HER1). In addition, environmental factors like UV radiation and certain viruses have been also linked to epithelial–mesenchymal transition {30847945;32867783}. As a result, mutagenicity, construction of a new vascular network, and remodelling of the extracellular mesenchymal matrix perpetuate the development of pterygium {16714259}.

Histopathology

Pinguecula and pterygium are characterized by basophilic elastotic degeneration of the substantia propria accompanied by fibrosis and telangiectatic vessels. The elastotic degeneration is more prominent in the conjunctival portion of a pterygium rather than the corneal portion. Other stromal features include elastoid globules, dystrophic calcification, and chronic inflammation. Sometimes the latter manifests as actinic granuloma, showing granulomatous inflammation with giant cells centred on elastotic fibres {33895150}. The overlying squamous epithelium can be normal or show acanthosis, squamous metaplasia, goblet cell hyperplasia, dyskeratosis, and increased melanin production {34104871}. In pterygium, the peripheral Bowman layer is effaced with associated superficial corneal fibrosis. In rare cases, squamous intraepithelial neoplasia {34206333} and melanocytic proliferations may be present {33828861;16487360}.

Cytology

Exfoliative or imprint cytology from limbal lesions can be used to detect squamous epithelial dysplasia or malignancy.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: interpalpebral paralimbal conjunctiva (pinguecula); corneal involvement in pterygium; variable actinic elastotic degeneration in conjunctival substantia propria covered by non-dysplastic squamous epithelium.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Conventional pingueculae follow a self-limiting course. The visual prognosis for pterygia depends on the involvement of the visual axis. In the absence of squamous neoplasia, rates of recurrence vary depending on several clinical variables and method of removal {34104871}.
