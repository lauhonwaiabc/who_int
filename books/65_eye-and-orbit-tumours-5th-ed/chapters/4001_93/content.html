4.0.0.1 Tumours of the retina and neuroepithelium: Introduction

The neurosensory retina is a complex neural network consisting of neurons (rod and cone photoreceptors, horizontal cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, ganglion cells) and supporting Müller radial glial cells and perivascular astrocytes and their processes organized into nine layers. It is supported by an underlying monolayer of hexagonal retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, and extends to the optic nerve posteriorly and to the non-pigmented inner ciliary epithelium anteriorly. The RPE extends posteriorly around the optic disc and anteriorly to the pigmented outer ciliary epithelium. Embryologically the retina, RPE, and epithelia of the iris and ciliary body derive from the inner (neurosensory retina) and outer layers of the optic cup.

As neuronal, non-proliferating tissues, the neurosensory retina and RPE are not prone to producing tumours, although retinocytoma, retinoblastoma, medulloepithelioma, and adenomas and adenocarcinomas of the RPE and the epithelia of the iris and ciliary body can occur.

In the fourth edition, tumours and tumour-like lesions of the retina and neuroepithelium were categorized together as per anatomical location. In this edition, under the neurosensory retina, RPE, and the iris and ciliary body neuroepithelia, the lesions are further classified to distinguish the clearly identified neoplasms from tumour-like lesions and congenital hamartomas. With emerging evidence of genetics, retinocytoma is classified under premalignant conditions {7073943;18785023;18211953}. A clear distinction is made between reactive and hamartomatous lesions and tumours of the RPE. The tumours of the neuroepithelium are classified as reactive and cystic lesions, or tumours such as adenoma, adenocarcinoma, and medulloepithelioma.

Retinoblastoma is the most common malignant intraocular tumour of childhood with a global incidence of 1 case per 16 000–18 000 live births {19704035}, now believed to have increased to 1 case per 13 880 live births because of increasing familial cases {33508429}. Rarely, RB1 sequence variants can result in a stable retinocytoma, although most retinocytomas degenerate into retinoblastoma {18211953;19653710}. Exceptional retinoblastomas result from high-level MYCN oncogene amplification {23498719}. Well-established protocols for genetic testing exist, including prenatal screening and consideration of early delivery {29056300;27712844}. Newer tools such as liquid biopsies of tumour-derived cell-free DNA from blood and intraocular fluids have emerging utility in prognostication and prenatal testing {33805427}.

The retinal glial tumours include astrocytomas, both syndromic (in tuberous sclerosis and, rarely, neurofibromatosis type 1) and non-syndromic forms.

The non-neoplastic RPE lesions include congenital hypertrophy, and simple and combined hamartomas, the latter of which are basically vitreoretinal in nature. Congenital RPE lesions are associated with syndromes like familial adenomatous polyposis {25326340;35321042}, Gardner syndrome, and neurofibromatosis type 2 {19476995}. The neoplastic tumours of RPE are extremely rare and include RPE adenoma and adenocarcinoma.

This section also covers cysts of the pigmented epithelium of the iris and ciliary body, and medulloepithelioma {23796765}, which is a rare congenital tumour of the ciliary body or optic nerve that may be associated with DICER1 mutations. Initially thought to resemble medulloepithelioma of the brain, later the molecular diversity at the cytogenetic and epigenetic levels enabled the identification of ocular medulloepithelioma as a distinct nosological entity {25807141}.
