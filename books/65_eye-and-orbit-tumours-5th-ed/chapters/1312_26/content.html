1.3.1.2 Junctional, compound, and subepithelial conjunctival naevi

Definition

Junctional, compound, and subepithelial conjunctival naevi are benign localized clonal proliferations of melanocytic naevus cells located (1) within the conjunctival epithelium and its invaginations, (2) at the epithelial–subepithelial junction and in the stroma, and (3) in the stroma with no junctional component, respectively.

Related terminology

Acceptable: conjunctival melanocytic naevus.

Subtype(s)

Junctional naevus; compound naevus; subepithelial (stromal) naevus; balloon cell naevus

Localization

Junctional melanocytic naevi are usually located at the juxtalimbal bulbar conjunctiva {14769591}. Most compound and subepithelial melanocytic naevi are located in the bulbar (commonly juxtalimbal) conjunctiva, caruncle, and plica semilunaris; rarely, they occur in the palpebral and forniceal conjunctiva {2657539;8883545;20683369;14769591}.

Clinical features

Conjunctival naevi are well-defined, variably pigmented (usually tan to brown) or amelanotic, mobile lesions, commonly arising on the bulbar conjunctiva followed by the caruncle; tarsal naevi are extremely rare {1476959;21128776}. As in the skin, junctional naevi are flat and compound, while subepithelial naevi show three-dimensionality. Compound naevi often show multiple small epithelial inclusion microcysts that are observed on the slit-lamp and suggest the benign nature of the lesion {2657539;34424954}.

Epidemiology

Junctional, compound, and subepithelial melanocytic naevi account for 3–6%, 70–78%, and 15–24% of biopsied conjunctival naevi, respectively {20622349;8883545;14769591;21128776}. Junctional melanocytic naevi have an M:F ratio of 2:1 and occur in childhood and adolescence. Compound and subepithelial melanocytic naevi are diagnosed at a median patient age of 22 years (range: 5–74 years) and 46 years (range: 12–87 years), respectively {20622349;2657539;8883545;14769591}. Compound and subepithelial melanocytic naevi have an M:F ratio of 1:1 and are more common in White populations.

Etiology

Conventional conjunctival naevi are acquired melanocytic naevi that arise from neural crest–derived melanocytes that have migrated into the conjunctival epithelium (junctional naevi) {29332123}. As in their cutaneous counterparts, the intraepithelial melanocytes migrate partially into the substantia propria and then ultimately into the subepithelial compartment (the equivalent of cutaneous dermal naevi) {2657539;21128776}. Naevus melanocytes mature and become smaller and spindle shaped with descent into the substantia propria and with progressive loss of pigmentation {34424954}.

Pathogenesis

Very few molecular analyses of conjunctival naevi are published compared with those of skin naevi. However, these studies confirm the parallels between the two lesions {29332123}.

Conjunctival naevi have mutually exclusive BRAF and NRAS mutations, which leads to constitutive activation of the MAPK pathway {27009410;27307047;26300491;12447372}. In a study investigating 25 conjunctival naevi, 9 (39%) were immunoreactive for NRAS p.Q61R and 13 (57%) for BRAF p.V600E. Next-generation sequencing identified an additional NRAS p.Q61K mutation in 1 immunohistochemistry-negative naevus case. GNAQ mutations are not typically present in junctional, compound, or subepithelial melanocytic naevi, but have been found in rarer conjunctival blue naevi {29332123}. Conjunctival naevi with NRAS mutations appear to be larger in diameter and associated with a younger age of onset than naevi with BRAF mutations {29332123}.

Macroscopic appearance

The naevi are discrete; flat or slightly elevated; circumscribed; pink, yellow-tan, brown, or non-pigmented; and located in the interpalpebral zone near the limbus {14769591}.

Histopathology

The three main histological types of melanocytic naevi are junctional, compound, and subepithelial.

Junctional naevi exhibit an intraepithelial proliferation of round to oval epithelioid cells and/or oval to cuboidal, intermediate, or lymphocyte-like naevus cells {2657539;21128776}. The nucleoli are usually basophilic without atypia. Mitotic figures are rare {2657539;21128776}. The naevus cells can be arranged as single, confluent, or irregular nests, and extend in a lentiginous fashion, but without pagetoid spread {2657539;21128776}.

Compound naevi contain naevus cells within the epithelium and the substantia propria, exhibiting maturation with depth. Solid and cystic surface epithelial invaginations of conjunctival epithelium (with goblet cells) are common between the naevus cells. Mitotic activity, if present, appears in the superficial layer {2657539;21128776}.

Subepithelial naevi are located in the substantia propria and often arranged as nests. They are more likely to be amelanotic or minimally pigmented {20142539}. The superficial naevus cells tend to be larger and more polygonal, whereas the deep naevus cells may display neuroid differentiation {2657539;20142539}. Sporadic pleomorphism can be seen. Mitotic figures are generally absent {20142539}. Solid and cystic conjunctival epithelial invaginations and entrapped nests are seen in about half of cases {20142539;14769591}.

Uncommonly, junctional and compound naevi may show disordered architectural alterations intermediate between conventional naevi and melanoma, such as increased diameter > 5 mm, peripheral extension of the junctional component, asymmetry, poor circumscription, irregular and horizontally confluent junctional nesting, and focal pagetoid spread. Aberrant alterations in the subepithelial component may include diminished or even reversed maturation, nodule formation, and occasional mitotic activity. On occasion, naevi may be inflamed with prominent lymphocytic infiltrates resembling halo naevi in the skin. Eosinophils may be prominent in some cases.

Immunohistochemistry

Most conjunctival naevi demonstrate positivity for HMB45 in the superficial areas only {11683945}, but homogeneous deeper staining for melan-A, SOX10, and MITF. They are usually negative for cyclin D1 and PRAME, whereas most conjunctival melanomas are positive for these markers {34268800;34089198;35356600}.

Differential diagnosis

For junctional melanocytic naevi, the main differential is conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesion (see section Conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesion). Junctional melanocytic lesions in an adult should be assumed to be conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial lesions until proven otherwise {19128387;2657539;21128776;33130046}. For compound and subepithelial melanocytic naevi, the two main differentials are conjunctival melanoma and extraocular extension of uveal melanoma. Conjunctival melanoma usually exhibits a larger size, aberrant architecture (e.g. confluent and hypercellular nesting), cytological atypia, and greater mitotic activity as compared with naevi. Melanoma cells are larger than naevus cells and show conspicuous nuclear atypia {2657539;30672666}. Adjoining melanoma in situ may be present.

Extraocular extension of a uveal melanoma will show widespread broad involvement of the biopsy base by melanoma cells with characteristic cytology and genetics {23222563;32273508} (see section Choroidal and ciliary body melanomas for details of cytology and genetics).

Cytology

Cytology shows oval cells (type A), oval to cuboidal cells (type B), and spindle-like cells (type C) {21128776}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a conjunctival melanocytic lesion composed of naevus cells arranged in orderly junctional and/or subepithelial nests, cords, and individual cell patterns with maturation and general absence of architectural and cytological abnormalities.

Desirable: entrapped conjunctival epithelial cysts and nests surrounded by naevus cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Rarely, a junctional or compound naevus of the conjunctiva can give rise to conjunctival melanoma {2657539;15763188;14769591}. Subepithelial naevi have a low risk of progression to melanoma {2657539}.
