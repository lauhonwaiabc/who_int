4.1.1.1 Astrocytic tumours

Definition

Astrocytic tumours of the retina are low-grade astrocytic neoplasms arising in the inner retina. This category of tumours includes retinal astrocytic hamartoma (RAH) (congenital; typically syndromic and associated most often with tuberous sclerosis and less commonly with neurofibromatosis type 1) and acquired retinal astrocytoma (non-syndromic).

Related terminology

Acceptable: astrocytic hamartoma of the retina; acquired retinal astrocytoma.

Not recommended: intraocular glioma; retinal phakoma.

Subtype(s)

Giant cell astrocytoma of the retina

Localization

Retinal astrocytomas are generally discrete, slightly elevated lesions in the inner retina that expand the nerve fibre layer. They are found most commonly in the posterior pole within the vascular arcades, adjacent to the optic nerve, or (less frequently) in the retinal periphery {22608477}.

Clinical features

Astrocytic tumours of the retina are usually asymptomatic and are detected incidentally or on screening of patients with suspected phakomatoses. They are discrete, slightly elevated lesions arising in the inner retina. Most tumours grow by direct extension but vitreous seeding has also been reported {18536607}. Macular tumours or those complicated by haemorrhage {17259921}, proliferative retinopathy, or exudation {15359261} may cause visual disturbance. RAH is thought to be congenital but may not be clinically apparent until later in life. It has been reported to occur in 36–44% of patients with tuberous sclerosis; the tumours were bilateral in 34–43% of patients and multiple in 40% of patients {22608477;11264130}.

A study of RAH by optical coherence tomography identified a gradual transition between normal retina and the optically hyperreflective mass with associated retinal disorganization (optically empty, so-called moth-eaten spaces) and posterior shadowing as characteristic features {27157148}. Presumed retinal pericapillary astrocytic hamartoma has been described as a form of RAH with a glistening granular appearance on fundoscopy and perivascular hyperreflectivity on optical coherence tomography {32980819}. Clinically, retinal astrocytomas may mimic retinoblastoma in children, and amelanotic melanoma, haemangioblastoma, and metastatic carcinoma in adults, particularly in patients lacking other syndromic manifestations. The clinical differential diagnosis also includes presumed solitary circumscribed retinal astrocytic proliferation {21911666;31118830}.

Epidemiology

Astrocytic hamartomas are often associated with a phakomatosis, most tuberous sclerosis or neurofibromatosis. Tuberous sclerosis has an estimated incidence of 1 case per 6000–10 000 live births {30016967}. In a review of 42 intraocular astrocytomas confirmed histologically, 57% were associated with tuberous sclerosis, 14% were related to neurofibromatosis, and 29% were sporadic {6421263}. A few cases have been reported in patients with retinitis pigmentosa {30280590} but most reports lack histopathological confirmation. RAH generally arises in children or young adults although some cases have presented at an older age. Acquired retinal astrocytoma generally occurs in older individuals who have no family history or clinical findings of any syndrome.

Etiology

RAH is most commonly associated with tuberous sclerosis, which is caused by a pathogenic variant in either TSC1 or TSC2.

Pathogenesis

The protein product of TSC1 is hamartin while that of TSC2 is tuberin, both part of a heteromeric protein complex known as TSC that inactivates the mTOR protein kinase pathway {12271141}. Disruption of TSC results in upregulation of the mTOR pathway and increases proliferation and cell growth. Elucidation of the pathobiology has led to the use of mTOR inhibitors in the treatment of tuberous sclerosis–associated tumours {27351628;35230473}. Many are centred in the inner retina, suggestive of the tumours originating from astrocytes of the nerve fibre layer. One case of giant cell astrocytoma of the retina displayed ultrastructural features of a Müller glial cell origin {6677857}.

Macroscopic appearance

Retinal astrocytic tumours are well-circumscribed, slightly elevated, yellow to grey-white lesions arising in the inner retina. RAHs are more poorly defined and translucent than acquired retinal astrocytomas, and they have associated calcifications {23960860;27493794}.

Histopathology

Two main histopathological patterns have been described {23960860;25171711}. The first is characterized by fascicles of spindle-shaped cells with cytologically bland nuclei. The second, seen in giant cell astrocytomas of the retina subtype, shows larger tumour cells, abundant glassy eosinophilic cytoplasm (gemistocytic), round nuclei, and prominent nucleoli {19873950}, morphologically similar to subependymal giant cell astrocytoma in the brain, and demonstrates a greater propensity for invasive growth. As is the case with subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, both glial and neuronal immunohistochemical markers can be expressed. Calcifications and necrosis are common in this second subtype of tumour. Although mitotic figures are rare or absent, these tumours can grow and eventually fill the posterior globe and are then termed aggressive retinal astrocytomas {15747752;22654497}. Rosenthal fibres, eosinophilic granular bodies, and other features of pilocytic astrocytoma are generally absent.

Immunohistochemistry

Retinal astrocytomas are usually diffusely positive for GFAP and S100. Most tumours are negative for neuronal markers; however, the larger gemistocytic cells may coexpress NSE and have variable staining for GFAP and S100 {25171711}.

Differential diagnosis

The histological differential diagnosis would include nodular and massive retinal gliosis, which lack abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, do not have features similar to subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, and have a very low (usually < 1%) Ki-67 proliferation index {23219067}. RAH is also distinguished from a combined hamartoma of the retina and retinal pigment epithelium, which is associated with neurofibromatosis type 2.

Cytology

FNAB reveals bland spindle cells with fibrillar cytoplasm {18536607;15019391}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a tumour arising in the inner retina; spindle-shaped and/or gemistocytic astrocytes; low mitotic activity.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

RAH generally remains stable and is not clinically aggressive {7872393}, but progressive growth and severe local complications have been reported in exceptional cases. The cases with large gemistocytic cells and necrosis sometimes develop iris neovascularization and glaucoma {15747752}. In contrast to RAH, acquired retinal astrocytoma exhibits a more aggressive clinical course with slow enlargement and progressive exudation {15019391}. Current treatment modalities include photodynamic therapy {33133446;17305725} in combination with verteporfin {18759802}, laser photocoagulation, and plaque brachytherapy {19264224}. Aggressive retinal astrocytomas may require enucleation of the globe.
