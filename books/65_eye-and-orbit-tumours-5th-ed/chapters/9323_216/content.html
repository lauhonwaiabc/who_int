9.3.2.3 CD30-positive lymphoproliferative disorders

Definition

Primary cutaneous CD30+ lymphoproliferative disorders (CD30+ LPD) include lymphomatoid papulosis (LyP) and primary cutaneous anaplastic large cell lymphoma (C-ALCL). LyP is characterized by self-healing but recurrent papulonodular skin lesions, atypical CD30-positive T cells by histology, and an excellent prognosis. C-ALCL is composed of large cells with an anaplastic, pleomorphic, or immunoblastic cytomorphology; > 75% of the tumour cells express CD30.

Related terminology

Acceptable: ocular adnexal T-cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

Cutaneous anaplastic large cell lymphoma and lymphomatoid papulosis

Localization

Ocular adnexal CD30+ LPDs typically arise in the eyelid skin, with C-ALCL found to affect mostly the upper eyelid {10571346;9709754;28209503;27894880}. They may be primary but are usually secondary to systemic involvement.

Clinical features

Ocular adnexal CD30+ LPDs usually present with unilateral erythema and/or ulceration and swelling of the eyelid, or masses with or without pain, foreign body sensation, or tearing {9709754;32732342;28209503;27894880;30144455}.

LyP papulonodular lesions have a typical waxing and waning course with different stages of evolution coexisting, and may show partial or complete regression, but relapse is common. LyP may persist for months or several years {7104214;10845893;27894880;28209503;21982062}.

C-ALCL may present with a solitary eyelid-skin nodule often with ulceration {10845893;14639383;28209503;27894880}. Like LyP, it can show partial or complete regression but relapse is common.

Epidemiology

The majority of ocular adnexal T-cell lymphomas (OATCLs) are secondary to systemic disease or direct extension. Primary OATCLs constitute much less than 1% of ocular adnexal lymphomas. Subtypes and their frequency are different for the eyelid compared with the rest of the ocular adnexa, where they are extremely rare.

CD30+ LPDs are the second most common cutaneous T-cell lymphoma with mycosis fungoides being the most common.

LyP most often affects young adults (median age 35–45 years), but children may also be affected, and has a female preponderance {15023774;14710887;10845893;21982062;28209503;27894880;14639383}.

C-ALCL generally occurs in people aged > 50 years (median age 60 years), but children can also be affected, and shows male predominance, similarly to other OATCLs {10845893;27894880;28209503;30635287;10571346;9709754;28399341;30144455}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Atopy and staphylococcal superantigens have been pathogenetically linked to LyP {32049976}.

A viral etiology has been suggested, but no association has been established {2877013;8227605;8393923;11168749}. Endogenous retroviral elements have been detected, but their etiological role is unclear {12584181;31883959}.

Pathogenesis

CD30+ LPDs generally lack the translocation t(2;5) and ALK expression, unlike systemic ALCL {8605362}. Also described are DUSP22 downregulation; the NPM::TYK2 translocation; STAT mutations and oncogenic fusion transcripts activating the JAK/STAT pathway; and other frequent chromosomal aberrations including 7q3 gains, and 6q16-21 and 13q34 losses {21169992;20130593;25461779;19710685;25349176;27894880;32147503}. SATB1 is expressed in the vast majority of LyP cases {29510190}.

Clonal TR rearrangements have been demonstrated in some LyP cases and associated lymphomas {8618007;25666664;31564712;27894880;30144455}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

There are six histological subtypes (A–E and 6p25.3 rearrangements) of LyP with distinct histological and/or genetic features {7104214;10571346;10845893;14710887;15096372;29719017;20661014;23026936;23648461}. Broadly, they may comprise diffuse infiltration of small to medium- or large-sized atypical lymphocytes with cerebriform nuclei; occasional large cells (centroblasts, immunoblasts, or Reed–Sternberg–like cells); and scattered plasma cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, and histiocytes.

C-ALCL shows anaplastic lymphocytes with round / oval / irregularly shaped nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and abundant cytoplasm. Similarly to LyP, ulcerating lesions with mixed inflammatory cells are also common {12653754;10571346;10845893;18095994;7751878;30635287}.

Epidermotropism may be present in both proliferations. Lymphovascular invasion is rare.

Immunohistochemistry

Atypical lymphoid cells express CD30 and have an activated CD4+ and/or CD8+ T-cell phenotype depending on subtype. C-ALCL has frequent expression of cytotoxic markers including CD56, TIA1, and granzyme B, and variable loss of CD2, CD3, CD5, and CD7 {10571346;10845893;10672058;9347791;24394302;30635287}. CD15 is expressed in approximately 40% of cases {19416236;18234282}. Tumour cells are negative for B-cell–associated antigens.

Differential diagnosis

C-ALCL and LyP can show significant overlapping histological and phenotypic features among themselves, and also with other cutaneous or systemic TCLs.

Clinicopathological correlation is essential to differentiate LyP not only from these lymphomas, but also from a wide variety of infectious and inflammatory skin diseases that can contain substantial numbers of CD30+ cells {20043512;16412214;18616762}.

The term “borderline lesions” refers to cases in which, despite careful clinicopathological correlation, a definite distinction between primary C-ALCL and LyP cannot be made at diagnosis, but evolution of clinical presentation during follow-up discloses whether such patients have C-ALCL or LyP {10845893;14710887;30635287;7751878}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clonal TR gene rearrangements may be detected in C-ALCL and in some LyP cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: atypical lymphoid cells expressing CD30 (> 75% of tumour cells in C-ALCL) and T-cell markers; for LyP: a waxing and waning papulonodular rash with spontaneous regression; exclusion of mycosis fungoides; exclusion of reactive process; for C-ALCL: clonal T-cell population; lymphoid cells with anaplastic, pleomorphic, or immunoblastic morphology; no waxing and waning lesions (exclude LyP); exclusion of mycosis fungoides and other TCLs.

Desirable: for Lyp: detection of monoclonal rearrangement of TR genes may be helpful in select cases (especially in subtype A).

Staging

LyP: In patients with the typical clinical presentation and no indication of extracutaneous disease on physical examination or blood tests, there is no need for further staging {21841159;20026848}.

C-ALCL: The TNM classification system for primary cutaneous lymphomas other than mycosis fungoides is applied {17339420}.

Prognosis and prediction

CD30+ LPDs have a favourable prognosis with absent or low mortality and therefore exclusion of other TCLs is essential to avoid overtreatment.

LyP can have an increased risk (15–50%) of a second lymphoma, particularly mycosis fungoides and C-ALCL, and some non-haematological malignancies, such as cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma, and organ-based malignancies. Long-term follow-up is advised owing to increased risk of associated lymphomas {21841159;14710887;10506709;26518172;10845893;21982062;26668250;14639383;1532439;28614957;31715046}.

For C-ALCL, worse outcomes are associated with extensive skin lesions on the legs or age > 60 years {20026848;14639383;25068227;19528422;34081802;32732342;28209503;10571346;27894880}.
