2.4.2.1 Invasive cutaneous melanoma

Definition

Invasive cutaneous melanomas of the eyelid are a family of malignant invasive melanocytic neoplasms derived from surface epithelial melanocytes, characterized by a radial or vertical growth phase.

Localization

Lentigo maligna melanoma (LMM) and spindle cell and desmoplastic melanoma, which are strongly related to chronic sun damage, tend to occur commonly on the head and neck, including the eyelid and periocular skin. All other melanoma types (see the Skin tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Skin Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]]) may occur at any site of the skin, including the eyelid or periocular region, but are less frequent.

Clinical features

The clinical appearance differs depending upon the type of melanoma. LMM and spindle cell and desmoplastic melanoma occur on chronic actinic damaged skin of elderly patients. In contrast, melanoma in skin with a low degree of cumulative sun damage (low-CSD melanoma) / superficial spreading melanoma (SSM) arises in middle-aged patients, and nearly two thirds of patients are lighter skinned (Fitzpatrick skin types I–III). Dermoscopic examination of lentigo maligna typically reveals perifollicular granular grey dots, with formation of dark rhomboidal structures, in later stages eventuating in obliteration of follicular ostia.

All melanomas range widely in pigmentation, from very dark and heavily melanized to hypomelanotic and amelanotic. Often they are multicoloured. Most tumours are asymmetrical and the borders are poorly defined, with the exception of nodular, naevoid, and malignant Spitz tumours (Spitz melanoma). The latter are usually well circumscribed and symmetrical.

Epidemiology

Eyelid melanoma accounts for approximately 1% of all cutaneous melanomas, representing < 1% of all eyelid malignancies. The incidence rose from 0.2 cases per 1 million person-years in 1978 to 1 case per 1 million person-years in 2016. It affects patients of all ages (range: 4–103 years) but most commonly elderly individuals (median age at diagnosis: 71 years), and it mainly affects White individuals (95.6%). The lower eyelid is more frequently the site of origin than the upper eyelid (66%).

In terms of histological types, LMM is the most commonly diagnosed (24%), followed by low-CSD melanoma / SSM (21.8%), and nodular melanoma (7.7%). The remaining cases (5%) are either rare subtypes or NOS {30578807;1775323}.

Etiology

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure is the main etiological factor {26091043}. Other factors include pre-existent naevi with dysplasia, or large congenital naevi {8970587}.

Pathogenesis

Most of our understanding of eyelid-skin melanoma is extrapolated from studies of cutaneous melanoma located elsewhere on the body. Melanomas are linked to sun exposure and mostly show a UV radiation mutation signature as recognized by cytosine to thymidine transition at dipyrimidine sites. The use of tanning beds has been linked to an increased rate of melanoma in young women {26818409}. Melanomas arise through the accumulation of mutations that activate growth-promoting pathways and ablate tumour suppressor mechanisms. Mutations activating the MAPK pathway and those related to cell-cycle G1/S checkpoint control or chromatin modification are common. TERT promoter mutation, which leads to cell immortalization, is the most prevalent in cutaneous melanoma {26091043;23348506}. Although the subtypes share alterations in these pathways, the manner in which the pathways are deregulated shows considerable variation among the subtypes. Melanomas related to high CSD, such as LMM and spindle cell melanoma, have an extremely high mutation burden with approximately 30 mutations/Mb of DNA {26214590} and even 62 mutations/Mb in desmoplastic melanoma {26343386}. In contrast, low-CSD melanoma / SSM, which is related to sunburn in childhood and intermittent sun exposure throughout life, has about 15 mutations/Mb. Findings from molecular pathology indicate that low-CSD melanoma / SSM mostly derives from a naevus background with or without dysplasia. Therefore, BRAF p.V600E or NRAS gain-of-function mutations are mostly found. In high-CSD melanomas, inactivation mutations of NF1 are frequent and BRAF mutations rare. If present, these are BRAF non-p.V600E {26091043}. For further details, see the Skin tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Skin Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]].

Macroscopic appearance

Lesions are variably pigmented, flat or nodular, and asymmetrical in shape and structure. They are multicoloured, light brown, dark brown, blue-black, or black, with or without ulceration and mostly with irregular borders.

Histopathology

There is intraepidermal and dermal proliferation of atypical melanocytes or initially dermal atypical proliferation without an epidermal component. Low-CSD melanoma / SSM is characterized by a low degree of sun exposure, in contrast to LMM and spindle cell and desmoplastic melanoma. These melanomas show a high degree of actinic damage with solar elastosis, atrophic epidermis, and often a loss of rete ridges. Low-CSD melanoma / SSM has a prominent intraepidermal pagetoid spread, whereas LMM and spindle cell and desmoplastic melanomas have a lentiginous intraepidermal pattern with single cell proliferation. Spindle cell melanoma has a spindle-cell morphology, naevoid melanoma is naevus like, and Spitz melanoma shows a Spitz naevus–like morphology. Spindle cell melanoma differs from desmoplastic melanoma only in the cell content, which is much less in desmoplastic melanoma. Malignant blue naevus is dermally located, without an epidermal component, and remnants of blue naevus are often present. In contrast to other melanoma types, nodular melanoma grows vertically without a previous radial growth phase {6500548;3699806;5773814;26645730;30250577;16446713}.

Further details for each type can be found in the Skin tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Skin Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]]. Diagnostic reports should include tumour thickness; Clark level; ulceration; mitoses/mm2; tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (brisk/non-brisk); regression (if present, less/more than 50%); vascular invasion; angiotropic, syringotropic, or perineural dissemination; distance to resection margins of invasive and non-invasive melanomas; and associated naevus. Evidence of molecular defects present, particularly BRAF mutation, should be included in the integrated report.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In cases difficult to distinguish from naevi, especially Spitz naevi, next-generation sequencing may be helpful to achieve a correct diagnosis. The identification of the driver mutation and a high mutation burden and/or numerous gene amplifications or deletions together with the morphology supports the diagnosis of melanoma {34757982}. Identification of targetable mutations is important in patients with metastases or at high risk of metastasis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: changes supporting a diagnosis of melanoma (a cytologically atypical, nested, and individual cell intraepidermal melanocytic proliferation; an invasive component of similar atypical melanocytes may be seen); low to moderate CSD (solar elastosis) for low-CSD melanoma; pre-existing lentigo maligna for LMM.

Desirable: intraepidermal pagetoid scatter or a confluent lentiginous proliferation; extension of the intraepidermal in situ melanoma three rete ridges beyond any dermal invasive component; mutation status for BRAF.

Staging

If metastases are not already present, sentinel node biopsy should be performed for patients at high risk of metastatic disease (> pT2) {25436498;19766318}.

Prognosis and prediction

Poor prognostic factors include vertical growth phase, increased tumour thickness, ulceration, high mitotic rate, few tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, lymphovascular invasion, microscopic satellites, and angiotropism.

Surgical excision of eyelid melanoma with 5–10 mm margins, with evaluation of margins on paraffin sections, and delayed reconstruction can lead to excellent local control rates {25053759}.

Many melanomas are amenable to tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatment and/or immunotherapy.
