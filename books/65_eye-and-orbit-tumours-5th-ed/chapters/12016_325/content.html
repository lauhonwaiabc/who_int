12.0.1.6 Goldenhar syndrome

Definition

Goldenhar syndrome (GS) is a constellation of congenital anomalies due to impaired formation of the face, ears, eyes, and skeletal system, as well as variable abnormalities of internal organs, due to anomalous development of the first and second branchial arches.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

164210 Craniofacial microsomia; CFM

ICD-11 coding

LD2F.16 Otomandibular dysplasia

Related terminology

Acceptable: craniofacial microsomia; oculoauriculovertebral syndrome; otomandibular dysplasia; oculoauriculovertebral dysplasia; facioauriculovertebral sequence; Goldenhar–Gorlin syndrome.

Not recommended: first and second branchial arch syndrome; hemifacial microsomia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

GS is unilateral in 70–90% of cases. Epibulbar dermoids are most common at the inferotemporal limbus but may also occur nasally. They may affect the superficial cornea only, involve the full-thickness stroma, or extend through the entire cornea into the anterior chamber. Lipodermoids are more common superotemporally and are less specific for GS. Eyelid colobomas are most frequently located at the junction of the middle and inner thirds of the upper eyelid {17100205;33367184}.

Clinical features

GS shows variable expressivity; the classic disease phenotypic triad involves mandibular or hemifacial hypoplasia, ocular and auricular malformations, especially limbal dermoids and accessory tragi, and vertebral anomalies, particularly of the cervical spine. CNS malformations and cardiac and urogenital anomalies are variably encountered {28623555}. The ophthalmological features typically include the upper eyelid with or without intraocular colobomas (6–75%), epibulbar choristoma or dermoid/dermolipoma (30–50%), and less frequently microphthalmia/anophthalmia, strabismus (particularly Duane syndrome), cataract, unequal palpebral fissures, ptosis, decreased corneal sensation, microcornea, and rarely posterior segment abnormalities such as optic nerve hypoplasia {4697181;26892494;30510760;31760545;6514297;4050929}. Abnormalities of the lacrimal drainage system including nasolacrimal duct obstruction and atresia may cause recurrent dacryocystitis {4050929;30510760}. GS in association with complex choristoma, linear naevus sebaceus syndrome, and/or encephalocraniocutaneous lipomatosis has been reported {30510760}.

Epidemiology

GS is the second most frequent craniofacial malformation after cleft palate and occurs in about 1:5000 births, although estimations based on specific populations vary widely. Most, but not all, large studies show a male predominance {4697181;31760545;4050929}.

Etiology

GS is typically sporadic and the etiology in most cases is poorly understood. Inheritance typically occurs in an autosomal dominant fashion when a family history is present.

Pathogenesis

GS results from late first trimester abnormal development of structures derived from the first and second branchial arches. Numerous copy-number variants have been detected in patients with clinical GS, including 22q11.2 deletions and 14q22.3 duplications (OTX2). SNVs have also been described in various candidate genes, including MYT1 {35110414}. Recently, heterogeneous loss-of-function variants in SF3B2 at chromosome 11q13.1 have been identified in 25% of patients with a family history of GS and 3% of sporadic cases via whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing of a large cohort; inheritance was dominant with interfamilial and intrafamilial phenotypic variability {34344887}. GS is also more common in twins; in infants of mothers with diabetes mellitus; and in children born to mothers with potential disruptions in placental vascularization, including exposure to certain medications, hypertension, and smoking {28623555}.

Macroscopic appearance

This is the same as in the sporadic tumour types involved in the syndrome.

Histopathology

As in sporadic cases, epibulbar dermoids are composed of dense subepithelial collagen bundles resembling dermal collagen rather than substantia propria. Dermal appendages including pilosebaceous and apocrine/eccrine units are present to varying degrees. Variable amounts of fat can be observed; dermolipomas contain abundant adipose tissue. Complex choristomas composed of particularly lacrimal gland, cartilage, and smooth muscle are also seen {26892494;2655139}. Complex choristoma constituted 10.8% of all epibulbar choristomas in one of the series, and GS was found in 38% of these cases, while osseous choristoma was less common (1.7%) and isolated {30510760}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Currently, genetic testing is not necessary for diagnosis. While a variety of alterations, including copy-number variants and SNVs, are correlated with the development of the GS phenotype, these findings are not diagnostic or identifiable in many cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: facial asymmetry with preauricular tags; an ocular finding such as epibular dermoid or eyelid coloboma.

Note: specific diagnostic criteria are still debated.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of patients with GS have normal intelligence and lifespan. Without systemic complications, the prognosis is good. Newborns may experience difficulty in breathing, feeding, and sleeping due to mandibular and other hypoplasias. Eyelid reconstruction may be needed early in life owing to exposure keratopathy and corneal ulceration from eyelid coloboma {25419956}. Dermoids and lipodermoids may induce astigmatism or cause direct visual obstruction, leading to amblyopia; early surgical excision may prevent these complications {33367184}.
