8.1.2.2 Arteriovenous malformation

Definition

Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are rare fast-flow vascular lesions composed of anomalous arterial and venous vessels connected directly without an intervening capillary bed.

ICD-O coding

9123/0 Arteriovenous malformation

ICD-11 coding

LA90.3Y Other specified peripheral arteriovenous malformations

Related terminology

Not recommended: arteriovenous haemangioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lesions arise within the orbit and may extend into the surrounding tissues (e.g. nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, deep facial tissues, intracranial space) {34224454}. Different structures of the eye can be affected {35226863;34710889}.

Clinical features

AVMs are usually present at birth showing a significant arteriovenous shunting with raised skin temperature and pulsation if superficial. Haemorrhage and ischaemia due to arterial steal are relatively uncommon in the periorbital region. Deep-seated lesions may become apparent at a later age when shunting is low-grade {26055853;26839221;31950476}. Orbital AVMs can cause significant morbidity including chronic pain, decreased vision, and cosmetic disfigurement with exophthalmos {18716969}. When the compromised vessels rupture, the consequences can be devastating {33069286}. Doppler ultrasonography and MRI are the techniques most often used for their investigation {20888460}.

Epidemiology

AVMs in the orbit and eye are rare {18716969}. There is no sex predilection {20888460}.

Etiology

Vascular malformations arise during embryogenesis owing to mosaic mutations or, more rarely, germline mutations leading to developmental errors in the vascular system {31950476;26839221;34166070}. Most AVMs are sporadic with a few cases occurring within familial disorders {33069286}.

Pathogenesis

Several members of the MAPK signalling pathway have been implicated in sporadic AVMs. They include hyperactivating changes in the RAS genes and MAP2K1 {33069286}. AVMs in familial disorders are driven by changes in RASA1 and EPHB4 {33069286}.

Macroscopic appearance

AVMs are composed of a tangle of large thick-walled vessels and cystic cavities.

Histopathology

The histological appearance of AVMs is variable. There are numerous large-calibre arteries and thick-walled veins. The arteries are often tortuous and the veins show intimal and adventitial fibrosis. Arterioles, venules, capillaries, dilated lymphatics, and undefined vessels may be present. The background is often fibrous or fibromyxoid. The overlying skin may show a proliferation of small vessels probably due to arteriovenous shunt–related tissue ischaemia {26839221;31950476}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: specific clinical findings (pain, warmth, and bleeding episodes) and radiological features (see Clinical features).

Desirable: histology showing vessels of different sizes, including both arteries and veins; fibrointimal thickening in veins.

Staging

The Schobinger staging system describes four stages of progression {20888460;26055853}:

Stage I: asymptomatic pink patches or maculae that often mimic capillary malformation; increased warmth or thrill to palpation

Stage II: vessels becoming dilated and prominent; increased warmth and thrill; prominent draining veins; skin may thicken and become more purple

Stage III: destruction of deeper tissues, including bone; pain and haemorrhage

Stage IV: cardiac compromise from increased blood flow.

Prognosis and prediction

AVMs are the most problematic vascular lesions to manage. They never regress spontaneously {33069286}. When treatment is indicated (pain, bleeding, enlargement, ulceration), a multidisciplinary approach, including embolization and resection, is often necessary {18716969;20888460}. Recurrence is common, particularly when the nidus is fed from small vessels {20888460;34224454}. Newer strategies including mTOR and VEGF inhibitors show promise for future medical management {33069286;34166070;34224454}.
