10.2.1.4 Osteosarcoma

Definition

Osteosarcoma is a high-grade sarcoma in which the tumour cells produce bone. A rare low-grade type exists called low-grade central osteosarcoma (LGCOS), which is a low-grade malignant bone-forming neoplasm that originates within the intramedullary cavity and consists of fibroblastic tumour cells with low-grade nuclear atypia and well-formed neoplastic bony trabeculae.

Related terminology

Acceptable: for low-grade central osteosarcoma: well-differentiated intramedullary osteosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Subtypes of high-grade osteosarcoma: telangiectatic osteosarcoma; small cell osteosarcoma

Localization

Osteosarcoma is much more common in the extremities, but the orbit may rarely be involved.

Clinical features

There is usually a relatively short history of a painful, enlarging mass and occasionally restricted movement of the eye.

Epidemiology

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary high-grade sarcoma of the skeleton, but presentation in the orbital bones without a history of irradiation is very uncommon. A bimodal age distribution is known but most cases develop between 14 and 18 years and a second smaller peak occurs in older adults.

LGCOS is rare, accounting for 1–2% of all osteosarcomas. It most commonly affects young adults, with a peak incidence in the third decade of life {2306687}. There is a mild female predilection {18425560;20601938}.

Etiology

Ionizing irradiation used in the treatment of retinoblastoma is the strongest risk factor for developing secondary osteosarcoma and appears to be dose dependent. It tends to develop in areas with lower potentially mutagenic doses at the margins of the radiation field.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis and cell of origin of osteosarcoma are unknown, though mesenchymal stem cells have been suggested in murine models. Many potential driver genes have been identified {28643781}. Although these mutations occur early in disease development, it seems likely that chromothripsis caused by an unknown trigger initiates chromosomal instability and subsequent tumour development {28643781}. Amplification of 12q13-q15 involving MDM2 and CDK4 is common in low-grade osteosarcoma {30001240;9619593;21336260}. MDM2 amplification is maintained in high-grade progression. A small subset of LGCOSs lack MDM2 amplification.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteosarcoma presents itself as a destructive mass with destruction of the cortex. The cut surface is heterogeneous, depending on the type and degree of mineralization of the predominant matrix. Most tumours are tan-white and in high-grade cases they may show areas of necrosis. The telangiectatic subtype shows a haemorrhagic multicystic appearance.

Histopathology

High-grade osteosarcoma has a broad histomorphological spectrum. Essential to the diagnosis is the identification of neoplastic bone formation. The tumour grows with a permeative pattern, replacing the marrow space and encasing and eroding pre-existing trabeculae. The neoplastic cells typically demonstrate severe anaplasia and pleomorphism, and they may be fusiform, plasmacytoid, or epithelioid. Neoplastic cells often become small and normalized in appearance (mimicking benign osteocytes) when surrounded by bone matrix. Mitotic activity is usually brisk, and abundant atypical mitotic figures are often present, which are useful in the differential diagnosis of benign mimics.

Bone matrix is eosinophilic on H&E-stained sections if unmineralized and basophilic/purple if mineralized, and it may have a pagetoid appearance imparted by haphazardly deposited cement lines. Distinguishing unmineralized matrix (osteoid) from other eosinophilic extracellular materials such as collagen or compacted fibrin matrices may be difficult and subjective. High-grade osteosarcoma may have different histological patterns. Currently, there is no relationship between histological patterns, treatment, and prognosis {12044509}.

High-grade osteosarcoma commonly contains varying amounts of neoplastic cartilage and/or fibroblastic components; on the basis of the predominant matrix, they are subdivided into osteoblastic (76–80%), chondroblastic (10–13%), and fibroblastic (10%) patterns {12044509}. In osteoblastic osteosarcoma, neoplastic bone is the principal matrix and varies from thin, lace-like trabeculae to compact bone. When the latter is pronounced, the tumour is designated as a sclerosing osteosarcoma.

In telangiectatic osteosarcoma, the tumour is composed of blood-filled or empty cystic spaces closely simulating an aneurysmal bone cyst. The septa show variable thickness and are populated by pleomorphic cells showing substantial nuclear hyperchromasia. Atypical mitoses are easily identified. Osteoid formation is usually focal and confluent but may be absent in a biopsy. The septa also contain osteoclast-type giant cells.

Small cell osteosarcoma is composed of small cells with scant cytoplasm, associated with osteoid production. Nuclei are round to oval and the chromatin may be fine to coarse; mitoses can easily be found. Particular care must be taken to distinguish osteoid from fibrin deposits that may be seen among Ewing sarcoma cells.

Low-grade central osteosarcoma is composed of mildly to moderately cellular fascicles of spindle cells with mild nuclear atypia in a fibrous stroma. These are admixed with a neoplastic bone component, often with a parallel arrangement. Permeation of host bone is invariably present. Cortical destruction with soft tissue infiltration may also be present. Mitotic activity is low. In 10–36% of cases, the tumour may progress to high-grade sarcoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No specific diagnostic molecular pathology tests are available for high-grade osteosarcoma. Testing for MDM2 amplification is helpful to differentiate low-grade osteosarcoma from benign mimics. Unlike fibrous dysplasia, LGCOS lacks GNAS mutation {26248895}. The lack of MDM2 amplification cannot exclude the diagnosis of LGCOS.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

High-grade osteosarcoma

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; bone production by tumour cells; permeative and destructive growth pattern.

Desirable: tumour cells with high-grade atypia; atypical mitotic figures frequently present.

Low-grade osteosarcoma

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; intramedullary location; predominantly fibroblastic osteosarcoma with mild nuclear atypia and well-formed neoplastic bony trabeculae.

Desirable: MDM2 amplification.

Staging

The eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control’s (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours stages osteosarcoma of the skull and facial bones on the basis of the greatest tumour dimension (≤ 80 mm is T1; > 80 mm is T2) and discontinuous involvement of the primary bone site (T3). The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer’s AJCC cancer staging manual stages osteosarcoma arising in the craniofacial bones on the basis of the presence or absence of distant metastasis, histological grade, and greatest tumour dimension (≤ 80 mm or > 80 mm).

Prognosis and prediction

Aggressive local growth and rapid haematogenous dissemination characterize the clinical course. Pulmonary metastases are the most frequent sites of systemic disease. High-grade osteosarcoma is treated with preoperative and postoperative chemotherapy; local control is achieved via surgical resection with wide margins. Radiation can be used for unresectable tumours if not the cause of the tumour {20943636}. The use of chemotherapy has a dramatic impact on outcome {21703851;22055968}. Patients presenting with metastatic or recurrent disease have a survival rate of < 30% {12743156;18754698}. The histological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy remains one of the most important prognosticators of overall and disease-free survival {21703851;22055968}. A good response is usually defined as ≥ 90% necrosis {6577010;29200101}.

LGCOS has a good prognosis when widely resected, with a metastatic rate of < 5% and 5-year and 10-year overall survival rates of 90% and > 80%, respectively {22851905;8542697;2306687}. The tumour invariably recurs after curettage and incomplete excision.
