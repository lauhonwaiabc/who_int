8.0.0.1 Tumours of the orbit: Introduction

The orbit is the space behind and around the eye that nestles the latter within the bony orbital cavity of the skull and allows for its movement. The volume of the adult orbit is approximately 30 cm3 and its shape is a square-based pyramid with four bony walls and the apex pointing posteriorly [[Tasman W, Jaeger EA, editors. Embryology and Anatomy of the Orbit and Lacrimal System. Duane’s Ophthalmology. Philadelphia (PA): Lippincott Williams & Wilkins; 2007.]]. It contains the main lacrimal gland, the optic nerve, and the extraocular muscles, with the remainder of the space occupied by adipose tissue, fascia, blood vessels, and peripheral nerves. The space inside the cone formed by the extraocular muscles is designated the intraconal space, while that outside the muscles is extraconal.

The orbit can be the location for a diverse array of tumours, similar to those in many other parts of the body (see #36423Table 9.01). Thus, the possibility of a primary tumour, metastatic tumour, or direct extension from adjacent structures must be considered. The latter includes tumours of the eye, eyelids, conjunctiva, nasolacrimal drainage system, brain, and paranasal sinuses.

As the orbit is a confined space, larger tumours produce effects on the eye, such as proptosis, displacement, and compression, as well as restriction of extraocular motility. Rapid enlargement of a tumour within the orbit, such as owing to haemorrhage into a lymphangioma, may produce a visual emergency, similar to those in the intracranial cavity, because of lack of space for expansion. Malignant orbital tumours may invade the surrounding bony walls of the orbit or extend through the cavernous sinus into the brain.

After clinical examination, patients presenting with a presumed orbital tumour are usually assessed by CT, MRI, or both. Most patients will require a core needle or open biopsy for definitive diagnosis before a decision on treatment, which may be complex and multidisciplinary. As the orbit is also host to a variety of inflammatory processes that can masquerade as neoplasms, it may be difficult to distinguish between these without an adequate tissue sample. The orbit is accessible for cytological examination which may be particularly helpful in assessing recurrent or metastatic tumours {31276737;35601859}.

This chapter, new in the fifth edition, covers tumours not seen elsewhere in the ophthalmic structures. Tumours of the optic nerve, lacrimal gland, and lacrimal drainage system are discussed separately in section Tumours of the optic disc and optic nerve, section Tumours of the lacrimal gland, and section Tumours of the lacrimal drainage system, respectively. Haematolymphoid and soft tissue and bone tumours of all of the ocular structures, including the orbit, are discussed in section Haematolymphoid tumours and section Soft tissue and bone tumours.

In this volume, vascular anomalies are divided into malformations and tumours, according to the recommendation of the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) [[International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies [Internet]. Milwaukee (WI): International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies; 2018 [Cited 2022 Apr 18]. ISSVA Classification of Vascular Anomalies. Available from: https://www.issva.org/classification.]] {29874693;31864528;35216474}, though this may not apply well to some orbital tumours. Vascular malformations are covered in section Tumours of the orbit and vascular tumours in section Soft tissue and bone tumours.

Finally, there are many tumours which have been described in the orbit in only a few instances and did not warrant a full section. These are briefly described in #36425Table 11.02 in section Soft tissue and bone tumours.
