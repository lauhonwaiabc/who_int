10.1.5.3 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a benign neuroectodermal tumour with Schwann cell differentiation composed of epithelioid cells with abundant lysosome-rich granular eosinophilic cytoplasm.

ICD-O coding

9580/0 Granular cell tumour

9580/3 Granular cell tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2E89.1 & XH09A9 Benign tumours of uncertain differentiation, soft tissue & Granular cell tumour, NOS

2B5F.2 & XH90D3 Sarcoma, not elsewhere classified of other specified sites & Granular cell tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Not recommended: Abrikossoff tumour; granular cell myoblastoma; granular cell schwannoma; granular cell nerve sheath tumour.

Subtype(s)

Granular cell tumour, malignant

Localization

Granular cell tumours typically affect the skin and mucosa {26637199;6246310}. They are often solitary lesions, but 7–29% of patients may display multiple lesions {24082205}. In the eye, these tumours are preferentially observed in the soft tissues of the orbit, particularly involving the extraocular muscles {28558180}.

Clinical features

Granular cell tumours generally present as slow-growing and painless solitary lesions resulting in worsening proptosis, diplopia, and restriction of movement, given the predilection for an extraocular muscle location {21464781;34406152}. Pain and signs of optic nerve involvement are rare {34406152}.

Epidemiology

Granular cell tumours of the orbit have a mode of incidence in the fifth and sixth decades of life, with an equal distribution between men and women {34406152}. The malignant form is exceedingly rare.

Etiology

Granular cell tumours are predominantly sporadic, but there are reports of the occurrence of these tumours in the context of Bannayan– Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome {12833416}, neurofibromatosis type 1 {26637199}, Watson syndrome, LEOPARD syndrome (multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, retardation of growth, and sensorineural deafness) {19054014}, and neurofibromatosis–Noonan syndrome {22915371;20537083}.

Pathogenesis

Whole-exome and targeted sequencing analyses of these cancers have revealed loss-of-function mutations affecting ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, which encode accessory components of the vacuolar H+ ATPase {30166553}, and less frequently loss-of-function mutations affecting other genes encoding other components of the vacuolar H+ ATPase {30597645;33289181}. Loss of function of ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2 has been shown to be oncogenic and causative of the intracytoplasmic accumulation of early and recycling endosomes, which confer the characteristic granular eosinophilic cytoplasm of granular cell tumours and are pathognomonic {30166553}.

Macroscopic appearance

Granular cell tumours are uninodular and firm homogeneous masses, tan to white in colour {24653826;34406152}.

Histopathology

Granular cell tumours display an infiltrating growth pattern with poorly defined borders. The tumours are composed of sheets, nests, and trabeculae of epithelioid to polygonal cells with abundant cytoplasm containing many eosinophilic PAS-positive/diastase-resistant cytoplasmic granules. Nuclei are usually centrally located, ranging from small and uniform to larger and vesicular with prominent nucleoli {10410172;26637199;9669341}. Mitotic figures are generally scant. The exceedingly rare malignant granular cell tumour may display increased cellularity, prominent spindling, a high N:C ratio, marked pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity, and necrosis {9669341;21282016;26637199}.

Immunohistochemistry

Granular cell tumours display strong and diffuse expression of S100, SOX10, inhibin, and calretinin {22495377;22804786;26637199}. CD68, CD63 (NKI/C3 clone), and NSE can also be positive, probably owing to nonspecific reactivity with the abundant lysosomal granules {26637199}. TFE3 and MITF expression, in the absence of rearrangements of these genes, is common {26009539;31788112;17284958}, whereas HMB45 is consistently negative {17284958;26637199}. CD163 is negative, a useful marker separating granular cell tumours from histiocytic disorders.

Cytology

Useful cytological features for a diagnosis of granular cell tumours include discohesive cells, abundant granular cytoplasm, a low N:C ratio, and bland nuclei {33493367;30589232;25379050}. Carcinomas (in particular apocrine carcinomas) and histiocytic processes should be excluded.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nests, sheets, and trabeculae of large epithelioid to polygonal cells with abundant granular cytoplasm.

Desirable: diffuse strong expression of S100 and SOX10.

Staging

Not relevant unless malignant

Prognosis and prediction

The treatment for most granular cell tumours of the orbit is wide surgical excision with complete removal, which is often curative {34406152}. Malignant granular cell tumours may require more aggressive approaches, including chemotherapy and radiation therapy, given the risk of distant metastatic dissemination in as many as 50% of cases {26637199}.
