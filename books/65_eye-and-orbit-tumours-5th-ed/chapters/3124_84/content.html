3.1.2.4 Iris melanoma

Definition

Iris melanoma is a malignant melanocytic neoplasm arising from iris melanocytes.

ICD-O coding

8720/3 Iris melanoma

8720/3 Ring melanoma

8720/3 Diffuse iris melanoma

ICD-11 coding

2D07.2 Melanoma of iris

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Ring melanoma; diffuse iris melanoma

Localization

Iris melanoma is most commonly located in the inferior quadrant {22370659}, especially in male patients. In female patients it is more often located in the temporal quadrant {34980044}.

Clinical features

Iris melanoma is often asymptomatic but the patient may notice a variably brown enlarging spot or nodule, or diffuse colour change, on the iris. The tumour can be pigmented (82%), non-pigmented (10%), or mixed (8%) in colour. It is associated with corectopia (45%), ectropion uveae (24%), hyphaema (3%), secondary glaucoma (35%), chamber angle seeding (28%), and extraocular extension (3%) {22370659}.

Clinical patterns include plateau, dome shaped, or the rare tapioca appearance {22370659}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of all uveal melanomas is on average 5.1 per million {21704381;17498805}, and iris melanomas account for 3–5% of all uveal melanomas {19667335}, suggesting an incidence of about 0.2 per million. No sex predilection is known. Most patients are White, and iris melanomas are rare in individuals of Asian or African ancestry.

Etiology

Iris melanoma arises de novo or from a prior iris naevus {32273508}. Risk factors for iris melanoma include light-coloured eyes, fair skin, congenital ocular melanocytosis, dysplastic naevus syndrome, and germline BAP1 pathogenic variants {28203054}. Ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure may play a role in tumorigenesis of iris melanomas given the direct exposure of the iris to sunlight and the more frequent location of iris melanoma on the inferior half of the iris, where sun exposure is most prominent {23290981;20881029}.

Pathogenesis

Iris melanomas arise from neural crest–derived melanocytes, which are normally scattered within the iris stroma. A tendency for cytological dedifferentiation from normal melanocytes to the spindle-cell morphology, and further to the epithelioid phenotype, is reported during the natural evolution of these lesions {1794785}. This evolution theory is supported by the observation that borderline malignant iris naevi may harbour driver mutations in BAP1 and EIF1AX that confirm their clinical and histopathological borderline malignant nature {29371009}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of an iris melanoma specimen is a brown variably pigmented tumour. In the case of a sectoral iridectomy, an effort should be made to document resection margins.

Histopathology

The Callender system can be applied to iris melanoma cell types. The cells can be spindle shaped and epithelioid. Spindle A cells are fusiform and cohesive, with poorly defined cell borders, eosinophilic tapering cytoplasm, and frequently a longitudinal nuclear groove; nucleoli are inconspicuous. Intranuclear pseudoinclusions are usually absent. Spindle B cells are fusiform and cohesive, and have poorly defined cell borders, eosinophilic tapering cytoplasm, spindle- or cigar-shaped nuclei with aberrant chromatin (clumped and pressed up against the nuclear membrane), and a prominent nucleolus. Epithelioid cells are non-cohesive and possess well-defined eosinophilic cytoplasm, ovoid to circular nuclei, aberrant chromatin, and a prominent nucleolus. Some iris melanomas may show benign naevus cells at the periphery of the tumour {14249186;14269906;13558786;7305709;4012597;7890505;1153157;2076286;21911649}. Any of these cell types can be amelanotic or pigmented to varying degrees. Mitotic figures tend to be seen in spindle B and epithelioid cell populations rather than in spindle A cells. Iris melanomas, like their ciliary body and choroidal counterparts as a whole can be of spindle, mixed, and epithelioid type. While most cases are of spindle type {4014387;21911649}, the diffuse architectural pattern is usually of mixed-cell type {12153810}.

At low magnification, iris melanoma can be nodular and well defined, multinodular, and diffuse, and a ring melanoma. A particular multinodular pattern is called the tapioca pattern. It is composed of amelanotic tumour cells arranged as multiple nodules over the iris surface. Iris melanoma cells invade the iris stroma as sheets or fascicles, and as individual cells they can efface the normal iris vessels, unlike naevi, and can form a plaque on the surface of the iris. The plaque is cohesive if formed from spindle cells but is rather non-cohesive if formed from epithelioid cells. The cells on the surface tend to be smaller in size compared with the melanoma cells in the deeper stroma {14249186;14269906;13558786;7305709;4012597}. This is thought to be secondary to aqueous humour modification of their cytology {19019926;31049323}. Melanoma cells can shed into the anterior chamber and lodge in the trabecular meshwork distant from the site of origin; invade the ipsilateral trabecular meshwork; invade the iris pigment epithelium, sclera, cornea, aqueous outflow channels, and ciliary body; and abut the lens inducing a sectoral cataract. Involvement of the circumferential trabecular meshwork with or without the anterior ciliary body produces a ring melanoma {14249186;14269906;13558786;7305709;4012597;1153157;2076286;21911649}.

Ring melanomas that involve circumferentially > 6 clock hours and invade the chamber angle and ciliary body {4016045} and the diffuse subtype of iris melanoma {12153810} are associated with raised intraocular pressure and worse prognosis for vision and life.

If a biopsy is rather superficial, it can sample mostly surface melanoma cells that may look bland or naevoid, making it difficult to secure a firm diagnosis of melanoma. Under such circumstances, it is preferable to sample the deeper iris stromal component, which is less modified and will retain a spindle B or epithelioid cytology, or both, facilitating a secure diagnosis {1637283;8594510;16024840;17446506;24964795;21856690}.

Immunohistochemistry

Iris melanomas are positive for S100, melan-A, HMB45, and SOX10. They may show loss of BAP1 nuclear immunoreactivity {10333110;10985669;29371009}.

Cytology

FNAC shows individually dispersed or small cohesive groups of atypical cells {7162114;1637283;16547317} with spindle-shaped or rounded pleomorphic nuclei, with nucleoli, with coarsely hyperchromatic chromatin, within eosinophilic cytoplasm that is spindle shaped in spindle melanoma cells and ovoid in epithelioid melanoma cells. Variable numbers of cytoplasmic finely granular melanosomes are present. Binucleation and mitotic figures can be seen {1637283;16547317}. About 5% of FNAC cases show insufficient cells for a reliable diagnosis {26065351}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Iris melanomas show chromosomal aberrations similar to those seen in choroidal and ciliary body melanomas. Cytogenetically, most have a low metastatic risk profile, reflecting their relatively indolent clinical course. However, monosomy 3 is well described in some iris melanomas, as well as loss of 9p and gain of 8q {20881029;27098748;21336070}. The prognostic significance of such findings in an iris melanoma is still unclear. Driver mutations in iris melanoma are similar to those in choroidal and ciliary body melanomas, and include mutations of GNAQ, GNA11, EIF1AX, and SF3B1 {28700778;29371009}, as well as BAP1 {25974357;29371009}. Additionally, in some studies, iris melanomas have occasionally shown mutations similar to those seen in cutaneous melanoma, namely in NRAS, BRAF, PTEN, KIT, and TP53 {29371009}. Whole-genome sequencing also suggests genomic features associated with UV radiation damage {32415113}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a melanocytic lesion clinically originating from, or histopathologically mainly located in, the iris; highly cellular; composed of cytologically atypical melanocytes.

Desirable: presence of spindle B or epithelioid melanoma cells, or both; loss of nuclear BAP1 protein expression, chromosomal aberrations, or driver mutations may be used in support of the diagnosis of a melanoma or borderline malignancy over a naevus.

Staging

Iris melanomas are staged according to the most recent Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) edition TNM classification for uveal melanomas that includes a separate system for iris melanomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis for a melanoma limited to the iris is favourable compared with that for melanomas that involve the choroid or ciliary body, presumably in part because of smaller tumour size at the time of treatment {34819035}. Metastases have been reported in 5% of patients by 5 years, 9% by 10 years, and 11% by 20 years {22370659}. Risk factors for metastatic disease and tumour-related mortality include initial diagnosis at older age, elevated intraocular pressure, increased tumour thickness, posterior tumour margin at the chamber angle or iris root (vs midzone), and extraocular extension {34819035;22370659;11150284}. Although gene expression profiling may be performed, its prognostic utility in iris melanoma is not well established because of the generally lower risk of metastatic disease compared with in more posterior uveal melanomas, even in the presence of prognostically poor driver mutations {23283188}. Treatment modality (i.e. resection, radiotherapy, or enucleation) does not affect metastatic risk {11150284}.
