4.2.1.5 Hamartoma of the retinal pigment epithelium

Definition

Simple and combined hamartomas of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) are hamartomas that are composed of normal retinal elements but lack the distribution and organization of the normal retina and RPE. Congenital simple hamartoma of the RPE involves only the RPE, whereas combined hamartoma of the retina and RPE (CHR-RPE) involve cellular elements of both the retina and the RPE.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa

Related terminology

Not recommended: retinal hamartoma; hyperplasia of the retinal pigment epithelium; juxtapapillary hamartoma of the retina.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Simple RPE hamartoma usually is located in the macula. Combined hamartoma occurs most commonly on, or adjacent to, the optic disc, but also may occur in extrapapillary areas of the fundus.

Clinical features

Combined hamartoma of the retina and RPE typically appears as a poorly defined grey retinal mass with blood vessels that are tortuous or straightened by secondary retinal traction caused by excessive glial tissue on the lesion’s surface {2697919;6521994;18995912}. Variable amounts of pigment, telangiectatic vessels, and white glial tissue occur. CHR-RPE can vary markedly in size, ranging from 1 mm to > 10 mm in diameter with a mean diameter of 7.6 mm {18995912}. The lesions may be slightly elevated, and their thickness can gradually increase over time. Visual acuity is variable and depends on the size and location of the lesion and the amount of retinal traction. Affected children often present with visual loss when they fail preschool screening. Macular tumours can cause profound visual loss {18995912}. Most combined hamartomas are stable lesions, but retinal traction secondary to glial proliferation can cause progressive visual loss. Vitreous haemorrhage, exudation, and choroidal neovascularization occur rarely.

Fluorescein angiography shows early hypofluorescence due to blockage in hyperpigmented areas. Abnormal dilated capillaries, tortuous vessels, and leakage may be observed in the later phases {6521994}.

Ocular coherence tomography of CHR-RPE discloses a thickened mass of focally disorganized retina with a sawtooth or folded pattern of retinal folds caused by vitreoretinal traction secondary to prominent epiretinal membranes {25102194}.

Simple hamartomas of the RPE are very rare small, dark, elevated lesions that usually are located within the macula {12750104}. They are asymptomatic and most are found incidentally in eyes with good vision. Optical coherence tomography discloses a full-thickness retinal lesion with abrupt margins, high-surface reflectivity, and deep optical shadowing.

Epidemiology

Combined hamartomas of the retina and RPE are rare lesions. There is a slight male predominance of affected patients {2697919;6521994;18995912}. The mean and median patient age at initial diagnosis in one large series was 11.9 months and 7.5 months, respectively, with a range of 0.4–60 months {18995912}. The lesions are almost always unilateral and sporadic. However, CHR-RPE is associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 {10743927;12201346} and type 2 {18766994;27958212;25922868;1757856}, and also has been reported in association with naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) {27595027;10900122}, branchio-oculo-facial syndrome {28399346;15734008}, branchio-otic syndrome {24698626}, benign branchial cleft cysts {22420620}, juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma {11438076}, and Poland anomaly {15131695}. Bilateral combined hamartomas have been reported as the presenting feature of a severe phenotype of neurofibromatosis type 2 {25922868}.

Etiology

Simple and combined hamartomas of the RPE are probably congenital lesions; the median age at diagnosis is quite young, and the lesions have been observed in infants. A number of systemic associations have been reported including neurofibromatosis type 1 and neurofibromatosis type 2.

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

CHR-RPEs are composed of disorganized glial tissue intermixed with numerous blood vessels and with cords and tubules of proliferated RPE. A glial epiretinal membrane is present on the surface of the retina. Pigmented cells may be present in the membrane {2697919}.

Histopathology of a single case of congenital simple hamartoma of the RPE revealed a nodular proliferation of hyperplastic RPE that had undergone fibrous metaplasia {24603418}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis may be influenced by the patient’s age at presentation. In younger patients presenting with leukocoria, retinoblastoma is an important differential diagnostic consideration. In older patients, the differential diagnosis of pigmented CHR-RPE includes uveal melanoma and melanocytoma. Most cases of CHR-RPE can be readily distinguished from retinoblastoma and uveal melanoma. Less-common conditions that should also be considered are adenoma of the RPE, persistent fetal vasculature, and morning glory disc malformations.

Cytology

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: well-circumscribed, mostly flat, pigmented lesion in the posterior pole.

Desirable: glial tissue intermixed with numerous blood vessels and with cords and tubules of proliferated RPE; linear dragging of vessels by vitreoretinal traction; normal retinal elements with abnormal distribution and organization.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

RPE hamartomas are benign. Patients with CHR-RPE can experience progressive visual loss due to tumour growth or complications associated with the tumour. In one series, 60% of patients with macular tumours lost three or more lines of Snellen visual acuity at 4 years, compared with 13% of those with extramacular tumours {18995912}. Some patients have visual loss due to amblyopia, which can be improved with treatment. Vitreoretinal surgery can improve vision in some patients’ progressive vitreoretinal traction {19276871}.
