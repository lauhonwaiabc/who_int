9.0.0.1 Haematolymphoid tumours: Introduction

This chapter on tumours of haematolymphoid origin of the ocular region describes reactive lesions of the conjunctiva and orbit, intraocular lymphomas (i.e. vitreoretinal lymphoma and primary choroidal lymphoma), ocular adnexal lymphoma of B-cell type and T/NK-cell type, myeloid tumours, and histiocytic and dendritic neoplasms.

All of these tumours are extranodal neoplasms, as there are no lymph nodes in the ocular region. Each of the haematolymphoid and histiocytic/dendritic neoplasms are described in more detail in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid Tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO Classification of Tumours Series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

The ocular adnexa are affected by many reactive conditions and are part of the eye-associated lymphoid tissue and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue system {20502024}. A table is provided in this chapter (see #38459Table 10.01 in section Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia) to help enable the differentiation between reactive lymphoid hyperplasia and some common B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) that affect the ocular adnexa. An entity that is commonly asked to be excluded is IgG4 disease, which is dealt with in section IgG4-related disease, clearly outlining the required features to make this diagnosis {25809420}, and with an emphasis on clinicopathological correlation.

The B-cell NHLs are the most common lymphoma type affecting this region. T-cell lymphomas are rare; however, they may occur in the eyelids as primary or secondary tumours. The chapter commences with the intraocular lymphomas – vitreoretinal lymphoma and primary choroidal lymphoma – each differing from the other significantly with respect to NHL subtype and clinical course.

Vitreoretinal lymphoma – a high-grade B-cell lymphoma, arising in an immune-privileged environment {18865105} – is notoriously difficult to diagnose and is a masquerader in chronic uveitis cases. It should always be considered in the differential diagnosis, in both immunocompetent and immunodeficient patients, to decrease diagnostic delays. Repeated vitrectomies and/or chorioretinal biopsies may be required before a definitive diagnosis is achieved. Molecular testing for either clonality or MYD88 and/or CD79B mutations is of value {34448823}. In contrast, primary choroidal lymphoma is a low-grade B-cell lymphoma, mimicking uveal effusion syndrome and typically diagnosed on FNAB. It responds well to low-dose radiotherapy {18954321}.

A major advantage when diagnosing ocular adnexal lymphomas is that they are usually visible, and more accessible for biopsy. This provides the unique opportunity to correlate clinical appearance and clinical behaviour with the histological, immunophenotypic, and genetic features of these conditions. The most common orbital tumours are NHLs, with the extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma being the most frequent type {31177189}.

In recent years, genome-wide studies have contributed to a better understanding of molecular pathways involved in the pathogenesis of the various types of NHL. This has resulted in the identification of additional diagnostic and prognostic criteria, and of new potential therapeutic targets. Thus, the integration of histological, immunophenotypic, and genetic markers, together with clinicopathological correlation, is essential for an accurate diagnosis of ocular lymphoma.

Finally, this chapter briefly addresses the histiocytic and dendritic cell disorders seen in the ocular region, predominantly in the ocular adnexa. They are supposed to originate from and/or show differentiation towards cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system, which includes monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The term “histiocyte” has been variably defined and used, equating with macrophages or also including dendritic cells. In contrast, the adjective “histiocytic” has been used for a broad spectrum of disorders, which morphologically show large “epithelioid” cells with poorly defined, mostly moderately stained cytoplasm (e.g. histiocytic haemangioma, histiocytic breast carcinoma metastases). In general, clinicopathological correlation is the gold standard of diagnosis for histiocytic and dendritic cell disorders. Amendments since the last edition relate to genetic alterations which are shown to be linked to biological behaviour such as in Langerhans cell histiocytosis, in which BRAF p.V600E is highly associated with increased risk of relapse {27382093}. In those cases, BRAF p.V600E can serve as a therapeutic target {30157397}. In addition, other genetic alterations such as rearrangements in RET and ALK that may be targeted by selective inhibition have recently been identified in patients with histiocytosis {31768065}.
