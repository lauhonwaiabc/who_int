4.1.2.1 Retinocytoma

Definition

Retinocytoma is a benign primary tumour of the neurosensory retina with biallelic RB1 sequence alterations in photoreceptor precursor cells showing differentiation and a lack of proliferation. Dedifferentiation and acquisition of additional mutation may lead to malignant transformation into retinoblastoma.

ICD-O coding

9510/0 Retinocytoma

9514/1 Retinoblastoma, spontaneously regressed

ICD-11 coding

2F36.Y & XH5AV1 Other specified benign neoplasm of eye or ocular adnexa & Retinocytoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: retinoma; spontaneously regressed retinoblastoma; spontaneously arrested retinoblastoma; retinoblastoma group 0; benign variant of retinoblastoma; spontaneous cure of retinoblastoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Retinocytomas are primary tumours of the neurosensory retina with no preferential localization {10676785}.

Clinical features

While retinocytomas are often asymptomatic {10676785}, blurred vision and strabismus occasionally develop {10676785}. These tumours can occur in the same or contralateral eye with retinoblastoma, and have been found in phthisical eyes {1754160}. Leukocoria, a frequent presentation in retinoblastoma, is rarely seen in retinocytoma and usually occurs in younger patients {34051357}. While 13% of cases can be bilateral {1754160;10676785;25378879}, the tumour can be unifocal or multifocal. Unifocal tumours prevail in asymptomatic patients with no family history and tend to be larger with more calcification, alteration in the retinal pigment epithelium, and chorioretinal atrophy {34051357}. Second malignancies have developed in some patients with germline RB1 sequence alterations {1754160;15255114;22328814}. Retinocytomas can also be revealed after overlying retinoblastoma treatment {19667353;18211953}. Enhanced depth optical coherence tomography and high-resolution (20 MHz) ultrasonography can help to narrow the differential diagnosis {30395669;29470300}, but optical coherence tomography alone cannot differentiate between retinoblastoma and retinocytoma {31448879}. Clinically, the differential diagnosis includes mainly retinoblastoma, and astrocytic hamartoma.

Epidemiology

Retinocytoma is usually diagnosed at an older age than retinoblastoma, affecting only 14% of patients under 5 years of age. In two large series, the mean age at diagnosis of retinocytoma was 9 years and 28.7 years {34051357;22328814}. The reported incidence of retinocytomas is variable and they can be found incidentally or through familial testing {23584660}. It has been estimated that 2% of patients with mutated RB1 have retinocytoma. In a large series of patients with retinoblastoma, 3% had retinocytomas {34051357}. Based on histopathological examination, a 15.6–20.4% incidence was found of well-differentiated tumour areas consistent with retinocytoma within eyes enucleated for retinoblastoma {18211953;19653710}. One fourth of retinocytomatous foci are located in the base of endophytic retinoblastomas consistent with precursor lesions {19653710}. There appears to be no race or sex predilection, but the total number of reported cases is low.

Etiology

Like retinoblastoma, retinocytoma results from biallelic mutation of the RB1 tumour suppressor gene located at chromosome 13q14.2. However, compared with retinoblastoma, it has low levels of genomic instability and high expression of senescence-associated proteins {18211953}.

Pathogenesis

Retinoblastoma and retinocytoma share the same genetic changes {19390654}. However, retinocytoma may develop if the second mutant allele occurs at a later stage of cell maturation, when the precursor cell has limited mitotic capability {17657745}. Other possible explanations include low-penetrance RB1 variants {21615945} or epigenetic modification through RB1 promoter hypermethylation {24433356}. Upregulation of senescence-associated proteins p16 (p16INK4A) and p130 may account for the non-proliferative nature of retinocytoma {18211953}. Retinocytoma displays greater genomic stability than retinoblastoma {18211953}, although both entities have copy-number changes in MDM4, MYCN, and E2F3. MDM4 gain may affect the early transition from normally developing retina to retinocytoma, while MYCN and E2F3 progressive gain may help drive tumour progression {18785023}.

Macroscopic appearance

Retinocytomas appear as small semitranslucent tumours at the level of the retinal pigment epithelium that contain foci of calcification and are surrounded by mild to moderate pigmentary changes.

Histopathology

Retinocytoma replaces the neurosensory retina and expands into the vitreous, but does not invade the choroid. Within eyes enucleated for retinoblastoma, foci of viable retinocytoma appear relatively eosinophilic and paucicellular compared with adjacent undifferentiated tumour on low-magnification microscopy of H&E-stained sections. Retinocytoma is composed of a uniform population of bland cells that have round or oval nuclei that are relatively uniform in size and have evenly dispersed chromatin. Necrosis, apoptotic cells, and mitotic activity are absent {6626001}. In some cases, the cytoplasm appears clear rather than eosinophilic. Photoreceptor differentiation is a characteristic feature, but it is not present in all cases. Advanced photoreceptor differentiation is characterized by linear arrays and bouquet-like aggregates of bulbous eosinophilic neoplastic photoreceptor inner segments called fleurettes that are joined by zonulae adherentes forming short segments of neoplastic external limiting membrane {4190798}. The cellular processes of the neoplastic photoreceptors are often surrounded by Alcian blue–positive, hyaluronidase-resistant acid mucopolysaccharide. Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes generally are not present in retinocytoma. Foci of calcification are common, tend to be denser, lack cellular profiles, appear fractured, and occur in viable parts of the tumour, unlike the dystrophic calcification in retinoblastoma that occurs within necrosis.

Retinocytomas do not express Ki-67, consistent with a lack of proliferation, or p53 {19667353} and lack nuclear staining for RB1 protein. As noted above, about one fifth of retinoblastomas that are enucleated contain foci of retinocytoma thought to represent tumour precursors. Foci of well-differentiated tumour consistent with retinocytoma also can be encountered as a residual mass after eye-conserving therapy for retinoblastoma. The benign tumour probably persists because it is relatively unresponsive to treatment that destroys the contiguous retinoblastoma. It is believed that retinocytoma is relatively rare clinically because the vast majority of cases progress to retinoblastoma after they acquire additional mutations in critical cancer pathways {18211953;26310174}.

Cytology

FNAB of eyes containing retinocytoma generally is not performed, owing to concerns about extraocular extension.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Retinocytoma is initiated by germline and/or somatic loss-of-function mutation in both alleles of RB1 {17437278;18211953}. Sequencing to detect RB1 germline mutation in blood or buccal swabs {31997559} and somatic mutation in tumour tissue {31997559} can help establish the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: retinal tumour composed of small bland cells with round or oval nuclei; no evidence of pleomorphism, mitosis, or necrosis.

Desirable: photoreceptor differentiation (fleurettes); low Ki-67 proliferative index.

Staging

The same staging system is used as for retinoblastoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Retinocytoma typically remains stable with no evidence of progression over an extended period of time {15792319}; malignant transformation to retinoblastoma occurs in 4–12% {10676785;22328814} of cases, reaching 15.3% by 10–20 years {34051357}. Histopathologically confirmed cases of malignant transformation of retinocytoma have been reported {2780006}. Late malignant change of retinocytomas is a plausible explanation for the development of retinoblastoma in adults {21526971}. Increasing thickness is the only factor predictive of malignant transformation {34051357}.

Patients diagnosed with retinocytoma should receive close lifelong observation and they and their offspring should be offered genetic counselling {34051357}. Evaluation of first-degree relatives of patients with retinoblastoma is recommended to exclude retinocytoma {23584660}.

Retinocytoma has been associated with two patterns of reactivation: benign cystic growth or malignant transformation {22328814}, which can be rapid with vitreous seeding {2780006;10676785}. Occasional cases of retinocytoma may regress spontaneously {15689823}. However, aggressive endophytic and exophytic growth before spontaneous regression can occur {22328814}. Occasional cases may result in vitreous haemorrhage necessitating vitrectomy {31810573}. Cases progressing to retinoblastoma have been treated successfully with intra-arterial chemotherapy {27316281}. Malignant transformation of retinocytoma in an adult with a fatal outcome has been reported {21526971}.
