0.1.1.1 Introduction to eye and orbit tumours

Ophthalmic pathology has its roots in gross inspection of diseased ocular tissues, including when Morgagni described the gross appearance of cataracts in 1761 {26210596}. Individuals have been important in advancing the field, including James Wardrop, a Scottish surgeon who described in his Morbid anatomy of the human eye the clinical and gross appearance of retinoblastoma, which he called “fungus haematodes”. Wardrop also described the first well-documented case of metastatic uveal melanoma {28834323}. A confluence of technologies, including the invention of the microscope and ophthalmoscope, and Virchow’s concept of cellular pathology, enabled clinicopathological correlation of ophthalmic disease, most notably, ocular tumours. An example of this was Flexner and Wintersteiner’s observation of the presence of cellular rosettes in retinoblastoma. There were many individuals who advanced the field in the 1900s, most of whom were ophthalmologists. In the mid-century, several pathologists focused their practices on ophthalmic pathology. There continued to be significant input from ophthalmology-trained ophthalmic pathologists into the interpretation of ocular and ocular adnexal tumours.

The original WHO Histological typing of tumours of the eye and its adnexa was published in 1980. There was a set of Kodachromes that accompanied this book, which were widely used as teaching materials. Eighteen years later, in 1998, the second edition was published. There was no third edition, and it was not until 2018 that the fourth edition was published, some 20 years after the second edition. That volume, the last in the fourth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours series (the WHO Blue Books), brought the eye tumours volume into alignment with other volumes in the series. As in the past, clinicopathological correlation between ophthalmologists and pathologists enhanced interpretation of ocular tumour specimens.

In this, the fifth edition, we have added sections on adnexal tumours and a chapter on orbital tumours, which were not present in the fourth edition, and we have advanced the description, definition, and understanding of tumours of the eye and orbit even further. We have amalgamated sections on haematolymphoid tumours, soft tissue and bone tumours, metastases, and genetic tumour syndromes, following the format of other volumes of the fifth edition. This volume is comprehensive, accounting for almost all the tumours of the eye and ocular adnexa that an ocular oncologist and/or ophthalmic pathologist might encounter.
