3.2.1.3 Bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation

Definition

Bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation is a paraneoplastic syndrome characterized by diffuse proliferation of predominantly benign-appearing melanocytes in the uveal tract, usually in both eyes, in patients with an underlying extraocular malignancy, often leading to an exudative retinal detachment and frequently associated with progressive cataracts.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Acceptable: paraneoplastic uveal melanocytic hyperplasia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Paraneoplastic bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation may be panuveal (iris, ciliary body, and choroid) or it can affect only a part of the uveal tract, although it usually involves the choroid in all patients.

Clinical features

Patients experience an otherwise inexplicable rapidly progressive bilateral vision loss, which is essentially refractory to treatment. Unilateral ocular involvement is exceptional {28005580}. The symptoms can precede the detection of the underlying systemic malignancy. Bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation leads to damage to the retinal pigment epithelium, and that destruction is an important part of its fundus (and ancillary histopathological) findings. Important signs include multiple subtle reddish patches at the level of the retinal pigment epithelium corresponding to areas of hypoautofluorescence that are hyperfluorescent upon fluorescein angiography {27861254}, multiple pigmented and non-pigmented uveal melanocytic lesions that are hypofluorescent upon indocyanine green angiography, exudative retinal detachment, and rapidly progressive cataracts {2322154;14609707;27861254}. The fundus autofluorescence image with a reticular, often called leopard spot / giraffe, pattern of hyperautofluorescence {29948498;33492075;35724372} is a key diagnostic finding. It corresponds to destruction of the retinal pigment epithelium seen in optical coherence tomography. The ocular findings are associated with an underlying malignancy, most commonly a carcinoma of the ovary or endometrium in women and a carcinoma of the lung in men {11176070}. Less-common primary sites of the underlying primary neoplasm are the pancreas, lung, colon, and kidney {27861254;22803781;14609707}. Non-ocular melanocytic proliferation associated with bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation affects as many as 25% of patients in the oral and genital mucosa, skin, and conjunctiva {1751469;14609707;23788206;33974277;35087819}.

Epidemiology

The exact incidence is unknown. Median age, depending on the study, ranges from 58 to 65 years, with an average of 64 years; the sex distribution is equal {11176070;14609707;28636126}.

Etiology

The precise mechanism has yet to be fully elucidated, but in the IgG fraction of the serum of patients with bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation, a cultured melanocyte elongation and proliferation factor (CMEP factor) that stimulates melanocyte proliferation has been detected, which is thought to be released from the underlying extraocular primary malignancy {22791177;25908835}.

Pathogenesis

Circulating CMEP factor, released from the extraocular primary malignancy, induces proliferation and elongation of melanocytes in vitro {22791177;25908835}. Recently, elevated HGF levels, produced by the extraocular malignancy, and autoantibodies to HGF have been proposed as a novel pathogenesis for bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation {30370177}. This is supported by the detection by immunohistochemistry of the HGF receptor, c-Met, on melanocytic cells of patients {35087819}. Molecular genetics has shown gain of chromosomes 5, 6, and X {22803781;24124896}, loss of chromosome 19 {22803781}, and polysomy 8q {27171825}. The lack of consistent chromosomal findings supports a hyperplastic rather than a primary neoplastic process.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearance shows diffuse thickening of the uveal tract, usually by a mostly amelanotic lesion coloured cream to tan that may be punctuated with pigmented islands. The thickening, which can be detected by B-scan ultrasonography and ultrasound biomicroscopy, can be panuveal or involve only parts of the uveal tract, but it almost always involves the choroid. An overlying retinal detachment and associated cataract (if the ciliary body is involved) are common, and pigment epithelial cysts of the ciliary body and iris may arise.

Histopathology

The uvea is thickened by a proliferation of melanocytic cells that spare the choriocapillaris. The cells are spindle shaped with elongated oval nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant cytoplasmic melanin {7065942}. In some cases, discrete islands of atypical, pigmented, epithelioid melanocytic cells are observed that can resemble uveal melanoma and can exhibit necrosis {7065942;6704361;3423428;35087819}. Mitotic activity is rare. In some patients, melanocytic cells can be seen within the scleral emissary channels.

The proliferating melanocytes stain positively for melan-A, HMB45, and SOX10 {9355192;3508781}, do not overexpress p53 {9355192}, and show a low proliferation rate with Ki-67 and cyclin D1 {27171825;14609707;3508781}. They are PRAME-negative and retain nuclear BAP1 expression {35087819}.

Differential diagnosis

This includes diffuse uveal melanoma that will demonstrate widespread cytologically atypical melanocytic cells with spindle, epithelioid, or mixed morphology, demonstrable nucleoli, and choriocapillaris effacement. Islands of tumour divided by extracellular matrix networks and loops, mitotic activity, and necrosis may be associated with the typical genetic findings in uveal melanoma.

Rare cases of histologically confirmed uveal melanoma arising in the setting of bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation are reported {28636126;35087819}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis can be helpful: GNAQ, GNA11, and BRAF p.V600E mutations and 3p loss have not been reported {27171825;22803781}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bland, mostly spindle-shaped melanocytic cells diffusely in the uveal tract with sparing of the choriocapillaris with elongated oval nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, scant cytoplasmic melanin, and no mitotic activity, in the context of clinical evidence of bilateral uveal involvement and an underlying extraocular primary malignancy.

Desirable: positive immunohistochemistry with one melanocytic marker (melan-A, HMB45, SOX10).

Staging

Staging is not relevant (the initiating tumour should be staged).

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for life depends on the response of the extraocular primary tumour to therapy. The mean survival is 16 months, and nearly all patients die from dissemination of the primary malignancy {28636126}. Until recently, the loss of vision from bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation was considered to be progressive and irreversible, but stabilization of ocular symptoms with plasmapheresis was recently reported {25908835}. Some later publications have shown (at least short-term) definite benefit from intravitreal steroids and nonspecific treatment {32649362;29948498}.
