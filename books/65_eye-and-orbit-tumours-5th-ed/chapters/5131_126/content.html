5.1.3.1 Meningioma of the optic nerve

Definition

Meningioma of the optic nerve is a neoplasm arising from precursors in the optic nerve sheath.

ICD-O coding

9530/0 Meningioma of the optic nerve

ICD-11 coding

2A01.0Z Meningiomas, unspecified

Related terminology

Acceptable: optic nerve sheath meningioma.

Subtype(s)

Morphological subtypes are similar to intracranial meningiomas.

Localization

Primary optic nerve sheath meningioma (ONSM) arises from the intraorbital (92%) or intracanalicular (8%) portion of the optic nerve sheath. Secondary involvement of the optic nerve and orbit by intraorbital extension of intracranial sphenoid wing tumours is more common {15534587}. Rarely, meningiomas develop ectopically in the orbit, unassociated with the optic nerve sheath {10588262}.

Clinical features

The classic triad at presentation consists of visual loss, optic atrophy, and optociliary shunts, with visual loss being the most common {1475751}. Although considered infrequent initial symptoms, proptosis and strabismus affect approximately half of patients. Orbital compartment syndrome is only rarely encountered {17534836;30498385}. Tumour growth may accelerate during pregnancy {24911098;23809100}. Nearly all optic nerve meningiomas are unilateral. Bilateral cases are more likely to be secondary tumours extending from the skull base and/or tuberculum sella. Bilateral primary ONSMs typically affect children and may be associated with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) {19026318;23809100;30498385}. Diagnosis often is made based on clinical presentation and imaging findings, and a tissue diagnosis is not always pursued owing to the surgical risk to sight. ONSMs are often hypointense on T1- and hyperintense on T2-weighted MRI and show contrast enhancement in a tubular pattern, which looks like rings on coronal and tram tracks on axial sections {34229893}. Given the elevated SSTR2 density in meningiomas, 68Ga-DOTATOC PET-CT can be useful in challenging cases {34229893}.

Epidemiology

Primary optic nerve (sheath) meningiomas account for 2–10% of all orbital tumours and 1–2% of all meningiomas {34229893}. There is a female predilection with the majority presenting in the fourth decade of life (range: from childhood to the eighth decade of life). The age at presentation is earlier in men {20441509;30498385}. Paediatric cases are even less common, comprising < 5% of ONSMs {1537660}.

Etiology

Most ONSMs are sporadic and their etiology is unknown. A subset arises in the setting of NF2 syndrome or after irradiation. Incidence of NF2 in the setting of paediatric ONSM is higher than in the adult population and has been reported as 29% {29146762}. The incidence of ONSMs in the setting of NF2 is 2–8%, much higher than in the general population {16534058;26564072}.

Pathogenesis

Genetic alterations of ONSMs, especially sporadic cases, are not well studied given they are uncommon and only a small subset of cases undergoes surgical intervention. Based on a small series of mostly sporadic intraorbital tumours, primary ONSM and secondary meningioma extending from intracranial locations appear to be genetically distinct {24773246}.

Macroscopic appearance

Optic nerve meningiomas are well circumscribed and surround the nerve, imparting a tubular appearance. Orbital apex tumours tend to cause bulbous expansion of the optic nerve with distal tubular enlargement. Occasionally, tumours invade the nerve itself {15534587}.

Histopathology

Morphological classification and grading conform to the same criteria used for CNS meningiomas with most cases assigned to WHO grade 1. Meningothelial and transitional subtypes predominate at this location {19026318;20441509}. However, the true distribution of morphological subtypes is not clearly defined, since surgical intervention is less frequent. Meningothelial meningiomas are composed of lobules of epithelioid cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders separated by delicate collagenous septa. Nuclear holes and pseudoinclusions are common. Transitional meningiomas contain a mixture of meningothelial and fibrous features, often accompanied by psammoma bodies. Fibrous meningiomas are characterized by spindle cells arranged in fascicles, with variable amounts of intercellular collagen. In a series that included 21 ONSMs, only 1 case with angiomatous features (abundant, often hyalinized, small blood vessels) fulfilled WHO grade 2 criteria on the basis of increased mitotic activity {20441509}. Other grade 2 cases have been reported, including 2 cases in children with local recurrences {25585303;24705258}. However, studies critically evaluating the value of histological grading in ONSMs are lacking. CNS WHO criteria do not explicitly address whether invasion of the optic nerve proper is equivalent to brain invasion, owing to the rarity of such cases. Optic nerve invasion has been reported in 5 of 47 cases in two relatively large series, but follow-up was available for only 1 patient, who was alive and well after 28 years {14522782;20441509}.

Immunohistochemistry

Meningiomas stain positive for EMA and SSTR2A, with fibrous meningiomas exhibiting less extensive EMA staining. Fibrous meningiomas are also more likely to stain positive for S100.

Cytology

Intraoperative smear preparations reveal cohesive clusters of epithelioid cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and short, wispy cytoplasmic processes. Whorls, psammoma bodies, and intranuclear holes or pseudoinclusions may be present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not relevant in most cases. While most secondary meningiomas harbour monosomy 22 or 22q loss, along with deletions of chromosomes 1p, 6q, and 19p, primary ONSMs seem to harbour alterations in chromosomes 2, 10, 17, and 20 {24773246}. The presence of CDKN2A homozygous deletion or TERT promoter mutation would be sufficient for designation of CNS WHO grade 3 in intracranial tumours {11958372;26668184;28314689}. ONSM has not been studied regarding the presence or prognostic role of these alterations. But they are predicted to be associated with a worse prognosis, similar to in intracranial tumours.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: classic histopathological features matching with at least one of the meningioma subtypes, or suggestive histopathological features combined with molecular changes supportive of meningioma.

Desirable: EMA immunopositivity.

Staging

Staging might be relevant only for grade 3 meningiomas with the potential for metastasis.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with primary optic nerve meningioma have an excellent prognosis for survival, but the visual prognosis is poor, with most patients experiencing permanent vision loss in the affected eye {25133857;23809100}. Of note, patients with tumours that demonstrate radiographic evidence of stabilization in size may even show vision improvement {30498385}.
