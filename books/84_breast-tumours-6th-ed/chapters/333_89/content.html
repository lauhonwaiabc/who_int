3.3.3 Adenoid cystic carcinoma

Definition

Adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC) is a rare triple-negative breast cancer which has morphological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features similar to its salivary gland counterpart.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: carcinoma adenoides cysticum; adenocystic basal cell carcinoma; cylindromatous carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Classic adenoid cystic carcinoma; solid-basaloid adenoid cystic carcinoma (SB-AdCC); adenoid cystic carcinoma with high-grade transformation

Localization

AdCC can arise in any quadrant of the breast, with the retroareolar region being the most frequently affected site {22154460;28043647}.

Clinical features

AdCC usually affects elderly women. There are single case reports of AdCC in men {24403896} and adolescents {22691333}. Most of the reported cases presented as a palpable mass (mainly in elderly patients), but AdCC can present as a small nodule in younger patients and populations attending breast screening programmes {22154460}. AdCC is usually unifocal {22154460}, although a multifocal case is on record {20190690}.

Epidemiology

Breast AdCC is relatively rare, described in case series to account for 0.1–3.5% of all breast cancers {30189404;22154460}, but owing to this limited number of cases, its epidemiology is not well known.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most of the classic AdCCs investigated to date have harboured the MYB::NFIB fusion gene, demonstrated by FISH or next-generation sequencing {29149504}, similar to what is observed in the salivary gland counterpart. AdCCs lacking the MYB::NFIB fusion gene may show MYBL1 rearrangements or MYB amplification {29149504}. The most frequently mutated genes in the classic subtype include MYB, BRAF, FBXW7, SMARCA5, SF3B1, and FGFR2. On rare occasions, activating mutations in RAS pathway genes have been described. AdCCs with high-grade transformation present mutations in EP300, NOTCH1, ERBB2, and FGFR1 (in addition to the MYB::NFIB fusion gene), and they seem to lack mutations affecting common forms of triple-negative breast cancer (such as TP53 mutations) {27491809}. MYB and MYBL1 rearrangements have been found to be less frequent in the solid-basaloid subtype than in the classic subtype {25217885;34599282;37660928}. The most frequently mutated genes in SB-AdCC include chromatin modifier genes (CREBBP) and/or genes involved in the NOTCH pathway (NOTCH1, NOTCH2) {34599282;31857685;37660928;39389890}. However, SB-AdCC without the MYB::NFIB fusion often shows overexpression of MYB, suggesting alternative methods for MYB activation {31857685;37660928}. TP53 mutations are uncommon in SB-AdCC, which is in distinction to triple-negative invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST).

Recently, attention has been directed towards the microRNA expression profile in AdCC of the salivary, lacrimal, and mammary glands. In salivary and lacrimal glands, the microRNA expression profiles differ between normal tissue and AdCC neoplastic tissue. In contrast, normal breast tissue and AdCC neoplastic tissue share the same microRNA expression profile. In addition, breast AdCC shows differences from the salivary and lacrimal gland counterpart microRNA profiles {25240490;29467480}. These differences could help improve our understanding of the differing clinical behaviours according to the site of origin.

Macroscopic appearance

AdCC presents as a well-circumscribed nodule with pushing borders, ranging from a few millimetres to as large as 120 mm {28043647}.

Histopathology

Breast AdCC shows the same morphological spectrum observed in salivary gland AdCC. Three subtypes have been defined, on the basis of architectural and cytological features: classic AdCC, SB-AdCC, and AdCC with high-grade transformation {28043647}.

Classic adenoid cystic carcinoma

At low magnification, this subtype shows a central cribriform area surrounded by a peripheral area with predominant tubular architecture. Both areas show the same cellular composition, namely epithelial and myoepithelial cells. The glandular spaces in both areas are lined by luminal-type epithelial cells polarizing around true extracellular lumina and produce mucins, evidenced by Alcian blue (pH 2.5) staining. The surrounding stroma infiltrates into the neoplastic nests, among the myoepithelial cells, forming irregular spaces called pseudolumina. Pseudolumina are filled with stromal matrix, including stromal cells and basement membrane material, and they often contain small capillaries and fibroblasts. Rare cases show foci of sebaceous or squamous differentiation {3022669}. Classic AdCC lacks marked nuclear atypia and necrosis, and the number of mitoses is very low. Perineural invasion may occasionally be present. An in situ component is rarely seen. Although these tumours are typically circumscribed grossly, the periphery of the neoplasm may be infiltrative, making it difficult to grossly assess resection margins.

Immunohistochemistry is useful to confirm the different cell types. Epithelial cells are consistently positive for the low-molecular-weight cytokeratins CK7 and CK8, as well as for EMA. In some cases, the luminal cells are positive for CK5/6 {27240462}. Myoepithelial cells are demonstrated with CK14, CK5/6, and p63 {15846389}. The myoepithelial cell component can be additionally positive for other myoepithelial markers (myosin heavy chain, calponin, S100, and CD10). KIT (CD117) is usually strongly positive in the luminal component of AdCC, and it is sometimes used to differentiate AdCC from other breast tumours; however, it is important to remember that focal KIT positivity can be present in numerous other breast tumours, including adenomyoepithelioma {16050957}. Stains for the basement membrane antigens collagen IV and laminin decorate the pseudolumina. Classic AdCC usually displays strong and diffuse nuclear MYB staining which is most often due to MYB::NFIB fusion {28498281}.

Classic AdCC of the breast usually lacks ER and PR positivity and ERBB2 amplification; it is therefore included in the group of triple-negative tumours of favourable outcome {20306833;20653964}. Focal ER and PR positivity can be seen in a minority of classic AdCC cases {12001107;20653964}. Positivity is most probably localized in the epithelial cell component.

The tubular component of classic AdCC should be differentiated from microglandular adenosis and tubular carcinoma {29949742}. In contrast to microglandular adenosis and tubular carcinoma, AdCC is composed of two cell types (epithelial and myoepithelial). In addition, the tubules contain mucins and basement membrane material. Tubular carcinoma shows strong positivity for ER and PR. Similarly, the cribriform areas of AdCC can raise suspicion for an invasive cribriform carcinoma, but this diagnosis can be excluded on the basis of the dual cell population of AdCC and ER and PR immunostaining, which is strongly and diffusely positive in invasive cribriform carcinoma and negative in the vast majority of AdCCs. AdCC should also be differentiated from collagenous spherulosis, which rarely forms a mass lesion and is characterized by basement membrane material–filled spaces in benign intraductal proliferations, such as usual ductal hyperplasia. KIT and MYB immunostaining is typically negative or weak and focal in collagenous spherulosis {16810311;28498281}.

Solid-basaloid adenoid cystic carcinoma

This subtype was described in 2002 by Shin and Rosen {11914618}. In addition to the classic features of AdCC, SB-AdCC is characterized by solid nests composed of basaloid cells, with marked nuclear atypia, high mitotic count, and necrosis. Perineural invasion is a frequent finding in this subtype. The stroma is often hyalinized or myxoid. Dual cell populations are less conspicuous in SB-AdCC. Small eosinophilic duct-like structures among the basaloid cells may be highlighted by CK7 or CK5/6 immunohistochemical staining. MYB immunostaining is usually positive in SB-AdCC {38973399}. But the mechanisms underpinning MYB expression in SB-AdCC may differ from those driving MYB expression in classic AdCCs. p63 is usually negative or focally positive {11914618}. SB-AdCC should be differentiated from carcinomas with basaloid morphology and small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC). Differential diagnosis, which can be difficult because these lesions share similar morphological features, is based on the finding of typical AdCC areas. Negativity for neuroendocrine markers can help to exclude neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC).

Adenoid cystic carcinoma with high-grade transformation

This subtype has been well delineated in the salivary glands {18059225}. In the breast, rare cases of AdCC associated with high-grade carcinomas have been described. Specifically, cases of AdCC showing multiple areas of differentiation {18283489}, small cell carcinoma {16325513}, invasive ductal carcinoma {19233861}, and malignant adenomyoepithelioma {25056281} are on record. In a case of AdCC described in association with an invasive ductal carcinoma (i.e. invasive breast carcinoma NST), similar molecular alterations were shared by the two components; in addition, mitochondrial DNA analysis demonstrated a clonal relationship between the two components, suggesting that AdCC neoplastic cells can acquire aggressive potential {19233861}.

Cytology

FNAB smears are moderately to highly cellular with basal cells in tissue fragments and in fragments where hyaline balls are surrounded by the basal epithelium, with stripped oval myoepithelial nuclei in the clean background. The stromal balls are larger, rounder, and more numerous than those seen in collagenous spherulosis, and tissue fragments of hyperplastic ductal cells with myoepithelial cells, apocrine sheets, and a proteinaceous background are not seen. Immunohistochemistry for KIT on cell blocks is positive in adenoid cystic carcinoma {26681973;26183224}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Most classic AdCCs harbour a t(6;9)(q22-23;p24-23) translocation that creates a MYB::NFIB fusion gene, which can be demonstrated by FISH or next-generation sequencing. MYBL1 rearrangements or MYB amplification have been demonstrated in cases lacking the MYB::NFIB fusion gene {29149504}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For classic AdCC: an invasive carcinoma with neoplastic cells arranged in tubular, cribriform, and solid patterns, composed of epithelial and myoepithelial cells; glandular lumina filled with epithelial mucins and pseudolumina filled with stromal matrix, basal lamina, and fibroblasts For SB-AdCC: solid nests and/or trabeculae of basaloid tumour cells in myxoid or hyalinized stroma. The tumour cells are usually larger and more pleomorphic than those of classic AdCC, with increased mitotic figures.

Desirable:

Immunohistochemistry to confirm the presence of different cell types and/or molecular tests to confirm the genetic aberrations in AdCC.

Staging

AdCC is staged according to the criteria adopted for other types of invasive breast carcinomas.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognostic impact of histological grade in breast AdCC has been debated, with conflicting results in the literature {2474845;2824330}. A new grading system, incorporating growth pattern, percentage of basaloid morphology, and mitotic count, was proposed and found to correlate with recurrence-free survival. Basaloid morphology and solid growth pattern were both strongly associated with poorer outcomes {36853781}.

Classic adenoid cystic carcinoma

Despite the triple-negative phenotype, the classic subtype usually shows favourable behaviour. Regional and distant metastases are rare, and complete surgical excision is usually curative. In rare cases, focal positivity for ER and PR has been described {12001107;20653964}, but the therapeutic significance of this positivity is unknown. Classic AdCC constitutes the vast majority of breast AdCC and is included in large patient cohorts. It usually presents at an earlier stage, with a low rate of axillary involvement {28008158;20653964;23456318}. Long-term follow-up has revealed higher survival rates than with invasive breast carcinoma NST {20653964;23456318} in all but one study {28008158}.

Solid-basaloid adenoid cystic carcinoma

Axillary node metastases and perineural invasion can frequently be observed {25217885;26378056;10515126;11914618}. In cases with long-term follow-up information, patients have developed local recurrences and distant metastases to lung, bone, and skin {25217885;26378056}. Patients with the basaloid subtype may survive for an extended period despite the presence of lung metastases. Limited data are available on SB-AdCC treatment. Responses were poor in neoadjuvant-treated patients, without any achieving pathological complete response {37660928}. NOTCH1 inhibitors may have therapeutic effect in patients with SB-AdCC harbouring NOTCH1 activating mutations {27870570}.

Adenoid cystic carcinoma with high-grade transformation

Because of the small number of reported cases and the wide variety of features observed in the high-grade component, conclusions cannot be drawn about the optimal treatment strategy.
