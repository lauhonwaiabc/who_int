3.2.7 Mucinous carcinoma

Definition

Mucinous carcinoma (MC) of the breast is a special type of invasive breast carcinoma (IBC) characterized by epithelial tumour cells, with low- to intermediate-grade nuclei, suspended in extracellular mucin and with no features of other IBC types.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: colloid carcinoma; mucoid carcinoma; mucinous adenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The localization of MC is similar to that of other types of breast carcinoma.

Clinical features

MC appears mammographically as a well-circumscribed or lobulated mass, and it may mimic a benign process. Sonographically, most tumours are hypoechoic {20615642}. MRI reveals a persistent enhancement pattern and hyperintensity on MRI T2-weighted images {21071383}.

Epidemiology

MC accounts for approximately 2% of all breast carcinomas {18026874;2416216;8017565;9120527}. It often occurs in older women {18026874;18809061}, with a median patient age of 71 years, although it can occur in young patients {18026874;10334529}.

Etiology

See section Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction.

Pathogenesis

MCs are of the luminal A molecular subtype {18720457}, and they are transcriptionally distinct from grade- and molecular-matched IBCs of no special type (NST) {19633645}. The transcriptomic features of type A MC differ from those of type B MC, with the latter showing a pattern of gene expression that is similar to that of neuroendocrine breast tumours {19633645}. Pure MCs show a low level of genetic instability, less frequently harbour concurrent 1p gains and 16q losses and somatic mutations of PIK3CA and AKT1 (hallmark genomic alterations of low-grade ER-positive IBC-NSTs) {20815046;22705004;16353168;30649385;30789657}, and exhibit aberrant DNA methylation of MUC2 {30789657}. The most frequently mutated genes in MC are GATA3, KMT2C, and MAP3K1 {30649385}. The different morphological components of mixed MCs are clonally related and display genomic profiles remarkably similar to those of pure MCs {20815046;30649385}. Microsatellite instability, a common feature of colorectal MC, is exceedingly uncommon in breast MC {21228925}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross examination shows a glistening and gelatinous nodule with pushing margins and a soft, viscous consistency. The tumour size ranges from < 10 mm to > 200 mm {18026874;18809061}.

Histopathology

MC is characterized by clusters or sheets of neoplastic cells suspended in extracellular mucin, partitioned by delicate fibrous septa containing capillary blood vessels. Because other types of breast cancers can have mucin production, the diagnosis of the pure form of this favourable histological type of breast cancer should be one of exclusion {17873114;29413660}. An MC diagnosis is reserved for cancers that are composed of clusters of epithelia with low to intermediate nuclear grade that are ER-positive and ERBB2 (HER2)-negative, floating in mucin. Cancers with high nuclear grade or HER2 positivity are best classified as IBC-NST with mucin production since they are often associated with more-aggressive biological behaviour. Mimics of mucin production, such as the chondromyxoid matrix of matrix-producing forms of metaplastic carcinoma (which would be ER-negative) should also be excluded.

MC clusters vary in size and shape and may show central lumen formation. As described by Capella et al. {6254868}, type A MCs are relatively hypocellular, with a large amount of extracellular mucin, whereas type B MCs tend to be hypercellular and consist of large epithelial clumps or sheets that often show neuroendocrine differentiation {6254868}. MC typically has a low mitotic count.

Pure MC can only be diagnosed on excision since it requires a mucinous component of > 90%; mixed MC has a mucinous component that accounts for between 10% and 90% of the tumour. A mucinous component of < 10% should be noted. The use of the term “mucinous features” on a core biopsy sample may clinically imply that the cancer is likely to be classified as a pure MC and therefore this terminology should ideally only be used if the carcinoma in the biopsy sample has features consistent with a pure MC diagnosis (i.e. it is not high-grade, not mixed morphologies). Tumours with mixed morphologies should be clarified as to the various components present.

MCs can display micropapillary-like morphology and have peripheral EMA (MUC1) staining since they have altered polarity such that the cells secrete mucin towards the stroma {12717243;24439232;24439234;23517923}. There have been studies examining a potentially distinct micropapillary pattern of MC where this morphology is particularly prominent, often featuring higher nuclear grade, HER2 positivity, and psammomatous calcifications {20962055;23517923;25953688;18387988;32358590;12378595}. Compared with conventional pure MC, the cancers in these series tend to occur at a younger patient age, display more frequent lymphovascular invasion and lymph node metastases {20962055;23517923;25953688;32358590}, and have a worse prognosis {25953688;32358590}. Genetic alterations that have been identified in these cancers are described as intermediate between those of MC and invasive micropapillary carcinoma {30843622}, with a higher frequency of TP53 mutations {32358590}. However, when high nuclear grade and HER2-positive forms are excluded, it is not clear that the micropapillary growth pattern alone is associated with a worse prognosis or a distinct biology {30066338;32358590}. In addition, there is not currently a clear definition of what threshold of micropapillary morphology might be clinically relevant. These findings underscore the need to exclude high-grade and HER2-positive cancers that are producing mucin from the diagnosis of an MC. When a micropapillary growth pattern is particularly prominent in a case that meets diagnostic criteria for a pure MC, it can be noted in the pathology report along with any other features, such as angiolymphatic (lymphovascular) spread, that may suggest less-favourable biological behaviour. However, it is not clear currently that there is a distinct micropapillary subtype of MC.

A conventional invasive micropapillary carcinoma (without mucin) may coexist with an MC. These tumours should be classified as invasive micropapillary carcinoma if the micropapillary component (without mucin) comprises > 90% of the tumour, with mention of the presence of the mucinous element. If the invasive micropapillary carcinoma constitutes between 10% and 90% of the tumour, with the mucinous component accounting for the remainder, classification as a mixed micropapillary and mucinous carcinoma is appropriate.

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) with abundant extracellular mucin has been described and is not classified as an MC {19490472;20550696;22867429;26023557;27315432;28528509;29850340;30032676;31467758;31695973;33281451;34266670;35477749}. A non-mucinous ILC component was present in all cases, often pleomorphic ILC {35477749}. Most cases are ER-positive and HER2-negative, with absent or reduced E-cadherin expression. ILCs with abundant extracellular mucin and HER2 overexpression or amplification have been reported {33281451;35477749}. Next-generation sequencing data support an overall lobular-type genomic profile, characterized by CDH1 alterations {35477749}.

Carcinoma with signet ring–cell differentiation consists of cells with abundant intracellular mucin that pushes the nucleus aside, creating the characteristic signet-ring cytomorphology. Carcinomas with signet-ring cells without extracellular mucin are not classified as MCs. Signet-ring cytomorphology is most common in ILC but may also be present in IBC-NST and rarely in other special histological subtypes. Therefore, carcinomas with signet ring–cell differentiation do not represent a distinct entity, and no specific clinical or molecular features have been described. Primary carcinomas of the breast with signet ring–cell differentiation must be distinguished from metastases to the breast of signet ring–cell carcinomas from other organs, in particular from the gastrointestinal tract.

The differential diagnosis of MC includes non-neoplastic mucocoele-like lesions (MLLs) with stromal mucin, which can pose a diagnostic challenge {17873114}, especially in core biopsy material {19912367;23534893;17873114}. The absence of cytological atypia in the epithelium lining the mucin-filled ducts and the presence of myoepithelial cells adherent to the detached epithelial strips present in the mucin pools favour (non-atypical) MLL over MC {17873114}. Neovascularization of mucin is more common in MC than in MLLs, which has been shown to be a useful diagnostic adjunct {34545857}. The upgrade rate at excision of radiology–pathology concordant (non-atypical) MLLs in a core biopsy targeting calcifications is < 3% {19912367;23534893;25539258;23402386;23161710}. MLLs without atypia do not significantly increase the risk of subsequent carcinoma {26826407;29024198}. Some MLLs are associated with atypical ductal hyperplasia, ductal carcinoma in situ, or MC {8764744;8302709;26826407;29024198}. Small mucinous pools containing epithelial cells in stroma adjacent to ductal carcinoma in situ, with intraluminal mucin, may represent extravasation of mucin from ruptured ducts rather than invasion, and this distinction can be problematic. It may be appropriate to express this uncertainly and acknowledge the possibility of microinvasion or a small focus of invasion.

MC is ER-positive, usually PR-positive {18026874;18809061;10334529}, and HER2-negative {23517923;25953688}. Tumours with ER negativity or HER2 positivity are more appropriately diagnosed as IBC-NST with mucin production. Peripheral staining for EMA may be seen in conventional MC and is not necessarily indicative of the micropapillary pattern of MC {24439232}.

Cytology

MC produces abundant fibrillary extracellular mucin within which a variable number of crowded 3D epithelial tissue fragments and single cells of small to medium size, with mild to moderate nuclear atypia and occasional intracytoplasmic vacuoles, are suspended with occasional dissected branching capillary vessels {12931549;16329537;22129179}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: epithelial tumour cells with low- to intermediate-grade nuclei suspended in pools of extracellular mucin and no features of other IBC types; > 90% mucinous component.

Desirable: ER-positive; HER2-negative.

Staging

MCs are staged similarly to other types of IBC (see section Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction).

Prognosis and prediction

Pure MC is generally associated with low rates of local and distant recurrence and excellent 5-year disease-free survival {18026874;18809061;10334529;17330844}. Late distant metastases may develop {2416216;2545592}. In a retrospective analysis of 11 422 pure MCs in the SEER Program database {18026874}, the breast cancer–specific survival rates at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years were 94%, 89%, 85%, and 81%, respectively. In a multicentre cohort study of 943 pure MCs, the 5-year recurrence-free survival and overall survival were 94.9% and 98.1%, respectively, superior to those of invasive ductal carcinoma (IBC-NST) and invasive lobular carcinoma {38744306}. There is no prognostic difference between type A and type B MCs {8017565;3028120}. Mixed MCs (MC 10–90%) do not have the same favourable prognosis as pure MCs {2827884;3028120;14308235}. Most pure MCs have a low or intermediate recurrence score (RS) by the 21-gene assay. In a series of 33 pure MCs from a single institution, the median RS was 13 (range: 3–28) {28577081}, and all but one tumour had an RS of < 25 {28577081}. In a retrospective analysis of the 21-gene assay central database, 90.6% of 16 116 carcinomas classified as mucinous had an RS of ≤ 25, with a mean score of 15 {30094484}.
