6.1 Mesenchymal tumours of the breast: Introduction

Incidence

The incidence of soft tissue sarcomas is fairly consistent, at 30–50 cases per 1 million person-years {22033323;8042499;22517534;35208522} [[Ferlay J, Colombet M, Bray F. Cancer Incidence in Five Continents, CI5plus: IARC CancerBase No. 9 Internet. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. Available from: https://ci5.iarc.who.int. ]]. Benign soft tissue neoplasms as a group show an incidence at least 100-fold higher. Individual benign mesenchymal entities range from frequent (e.g. lipoma) to rare (e.g. spindle cell haemangioma) {7282321}. Sarcomas are an extremely rare subset of primary breast tumours, far outnumbered by carcinomas, fibroepithelial neoplasms, and benign mesenchymal tumours.

Excluding haemangiomas, the most common benign, intermediate, and malignant primary mesenchymal neoplasms of the breast are myofibroblastoma, desmoid fibromatosis, and mammary angiosarcoma, respectively. Vascular lesions are relatively more common than other mesenchymal tumours in the breast; the WHO Classification of Tumours is working to harmonize the nomenclature of vascular lesions with the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification system. However, the sixth edition Breast tumours volume of this series has not fully aligned with ISSVA, with respect to those vascular lesions relevant to the breast.

Other mesenchymal tumour types arise only rarely within breast parenchyma, although a range of mesenchymal tumour types arise in the skin or subcutaneous tissue overlying the breast; selected neoplasms are also covered in this section. Some tumour types included in prior editions (e.g. nodular fasciitis, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, schwannoma, neurofibroma, smooth muscle tumours, and adipocytic tumours) have been removed and instead are fully covered in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588. ]].

More-numerous sarcomas and more sarcoma types are seen in adults than in children, but sarcomas constitute only 1% of malignancies in adults {28055103}. They arise mainly in the limbs (in particular the thighs), trunk, head and neck, and retroperitoneum. Sarcomas account for about 10–15% of paediatric malignancies, but their overall incidence rate is much lower in children than in adults, and fewer distinct entities are encountered {25435119;27542645}.

Etiology and pathogenesis

Most soft tissue neoplasms arise spontaneously, with an unknown pathogenesis, but for some cases a clear etiology can be discerned. Sarcomas can arise in fields of prior therapeutic irradiation {9279573}. The most frequent is postradiation angiosarcoma after treatment for breast carcinoma, which typically arises in the skin of the breast but may involve mammary parenchyma {30676608;30703514}. Atypical vascular lesions also can arise in breast skin after radiation therapy {16006792;24943869}. Angiosarcoma can complicate longstanding lymphoedema, in particular after radical mastectomy (as seen in Stewart–Treves syndrome). Viruses associated with soft tissue tumours include EBV (associated with some smooth muscle tumours in immunosuppressed patients) {16330945} and KSHV/HHV8 (associated with Kaposi sarcoma) {9044510}. Associations with chemical exposure, immunosuppression/transplant, and chronic tissue irritation have been described in a subset of sarcoma cases {18514694}. Soft tissue tumours (both benign and malignant) are also associated with several inherited syndromes, including Li–Fraumeni syndrome (associated with multiple sarcomas, as well as phyllodes tumours), neurofibromatosis (associated with nerve sheath tumours), Maffucci syndrome (associated with cartilaginous and vascular tumours), familial adenomatous polyposis (associated with desmoid fibromatosis), tuberous sclerosis (associated with angiomyolipomas of the kidney and liver), and Cowden syndrome (associated with lipomas and haemangiomas). These syndromes are discussed in more detail in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588. ]]. Somatic genetic factors, such as recurrent chromosomal translocations, drive the pathogenesis of a significant subset of soft tissue neoplasms. The cell of origin of most sarcomas is unknown, and precursor or in situ lesions are not recognized.

Clinical features

Benign and malignant soft tissue and neural tumours typically present as painless masses with varied growth rates. The vast majority of benign soft tissue tumours are < 50 mm {26839096}. Sarcomas can grow to be much larger. Because of their rarity, breast tumours thought to be mesenchymal should be referred to a specialist multidisciplinary centre before biopsy or surgery for optimal management {27891213}. The majority of suspected mesenchymal tumours of the breast in fact represent metaplastic carcinomas or malignant phyllodes tumours. Of note, alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma has a predilection for metastasizing to the breast {20730885}.

Histopathology

Malignant soft tissue neoplasms sometimes exhibit nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic activity, and necrosis, although translocation-associated sarcomas may be cytologically uniform. Some benign tumours can also show one or more of these features; for example, nuclear atypia can be seen in schwannoma with ancient change. Metaplastic breast carcinomas and malignant phyllodes tumours can mimic sarcomas. Both may demonstrate heterologous mesenchymal elements such as chondrosarcomatous and osteosarcomatous differentiation and thereby mimic chondrosarcoma and osteosarcoma. Primary liposarcomas of the breast are extremely rare {26542423}, but phyllodes tumours can show liposarcoma-like areas. Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma is a close histological mimic of desmoid fibromatosis. Immunohistochemistry is generally needed to confirm the tumour cell lineage and diagnosis {11474284;35838633}.

Genetics

Benign soft tissue tumours feature simple genetic properties, with diploid karyotypes perhaps adorned by a single characteristic chromosomal rearrangement. In malignancy, two genetic classes exist: simple-karyotype sarcomas associated with a recurrent mutation or translocation (e.g. synovial sarcoma) and complex-karyotype sarcomas with numerous chromosomal aberrations but generally lacking recurrent mutations (e.g. leiomyosarcoma) other than in TP53 {12796356}. As an intermediate tumour that recurs locally but does not metastasize, desmoid fibromatosis is generally associated with a single somatic mutation in CTNNB1 or much less commonly APC {28578783;28961825}. The identification of recurrent genetic alterations in numerous soft tissue neoplasms has been exploited to develop many new surrogate immunohistochemical and in situ hybridization markers for diagnostic applications {33921435;38135060}.

Diagnostic procedures

Investigation includes clinical assessment of the size and depth of the tumour, the use of imaging modalities (CT and MRI), and biopsy. Imaging can be used to assess the extent of a primary tumour, to determine its relationship to anatomical structures, and to identify metastases. Virtually all suspicious breast lesions necessitate diagnostic sampling. Core needle biopsy can provide diagnostic information on malignancy, subtype, and grade, with high sensitivity and specificity {9259971;25663366}; however, in cases with diagnostic uncertainty and/or limited sampling, a descriptive diagnosis with deferment to surgical excision is appropriate.

Tumour behaviour

The Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588. ]] recognizes four tumour behavioural categories: benign, intermediate (locally aggressive), intermediate (rarely metastasizing), and malignant. Benign tumours are usually cured by local excision and rarely recur locally (and any recurrences are non-destructive). Intermediate tumours can be either locally aggressive (e.g. desmoid fibromatosis, which locally infiltrates surrounding tissues) or rarely metastasizing (tumours with a very low [< 2%] but definite risk of metastasis, e.g. inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour). Malignant tumours such as angiosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma infiltrate, recur locally, and commonly metastasize (i.e. in > 20% of cases).

Grading

Grading is an attempt to predict clinical behaviour on the basis of histological variables, but it can only be performed using material from primary untreated neoplasms. It is not applicable to all sarcomas. The most widely used system for grading soft tissue sarcomas is the 3-tiered system developed by the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) {17090186;24378389}. It uses a combination of tumour differentiation, mitotic activity, and necrosis to categorize tumours as being of low-, intermediate-, or high-grade. The prognostic use of grading mammary angiosarcoma has been debated {3179927;18813119}, but a recently proposed 2-tiered system for primary angiosarcomas has shown promise for predicting disease-specific and overall survival {36376999}.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging systems are widely used [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. ]] [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017. ]]. Sarcoma staging incorporates histological grading and site of involvement along with histological type, tumour size, extent of lymph node involvement, and presence or absence of distant metastasis.

Prognosis and predictive factors

Complete excision of sarcomas is the most important factor in preventing local recurrence {10762742;12733153}. Some tumours are relentlessly recurrent, often with late metastases {21357725;10791853}. Factors generally associated with a greater risk of metastasis are larger tumour size and higher grade. For some benign and intermediate mesenchymal breast neoplasms (e.g. desmoid fibromatosis, nodular fasciitis, haemangiomas, and myofibroblastoma), there is a movement towards increased non-operative management in the setting of appropriate radiological–pathological correlation, or at least no additional surgeries after excision with positive margins.
