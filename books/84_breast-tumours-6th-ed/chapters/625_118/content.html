6.2.5 Primary angiosarcoma

Definition

Primary angiosarcoma of the breast is a malignant endothelial neoplasm of mammary parenchyma, not associated with radiation exposure.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: haemangiosarcoma; lymphangiosarcoma; malignant haemangioendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The localization is parenchymal rather than cutaneous.

Clinical features

Most patients present with a poorly defined, rapidly growing mass, swelling, or asymmetry of the breast {17325063}. Two thirds of the neoplasms measure > 50 mm {29205355}. Mammography most often shows a non-calcified mass or focal asymmetry. Ultrasonography reveals either a hyperechoic or mixed hyperechoic and hypoechoic mass. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI reveals typical malignant enhancement characteristics. Core needle biopsy is the preferred diagnostic technique, because FNA is associated with a high false negative rate (40%). Axillary lymph node involvement can rarely occur {29208319}. The most frequent sites of distant metastatic disease are the lungs, liver, and bones. Bilateral cases have been reported; such cases probably represent contralateral metastasis {32123305;34263505;36803941}.

Epidemiology

This rare neoplasm is estimated to account for about 0.05% of all primary breast cancers. Angiosarcomas are the second most common primary breast sarcoma, after malignant phyllodes tumours. Described cases have a median age of 40 years at diagnosis {18813119;16288486}, compared with the median age of 70 years for secondary angiosarcoma.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Recurrent alterations have been identified in KDR (VEGFR2) and PIK3CA {32042194;32123305;36376999}. Poorly differentiated cases may harbour TP53 mutations {36376999}. A small subset shows mutations in PLCG1, a member of the VEGFR2 signalling pathway {26735859}. Truncating mutations in PTPRB, a tyrosine phosphatase specific to vascular endothelium that inhibits angiogenesis, have also been reported {32123305}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours are haemorrhagic, diffuse, or multinodular masses that vary from 7 to 250 mm (mean: 67 mm, median: 50 mm) {18813119}. They can be either circumscribed or poorly defined. Better-differentiated tumours often have a haemorrhagic, spongy appearance. Poorly differentiated tumours are more solid and fleshy, consisting of firm grey-white tissue with areas of necrosis, and they may show areas of cystic, spongy, haemorrhagic vascular tissue peripherally. The extent of the tumours cannot be reliably determined on gross examination.

Histopathology

The tumours are typically located deep within breast parenchyma, with or without cutaneous involvement, showing an infiltrative or poorly defined border {18813119;24384851}. A wide morphological spectrum is encountered, generally with a predominant vasoformative or solid component identified. In contrast to secondary angiosarcomas, primary lesions more often show well-formed, small to medium-sized anastomosing vessels that dissect through fibroadipose tissue and disrupt the lobular unit {24384851}.

The neoplastic vascular channels in angiosarcoma have dilated, compressed, or angulated lumina. The lining endothelial cells range from bland to variably atypical, usually spindled, with hyperchromatic nuclei that may be plump or flattened. Well-differentiated angiosarcomas are composed of well-formed vessels lined by flattened endothelial cells with minimal atypia. Dissection of irregular, angulated vascular channels through adipose tissue and mammary lobules indicates malignancy. A lobular-type growth pattern, typically seen in haemangioma, is absent. Lesions with an intermediate appearance show endothelial multilayering, hobnailing, or papillary-like projections. Poorly differentiated angiosarcomas have solid, cellular foci comprising sheets of spindled to epithelioid cells intermingled with variably formed anastomosing vascular channels, associated with prominent blood lakes, mitotic figures, and areas of necrosis. The vascular channels may be poorly formed, with complex dissecting patterns, or they may be compressed with subtle cleft-like spaces suggesting vascular differentiation. Epithelioid angiosarcomas are uncommon in primary tumours; they have a solid appearance, with sheets of large atypical epithelioid to polygonal cells with ovoid vesicular nuclei, large nucleoli, and relatively abundant cytoplasm.

Angiosarcomas express endothelial markers, with strong, membranous CD31 staining and nuclear ERG immunoexpression noted. Variable CD34 and podoplanin (recognized by D2-40) positivity can be seen. Angiosarcomas, in particular of the epithelioid pattern, may also express keratins, leading to erroneous interpretation as carcinoma {17284115;1718176}. Aberrant expression of KIT, synaptophysin, chromogranin, and CD30 can be seen {24846674;11865845}.

Cytology

FNAB features reflect the range of angiosarcomas: cellularity is variable and tissue fragments of vasoformative spindle cells and single spindle cells in a blood background are usually found, although epithelioid and poorly differentiated angiosarcomas do occur.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant vascular proliferation; location within the breast parenchyma.

Desirable: diffuse infiltrative growth pattern, with dissection of adipose tissue and breast stroma.

Staging

Angiosarcomas of the breast are staged using the soft tissue sarcoma system, with pT stage dependent on tumour size.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour size has been shown to be a prognostic factor for angiosarcoma {30820717;38722225}. Total mastectomy had historically been the mainstay of therapy, but rates of breast-conserving surgery have been increasing. The type of local therapy is largely dependent on the tumour-to-breast size ratio. In the SEER Program data, patients treated with breast-conserving surgery had better overall survival than patients who underwent mastectomy {28449661;37158932}, but this is probably confounded by the prognostic impact of tumour size. There have been no randomized clinical trials assessing the appropriate margin width after surgical resection. Locoregional recurrences are reported in half of all patients {17541936}. Because the incidence of nodal metastasis is relatively low, axillary lymph node sampling is not routinely performed unless there is clinical suspicion of nodal involvement {17541936;20119983;35426390}.

Median overall survival was recently reported as 38 months, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival being 80%, 39%, and 25%, respectively {37158932}. However, outcomes notably show considerable heterogeneity. A 3-tiered grading system has been historically used for primary breast angiosarcomas {7199829;3179927}. Different series have shown mixed results examining correlation with prognosis {18813119}, and there are many examples of morphologically better-differentiated tumours exhibiting metastasis and poor outcome. Yet a recent study refining primary angiosarcoma grading to a 2-tiered system (low-intermediate and high) demonstrated that histological grade was an independent predictor of disease-specific and overall survival {36376999}.

Primary breast angiosarcomas are rare, and in many case series they are combined with more frequent secondary (radiation-associated) angiosarcomas, despite differences in pathogenesis. No standard guidelines exist for the use of radiation or systemic therapy, with multimodal combinations used mainly in the adjuvant and metastatic setting. Anthracycline and taxane-based regimens have been the frontline chemotherapy for angiosarcomas {25459395}, showing variable results. The role for radiation after resection has not been well established {32889893}.
