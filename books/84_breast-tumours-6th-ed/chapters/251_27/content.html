2.5.1 Papillary neoplasms: Introduction

Papillary neoplasms are composed predominantly of papillae, each of which consists of a fibrovascular core covered by epithelium with or without a myoepithelial cell layer, depending on the type of papillary neoplasm. The nature of the epithelium determines whether a papillary neoplasm is benign, atypical, or malignant. Apocrine metaplasia commonly has papillary architecture. Usual ductal hyperplasia may also have thin papillary fronds. However, the adjective “papillary” should not be used when reporting these two lesions, because it may lead to mismanagement. Furthermore, usual ductal hyperplasia with focal papillary features in the absence of a discrete papillary aggregate/nodule should not be overdiagnosed as papilloma. The assessment of epithelial atypia is facilitated by considering the space between adjacent fibrovascular cores as equivalent to a duct space and applying the criteria used for the interpretation of intraductal proliferations. A cut-off size of 3 mm is used to distinguish atypical ductal hyperplasia and low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) involving a papilloma.

Most papillary neoplasms are confined within ducts, which tend to be distended and cystic, with a thick fibrous wall. The intraductal papillary neoplasms of the breast include papilloma, papilloma with atypical ductal hyperplasia or DCIS, papillary DCIS, and solid papillary carcinoma (SPC) in situ; encapsulated papillary carcinoma (EPC) is also included in this group. All benign intraductal papillary neoplasms are surrounded by a continuous layer of myoepithelium, but the presence and distribution of myoepithelial cells along the papillae and at the periphery of atypical and malignant lesions varies according to the lesion type. Papillary lesions completely devoid of myoepithelium include invasive papillary carcinoma and EPC, although the latter may have a few scattered residual myoepithelial cells. In addition to invasive SPC, some SPC in situ may also be partially devoid of myoepithelial cells. See the sections that follow for more detailed discussion of EPC and SPC.

Myoepithelial cells may sometimes be difficult to identify in routine H&E-stained sections, but they can be visualized with the use of immunohistochemical stains for myoepithelial antigens (see #48220Table 2.03). These markers vary in sensitivity and specificity {10721417;15762278}. Reactive myofibroblasts adjacent to nodules of papillary carcinoma often express myoepithelial antigens that react with calponin and SMA {17525625;15762278}. Pericytes associated with capillaries within the fibrovascular cores and at the periphery of a papillary lesion may express actin, calponin, and SMMHC; care should be taken not to misinterpret these as myoepithelial cells {17525625}. Scattered neoplastic epithelial cells, especially in papillary carcinomas, may express p63, a phenomenon that is seen in the more-peripheral cells, thereby mimicking myoepithelial cells {12502930}.

Correct categorization of a papillary lesion in a core biopsy can be difficult because of fragmentation and the inability to evaluate the entire epithelial–stromal interface of a larger lesion. The presence of a myoepithelial cell layer running along the fibrovascular cores is usually strong evidence of an underlying papilloma. In this scenario, the neoplastic nature of the epithelial component is classified according to its morphological features (and immunohistochemical features, if needed), ranging from atypical ductal hyperplasia / low-grade DCIS, to intermediate- or high-grade DCIS. When there is a complete lack of a myoepithelial cell layer within the lesion, with or without myoepithelial cells at the periphery, or a lack of any residual benign hyperplastic epithelium, the findings are often suggestive of at least a papillary carcinoma in situ (including papillary DCIS, SPC in situ, and EPC). While papillary DCIS can be any nuclear grade, EPC and SPC in situ typically are composed of neoplastic-appearing low- to intermediate-grade neoplastic epithelium that is uniformly ER-positive. When a definitive diagnosis cannot be reached on a core biopsy sample, a differential diagnosis can often be provided with an overall diagnostic category of “atypical papillary lesion” or “at least papillary carcinoma in situ”, with deferral to an excision for more complete characterization of the process.

FNAB cytological diagnosis of papillary lesions is challenging, with benign and malignant lesions showing overlapping features. When a papillary lesion is suspected, prompt histological evaluation is warranted for accurate diagnosis {18552172}.

Breast excision specimens that have undergone prior core needle biopsy often have displaced epithelium within the biopsy site, a finding that is inversely related to the time interval between biopsy and surgical excision {10541110;16253028}. This phenomenon is particularly evident after core biopsy of a papillary neoplasm {16253028}. Displaced epithelium within the core biopsy site should not be overdiagnosed as invasive carcinoma. Displaced epithelial cell clusters or single cells may also be found in the axillary lymph node sinuses, often in association with giant cells and haemosiderin-laden macrophages {10664628;19200573;18852675}. In either setting, immunohistochemical staining for myoepithelial antigens is usually non-contributory, because the myoepithelial cells might not have been displaced with the epithelium; only a positive result can be interpreted with confidence.

Infarction of a papillary lesion may occur after FNAB or core needle biopsy, or it may be secondary to torsion of the stalk. Infarction is unrelated to the type of papillary neoplasm, but it may be more common in larger tumours. In some cases, the use of myoepithelial and/or epithelial markers might be helpful to highlight the underlying architecture and cell composition, and possibly to determine the nature of the papillary neoplasm, with the understanding that lack of staining should not be interpreted as a negative result, and that limited tissue preservation often precludes a definitive interpretation.

Compared with the fifth edition, the sixth edition has introduced the following changes in the classification of papillary lesions.

In the fifth edition, SPC was classified as in situ or invasive. In the sixth edition, the diagnostic criteria of SPC in situ remain unchanged, but invasive disease has been further refined into SPC in situ with invasion and invasive SPC. SPC in situ with invasion designates SPC associated with strands or large irregular clusters of tumour cells with infiltrative growth, often present within pools of extracellular mucin. Invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) and mucinous carcinoma are the most common types of invasive carcinoma in this setting. Invasive SPC consists of tumour nodules devoid of myoepithelium with ragged contours creating a geographical jigsaw pattern within a desmoplastic stroma.

In the fifth edition, it was recommended that EPC with high nuclear grade, increased mitotic activity, and/or a triple-negative or ERBB2 (HER2)-positive phenotype be graded, staged, and managed as invasive disease. Data to support this approach are very limited. Therefore, the editorial board for the sixth edition recommends that such rare lesions should be categorized as EPC (which by convention is staged as DCIS), but with a note or comment enumerating the unusual features. Nonetheless, in these cases thorough histological examination of the surgical excision specimen is advised to exclude the possibility of a high-grade invasive carcinoma with pseudopapillary growth secondary to central necrosis. Multidisciplinary review for collective management is also recommended.
