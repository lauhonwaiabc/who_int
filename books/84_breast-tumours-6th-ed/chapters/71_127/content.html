7.1 Haematolymphoid tumours of the breast: Introduction

This section deals only with those haematolymphoid neoplasms which most commonly involve the breast. Other haematolymphoid neoplasms may arise primarily in the breast or involve one or both breasts secondarily in patients with systemic diseases. The reader is referred to the fifth edition Haematolymphoid tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637. ]] for additional information on neoplasms not listed in this volume.

Primary lymphoma of the breast is typically defined as lymphoma confined to one or both breasts and/or regional lymph nodes, in the absence of a prior history of lymphoma {3922871;8333556;17932394}. However, some studies have used criteria less stringent than those for other extranodal lymphomas: presentation with the dominant mass or symptom in the breast in a patient without known lymphoma, even if distant involvement is found on staging {3545107;2249200}.

Primary lymphoma of the breast is uncommon, accounting for < 0.5% of all malignant neoplasms of the breast {12051628;2249200;31428995} and approximately 2% of all extranodal lymphomas {5007387;10717152;19958938}. According to SEER Program data, the incidence of primary breast lymphoma per 1 million women has increased over recent decades, from 0.66 cases in 1975–1977 to 2.96 cases in 2011–2013 {28376147}.

Most patients are middle-aged or elderly women, although younger women can also be affected, including pregnant or lactating women {28376147;19199367;11967901}. Rarely, men are affected {2249200;22323922;30567057;17932394}. Patients usually present with a painless unilateral breast lump, but in some cases the tumour is detected on radiological examination {17541937}. As many as 11% of cases show bilateral breast involvement {28376147;20799767;17541937;11967901;17932394}.

Although any type of lymphoma can occur as a primary breast lymphoma {8116797;19570964}, the most common histological types include diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT lymphoma) {28376147;17541937;19199367;12051628}. Burkitt lymphoma presents occasionally as a primary breast tumour, more frequently in areas where the disease is endemic. Breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma accounts for 2% of all breast lymphomas and is increasingly recognized owing to enhanced awareness and improved guidelines for the evaluation of capsulectomy specimens {28376147;33234792}. Treatment and prognosis depend on the specific type of lymphoma.

Other haematolymphoid neoplasms that have been described to involve the breast include plasma cell neoplasms, which should be distinguished from MALT lymphoma with prominent plasmacytic differentiation. Histiocytic sarcoma, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, and interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma have rarely been reported to occur as primary tumours of the breast {25031040;12189511;10196389;15806378}. Of particular interest is a reported case of follicular dendritic cell sarcoma accompanied by abundant myxoid stroma, mimicking mucinous carcinoma {10196389}.

Myeloid sarcoma has been described to occur in the breast either as a solitary lesion not accompanied by leukaemia or as a local manifestation in a patient with diagnosed acute myeloid leukaemia {25890617;29684340;15628906}. Histologically, it can potentially be mistaken for invasive lobular carcinoma owing to a single-file non-cohesive growth pattern and lack of immunoreactivity for E-cadherin.
