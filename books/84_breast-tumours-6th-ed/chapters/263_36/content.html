2.6.3 Lobular carcinoma in situ

Definition

Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) is a non-invasive neoplastic proliferation of discohesive cells, originating in the terminal duct lobular units (TDLUs), with or without pagetoid involvement of terminal ducts. More than half of the acini in a TDLU are filled and expanded by the neoplastic cells.

Related terminology

Acceptable: lobular neoplasia (acceptable only for classic lobular carcinoma in situ).

Not recommended: non-classic lobular carcinoma in situ; variant lobular carcinoma in situ; lobular intraepithelial neoplasia.

Subtype(s)

Classic lobular carcinoma in situ; florid lobular carcinoma in situ; pleomorphic lobular carcinoma in situ

Localization

Classic LCIS is multicentric in the ipsilateral breast in as many as 80% of patients and bilateral in 30–67% {1853802;5915941;218506;5394401;10490198} [[Haagensen CD, editor. Diseases of the Breast. 3rd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 1986. ]] [[Tavassoli FA, editor. Pathology of the breast. 2nd ed. Stamford (CT): Appleton & Lange; 1999. ]]. Pleomorphic LCIS and florid LCIS are usually unifocal and continuous in distribution, often colocalized with invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) in the background of multicentric classic LCIS {21287281;30489319}.

Clinical features

Classic LCIS has no specific clinical features; the lesion is usually an incidental microscopic finding in breast biopsies performed for other indications {19970575}. Classic LCIS is unlikely to be detected on imaging and therefore unlikely to be the target of imaging abnormalities. However, studies reviewing series collected in the context of screening programmes report that classic LCIS may be incidentally associated with grouped amorphous or granular calcifications in as many as 65% of the cases. Stromal distortion or heterogeneous non–mass like enhancement with persistent enhancement kinetics on MRI may be also observed {27060553;23498988;36238391;38117388}. In contrast, florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS are usually associated with microcalcifications detected on screening mammography {19701073;28612228;28929550;25893410;24372322;23809857;12379750;20586632}. Linear mammographic calcifications correlate with larger lesions {27060553}.

Epidemiology

LCIS is found in 0.5–3.6% of otherwise benign breast biopsies and 0.04–1.2% of reduction mammoplasty specimens {30238276;872058;11161169;1853802;23656973;10430122;27538687;1748429;15731670}. Epidemiological studies found that the incidence rates of LCIS increased rapidly after the introduction of mammography (1978–1998) and declined sharply between 2001 and 2004, probably owing to decreased use of postmenopause hormone replacement therapy {12353815;15824180;26431342}. Classic LCIS is predominantly diagnosed in premenopausal women (mean age: ~50 years) {1853802;11348305;209887;26371145}. Florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS are diagnosed primarily in postmenopausal women (mean age: ~60 years) {19701073;29102711;30489319;23809857}.

Etiology

Many risk factors for LCIS are similar to those established for ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive breast carcinoma. Population-based studies have reported an increased risk of classic LCIS associated with a family history of breast cancer, a previous benign breast biopsy, nulliparity, older age at first full-term birth, older age at menopause, and high mammographic density {25603785;11734598;12611452;28637226;10919740;8630296}. In postmenopausal women, hormone replacement therapy increases the risk of classic LCIS {28637226;17081916;10919740}. Models used to improve risk stratification of patients with high-risk breast lesions and to personalize breast cancer surveillance may overestimate invasive breast cancer risk in patients with LCIS {31559544;29925933}.

CDH1 (the gene encoding the cell adhesion protein E-cadherin), localized at 16q22.1, plays an important role in the pathogenesis of lobular lesions. Germline CDH1 mutations, which are highly penetrant, are associated with an increased risk of hereditary diffuse gastric cancer and bilateral classic LCIS, with or without ILC {29929997;24366306}; the risk of florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS is unknown. Higher frequencies of pure classic LCIS in association with germline CHEK2 variants are also reported {31263054}. Several germline SNPs associated with an increased risk of ER-positive breast cancer also show an association with ILC, and more specifically with classic LCIS {24743323}.

Pathogenesis

CDH1 inactivation, which leads to loss or impaired function of E-cadherin, is an early event and hallmark of lobular lesions {9365159}. E-cadherin mediates cell–cell adhesion and plays a key role in the maintenance of lobular architecture. It can also inhibit the growth and invasion of breast cancer cells {11597316;21346732}. Defective E-cadherin results in loss of cell–cell adhesion, increased cell proliferation, and altered organization of the lobules, giving rise to the characteristic discohesive appearance of lobular neoplastic lesions in mouse models {21282721;17097565}. CDH1 mutation is identified in 81% of classic LCIS cases. Most of the mutations seen in classic LCIS have been found to be associated with an age-related mutation signature, consistent with mutation signatures observed in ILC and ER-positive invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST) {30185420}. Molecular analysis has demonstrated that classic LCIS is a clonal neoplastic proliferation and a non-obligate precursor lesion to invasive carcinoma. Studies have indicated that classic LCIS, florid LCIS, and pleomorphic LCIS all harbour recurrent chromosomal gain at 1q and loss at 16q, a pattern typical of the low-grade breast neoplasia pathway {20155841;19701073;11274638;9788628;23809857;31907376;31932682}. Comparative genomic hybridization and massively parallel sequencing studies have revealed that classic LCIS can be clonally related to ILC and occasionally to DCIS, providing genetic evidence in support of classic LCIS as a non-obligate precursor to ILC {15197797;15957152;26643573;30185420}. Studies have also shown that florid LCIS, pleomorphic LCIS, and ILC share a common mutation repertoire and copy-number alterations with paired synchronous classic LCIS, indicating a shared clonal ancestry {31907376}. Florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS exhibit greater genomic instability than classic LCIS, with increased copy-number alterations and gene amplifications {20155841;19701073;15957152;23809857;31907376}. Pleomorphic LCIS has been found to have higher mean non-synonymous mutations than paired classic LCIS {31907376}. Florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS frequently harbour mutations in the tyrosine kinase domains of ERBB2 and ERBB3 (and aberrations in other genes including TP53 and CCND1) or ERBB2 amplification {31907376;31932682}. These findings indicate that florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS are genetically more-advanced lesions than classic LCIS. The most frequently mutated genes in classic LCIS are CDH1, PIK3CA, and CBFB {30185420;26643573;28095868;25773929}. A microarray gene expression profiling study demonstrated that classic LCIS is heterogeneous at the transcriptomic level {25601220}.

Macroscopic appearance

Classic LCIS is not associated with any grossly recognizable abnormalities {19970575;209887}. In contrast, florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS may appear as mass-forming lesions or show a gritty tissue texture {29413653}.

Histopathology

Classic LCIS is characterized by discohesive proliferations of type A and/or type B epithelial cells, similar to those in atypical lobular hyperplasia. Type A cells are small cells with uniform hyperchromatic nuclei (1–1.5 times the size of a lymphocyte), whereas type B cells have slightly larger vesicular nuclei (2 times the size of a lymphocyte), with mild variability in size and shape and small nucleoli {12379750}. More than 50% of the acini in a TDLU must be filled and expanded by the neoplastic cells to qualify as classic LCIS. Glandular architecture is not observed, and mitoses are uncommon. Classic LCIS may rarely show single-cell apoptosis and/or minute foci of necrosis. Classic LCIS can show pagetoid growth in ducts and may involve other lesions, including usual ductal hyperplasia, sclerosing adenosis, radial scars, papillary lesions, fibroadenomas, and collagenous spherulosis. Pagetoid spread of classic LCIS in adjacent ducts is more frequent than for atypical lobular hyperplasia {1748429}.

Florid LCIS has the cytological features of classic LCIS, but there is marked distention of TDLUs or ducts, creating a confluent mass-like architecture. Florid LCIS should have at least one of two architectural features: (1) little to no intervening stroma between markedly distended acini of involved TDLUs and (2) a size cut-off point at which an expanded acinus or duct spans approximately 40–50 cells in diameter {21044747;30489319;23809857;29413653}. Lesions with cytological features of classic LCIS that show distension of the acini that is borderline between classic LCIS and florid LCIS should be designated as classic LCIS, even when LCIS is extensive.

Pleomorphic LCIS is composed of larger cells with marked nuclear pleomorphism, with nuclei which are > 4 times the size of a lymphocyte / equivalent to the cells of high-grade DCIS, with or without apocrine features {12379750}. Binucleated or multinucleated cells are more frequently identified in pleomorphic LCIS as compared with classic LCIS or DCIS {26486735;31301938}. In surgical specimens, LCIS lesions that are borderline between classic LCIS composed of type B cells and pleomorphic LCIS should be categorized as classic LCIS composed of type B cells.

Pleomorphic LCIS and florid LCIS often demonstrate comedonecrosis and calcifications, but neither of the two findings is required for the diagnosis. Owing to the high prevalence (as high as 87%) of associated invasive carcinoma with these morphological subtypes, with the majority (84–100%) being ILC, detection of these lesions should prompt a careful search for subtle invasion in adjacent breast tissue {17063087;21287281;30489319;31301938}. Signet-ring cells may be seen in any morphological subtype of LCIS.

Loss of membranous E-cadherin expression is the characteristic immunohistochemical feature for all types of LCIS {23759937;7682767;15696125;12861063}. In approximately 15% of invasive lobular lesions, the neoplastic cells have conserved but aberrant E-cadherin expression, and this is recapitulated in LCIS. Therefore, fragmented, membranous, diminished, and/or cytoplasmic aberrant staining should not be used to make a diagnosis of DCIS {26768029;18379416;20871222}. E-cadherin antibodies directed against the intracytoplasmic domain show more frequent aberrant nuclear staining than those directed against the extracellular domain {39025406;31231125;18580099}. Some E-cadherin clones may show cross-reactivity with P-cadherin or reduced specificity for E-cadherin {38641322}. LCIS cells usually exhibit strong, diffuse cytoplasmic staining for p120 catenin and loss of expression of membrane β-catenin {26768029;17325485;22080244;15077190}. However, the distinction between LCIS and DCIS cannot rely solely on these immunohistochemical markers, which should be interpreted in conjunction with detailed morphological evaluation.

Classic LCIS and florid LCIS are typically diffusely and strongly positive for ER, have a low Ki-67 proliferation index, and rarely show ERBB2 (HER2) overexpression / ERBB2 amplification or TP53 mutation {9674874;1739917;1295456;1973459;9071897}. Pleomorphic LCIS is more likely to be ER-negative (especially apocrine pleomorphic LCIS), may be AR-positive, may demonstrate HER2 overexpression / ERBB2 amplification or TP53 mutation, and has a moderate to high Ki-67 proliferation index {19701073;24372322;30489319;23809857;12379750}. Approximately 10% of pleomorphic LCIS cases are triple negative {24372322;30489319}.

Cytology

FNAB of lobular neoplasia produces low cellularity with small cells with mildly atypical nuclei and eccentric cytoplasm often containing a single vacuole, scattered as single cells and loosely cohesive small sheets {2750704;8143551;12112810}. LCIS has been reported to be misdiagnosed as benign or non-diagnostic in > 50% of cases {12112810}, and similarly in liquid based cytology, lobular neoplasia presents with varying degrees of atypia, with or without a concomitant population of benign epithelial cells {15830370}. There are no reliable cytopathological criteria for distinguishing between classic LCIS, pleomorphic LCIS, and ILC {8143551;12112810;15830370}, and core needle biopsy should be recommended when these lesions are suspected on FNAB.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For classic LCIS: small discohesive cells with uniform hyperchromatic nuclei (type A) to slightly larger vesicular nuclei with mild variability (type B), filling and expanding more than half of the acini in a TDLU For florid LCIS: a proliferation of cells cytologically similar to those of classic LCIS but expanding the acini of the involved TDLUs so that little to no residual intervening intralobular stroma is present, and/or an expanded acinus or duct spans approximately 40–50 cells in diameter For pleomorphic LCIS: large discohesive cells with pleomorphic nuclei > 4 times the size of a lymphocyte (equivalent to the nuclei of high-grade DCIS), with or without apocrine features.

Desirable (for all three subtypes):

Loss of membranous E-cadherin staining, if needed.

Staging

However, the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging manual [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. Prognosis and prediction

LCIS is both a risk factor for and a non-obligate precursor of invasive breast carcinoma. The relative risk of subsequent breast cancer in patients with classic LCIS is 8–10 times the risk in the general population {872058;8780540;14716756;209887;1748429;210682}. The absolute risk of breast cancer is in the range of 1–2% per year, with a cumulative long-term rate of > 20% at 20 years and a lifetime risk of 30–40% {8839545;14716756;26371145;24615647;39270543}. The risk is bilateral, and the invasive carcinoma can be either ILC or NST {20541352;16110014}. However, ipsilateral cancers predominate, with an overrepresentation of ILC; LCIS is therefore also considered a non-obligate precursor lesion, albeit with a low risk of progression to invasive cancer {20541352;23117858;26371145;16604564;34428722;29023647;28455673}. This is further supported by molecular studies.

Current management for classic LCIS includes active surveillance and risk-reduction strategies for both breasts. Guidelines include recommendations for antiestrogen therapy as chemoprevention to reduce breast cancer risk in women with LCIS {23835710}. The management of classic LCIS diagnosed on core needle biopsy remains controversial owing to the wide range of reported upgrade rates (0–60%) to carcinoma (including invasive carcinoma and DCIS) on excision {11161169;27806840;23808465;22268169;15767810;15105449;20395524;18660792;22268170;17460455;26564004;10430122;18619840;16985141;12591964;27538687;23132235;22997389;20637429;27522516;21861212;16891208;22847124;12033958;20431477;22706860}. In these studies, the upgrade rates are higher when radiological–pathological correlation is not addressed or when LCIS represents the radiological target, and the rates are even higher (as high as 66%) in cases with biopsy for mass lesions and those with imaging–histology discordance {23808465;15767810;18660792;17460455;12591964;23132235;22997389}. Currently there are no guidelines for assessing radiological–pathological concordance, but it has been suggested that the likelihood of concordance is higher when LCIS is obtained at biopsy of calcifying lesions than of non-calcifying lesions {38472593}. Recent studies with concordant imaging findings have reported low (~1–4%) excisional upgrade rates of incidental classic LCIS (and atypical lobular hyperplasia) to carcinoma {23901123;18660792;23132235;18348299;26542585;21861212;16891208;22847124;27425222;32861602}. Therefore, patients with incidental classic LCIS on core biopsy showing concordant imaging–histology findings may be followed with high-risk screening and spared surgical excision [[American Society of Breast Surgeons. Resource Guide: Surgical Management of Benign or High-Risk Lesions. Columbia (MD): American Society of Breast Surgeons; 2024 cited 2025 Aug 11. Available from: https://www.breastsurgeons.org/resources/statements/. ]] [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): breast cancer risk reduction. Version 2.2025. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2025. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx. ]]. European guidelines recommend performing vacuum-assisted procedures when a core biopsy returns classic LCIS, followed by surveillance but not surgery when radiological–pathological concordance is met {38061151}. LCIS lesions that are borderline between classic LCIS composed of type B cells and florid/pleomorphic LCIS should be excised when found on core biopsy. There is no indication that excision of classic LCIS to negative margins is useful, and it is not necessary to assess or report the status of excision margins for classic LCIS, even in the setting of coexistent pleomorphic LCIS, florid LCIS, and/or ILC.

Information about the natural history of florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS is extremely limited, and the optimal treatment of patients with these LCIS subtypes after excision without evidence of invasive carcinoma remains unclear – including whether to achieve negative margins and whether there is any potential benefit of adjuvant radiation therapy {23231019}. Notably, the presence of florid/pleomorphic LCIS at the margin of breast-conserving surgery specimens performed for DCIS or invasive carcinoma does not appear to influence patient outcomes, but data are limited {35771367;37493892}. The reported recurrence rates of pleomorphic LCIS treated with conservative surgery with or without antiestrogen therapy vary from 0% to as high as 57%, and the recurrent lesions may be pleomorphic LCIS, DCIS, ILC, or invasive carcinoma NST {28612228;21631266;25893410;24372322;30362065;12379750}. It is unclear whether positive margin status affects the likelihood of recurrence, but results from these studies generally support the practical utility of surgical excision to negative margins for pleomorphic LCIS if feasible. It is recommended that margin status be reported for florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS to help inform further management decisions. However, there is no information about the optimal margin clearance {35869801}. The use of radiotherapy in patients with florid LCIS and pleomorphic LCIS is debated, and data are limited to very small retrospective series {25893410;24372322;21631266;29947004}. Some guidelines recommend treating pleomorphic LCIS in the same fashion as DCIS, after multidisciplinary discussion {31161190}, but there are no recommendations for the treatment of patients with florid LCIS.
