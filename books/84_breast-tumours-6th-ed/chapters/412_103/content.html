4.1.2 Fibroadenoma

Definition

Fibroadenoma is a circumscribed benign neoplasm of the terminal duct lobular unit with biphasic proliferation of epithelial and stromal components.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: adenofibroma.

Subtype(s)

Cellular fibroadenoma; juvenile fibroadenoma; myxoid fibroadenoma; complex fibroadenoma

Localization

Fibroadenoma shows no preferential location within the breast; it may be multifocal and bilateral.

Clinical features

Fibroadenomas often present as painless, solitary, firm, slow-growing, mobile, well-defined nodules that are usually < 30 mm. On mammography, they appear as nodular densities or calcified nodules. Multiple synchronous or metachronous fibroadenomas can be unilateral or bilateral. In one study of multiple fibroadenomas, the average number was 5.2 ± 2.4 {37094818}. Symptomatic fibroadenomas are rare in older women. Giant fibroadenomas, measuring > 50 mm, are uncommon but may occasionally occur in adolescent girls, potentially causing breast distortion. Fibroadenomas may also develop in men with gynaecomastia {8159950}. Juvenile fibroadenomas, which are more common in but not exclusive to adolescents, can be large and exhibit rapid growth {12110805}.

Epidemiology

Fibroadenomas are rare before menarche and are most common in adolescent girls and women aged < 35 years, except for complex fibroadenomas, which tend to occur about two decades later {26264469}. Juvenile fibroadenomas are more commonly described in young African Americans {28299887;5813207}.

Etiology

Most fibroadenomas are sporadic. A small proportion of myxoid fibroadenomas occur in women with Carney complex {2069209}. Cyclosporin immunosuppression has been associated with the development of multiple large fibroadenomas in adolescent girls and young women {16350824;20534252;9038560;28556594}; these lesions stop growing or regress after therapy is switched to tacrolimus {20534252}.

Pathogenesis

Fibroadenomas are hormone sensitive {16554735} and may grow rapidly during pregnancy. The epithelium and stroma are non-clonal, but monoclonality has been demonstrated in areas of stromal expansion {12210075}. Numerical abnormalities of chromosomes 16, 18, and 21 have been reported {15474152}, but no consistent aberrations are found {29248888}.

Sequencing studies show that about 60% of fibroadenomas (except the myxoid variety) harbour MED12 mutations {25038752;28513873} (mostly in codon 44 of exon 2 {25038752;25855048;26109290;26093648}), which are associated with dysregulated estrogen signalling and extracellular matrix organization {25038752}. The spectrum of MED12 mutations in fibroadenoma is nearly identical to that reported in uterine leiomyomas {25038752} and is more frequent in intracanalicular than pericanalicular fibroadenomas {26093648}. MED12 exon 2 mutations are somatic and detected in stromal cells, sparing the epithelial compartment {25038752}. Some reports show the frequency and pattern of MED12 mutations in juvenile fibroadenomas to be similar to those in conventional fibroadenomas {26109290}, but others report a lower rate of MED12 abnormalities in juvenile fibroadenoma, with additional differences in mutation spectrum {29969836}.

Apart from MED12 mutations, RARA mutations, albeit at lower frequency, are often found in both fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours {31662438}. In comparison, cancer driver gene and TERT promoter mutations common in phyllodes tumour are rare in conventional and juvenile fibroadenomas {29969836}, and so are other gene mutations, such as FLNA, SETD2, KMT2D (MLL2), BCOR, MAP3K1, NF1, RB1, PIK3CA, EGFR, TP53, and ERBB4 {26437033}. These genomic differences may be of potential clinical use in distinguishing fibroadenomas from phyllodes tumours.

Identical MED12 mutations observed in fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours from the same patient imply a clonal relationship between these two fibroepithelial lesions and suggest that some fibroadenomas may give rise to phyllodes tumours {28721388;25855048}.

There are two recent studies investigating epigenetic changes in fibroadenomas {35158935;38300122} using whole-genome methylation profiling. Both showed that fibroepithelial lesions (fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours) have unique methylation profiles compared with breast cancer and normal breast tissue. While fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours are more similar, they also independently cluster, suggesting each have distinct epigenomic profiles {38300122}.

Multiple fibroadenomas presenting clinically in a single patient are unusual. The literature mostly consists of single case reports and small series. A case report details multiple myxoid fibroadenomas requiring bilateral mastectomy in a young woman with features of Carney complex {38715866}. Another recent report {33882939} identified a pathogenic PTEN mutation (Cowden syndrome) in a young patient who presented with multiple, early-onset fibroadenomas, while multiple fibroadenomas have also been described {25521982} in a patient with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome and uniparental disomy of chromosome 11p15.5. Multiple fibroadenomas have been reported in association with cyclosporin A use in renal transplantation {9038560;20701786}.

Macroscopic appearance

Fibroadenomas are solid, ovoid, well circumscribed, and usually ≤ 30 mm, although they can be large especially in juvenile fibroadenomas. Sectioning reveals a uniform, rubbery, lobulated, whorled, greyish-white cut surface with intervening slit-like spaces. A glistening surface or calcifications may be apparent.

Histopathology

Fibroadenoma may exhibit a pericanalicular pattern, where stromal cells grow circumferentially around open ducts, and/or an intracanalicular pattern, where stromal compression of ducts creates cleft-like spaces. These patterns may occur singly or in combination {34505197}. The pericanalicular pattern is more common in juvenile fibroadenoma. The stromal component usually exhibits uniformly low cellularity and lacks atypia; but it may sometimes exhibit focal or diffuse hypercellularity, particularly in women under 20 years of age. Other features include bizarre multinucleated giant stromal cells, extensive myxoid change {2069209;28513873}, or hyalinization with dystrophic calcifications, and rarely ossification in postmenopausal women. Lipomatous, smooth muscle, and osteochondroid metaplasia may occur. Mitoses are uncommon but occasionally may be present, especially in fibroadenomas of young or pregnant patients. While most fibroadenomas are circumscribed, some may display an irregular interphase with adjacent stroma, which should not detract from a diagnosis of fibroadenoma if all histological features are typical. In lesions without distinct circumscription that merge within the background breast parenchyma, fibroadenomatoid hyperplasia, which usually occurs in the context of fibrocystic change, may be considered. Epithelial changes such as squamous and apocrine metaplasia, focal fibrocystic changes, sclerosing adenosis, and usual ductal hyperplasia, as well as myoepithelial proliferation, can occur {11345838}. Atypical ductal/lobular hyperplasia and ductal/lobular carcinoma in situ may infrequently involve fibroadenomas {11345838;15297206}. When atypical hyperplasia is confined to the fibroadenoma, evidence suggests that the subsequent cancer risk is not increased {11443606}. Invasive carcinoma may also involve fibroadenomas, typically a result of secondary involvement.

Juvenile fibroadenomas often demonstrate a pericanalicular growth pattern with a uniform mild to moderate increase in stromal cellularity, accompanied by usual ductal hyperplasia {25998513;28299887}, most commonly of gynaecomastoid type. Stromal cells are arranged in fascicles with no significant nuclear atypia. Stromal mitotic activity is usually low, at < 1 mitosis/mm2 (assessed in a minimal area of 2 mm2) {28299887;5813207}, though mitotic activity may be increased in younger patients.

Complex fibroadenomas have one or more of the following features: sclerosing adenosis, epithelial calcifications, papillary apocrine metaplasia, and cysts > 3 mm in size {8202095}. They are present in 3–4% of benign breast biopsies {26264469;20084540}, account for 16–23% of all fibroadenomas {8202095;18094314}, and are smaller than other fibroadenomas {18094314}. There is evidence suggesting that complex fibroadenomas without atypia carry a slightly increased relative risk (2.27–3.1 times the risk in the general population) for developing subsequent breast carcinoma {26264469;8202095;18094314}.

Myxoid fibroadenoma shows extensive myxoid stroma and may on rare occasions be associated with Carney complex {2069209}.

Cellular fibroadenomas have a pericanalicular growth pattern, mildly to moderately increased stromal cellularity, and usually < 1 stromal mitosis / mm2 (assessed in a minimal area of 2 mm2), but they lack the following features: marked stromal nuclear atypia and cellularity, exaggerated intracanalicular architecture, periductal subepithelial stromal condensation, and intratumoural heterogeneity {26768026}.

The diagnosis of fibroadenoma on core biopsy is reliable and accurate. However, the finding of a cellular fibroepithelial lesion on core biopsy, together with other features, raises the possibility of phyllodes tumour, prompting surgical excision in most cases to avoid missing any malignant components, which can be very focal {32771211;25125627;32461622;39947480}. A molecular test that may distinguish fibroadenoma from phyllodes tumour on core biopsy has been reported {26961242;33727697}. In some instances, a clear distinction cannot be made between a cellular fibroadenoma and a phyllodes tumour, and the term “benign fibroepithelial lesion/neoplasm” may be used, pending final classification on excision.

Cytology

Smears show moderate to high cellularity with predominantly large 3D ductal epithelial tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells, often irregular secondary lumina, and streaming of nuclei, along with smaller tissue fragments, branched tissue fragments, minimal dispersed cells, and a variable amount of stroma that can be rounded and myxoid or sclerosed and tufted. There are stripped myoepithelial cells or bare bipolar nuclei, often in large numbers in the usually clean background {29413655;9305388;2835896}. Dispersed single cells can be present, particularly in areas of crush artefact of the cellular smears, and some cases will show more-prominent single nucleoli, which can be misinterpreted as atypia, especially when the material is limited. There may be a finely granular magenta (Giemsa) proteinaceous background with metaplastic apocrine cells present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: circumscribed biphasic tumour; intracanalicular and/or pericanalicular growth pattern; no stromal overgrowth; absence of well-developed fronds; no stromal atypia; low mitotic activity in the stromal component.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Most fibroadenomas do not recur after complete surgical excision. In adolescents, there is a tendency for one or more new lesions to develop at another site or close to the site of the previous surgical treatment {30444280}.
