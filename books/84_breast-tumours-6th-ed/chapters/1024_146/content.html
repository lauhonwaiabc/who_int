Definition

Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants in TP53 and characterized by multiple primary neoplasms in children and adults, with a predominance of soft tissue sarcomas, osteosarcoma, breast cancer, brain tumours, and adrenocortical carcinoma.

Localization

Li–Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) is associated with cancers of the breast, soft tissue, bone, brain, and adrenal glands.

Clinical features

LFS is characterized by the early onset of a broad spectrum of cancers and a high lifetime cancer risk. The five core LFS-related cancers are adrenocortical carcinomas, breast cancer, CNS tumours, osteosarcomas, and soft tissue sarcomas, which constitute about 70% of LFS-related neoplasms {29076966}. In addition, a broad range of other tumours, including lymphomas, gastrointestinal malignancies (gastric and colorectal cancers), melanoma, and lung cancer, has been described in families with LFS {19204208;24706533;26101242}. More-recent studies have also reported cancers of the female reproductive organs (ovarian and endometrial carcinomas), the urogenital tract (carcinomas of the kidney and the prostate), and the eyes {14583457;28509937}.

The classic definition of LFS required sarcoma in a patient aged < 45 years, a first-degree relative with any cancer before 45 years of age, and a first- or second-degree relative with any cancer before 45 years of age or sarcoma at any age {29039119;3409256}. This clinical definition has since been expanded to allow a broader recognition of individuals and families with LFS (see #47788Box 10.02).

Patients and families without the full spectrum of classic criteria have been designated as having Li–Fraumeni–like syndrome (LFL), which has been further defined clinically by two sets of criteria, proposed by Birch and Eeles, respectively {29039119;8118819;8718514}. The Birch criteria are close to those of classic LFS but do not require a sarcoma, whereas the Eeles definition of LFL includes two first- or second-degree relatives with LFS component tumours at any age, rather than the three required by the classic criteria {29039119;8718514}. The investigation of other family genes and non-coding regions of the TP53 gene may explain the occurrence of LFL {30086788}.

About one third of breast cancers associated with LFS or LFL seem to occur before the age of 30 years, whereas they are less common after the age of 50 years. Approximately 3–8% of patients with breast cancer aged < 30 years are estimated to carry a TP53 germline pathogenic variant, regardless of family history {24706533}. In a cohort of 70 patients with breast cancer who were carriers of the NM000546.6:c.1010G>A p.(Arg337His) variant, 49 were aged < 45 years and 21 were aged > 55 years {24936644}. Therefore, early onset of breast cancer in the absence of other malignancies points towards LFS, whereas later onset of cancers has been encountered in LFL {22672556}. Soft tissue sarcomas are the second most frequent type of cancer, with a wide range of tumour onset age (6 months to 70 years), whereas osteosarcomas occur mainly in children {26014290}.

Epidemiology

More than 1500 germline variants associated with LFS, LFL, and probands with a family history or fulfilling the Chompret criteria are included in version R21 (January 2025) of the TP53 Database from 1990 onwards [[The TP53 Database Internet. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 cited 2025 Feb 21. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov. ]]. The incidence of TP53 germline variants in Europe and North America seems to be 1 case per 5000–20 000 individuals {19204208;12672316}. In southern Brazil, the founder mutation NM000546.6:c.1010G>A p.(Arg337His) occurs with an incidence of 1 case per 300 individuals and is present in 0.3% of the general population {16494995;24936644}. Within this patient population, the p.(Arg337His) mutation detected by tumour genomic profiling is frequently germline in origin {34270331}. There is recent evidence of the occurrence of de novo TP53 variants in about 10–20% of individuals with LFS; about 20% of these seem to occur during embryogenesis {29070607}. The highest pick-up rate of TP53 germline variants appears to be in patients with early-onset breast cancer {30240537}.

Etiology

The genetic basis of autosomal dominant LFS is the presence of a germline variant in the TP53 tumour suppressor gene {1978757;2259385}. A broad spectrum of TP53 variants involving the coding regions of the gene typically resulting in a dominant-negative or loss-of-function effect has been found in families with LFS, but 20–40% of individuals with LFS and the majority of families with LFL may lack detectable variants {14583457;12619118}. The lack of 100% concordance between TP53 variants and the classic LFS phenotype may be explained by posttranslational alterations, complete deletion, or the effects of modifier genes on the phenotype generated by the presence of specific germline alterations {21779515}. The most frequent alterations are missense variants at specific hotspot codons, mainly clustering in the DNA-binding domain and interfering with DNA binding or disrupting the binding surface structure necessary to modulate target gene transcription {20182602}. Other alteration types include nonsense, splice site, frameshift, large deletions, insertions, and complex rearrangements, with some variants occurring outside of the DNA-binding domain {27984644;26014290}.

In an open-resource TP53 database, > 1500 different germline variants are listed, with only four patterns accounting for about 80% of the variants: G:C>A:T at CpG islands in almost 50%, followed by A:T>G:C, G:C>A:T, and deletions (in ~10% each), predominantly involving 11 codons within the coding regions of exons 5–10, most commonly (> 3%) codons 175, 213, 245, 248, 273, 282, and 337 [[The TP53 Database Internet. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 cited 2025 Feb 21. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov. ]] {35352025}. Lesions within introns or the regulatory regions of the gene have been identified, although their functional significance is unclear and there are open questions about the significance of some TP53 variants occurring in cancer {28254861}. There is some evidence that genes of the Fanconi pathway, RECQ family genes, and other genes found in breast cancer are associated with LFL cancers {30086788}. Widespread use of next-generation and other sequencing technologies is uncovering higher numbers of variants in TP53, as well as in other genes in LFS and LFL {28509937}. This is leading to changes in the classification of Li–Fraumeni spectrum to include “phenotypic Li–Fraumeni syndrome” for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria in the absence of a pathogenic TP53 variant; “Li–Fraumeni syndrome” for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria with a pathogenic TP53 variant; “attenuated Li–Fraumeni syndrome” for individuals with a pathogenic TP53 variant who do not meet LFS testing criteria; and “incidental Li–Fraumeni syndrome” for those individuals who carry a pathogenic variant without a family history of cancer {34709361}.

Pathogenesis

The p53 protein is a multifunctional transcription factor involved in a wide range of biological processes {32404993;28886379}. Its best characterized functions are in the control of cell-cycle progression, DNA integrity, evolution of genomic alterations in the context of tumour progression {35978189}, and the survival of cells exposed to DNA-damaging agents. Evidence indicates that p53 also regulates other important processes, such as cell oxidative metabolism, the cellular response to nutrient deprivation, fertility, ferroptosis, and stem cell maintenance. The extent and consequences of the biological response elicited by p53 vary according to stress, the specific mutation conferred {28948977}, and cell type {28886379}. The functions of p53 rely mainly on its transcriptional activity, but it can also act via interactions with various proteins {21941372;30525089}.

In LFS-associated tumours, TP53 is usually inactivated by a second hit involving the wildtype allele. Mutant p53 isoforms differ from each other in the extent to which they have lost suppressor function and in their capacity to inhibit wildtype p53 in a dominant-negative manner {12826609;17311302}. In addition, some p53 mutants seem to exert oncogenic activity, but the molecular basis of this gain-of-function phenotype is still unclear {24651012;30224644}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of tumours in LFS is similar to that of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Breast cancer is by far the most frequent malignancy associated with LFS, accounting for 32% of cases (873 of 2741 included tumours) [[The TP53 Database Internet. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 cited 2025 Feb 21. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov. ]] {35352025;27328919}, with a median age at diagnosis of early to mid-thirties. Several studies have recently described the histopathological and molecular features of LFS-associated breast cancer {22392042;21761402;20805372;32636452;39629784}. Most invasive carcinomas are of no special type (NST; invasive ductal carcinomas) with rare instances of lobular, mixed ductal and lobular, and pure micropapillary carcinoma also reported {32636452}. Interestingly, at least 50% of breast cancers in patients with LFS are ERBB2 (HER2)-positive, with over half also showing ER coexpression {21761402}. No correlation between germline TP53 variant and histological features has been noted, except for the Brazilian NM_000546.6:c.1010G>A p.(Arg337His) variant, which appears to be associated with a lower rate of HER2-positive disease {39629784;35371985}. Approximately 30% of cases show ductal carcinoma in situ alone at presentation; this is often HER2-positive (> 50%) in studies with available data for HER2 in ductal carcinoma in situ. Malignant phyllodes tumours may also be associated with LFS, comprising 2–5% of breast tumours {11498785;39629784}, whereas male breast cancer seems to be uncommon.

Other cancers may involve many other organs aside from the breast, but soft tissue and bone sarcomas, brain tumours, and adrenocortical carcinomas are the most frequent {26014290}. The most common types of soft tissue sarcomas are leiomyosarcomas, rhabdomyosarcomas, liposarcomas, and undifferentiated sarcomas that can be challenging to classify. Osteosarcoma is the most frequent bone tumour. Among the brain tumours, glioblastomas and other types of gliomas, choroid plexus carcinomas, medulloblastomas, and ependymomas have been found.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Candidates for TP53 germline testing are identified based on the 2015 Chompret criteria (see #47788Box 10.02) {26014290} and the recommendations of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN; see #50843Box 10.03) {30333936}. In the era of testing by next-generation sequencing, it may be necessary to further broaden the clinical criteria to find a greater number of TP53 germline variants {28509937}. TP53, in addition to BRCA1, BRCA2, and other high-risk genes, is now routinely included in most germline testing panels {30086788} as well as in somatic tumour-only assays. Increased germline and somatic tumour testing has led to challenges in some cases distinguishing among germline pathogenic variants, known polymorphisms, variants of unknown significance (VUSs), somatic variants, and clonal haematopoiesis {31533767}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: germline pathogenic variants in TP53 and/or appropriate clinical history.

Staging

The staging is as for sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

In male patients, the specific cancer risks at ≤ 15 years, 16–45 years, and > 45 years are 19%, 27%, and 54%, respectively. In female patients, the respective risks in these same age groups are 12%, 82%, and 100%, with breast cancer incidence responsible for the observed sex difference {16912210}. Pathogenic variants in TP53 as seen in LFS confer an elevated risk of radiation-induced secondary malignancies and possibly increased sensitivity to low-dose radiation exposure by diagnostic methods such as mammography {23355100}. However, LFS may not be an absolute contraindication to the use of radiotherapy in certain clinical settings {32246378}. Current NCCN guidelines for screening recommend yearly breast MRI starting at 20 years of age and yearly breast MRI and mammography starting at 30 years of age (https://www.nccn.org).
