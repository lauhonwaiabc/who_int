2.7.0 Ductal carcinoma in situ: Introduction

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a clinically, radiologically, and histologically heterogeneous non-obligate precursor of invasive breast carcinoma. DCIS is a segmental disease originating in the terminal duct lobular unit and progressing within the duct system towards the nipple and into adjacent branches of a given segment of the duct system. Rarely DCIS may develop in the lactiferous ducts and extend to the nipple, resulting in Paget disease {8630874;10526872;8055451}. However, it should be noted that the term “ductal carcinoma in situ” encompasses a highly heterogeneous group of lesions that differ with regard to their mode of presentation, histopathological features, biological markers, genetic and molecular abnormalities, and risk for progression to invasive cancer. Heterogeneity may exist even within a given DCIS lesion {29148540;35852797}.

Historically, DCIS presented as a mass, but most cases are now identified through screening programmes. Approximately 20% of screen-detected breast cancer is DCIS [[Screening & Immunisations Team, NHS Digital. NHS Breast Screening Programme: England 2016-17. Leeds (UK): Health and Social Care Information Centre; 2018 Jan 31 updated 2018 Apr 4, cited 2018 Oct 11. Available from: https://files.digital.nhs.uk/pdf/m/f/breast_screening_programme__england__2016-17_-_report__v2.pdf. ]] {16411214}. Mammographically, DCIS can appear as clustered microcalcifications (62–98%) with linear branching or a casting pattern, or it can present as a mass (2–23%). However, most changes are indeterminate and some microcalcification patterns overlap with benign conditions; core biopsy is required for definitive diagnosis.

Our understanding of the natural history of DCIS is poor and largely based on histological review of small numbers of cases initially interpreted as benign. Although the proportion of cases developing invasive disease is similar, high-grade DCIS progresses more rapidly than low-grade disease, which may present with invasive carcinoma many decades later {15770688;25502729}. These data are supported by recent series showing that high nuclear grade is a risk factor for progression of non-resected DCIS to invasive cancer {34999428}, as are presentation as mammographic microcalcifications, young patient age, and lack of endocrine therapy {29398324}. Because of the paucity of natural history data, prediction of the likely behaviour of DCIS is largely extrapolated from analyses of recurrent disease. Historically it was reported that about 50% of lesions that initially present as DCIS recur as such, and about 50% recur as invasive carcinoma. However, more-recent data show that with prolonged follow-up, 35% of all ipsilateral recurrences are DCIS and 65% are invasive recurrences. Moreover, in situ recurrence occurs mostly < 5 years whereas invasive recurrences occur > 5 years, and these latter are not infrequently de novo cancer {33199800;35681052}.

The breast cancer–specific survival of women with DCIS, even those with recurrent cases, is extremely good {30092498}, although patients who develop invasive recurrence have a worse outcome {21398619;30092498}. The current standard of care for patients with DCIS is either breast-conserving surgery and radiotherapy or mastectomy. The definition of complete excision (clear margins) in DCIS is controversial, but current guidelines (based on meta-analyses and large datasets) recommend a 2 mm tumour-free margin {28000325;27528719;28742682}. The use of adjuvant radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery reduces recurrence by approximately half. This appears to be irrespective of age at diagnosis, tamoxifen use, method of DCIS detection, margin status, grade, presence of comedonecrosis, architecture, and lesion size {20956824}. Ongoing randomized clinical trials aim to identify low-risk disease, which can be managed conservatively with surveillance imaging {26296293;26025767} with early data suggesting the safety of this approach {39665585}. Similarly, sentinel lymph node biopsy, which had previously been undertaken for some patients with DCIS (e.g. large and high-grade disease or in patients receiving mastectomy), is no longer recommended based on the low yield and lack of significant difference in recurrence, ipsilateral invasive occurrence, or breast cancer mortality compared with no sentinel lymph node biopsy {32337481;31964523;35380620}. Tumour cells may be found in the axillary lymph nodes of patients with DCIS; in some cases, this represents spread from unidentified foci of invasive carcinoma, and extensive and thorough histological examination of the breast tissue is mandatory. However, mechanical displacement of epithelial cells from a previous needling procedure may explain other cases {14983487}.

Overall, there is wide variation in the treatment recommended for patients with DCIS {30092498;25441934}, especially in the use of adjuvant hormone therapy, and better prognostic and predictive markers are needed. Several genomic and molecular assays have more recently been developed to help tailor therapies for individual patients. A commercial panel of multiple immunohistochemical markers is available to assess the 10-year total and invasive recurrence risk and the need for radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery {33821346}. Similarly, a multigene panel that incorporates clinical data is also available {33830392}. Although of interest, these assays are costly and require further validation before they can become a standard part of clinical care.
