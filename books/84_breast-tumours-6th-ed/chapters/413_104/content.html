4.1.3 Phyllodes tumour

Definition

Phyllodes tumour is a generally circumscribed fibroepithelial neoplasm characterized by a prominent intracanalicular architectural pattern with expansile leaf-like hypercellular stromal fronds covered by benign epithelium and myoepithelium.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: cystosarcoma phyllodes; cystosarcoma phylloides; phylloides tumour.

Subtype(s)

Periductal stromal tumour

Localization

Phyllodes tumours may arise in any part of the breast, including the nipple {24977745}, and in ectopic breast tissue {12525768}.

Clinical features

Phyllodes tumours usually present clinically as unilateral, firm, painless, mobile masses. Large tumours may exceed 100 mm, distort the breast, and ulcerate the skin. Skin ulceration does not necessarily imply malignancy; large benign or borderline phyllodes tumours may cause skin ischaemia and secondary erosion. However, tumours that fungate through the skin as fleshy polypoid outgrowths are invariably malignant {25880837;38910772}. Bloody nipple discharge may be related to spontaneous tumour infarction or intraductal growth {17394506}. Multifocal or bilateral lesions are unusual {22049216}. Paraneoplastic syndromes such as hypoglycaemia {25525388} and hypertrophic osteoarthropathy {12714885} have been described.

Mammographic screening may identify smaller tumours measuring 20–30 mm, but the average tumour size is 40–50 mm. Reactive axillary lymphadenopathy can occur owing to tumour necrosis or infection {19141133;17496937}. Lymph node metastases are rare {26768026}. Mammography, sonography, and MRI typically reveal rounded, bosselated, circumscribed masses containing clefts or cysts and occasionally coarse calcifications {21353414;11972459}. However, imaging differentiation between fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours, as well as radiological prediction of phyllodes tumour grade, remains unreliable {29412020;24856515}.

Epidemiology

In populations of European descent, phyllodes tumours account for 0.3–1% of all primary tumours of the breast and 2.5% of all fibroepithelial neoplasms. The incidence is higher in multiracial Asian women {2855393;8387873}. Phyllodes tumours occur predominantly in older women (average age: 40–50 years), about 15–20 years later than fibroadenomas {22049216;29479819;29146885}. Malignant phyllodes tumours develop on average 2–5 years later than benign tumours {21856184;34190619}.

Etiology

The exact etiology of phyllodes tumours is unclear. Genetic risk factors are largely unknown, but phyllodes tumours have been described in patients with Li–Fraumeni syndrome {28688536}.

Pathogenesis

Phyllodes tumours originate from the intralobular and periductal stroma, with epithelial–stromal interactions contributing to pathogenesis {1327488}. Epithelial expression of some biomarkers is associated with stromal features indicative of tumour grade and may be important in neoplastic development {23404800;24438019;26056158}. Epithelial WNT ligand expression is correlated with stromal WNT pathway activation {11920740;12898599;22571452}. During malignant progression, the stroma becomes independent of epithelial interactions, resulting in autonomous stromal growth {11920740;22571452}. EGFR, KIT (c-KIT), and TP53 overexpression / genetic aberrations are associated with malignancy in phyllodes tumours {23404800;27207013;25534827;25216225}.

ERβ has been reported to be expressed in epithelial and stromal cells of fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours {16554735;27056456}.

Recurrent MED12 mutations in stromal cells are present in phyllodes tumours, supporting a shared pathogenesis with fibroadenomas, although malignant phyllodes tumours have a lower rate of MED12 mutations than do benign and borderline tumours {26437033;26832993}. Similar mutations in fibroadenomas and synchronous/subsequent phyllodes tumours from the same patients indicate that some phyllodes tumours may arise from fibroadenomas via a MED12-mutant pathway {19276971;8625047;29043292;28721388}. It is reported that fibroadenoma-like areas are histologically encountered in 35.9% of phyllodes tumours, and 15.4% of phyllodes tumours are accompanied by separately occurring fibroadenomas in the ipsilateral or contralateral breast {28751520}. A genomic panel incorporating 16 genes can be used to distinguish phyllodes tumour from fibroadenoma {31647027}.

Malignant/borderline phyllodes tumours without MED12 mutations and fibroadenoma-like areas show more alterations in cancer-related genes, indicating an additional pathogenetic mechanism (MED12 wildtype) independent of fibroadenomas {29043292}.

Apart from MED12 mutations, genomic studies have also implicated mutations in RARA, TERT, FLNA, SETD2, and KMT2D (MLL2) in driving the development of phyllodes tumours. Derangement of cancer driver genes such as PIK3CA, RB1, TP53, NF1, PTEN, BRAF, and EGFR promotes progression of phyllodes tumours to borderline and malignant grades {26437033}. The spectrum of genetic abnormalities may be helpful in phyllodes tumour grading. A distinct gene mutation panel that can help differentiate malignant phyllodes tumour from metaplastic carcinoma has been suggested {26860948}. Such gene mutation panels could be useful in refining phyllodes tumour grade as well {31272781}.

Chromosomal abnormalities increase with higher tumour grade. 1q gain and 13q loss are associated with borderline/malignant phyllodes tumours {27362571;27207013}. Deletion of 9p21 and loss of p16 expression are also identified {27207013;23916291;18288784}.

Phyllodes tumours are recently reported to have a distinct methylome, separating them from metaplastic breast carcinoma. In addition, malignant phyllodes tumours show different methylation patterns from non-malignant phyllodes tumours, highlighting the future diagnostic potential of DNA methylation as a classifier for malignancy {38300122}.

Macroscopic appearance

Phyllodes tumours form circumscribed, firm, bulging masses. Many benign tumours can be shelled out surgically owing to their clearly defined margins. The cut surface is tan or pink to grey and may be mucoid and fleshy with cystic degeneration. The characteristic whorled pattern with curved clefts resembling leaf buds is best seen in large tumours, but smaller ones may have a homogeneous appearance. Haemorrhage or necrosis may be present.

Histopathology

Phyllodes tumours typically exhibit an exaggerated intracanalicular growth pattern, with leaf-like projections extending into variably dilated elongated lumina. A pericanalicular pattern can also be seen. The epithelial component consists of luminal epithelial and myoepithelial cells stretched into arc-like clefts surmounting stromal fronds. Apocrine or squamous metaplasia and usual ductal hyperplasia can be present. Atypical ductal hyperplasia, ductal carcinoma in situ, lobular neoplasia, and invasive carcinoma can rarely occur within the tumour {28689011;15743740}.

In benign phyllodes tumours, the stroma is usually more cellular than in fibroadenomas. Stromal cellularity may be higher in the zone immediately adjacent to the epithelium, and it is often referred to as periepithelial or subepithelial accentuation of stromal cellularity. Areas of sparse stromal cellularity, hyalinization, or myxoid change are not uncommon {29449684}, reflecting stromal heterogeneity, which is a characteristic of phyllodes tumours. These fibroadenoma-like areas have been reported in 35.9% of phyllodes tumours {28751520} and may be mistaken for fibroadenomas in core biopsies.. The spindle cell stromal nuclei are monomorphic and mitoses are rare, usually < 2.5 mitoses/mm2 {15743740} (corresponding to < 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area). The presence of occasional bizarre or multinucleated stromal giant cells should not be interpreted as a marker of malignancy {8080361;11358046}. Necrosis and benign lipomatous, cartilaginous, and osseous metaplasia have been reported. The margins of benign phyllodes tumours are usually well delimited and pushing, although very small tumour buds may protrude into the surrounding tissue, and if left behind after surgery, they may be the source of local recurrence.

In the fourth and fifth editions of this series, the diagnosis of malignant phyllodes tumours necessitated the presence of all of the following features: marked stromal nuclear pleomorphism; stromal overgrowth defined by the absence of epithelial elements in one low-power microscopic field (40× magnification: 4× objective and 10× eyepiece) containing only stroma; increased mitoses (≥ 5 mitoses/mm2; ≥ 10 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area); increased stromal cellularity which is marked and usually diffuse; and an infiltrative border (see #51542Table 4.01). This approach aimed to avoid overdiagnosis of malignancy and improve diagnostic consistency. Emerging evidence, however, suggests that requiring all five adverse histological criteria may underdiagnose tumours with metastatic potential. Recent studies have highlighted cases where tumours classified as borderline phyllodes tumours by WHO criteria exhibited metastatic behaviour, emphasizing the need to refine the diagnostic framework. In two studies with 140 malignant phyllodes tumours {38934254;37694517}, rare tumours developed distant metastasis even with two or three of the above features; however, it is unclear whether these tumours were comprehensively sampled, which may have led to other adverse morphological features not being identified.

In a recent consensus review addressing this issue, an international group of breast pathologists with expertise and experience in diagnosing phyllodes tumours proposed that malignant phyllodes tumours can be diagnosed when at least four of the five WHO adverse criteria are present, rather than requiring the full set of adverse histological parameters, supplemented by comprehensive tumour sampling and clinical context. This view balances the risk of underdiagnosis with the need for standardized, reproducible diagnostic practices {40223225}. For now, the recommendation by the WHO Editorial Board is that four adverse criteria are sufficient for the diagnosis of malignant phyllodes tumour. It is important to document which criteria are used to support the malignant grade, including the criterion that does not, emphasizing the importance of complete provision of microscopic details.

Malignant phyllodes tumours are also diagnosed when malignant heterologous elements are present even in the absence of other features {26768026}. Although liposarcoma was traditionally regarded as a malignant heterologous component, there is evidence to suggest that metastatic risk is low when well-differentiated liposarcoma occurs as the sole heterologous element in a phyllodes tumour {27040923}. These abnormal adipocytes within phyllodes tumours lack MDM2 or CDK4 amplifications, in contrast to extramammary well-differentiated liposarcoma {26542423;27040923;26843318}. Therefore, a diagnosis of malignant phyllodes tumour should not be made based purely on the finding of well-differentiated liposarcoma, but also on the basis of other stromal features. Rare pleomorphic liposarcomas in phyllodes tumours have shown more-adverse outcomes {27040923}. Although myxoid liposarcoma has been described in phyllodes tumours, the lack of associated characteristic molecular aberrations calls into question its true existence within phyllodes tumours.

The diagnostic criteria for benign phyllodes tumours are relatively well defined and those for malignant phyllodes tumours are being revised to include the presence of at least four of five, rather than all five, adverse histological features. Borderline phyllodes tumours, however, represent a repository of tumours that are neither benign nor malignant, with their range of histological features being less precisely defined. In view of the consensus recommendation, cases showing as many as three adverse histological features are to be assigned a borderline grade. As the grading of phyllodes tumours requires evaluating the five histological parameters that exist on a continuum with subjectivity in interpretation, inevitable challenges of reproducibility exist as well as tumours with histological appearances straddling different grades {29413655}. Pathological reporting of phyllodes tumours is often incomplete, with full documentation of all histopathological features in only 15.3% of cases, while an additional 35.6% omitted one or two microscopic features {33990927}. As the combinational assessment of all histological parameters is essential for determining the grade of phyllodes tumours, it is important to ensure standardized documentation which may be facilitated by structured reporting protocols.

When a phyllodes tumour shows histological criteria that straddle different grades in an excision specimen, a pragmatic approach is to carefully assess all histological findings in the context of comprehensive sampling that addresses its morphological heterogeneity. In a well-sampled phyllodes tumour with histological features in both the benign and borderline grades, with the presence of more features in the benign category and slight deviation into borderline criteria (e.g. mitoses that exceed the usual number of < 2.5 mitoses/mm2 [corresponding to < 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area], or focal border irregularity), the case may be regarded as favouring benign phyllodes tumour with a comment on which histological parameters veer outside the benign category. If most features belong to the borderline rather than benign category, the case should be assigned as borderline. In the scenario of overlapping features between borderline and malignant phyllodes tumours, if features are predominantly in the borderline category with occasional histological parameters at the malignant end, the case may be deemed borderline with explanatory comments. This approach to tumours with overlapping histological grades requires detailing all microscopic criteria and including explanatory comments to assist clinical management, with multidisciplinary discussion being helpful. The limitations of grade have led to reliance on individual histological criteria that demonstrate correlation with outcomes as an alternative approach, though their relative impact remains controversial {29449684;28751520}. The Singapore nomogram uses weighted parameters to predict likely behaviour {22049216} and has been validated in various patient populations {27466383;24811489;28751520}, although it is unable to distinguish risk of local from risk of distant recurrences.

Appropriate sampling protocols are essential for accurate grading of phyllodes tumours. In large tumours or those in which there are features suggesting malignancy, extensive sampling with at least two sections per 10 mm of the largest tumour dimension is recommended to avoid missing the malignant component, which can be very focal due to inherent heterogeneity {39947480}.

Periductal stromal tumour shows a biphasic pattern of benign ductal elements in a cellular spindle stroma, with spindle cells displaying varying degrees of atypia and mitotic activity. Although periductal stromal tumour lacks the fronded architecture of typical phyllodes tumours, the finding of focal phyllodes features in recurrences {12604890} and the morphological coexistence of periductal stromal tumour in some phyllodes tumours favour periductal stromal tumour being regarded as a subtype of phyllodes tumour.

The main differential diagnosis for benign phyllodes tumour is fibroadenoma with a prominent intracanalicular growth pattern and/or increased stromal cellularity. Phyllodes tumours tend to have more-cellular stroma and well-developed expansile leaf-like processes. In the absence of stromal fronds, the presence of elongated, branching, and cleft-like ducts within cellular stroma may serve as a histological clue to the diagnosis of phyllodes tumour. The degree of stromal hypercellularity for benign phyllodes tumour is difficult to define, but it should be fairly uniform throughout the lesion, or closely accompanying the leafy fronds. Increased stromal cellularity adjacent to epithelium, at the epithelial–stromal interface, is often noticed. Intracanalicular fibroadenomas may also show leaf-like processes, but they are fewer and poorly formed, without increased stromal cellularity. Cellular fibroadenomas may have mitotic activity as high as 3.57 mitoses/mm2 (7 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area), counted in a minimum area of 2 mm2 {29969836}. Although fibroadenomas and benign phyllodes tumours may present similar recurrence risks {18397185;177964;6887274;22049216}, their distinction is important in the core biopsy setting because of differences in subsequent management. In the event of uncertainty, a diagnosis of benign fibroepithelial tumour is preferable, especially in a core biopsy, where radiological–pathological correlation will be helpful {29413655;39947480}.

Malignant phyllodes tumours may be confused with primary or metastatic sarcomas. In such cases, the diagnosis of phyllodes tumour depends on finding residual epithelial structures by extensive sampling, as the sarcomatous overgrowth may obscure and efface the epithelial component. However, the clinical outcomes of primary breast sarcomas and malignant phyllodes tumours appear to be similar {27541020;25387733}. Knowledge of the clinical history may aid in the distinction of primary versus metastatic sarcoma. Metaplastic carcinoma is another differential diagnosis of malignant phyllodes tumour, but an immunohistochemical demonstration of diffuse epithelial differentiation in the former and distinct genetic alterations {29066183} in the latter help to confirm the diagnosis. A recent study found MED12 and SETD2 to be mutated exclusively in malignant phyllodes tumours, not metaplastic breast carcinoma, with PIK3R1 mutations only in metaplastic carcinoma {39154782}, suggesting the role of targeted sequencing in differential diagnosis. Caution must be exercised in interpreting very focal keratin expression and p63 positivity in limited samples such as core biopsies, because positivity has been reported in phyllodes tumours (particularly the malignant ones) {22259180;25046342}.

Cytology

Benign phyllodes tumours produce cellular stromal fragments which may show significant nuclear atypia, and benign ductal epithelial tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells. These features overlap with those of fibroadenomas, and distinction may not be possible {20526738;9951596}. Borderline phyllodes tumours show increasing prominence of large hypercellular stromal fragments showing increasing nuclear enlargement and atypia, moderate to large numbers of dissociated often spindle stromal cells, and a lesser epithelial tissue fragment component {12365219;20094997;28738381;29413655}. Malignant phyllodes tumours show highly cellular smears, scant to no epithelial component, a high degree of stromal cellularity and nuclear atypia with large hyperchromatic and pleomorphic nuclei, and mitotic activity and more dispersed atypical single cells in the background, and appear frankly sarcomatous where the differential diagnosis is a rare primary or metastatic sarcoma {11015981;11827409;15282716}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The presence of TERT promoter mutations favours a diagnosis of phyllodes tumour over fibroadenoma {26437033;27255162;26355235;26832993;29497099}, and it is likely that this will become an adjunctive tool for distinguishing these tumours. A five-gene PCR assay has been developed into a clinical molecular test that can help distinguish phyllodes tumour from fibroadenoma on preoperative core biopsy {26961242}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: expansile stromal fronds and hypercellularity; dominant and exaggerated intracanalicular growth pattern with cellular stromal fronds capped by luminal/myoepithelial cell layers.

Desirable: in the absence of prominent stromal fronded architecture, significantly atypical stromal features within the background of a fibroepithelial growth pattern.

Note: Grading into benign, borderline, and malignant phyllodes tumours depends on assessment of the degree of stromal hypercellularity, atypia, mitotic count, overgrowth, and tumour borders (see #51542Table 4.01).

Staging

Because the stromal component of phyllodes tumours represents the neoplastic component, malignant phyllodes tumours may be staged as sarcomas using the ninth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, Oâ€™Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. ]].

Prognosis and prediction

Most phyllodes tumours are benign. Local recurrences usually occur within 2–3 years of diagnosis, at an overall rate of 12%, with ranges of 7–8%, 13–16%, and 18–25%, respectively, for benign, borderline, and malignant tumours {30617873;36328344}. Recurrences may be of a grade similar to or higher than that of the original tumour, with higher grade reported in 31.5% of cases {22049216}. Margin status at excision appears to be the most reliable predictor of local recurrence, except in benign tumours, where it seems to be less relevant {26743469;28766221;36328344}. Stromal overgrowth, stromal atypia, and mitotic activity are also important, particularly for distant recurrences {36328344;22049216;30617873}.

Axillary lymph node metastases are rare {26768026}. Distant metastases occur in about 2% of all phyllodes tumours and are seen almost exclusively in malignant phyllodes tumours (recently reported to be as much as 35% in malignant phyllodes tumour {37694517;38934254}). They usually occur within 5–8 years of diagnosis. Metastases have been reported in nearly all internal organs, but the lung and skeleton are the most common sites {21069366;22049216;38934254}. Most metastases consist of stromal elements only. Stromal overgrowth, marked stromal cellularity, large tumour size, and malignant heterologous elements are associated with metastases {29127495;38934254}. Clinically actionable mutations in malignant phyllodes tumours may provide treatment opportunities {39191445}.
