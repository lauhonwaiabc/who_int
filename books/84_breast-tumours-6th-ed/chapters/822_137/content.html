8.2.2 Carcinoma in situ

Definition

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) of the male breast is a non-invasive proliferation of cohesive neoplastic epithelial cells confined to the mammary ductal-lobular system exhibiting the spectrum of DCIS observed in the female breast.

Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) is a non-invasive neoplastic proliferation of dyscohesive cells, originating in the terminal duct lobular units, with or without pagetoid involvement of terminal ducts. More than half of the acini in a terminal duct lobular unit are filled and expanded by the neoplastic cells.

Mammary Paget disease (MPD) is an intraepidermal proliferation of malignant glandular epithelial cells in the nipple areolar region.

Subtype(s)

Ductal carcinoma in situ; lobular carcinoma in situ; mammary Paget disease

Localization

Carcinoma of the male breast is almost exclusively localized in the retroareolar region.

Clinical features

Carcinoma in situ of the male breast presents in three different clinical settings with variable symptoms and clinical features: as an occasional finding in gynaecomastia, in association with invasive carcinoma, or as pure carcinoma in situ. Symptom duration ranges from a few months to 8 years {9071896;9827718;29977557}. Pure carcinoma in situ most often presents as a retroareolar mass affecting older men, with a mean age range of 58–65 years {9071896;9827718}, and it can be bilateral {27612041}. Rarely, carcinoma in situ can affect young men and adolescents {9071896}. Large duct involvement is associated with bloody nipple discharge {27721718;29977557;9071896}. In MPD, there is an enlarging eczematous lesion of the nipple–areola complex {27394005;28541881}. Diagnosis by mammography alone can be difficult, and MRI can be helpful in reaching the correct diagnosis {27721718;29977557}.

Epidemiology

Data are limited, but male carcinoma in situ, which is almost exclusively DCIS, is associated with invasive carcinoma in as many as 46.2% of cases in case series {28084333;16138363}. Pure carcinoma in situ is rarer, with recent data indicating it constitutes 10% of all male breast cancers {29808286} and 18% of breast cancers occurring in young and adolescent boys {27650826}. The incidence of carcinoma in situ in gynaecomastia has been reported to be 6.76%, but this is based on a small case series {27612041}. A recent study of a cancer referral centre identified only 20 cases of pure DCIS over a 19-year period {36114939}. MPD accounts for 1.45% of all male breast cancers {27394005}.

Etiology

The etiological factors are similar to those of invasive carcinoma of the male breast and are discussed in section Invasive carcinoma.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is also similar to that of invasive male breast carcinoma (see section Invasive carcinoma). Data on the molecular profile of male carcinoma in situ, which are scant and specifically relate to DCIS, point towards a close relationship between in situ and associated invasive carcinoma, with similar mutations in PIK3CA, TP53, and GATA3 {28084333} and copy-number aberrations for common oncogenes {29203614}. In rare cases, columnar cell–like changes are seen adjacent to DCIS, and invasive carcinoma has shown the same mutation profile {28084333}. X chromosome aneusomy associated with AR copy-number gain has been identified in DCIS {29802469}.

Macroscopic appearance

Pure carcinoma in situ presents as an irregular, sometimes cystic mass {9071896}.

Histopathology

Although the entire spectrum of carcinoma in situ observed in the female breast can also be found in the male breast {28420303}, the vast majority (97.9%) of male carcinoma in situ is DCIS, LCIS being exceedingly rare {28084333}. Most pure DCIS is of low or intermediate nuclear grade {9827718} and of papillary or micropapillary architecture, whereas high-grade DCIS with comedonecrosis is usually associated with high-grade invasive carcinoma. The nuclear grade, and immunophenotype of DCIS usually correlate with the grade and type of associated invasive carcinoma. DCIS seems to be associated with invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST), whereas the rare cases of LCIS are reportedly variably associated with invasive lobular carcinoma, invasive carcinoma NST, or invasive carcinoma of mixed type {28084333}. The ER, PR, and ERBB2 (HER2) status in the in situ component mirrors that of the invasive component {28084333}.

On rare occasions, carcinoma in situ has the features of encapsulated papillary carcinoma and can lack a myoepithelial layer {27303554}. The diagnostic criteria are the same as per female breast cancer. MPD is similar to that in the female breast {27394005;29694313}. Columnar cell–like changes can be associated with DCIS {24267518;28084333} and are characterized by dilated ducts, lined by monomorphic mildly atypical cells with apical snouts. Rarely, microcalcifications and secretions are present {24267518;29977557}.

Cytology

The features of low/intermediate-grade DCIS, with solid, cribriform, and papillary architecture, and of high-grade DCIS are the same as in female patients {8143548}.

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Carcinoma in situ associated with invasive carcinoma is treated similarly to invasive disease. For pure DCIS, the prognosis is good. In one series, 4 of 31 cases recurred: 1 case as DCIS and 3 cases as invasive carcinoma {9071896}. Another series reported a disease-free survival rate of 92.9% with only 1 patient (out of 20 with pure DCIS) developing ipsilateral invasive carcinoma after 14 years {36114939}. It has been suggested that the presence of DCIS adjacent to invasive carcinoma is associated with longer overall survival in the luminal A and luminal B HER2-enriched groups {28084333}.
