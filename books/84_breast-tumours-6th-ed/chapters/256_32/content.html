2.5.6 Invasive papillary carcinoma

Definition

Invasive papillary carcinoma is an invasive carcinoma with fibrovascular cores covered by neoplastic epithelium.

Epidemiology

Invasive papillary carcinoma is extremely rare, accounting for < 1–2% of all invasive breast cancers. However, it is the most common type of rare male breast cancer (2–4%). It typically affects postmenopausal women ≥ 50 years of age {38601769}. Most cases reported as invasive papillary carcinoma in the literature are in fact encapsulated papillary carcinoma or solid papillary carcinoma {19492881;27362568;33462367}, or metastases of carcinomas primary to other sites, especially the ovary {15577686}.

Histopathology

Invasive papillary carcinomas display a frankly invasive growth pattern and are composed of mildly dilated ducts and microcysts containing papillary formations {30758118}. Myoepithelial cells are absent at the periphery and along the papillary stalks. Invasive papillary carcinoma is graded according to the Nottingham grading system. Tubule differentiation should be assessed based on the presence of structures in which there are clearly defined central lumina surrounded by polarized tumour cells. Tumours without (or with < 10%) tubule formation are scored 3 on a scale of 3.

These tumours should be differentiated from invasive micropapillary carcinoma and metastases to the breast from other organs. Invasive micropapillary carcinomas have a distinctive growth pattern characterized by clusters of cancer cells that lack fibrovascular cores and are surrounded by clear stromal spaces. Metastases of ovarian serous carcinoma, pulmonary papillary adenocarcinoma, and thyroid papillary carcinoma can be distinguished from breast carcinoma on the basis of careful morphological assessment and immunohistochemistry for site of origin–specific markers, as needed. Invasive papillary carcinoma is distinct from encapsulated papillary carcinoma and solid papillary carcinoma, which are classified according to their specific morphological features. Additionally, the term should not be used for non-papillary carcinomas arising in association with encapsulated papillary carcinoma or solid papillary carcinoma {19492881;27362568;33462367}.

Cases of high-grade invasive ductal carcinoma with zones of geographical necrosis can sometimes produce a pseudopapillary pattern, which may be mistaken for an invasive papillary carcinoma {35465400}.

Tumours with a growth pattern similar to that of encapsulated papillary carcinoma but displaying high nuclear grade, increased mitotic activity, triple negativity, or ERBB2 (HER2) overexpression have been described {25382726;30004109}. The possibility that these tumours may be pseudopapillary high-grade invasive carcinomas mimicking encapsulated papillary carcinoma needs to be excluded for proper management. On limited material such as core biopsies, this distinction may be challenging, and caution is advised in making a diagnosis of an invasive tumour. Evaluation of more representative tissue in excisional specimens with relevant adjunctive workup is required. Treating these lesions as invasive tumours purely on grade and biomarker (ER, PR, HER2) profile without definitive histological evidence of invasion is inappropriate. The fifth edition Breast tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581. ]] indicated that encapsulated papillary carcinoma with high nuclear grade, increased mitotic activity, and/or a triple-negative or HER2-positive phenotype should be graded, staged, and managed as invasive disease. Data to support this approach are very limited and the editorial board for the sixth edition now recommends that such rare lesions should still be categorized as encapsulated papillary carcinoma (which by convention is staged as DCIS), but with a note or comment enumerating the unusual features. Multidisciplinary review for collective management is recommended.

Cytology

Invasive papillary carcinoma is extremely rare and has a differential diagnosis including invasive micropapillary carcinoma, high-grade invasive carcinoma with pseudopapillary pattern secondary to extensive necrosis, and metastases. A specific diagnosis is not possible on FNAB or other sampling techniques including core needle biopsy.

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma of mammary origin with entirely papillary architecture.

Staging

These tumours should be staged according to their pathological size and the corresponding axillary lymph node status.
