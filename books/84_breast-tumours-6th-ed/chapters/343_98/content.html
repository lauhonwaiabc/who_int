3.4.3 Neuroendocrine tumour

Definition

Neuroendocrine tumour (NET), rare in the breast, is a malignant, well-differentiated epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN), supported by the presence of neurosecretory granules and a diffuse, uniform immunoreactivity for neuroendocrine markers.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: endocrine carcinoma; carcinoid tumour of the breast.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

No specific data are available.

Clinical features

There are no notable or specific differences in presentation from other tumour types. Clinical syndromes related to specific hormone production by breast NETs have not been documented. Serological tests detecting circulating neuroendocrine markers such as chromogranin A are not sensitive or specific enough to identify breast NETs {11820723}. According to the 2023 guidance paper from the European Neuroendocrine Tumour Society (ENETS), SSTR PET/CT imaging is crucial for detecting both primary and metastatic sites in NET {39387873}.

The diagnosis of breast NET should only be made after metastatic NET, solid papillary carcinoma, and invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) with neuroendocrine differentiation have been considered and excluded.

Epidemiology

Breast NETs are extremely rare. In a large series of breast carcinomas, grade 2 histology with > 50% neuroendocrine differentiation was as low as 0.5% {19020716}. In another single multi-institutional series of NENs, as reported in the fifth edition Breast tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581. ]], approximately 70% of cases were designated as NETs and the rest as NECs {36243799}. Most patients are in the seventh decade of life {10839613;11762336;26114478}.

Etiology

NETs are likely to have the same etiology as other ER-positive breast carcinomas.

Pathogenesis

The histogenesis of primary NETs of the breast is not clearly defined. Unlike in other organs, such as the lung, prostate, and gastroenteropancreatic system, neuroendocrine cells have not been consistently found in normal breast tissue {4025508}. Cases of ductal carcinoma in situ with predominant neuroendocrine features have been described {2579885;8712293}, either in the pure form or in association with an invasive component, suggesting a possible progression from in situ to invasive NET.

Primary NETs in the breast probably result from an early divergent differentiation of breast cancer stem cells into both neuroendocrine and epithelial lines. Prominent neuroendocrine differentiation is seen in distinct types of mammary carcinomas, such as solid papillary carcinoma and the cellular subtype of mucinous carcinoma, and it is also observed in a proportion of in situ (neuroendocrine ductal carcinoma in situ) and invasive carcinomas of other histological types. In addition, the transition from NET to NEC has not been reported in the breast, suggesting NETs and NECs could develop via different pathogenetic pathways as described in pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm (PanNEN) {30140036}.

A study that uses the terminology of NEC grade 2 (not small cell carcinoma) in the breast has reported a repertoire of somatic mutations intermediate between those of luminal A and luminal B breast cancers, with a significantly higher frequency of FOXA1, ARID1A, and DCHS2 mutations, while PIK3CA mutations were significantly less frequent than in luminal A cancers {27925203}. In addition, genomic and transcriptomic similarities between NECs and mucinous carcinomas of the breast have been described {27925203}.

Macroscopic appearance

NETs of the breast can grow as infiltrating or expansile tumours.

Histopathology

The histological patterns of NETs are similar to those described for NETs in non-endocrine organs. These include islets and nests (solid islet structure); anastomosing trabeculae and bands (trabecular structure); and tubules, acini, and rosette-like structures (glandular structure) {10839613;33032264}. Mixed patterns, often observed in typical NETs of other organs, are rarely present in the breast. Necrosis is usually absent and when present is spotty and may be associated with atypia. Cells are monomorphic, polygonal in shape, and medium sized, with abundant eosinophilic, sometimes granular cytoplasm and round, regular nuclei with dispersed, granular, salt-and-pepper chromatin usually with inconspicuous nucleoli. A NET, if deemed to originate in the breast after careful workup, should be graded according to the Nottingham grading system. The overall diagnostic agreement in distinguishing NET from solid papillary carcinomas and carcinomas NST with neuroendocrine differentiation is moderate (Cohen’s kappa: 0.4532) {36243799}. Solid papillary carcinoma and cellular mucinous carcinoma expressing neuroendocrine markers are distinct breast neoplasms and cannot be classified as NETs.

It is important to emphasize that metastatic well-differentiated NETs (previously referred to as carcinoids) or moderately differentiated NECs, in particular those of lung or gastroenteropancreatic origin, should be excluded before making a definite diagnosis of primary NET of the breast {37485220}. The presence of ductal carcinoma in situ, axillary node metastases, and the absence of a history of an extramammary primary NEN support the diagnosis of primary breast NET. Expression of ER (particularly using the 6F11 clone) and PR is not unique to the breast and gynaecological tract, and should not exclude a primary neuroendocrine lung neoplasm {19061285}.

Expression of chromogranin proteins and/or synaptophysin is the characteristic feature of NETs. INSM1 has been proposed as an accurate indicator of neuroendocrine differentiation. Similarly to standard neuroendocrine markers, INSM1 is unable to distinguish NET and NEC from the other breast cancer histotypes with neuroendocrine differentiation {35690220;34008122}. ER and PR are expressed in the majority of tumour cells {36243799}. NETs lack (ERBB2) HER2 expression and frequently express AR and GCDFP-15; the Ki-67 proliferation index is low {26114478;11504836}.

Cytology

This exceedingly rare tumour shows small nests and sheets of relatively uniform polygonal to cuboidal cells with distinct cell membranes, round nuclei with salt-and-pepper chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and eccentric finely granular cytoplasm, in an often finely granular background. Dispersed cells may be plasmacytoid or spindle, and some tissue fragments may have central branching thin fibrovascular strands. Neuroendocrine markers confirm the NET but the first consideration is to exclude a metastasis {9769124;31163417}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: non–high-grade neoplasm with histological features and immunoprofile characteristic of neuroendocrine differentiation with exclusion of solid papillary carcinoma, invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation, and metastasis.

Desirable: coexisting ductal carcinoma in situ.

Staging

NETs are staged according to the criteria for other types of invasive breast carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour stage and Nottingham histological grade, which encompass mitotic counts, are used as the main prognostic parameters {11762336}. NETs have a comparable outcome to invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation at similar stage and grade {36243799}. There are no data from prospective clinical trials on the optimal management of NETs of the breast as they are so rare, and these tumours have to be treated with the same strategy used for other types of invasive breast carcinoma to avoid unjustified treatment de-escalation, with a potential impact on patients’ health {36243799}.
