Index > Breast Tumours (6th ed.)

1.1 General introduction

The classification of breast tumours continues to evolve. The breast volume of the sixth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours is an update of the fifth edition, published in 2019 [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581. ]], and integrates new knowledge from research into global practice.

Alternative classification systems have been proposed, based on the mammographic anatomical site of tumour origin {35151954}; however, to date, these lack a substantive evidence base and have not achieved comprehensive clinical adoption. The WHO classification of breast tumours is the only recommended tumour classification system of breast tumours included in the reporting guidelines by international consortiums for cancer reporting {38719547} [[International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) Internet. Sydney (Australia): ICCR; 2025. Available from: http://www.iccr-cancer.org. ]], laboratory accreditation societies [[College of American Pathologists Internet. Northfield (IL): College of American Pathologists; 2025. Cancer Protocol Templates cited 2025 Jul 2. Available from: https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cancer-reporting-tools/cancer-protocol-templates. ]], and professional pathology organizations [[Deb R, Pinder SE, Boyd C, et al. G148 Dataset for Histopathological Reporting of Breast Disease in Surgical Excision Specimens of Breast Cancer. London (UK): Royal College of Pathologists; 2024 Nov. Available from: https://www.rcpath.org/profession/guidelines/cancer-datasets-and-tissue-pathways.html. ]].

The descriptions of breast tumours follow the familiar systematic approach of previous volumes, with the content organized in sequence from benign epithelial proliferations and precursors, through benign neoplasms, to in situ and invasive breast carcinoma, followed by fibroepithelial and nipple tumours, mesenchymal and haematolymphoid neoplasms, tumours of the male breast, and genetic tumour syndromes. The ICD-O topographical coding for the anatomical sites covered in this volume is presented in #48411Box 1.01.

Within the Introduction, two new sections have been added: a section relating to section small diagnostic samples (e.g. core biopsies) and an introduction to section digital pathology and artificial intelligence (AI), which are rapidly being integrated into diagnostic practice. An expanded section molecular classification has been added to one of the sections on section epithelial tumours.

Within the invasive carcinoma overview, the emerging roles of stromal response and the tumour microenvironment are discussed, with specific attention on tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and their impact on prognosis and response to chemotherapy. The Ki-67 proliferation index is not a parameter that is routinely evaluated in invasive breast carcinoma in all parts of the world, but its role in determining molecular subtypes is acknowledged.

An important change initiated in the fifth edition is the conversion of mitotic count from the traditional denominator of 10 HPF to a defined area expressed in mm2. This serves to standardize the true area over which mitoses are enumerated, because different microscopes have HPFs of different sizes. This change will also be helpful for anyone reporting using digital systems. The score thresholds for mitotic counts based on the diameter of the HPF and its corresponding area are presented in #48123Table 1.01.

There has been an increasing interest in papillary neoplasms; however, data on outcome remain a problem, creating considerable difficulties with clinical management. Deciding whether solid papillary and encapsulated papillary carcinomas are invasive has been a challenge, without robust data on outcome, limiting our ability to make decisions on classification that impact management. The sixth edition editorial board have discussed this and, owing to a lack of outcome data, have pulled back on the use of grade and biomarker profile (ER, PR, ERBB2 [HER2]) introduced in the fifth edition to push an encapsulated papillary carcinoma–like lesion into an invasive category.

In the section on section adenosis and sclerosing lesions, polymorphous adenocarcinoma has been removed as there are only rare case reports (considered as a low level of evidence) in the literature, and acinic cell carcinoma has been renamed acinic cell–like carcinoma, as it does not show classic gene rearrangement and NR4A3 protein overexpression, and has a molecular profile more similar to conventional triple-negative breast cancer and to carcinomas arising in association with microglandular adenosis.

While the classification of lobular cancers remains broadly similar to that in the fifth edition, there has been an increasing interest in morphological patterns, in particular the recognition of the solid papillary subgroup of the solid pattern of invasive lobular carcinoma. Increasing knowledge of its biology may lead to refining the classification system in the coming years.

Although neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are allocated their own sections, harmonized with those of other organ systems on the basis of an International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and WHO consensus statement {30140036}, it must be emphasized that true primary neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the breast are very uncommon. Many breast tumours that display neuroendocrine differentiation belong to recognized tumour types, such as hypercellular mucinous carcinoma and solid papillary carcinoma of both in situ and invasive forms. Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) does arise in the breast, often admixed with invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST). For well-differentiated NETs, it is prudent to exclude metastasis from another site. It is recommended that the classification of breast tumours displaying neuroendocrine differentiation be based on the recognizable morphological tumour type, be it invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), invasive mucinous carcinoma, or solid papillary carcinoma {24845113}. In this edition, the approach to classification of NENs remains essentially similar to that in the fifth edition, with an algorithm to aid diagnostic evaluation added. The previously termed “large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma” is regarded as an IBC-NST with neuroendocrine differentiation, with the only true primary neuroendocrine neoplasms in the breast being the vanishingly rare NET and the uncommon SCNEC. The independent prognostic impact of neuroendocrine expression in conventional IBC is uncertain. Use of immunohistochemistry to ascertain neuroendocrine expression in conventional IBC is discouraged.

Revised criteria for malignant phyllodes tumours are proposed, in light of recent studies that found metastatic behaviour in phyllodes tumours that did not meet all five adverse histological criteria of marked stromal atypia, marked stromal hypercellularity, ≥ 5 mitoses/mm2 (≥ 10 mitoses per 10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area), the presence of stromal overgrowth, and permeative tumour borders, as required in the fifth edition of this volume {38934254;40516609;37694517}. An international expert consensus recommended that malignant phyllodes tumours be diagnosed when at least four of the five adverse histological features are observed, acknowledging the rigidity and stringency of the previous definition, and representing a measured stance between the potential for overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis of malignancy in these challenging tumours {40223225}.

Phyllodes tumours with overlapping histological features between grades are also addressed, with pragmatic guidance provided for diagnosis.

In the sixth edition, classic adenomyoepithelioma incorporates benign and atypical forms, with atypical adenomyoepithelioma displaying cytological atypia, increased mitotic activity (as many as 5 mitoses/mm2, equating to 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area), and occasional necrosis. A suggestion to subtype malignant adenomyoepithelioma according to the characteristics of the malignant components was not endorsed by the editorial board as there were no data as yet on its clinical relevance, underscoring the commitment by WHO to classify tumours based on published, robust evidence. The use of the term “epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma” is not advocated, and malignant elements need to be described morphologically in malignant adenomyoepithelioma.

Mesenchymal tumours, haematolymphoid tumours, and genetic tumour syndromes are covered in dedicated sections, in alignment with the approach being taken throughout this sixth edition of the series.

The discussion of mesenchymal tumours of the breast in this edition has been limited to entities most relevant to the breast. Discussion of mesenchymal lesions that may be seen in the breast but that are also seen in other locations (e.g. nodular fasciitis, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, peripheral nerve sheath tumours, smooth muscle tumours, and adipocytic tumours) has been removed, and the reader is referred to the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588. ]] for a discussion of these lesions.

The section on section genetic tumour syndromes has been updated and terminology aligned with the Genetic tumour syndromes volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Genetic tumour syndromes Internet; beta version ahead of print. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2023. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 14). https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int/chapters/67. ]] and includes polygenic risk scores, which have been increasingly proposed to help guide risk assessment, including in breast screening.

Tumour classification is a dynamic process, integrating multiple sources of information and experience and evidence, which, within the context of multidisciplinary discussions, drives patient management. Accurate and standardized classification is also fundamental to accurate epidemiology data regarding changes and trends in disease prevalence.
