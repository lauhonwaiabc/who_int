5.2.3 Mammary Paget disease

Definition

Mammary Paget disease (MPD) is an intraepidermal proliferation of malignant glandular epithelial cells in the nipple areolar region.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: Paget disease of the breast; ductal carcinoma in situ involving nipple skin; Paget disease of the nipple.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

MPD involves the epidermis of the nipple and/or areola and may extend into the adjacent periareolar skin. Usually MPD is unilateral; bilateral synchronous and metachronous involvement may occur rarely {223445}. MPD may also involve a supernumerary nipple {7962832}.

Clinical features

Clinically, a large proportion of patients (42–98%) present with eczematous or erythematous changes of the nipple with or without nipple discharge and retraction. Ulceration secondary to replacement of the normal squamous cells by MPD cells may occur and mimic ulcerating invasive carcinoma. Rare cases present as a pigmented macule resembling a melanocytic lesion {19384061}. A palpable mass in the ipsilateral breast is present in 50% of cases and indicates an underlying invasive carcinoma.

MPD may represent recurrence of a prior breast carcinoma {15469484;24583835}.

Mammography of the ipsilateral breast reveals abnormalities suspicious for malignancy in 32–67% of cases {17952590;12115309;17028984;35242682}; breast and/or nipple calcifications are usually evidence of carcinoma {35242682}. Ultrasound usually shows nipple enlargement and increased vascularity (> 80% of cases), but may also show asymmetry, thickening, or flattening. MPD appears as abnormal nipple enhancement on MRI in about 85% of cases {38641371;15905723}, but cannot be distinguished reliably from nipple adenoma, although the latter usually is not associated with abnormal ipsilateral breast findings.

Epidemiology

The reported frequency of MPD varies depending on the stage of the underlying carcinoma, and whether only clinical MPD or also subclinical / only microscopic MPD is included. Clinical MPD was identified in 1.5% of all breast carcinomas in one case series {2434164} and microscopic MPD was present in 0.5% of cases in a more-recent series {17952590}. In recent decades the incidence of clinical MPD associated with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and/or invasive carcinoma has decreased in the US, with a relative increase of MPD alone (~12–15% of all MPD cases) {16933329}. Data on the incidence of MPD in low- and middle-income countries is limited, but MPD, including clinical MPD, is probably more common.

MPD almost exclusively affects women, with < 1% of reported cases occurring in men {8955252}. Described ages range from 26 to 88 years (mean: 54–63 years), and 20–30% of affected women are premenopausal. Patients with MPD and invasive carcinoma tend to be younger than patients with MPD and DCIS, while patients with MPD alone are the oldest group {16933329}.

Etiology

No specific risk factors for MPD have been identified, beyond those recognized for breast cancer in general {17028984}.

Pathogenesis

Two non-exclusive pathogenic theories have been proposed.

Epidermotropic theory: MPD occurs by retrograde extension of underlying DCIS, with or without associated invasive carcinoma, through contiguous spread along the lactiferous ducts all the way into the epidermis {35626023}.

Intraepidermal origin: In the absence of ipsilateral carcinoma, MPD may arise from Toker cells. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that in rare cases MPD cells have been found to be genetically different from the cells of the underlying breast carcinoma {14691919}. Atypical Toker cells have also been described {18614197}.

Macroscopic appearance

The nipple usually shows erythema or frank erosion that may also involve the areola and adjacent skin (clinical MPD) or may appear normal (subclinical MPD). Superficial scaling and crusting noted during physical examination may be removed during preoperative cleaning and the nipple may appear normal upon gross examination of the surgical specimen.

Pigmented MPD presents as a dark macule without the scale or crust seen in the more common type.

Histopathology

The histological hallmark of MDP is the presence in the epidermis of epithelial cells with abundant weakly eosinophilic or pale cytoplasm, and large vesicular nuclei, often pleomorphic, with prominent nucleoli and variable mitotic activity. The MPD cells are scattered singly or in clusters or form small acinar/glandular structures throughout the epidermis (pagetoid spread) {17376174;28525420}. The epidermis is often hyperkeratotic and acanthotic, especially in longstanding lesions. The underlying dermis shows a lymphocytic infiltrate. Skin adnexa can also be involved. MPD cells contain intracellular mucin in 40% of cases {8391688}, and may contain melanin due to phagocytosis (a possible diagnostic pitfall {19384061}). This finding may occur in patients without clinical skin pigmentation. The rare cases of pigmented MPD presenting clinically as a dark black macule resembling melanoma are associated with a marked increase of non-neoplastic melanocytes in the epidermis and adjacent dermis {29490872;25473229}.

Invasion from the epidermis into the dermis has been reported in 4–8% of cases of MPD {22895267;23276177;25288235;29431468}. It consists of clusters or isolated MPD cells invading into the superficial dermis with clear separation from any underlying invasive breast carcinoma, if present {22895267}. In MPD with superficial dermal invasion, a prominent dermal lymphocytic infiltrate is often present.

MPD usually is associated with an underlying carcinoma, which is most commonly high-grade invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST) (53–64%) or high-grade DCIS (24–43%). MPD without underlying carcinoma is rare (reported incidence: 0–13%) {18240020;16933329;12115309;20678619;15915373}.

Subclinical/microscopic MPD may be an incidental finding in a mastectomy specimen for DCIS or invasive carcinoma; this scenario accounted for 15% of cases of MPD in one series {9704964}.

Rarely, carcinoma cells may involve the epidermis of the breast skin outside the nipple, and recurrence of breast carcinoma resembling MPD has been reported secondary to core biopsy needle track seeding {26395914;32395381;36896205}.

Immunohistochemistry

MPD cells usually express low-molecular-weight cytokeratins (CK7 and CAM5.2); approximately half of the cases express GCDFP-15. Variable expression of CEA, EMA, S100, p53, and GATA3 has been reported {26944832;17471116;11064666}; CK20 is negative. Rare CK7-negative cases retain expression of GATA3 {26944832}. One study found TRPS1 expression in all MPD cases examined {36808637}. However, of note, skin adnexa can also be TRPS1-positive. MPD cells usually have the same immunoprofile as the underlying carcinoma {30124327;12777994;11455553}.

ERBB2 (HER2) is overexpressed in 80–90% of MPD cases; ER is expressed in 10–40%, PR in 0–30%, and AR in nearly 90% {18240020;17952590;17471116;20955381;15920545}.

Differential diagnosis

The histological differential diagnosis includes squamous cell carcinoma in situ (Bowen disease), and superficial spreading melanoma / melanoma in situ. The latter two malignancies are exceedingly rare in the nipple of female patients, but relatively more common in male patients.

Squamous cell carcinoma can express GATA3, but usually is positive for high-molecular-weight cytokeratins and p63, and negative for low-molecular-weight cytokeratins.

Melanoma is usually positive for S100, HMB45, melan-A, SOX10, MITF, and PRAME, and negative for keratins. One group has reported expression of PRAME in 50% of HER2-positive invasive breast carcinomas {38219495}, but there is no data on MPD. Rare cases of clinically pigmented MPD contain melanin pigment, usually in isolated dendritic melanocytes, thus raising the possibility of melanoma. These isolated cells express melanocytic markers (melan-A [MART1], SOX10) while the surrounding MPD cells express CK7, EMA, and HER2 {19384061}. In the rare cases of CK7-negative MPD, a panel of antibodies for other low-molecular-weight cytokeratins and melanoma markers is helpful {26944832}.

Toker cells, Toker cell hyperplasia, and atypical Toker cells must be considered, especially in the absence of clinical symptoms of MPD or an underlying mammary carcinoma. Toker cells are small cells with scant clear cytoplasm, usually located in the basal layers of the epidermis. They can be single or in small clusters, and may rarely form monostratified glands devoid of myoepithelium that project into the superficial dermis {4313654}. Toker cells express CK7 and CAM5.2 but differ from MPD cells in that they are small, lack malignant nuclear features, and are ER-positive and HER2-negative. Atypical Toker cells have been reported, showing only faint HER2 staining {18614197}.

MPD should also be differentiated from direct invasion into the epidermis and nipple by an underlying breast carcinoma.

Cytology

Scrape or imprint preparations have been used {35965905}, but not widely. Interpretation can be challenging, especially in cases with nipple erosion or pigmented lesions. Large cells, with a high N:C ratio and hyperchromatic nuclei, are present admixed with keratinous debris, small squamous tissue fragments, and necrosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HER2 is amplified in many cases of MPD, with tumour cells genetically similar to the underlying carcinoma cells in 80% of cases {14691919}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: high-grade adenocarcinoma cells scattered singly or in small clusters within the nipple/areolar epidermis.

Desirable: usually positive for low-molecular-weight cytokeratins and HER2.

Prognosis and prediction

MPD is often associated with an underlying carcinoma (95% of cases), and its prognosis depends on tumour stage {18240020;20678619}. The 5-year overall survival rates of MDP are 94–98% and 73–93% in the presence of DCIS and underlying invasive carcinoma, respectively {35626023}. Studies suggest that MPD with superficial invasion into the dermis is not associated with poor prognosis, with no reported recurrences or deaths {22895267;23276177;25288235;29431468}. Therefore, distinction of MPD with dermal invasion from skin involvement by locally advanced breast cancer is important because the latter has a worse prognosis. Mastectomy used to be the standard surgical modality in cases of MPD alone, but breast conservation with central lumpectomy and removal of the nipple–areola complex appears to be an alternative option with similar outcomes {16933329}.
