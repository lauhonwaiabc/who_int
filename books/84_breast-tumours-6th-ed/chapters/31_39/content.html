3.1 Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction

Epidemiology

Aetiology

/p>

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed female cancer, accounting for approximately 2.3 million new cases worldwide in 2022 and 23.8% of all female cancers. It is the leading cause of female cancer death worldwide with approximately 666 000 deaths in 2022 [[Ferlay J, Ervik M, Lam F, et al. Global Cancer Observatory: Cancer Today, v1.1 Internet. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024 cited 2025 Jun 12. Available from: https://gco.iarc.who.int/today. ]]. BC accounts for 11.6% of cancers in both sexes combined, i.e. it is the second most common cancer overall {38572751} [[Ferlay J, Ervik M, Lam F, et al. Global Cancer Observatory: Cancer Today, v1.1 Internet. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024 cited 2025 Jun 12. Available from: https://gco.iarc.who.int/today. ]].

Incidence rates of BC have been increasing in most low- and middle-income countries in recent decades. In high-income countries, such as the USA, Canada, the United Kingdom, France, and Australia, incidence rates decreased in the early 2000s, which was partly attributed to decreased use of postmenopause hormone treatment after publication of the Women’s Health Initiative trial linking postmenopause hormone use to increased BC risk {26359465;12117397;38572751;39026460}. However, with ageing populations, the global burden of this disease increased overall.

BC incidence varies geographically: it is four times higher in countries in Europe and in North America and Australia / New Zealand than in south and central Asia and middle Africa {38572751} [[Ferlay J, Ervik M, Lam F, et al. Global Cancer Observatory: Cancer Today, v1.1 Internet. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024 cited 2025 Jun 12. Available from: https://gco.iarc.who.int/today. ]]. BC mortality rates also vary according to the Human Development Index (HDI): 11.3 versus 15.3 per 100 000 in high-HDI and medium- to low-HDI countries, respectively. Furthermore, the proportion of young patients (< 35 years of age) varies in Asia from about 10% in high-income countries to up to 25% in low- and middle-income countries {17387549}. In high-HDI countries, women are usually diagnosed in their sixties. Chinese women are usually diagnosed in their forties {38204449}. Age-adjusted incidence rates for BC subtypes vary. Hormone receptor–positive / ERBB2 (HER2)-negative is the most common subtype in the US with 70% of all cases showing this hormone pattern, compared with 11% showing hormone receptor–negative / HER2-negative, 9% hormone receptor–positive / HER2-positive, and 4% hormone receptor–negative / HER2-positive patterns [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program Internet. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2018. Cancer Stat Facts. Female Breast Cancer Subtypes cited 2025 Jun 12. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/breast-subtypes.html. ]].

Etiology

The etiology of BC is multifactorial. Most studies point to ageing, hormones, diet, reproductive factors, and genetics as general BC risk factors. From descriptive epidemiological data it has emerged that BC is attributable to common lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption, obesity, and physical inactivity {16298215}. The disease occurs more frequently among women who have an early menarche, remain nulliparous, or have few children and an older age at first delivery {23084519;37430191}. The proposed mechanisms to explain the link between these factors and the increased risk of BC are the following: altered sensitivity of the mammary gland to later hormonal exposures; reduction in the number of stem or progenitor cells and, consequently, elimination of targets for malignant transformation; and changes in gene expression patterns resulting in reduced proliferation and increased differentiation {31548545;21509772;19190634}. There is also evidence from epidemiological studies that exogenous estrogens and progestogens play an important role in the development of BC, as diethylstilbestrol, the estrogen–progestogen combined oral contraceptive, and menopause therapy are established BC risk factors {23189749}. BC incidence rates increase more steeply with age before menopause (by ~8% per year) than after (~2% per year) {11092437}, when ovarian synthesis of estrogen and progesterone ceases, and ovarian production of androgens gradually diminishes.

The consumption of alcohol has been consistently associated with a moderate increase in the risk of BC, particularly hormone receptor–positive cancers {9480365;18067133;23193840}. Current evidence suggests increased risk between active smoking and BC {21363864;23193840;39813761}. In addition, one large meta-analysis has reported a link between active smoking and increased BC risk, which is stronger for women who initiate smoking before first childbirth, and it suggested that smoking might play a role in BC initiation {23449445}. The relationship between physical activity and risk of BC has been assessed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), which concluded that higher levels of activity are associated with a reduction in risk {12570341}.

Approximately 10% of all BCs develop in the context of family history. Variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, BARD1, RAD51C, RAD51D, ATM, and CHEK2 show a significant association with BC risk. BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 are associated with a high risk of BC. Mutations in CHEK2 and ATM are associated with a moderate risk {33471974;33471991}. Molecularly, 6% of patients with BC harbour pathogenic variants in hereditary breast and ovarian cancer genes: about half (3%) in BRCA1, BRCA2, and other high-risk genes (e.g. PALB2), and half (3%) in moderate-risk genes (e.g. ATM, CHEK2). The remaining 4% are as-yet unidentified factors that may be environmental, genetic (with polymorphisms identified via genome-wide association studies that are of medium or low penetrance and confer lower risks), or a combination of both {36307055}.

Germline BRCA1 mutations are associated with a risk for triple-negative cancers (ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-negative in 85% of cases), whereas germline BRCA2 and PALB2 mutations are more frequently associated with hormone receptor–positive BCs {25452441;28721639}.

Early menarche, late menopause, postmenopause hormone replacement therapy, nulliparity, and older age at first childbirth are associated with increases in the risk of hormone receptor–positive BC {24477977;26071880;27529149}. Parity may be associated with an increased risk of triple-negative tumours {27307466;26071880}. Longer duration of breastfeeding is associated with a decrease in the risk of all subtypes. A positive association between body mass index and BC risk among postmenopausal women is stronger for hormone receptor–positive cancers. A weak inverse association has been suggested among premenopausal women, in whom higher body mass index is possibly associated with decreased risk of hormone receptor–positive cancers but increased risk of triple-negative cancers {26071880;21191117}. There is some evidence that alcohol, smoking, and physical activity are associated with risk of hormone receptor–positive cancers {28877889}. Specific etiological risk factors are suggested to contribute differently to the risks of developing the various subtypes of invasive breast carcinoma (IBC) {21191117;27307466;28114999}.

A recent increase of early-onset BC has been reported in high-income countries and could possibly be explained by younger age at menarche, oral contraceptive use, nulliparity, older age at first birth, never breastfeeding, central obesity, physical inactivity, alcohol consumption, and fat intake {36068272}.

Pathogenesis

BC molecular alterations accumulate during transformation from a normal cell into a tumoural cell. Single-cell analyses and spatial transcriptomics have recently shed light on the pathogenesis of BC {39567687}. Copy-number changes and aneuploidy were observed in nearly all women. Common events associated with clonal expansions included loss of chromosome X, gain of chromosome 1q, and losses of chromosomes 10q, 16q, and 22. These aneuploid cells mapped to epithelial structures (terminal ductal-lobular units). Some of the copy-number changes were characteristic of ER-positive or ER-negative disease, supporting the idea of distinct cells of origin for ER-positive and ER-negative BCs {39567687}.

The most common molecular alterations in BC are chromosomal copy-number alterations, with few chromosomal fusions observed in rare specific histological types. Recurrent gene mutations in oncogenes (e.g. PIK3CA) or tumour suppressor genes (e.g. TP53) vary across molecular classes, but the tumour mutation burden in BC is in the lower range compared with other tumour types {23945592}.

Multiple linear models of BC initiation, transformation, and progression have been described that are based largely on hormone receptor status, morphology, retrospective molecular analyses, or mouse models. The ER-positive model recognizes flat epithelial atypia, atypical ductal hyperplasia, and ER-positive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) as the non-obligate precursors of invasive and metastatic BC. The ER-negative model recognizes ER-negative DCIS and microglandular adenosis as precursors of ER-negative cancers {21125683}.

At the cell-of-origin level, two leading models accounting for breast carcinogenesis have been described: the sporadic clonal evolution model and the cancer stem cell model. At the molecular level, there is evidence that strongly suggests that BC evolves along two divergent molecular pathways of progression, mainly related to hormone receptors. Molecular data have also demonstrated that ER-positive and ER-negative BCs are fundamentally distinct diseases, and that within ER-positive cancers, histological grade and proliferation are strongly associated with the extent, complexity, and type of genetic aberrations {20500230;25118203}. The first pathway (the ER-positive pathway) is characterized by gains of 1q, loss of 16q, infrequent amplification of 17q12, and a gene expression signature that is predominantly populated with genes associated with the ER-positive phenotype. These lesions express hormone receptors, lack HER2 overexpression and expression of basal markers, and have relatively simple diploid or near-diploid karyotypes. In general, this pathway consists of neoplastic lesions predominantly of the low- to intermediate-grade phenotype, in addition to a small subset (~9%) of morphologically defined high-grade tumours. The second pathway, designated the ER-negative pathway, is most commonly characterized by loss of 13q, gain of 11q13, amplification of 17q12, and a gene expression signature populated by genes associated with cellular proliferation and cell-cycle processes. This pathway consists predominantly of morphologically defined intermediate- and high-grade tumours {20500230}. PIK3CA mutations commonly occur in both pathways, and TP53 mutations are frequent in the ER-negative pathway {23000897}. Published data also suggest that the progression of a low-grade tumour to a high-grade tumour may preferentially occur in BCs of the luminal phenotype {19156836}. The ER-negative BCs include both HER2-positive and HER2-negative groups. In these groups (all of which are mostly high-grade, genetically unstable, and mostly aneuploid), TP53 mutations are common. In the ER-negative, HER2-positive group, PIK3CA mutations are also very frequent, in addition to 17q12 amplification. Important elements of ER-negative, HER2-negative BC biology include high proliferative activity, an increased immunological infiltrate, a basal-like and a mesenchymal phenotype, and deficiency in homologous recombination.

Macroscopic appearance

Most IBCs can be visualized or palpated as a grossly evident mass, with an irregular, stellate outline or nodular configuration. Classically, IBCs are firm or even hard on palpation, and they may have a gritty feel when cut with a knife (although some types of DCIS, such as the comedo type, can have a similar gritty texture and cannot always be easily distinguished grossly from invasion). However, some IBCs, including neoadjuvant-treated cases, may be grossly inapparent and require careful correlation with imaging at the time of gross examination and tissue sampling. Gross evaluation should include review of imaging findings (if performed), so that the number of lesions, expected size of lesions, and expected clips/markers and their locations can inform appropriate tissue sampling [[Allison KH, Mohsin SK, Dabbs DJ. Gross examination of breast specimens. In: Dabbs DJ, editor. Breast pathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. pp. 76â€“102. ]]. Radiography of larger specimens can be helpful in identifying clips, calcifications, and other clues to guide macroscopic examination {4993751;4709078;7066650}.

Ideally, surgical specimens should be differentially inked and serially sectioned into approximately 40–50 mm slices to ensure adequate fixation and detection of smaller invasive cancers {22031313;19792042}. Tissue sampling should be performed such that the T stage of the cancer can be accurately established {22031313;19792042;38719547}. Sampling between closely related gross lesions is also important so that the pathologist can document whether they are truly multiple or one larger cancer, for staging purposes. A cassette map of larger lesions is often required when the extent or size is not macroscopically obvious {19123733;1615916;19123732}. This is especially true after neoadjuvant therapy, because residual cancers can become softer and more difficult to palpate, and only a fibrous scarred area may be present (except in cases of absent or minimal response) {26019189;26205180;17785706}. Another setting that requires extensive tissue sampling is cases of extensive DCIS, so that any foci of invasion can be identified {19123730}.

Macroscopic assessment of skin ulceration, nipple changes, and separate skin nodules (if present) are also important for staging. Distances of grossly apparent cancers from the resection margins should be recorded and close margins sampled for microscopic analysis. Lastly, tissue handling parameters such as the time the specimen was removed by the surgeon, the time it was placed in fixative, and the amount of time between fixation and tissue sample processing should be recorded so that the cold ischaemia time (the amount of time between removal from the body and placement in fixative) and the time in fixative before processing are clear, because these parameters can affect receptor (ER, PR, and HER2) testing, grading, and lymphovascular invasion assessment [[Ellis IO, Al-Sam S, Anderson N, et al. Pathology Reporting of Breast Disease in Surgical Excision Specimens Incorporating the Dataset for Histological Reporting of Breast Cancer. London (UK): The Royal College of Pathologists; June 2016. ]] {29328775}.

Histopathology

IBC has a broad spectrum of histological appearances. Histological features with prognostic relevance in IBC include (1) histological subtype, (2) Nottingham grade (detailed below), (3) angiolymphatic invasion, and (4) associated in situ component. Other features of importance in the histological evaluation of IBC that are potentially relevant to treatment include tumour size, focality, distance to margins, and lymph node status. Additional histological features with potential prognostic relevance, such as tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and neoadjuvant treatment response, are also frequently included in IBC characterization. See the sections below on histopathological features with prognostic relevance for additional details.

It is also important to recognize when the histological features of a breast carcinoma are unusual or discordant with the hormone receptor or HER2 status {29413654}. For example, a low-grade cancer with either a negative ER status or a positive HER2 status would be highly unusual / discordant, and further workup would be recommended to ensure accurate histological typing, grading, and biomarker status. In this section we will describe histological features generally associated with ER and HER2 status. IBCs are organized by their morphological subtypes, including those of no special type (NST).

Histology of hormone receptor–positive breast cancers

Hormone receptor–positive BCs have a spectrum of morphologies and grades. They can range from well-differentiated cancers composed of bland cells forming tubules to poorly differentiated cancers composed of cells with substantial nuclear pleomorphism and more sheet-like growth. However, most BCs that are strongly ER-positive and HER2-negative are in the low- to intermediate-grade spectrum. Because the pathway to ER-positive BCs includes other non-obligate precursor/risk lesions with high ER expression, such as DCIS (predominantly of low to intermediate nuclear grade), atypical ductal hyperplasia, and flat epithelial atypia, as well as lobular in situ lesions, these lesions are frequently identified in the background of ER-positive IBC-NSTs, and they sometimes account for a large proportion of the initial imaging findings.

Low-grade IBC-NST and special types, such as classic lobular, mucinous, tubular, and cribriform, as well as encapsulated papillary and solid papillary carcinomas, are expected to be strongly ER-positive. Conversely, high levels of ER expression would not be expected in some special type IBCs, including metaplastic, adenoid cystic, secretory, and most invasive apocrine carcinomas. When there is discordance between the histological features and ER status, this should prompt verification of both the ER stain validity and the histological type/grade and, if confirmed, reporting with a comment stating that the findings are highly unusual {31928404}.

Of note, cancers with low percentages of ER-positive cells (and HER2 negativity) frequently have histological features more similar to those of high-grade triple-negative cancers {26846986}.

Histology of triple-negative breast cancers

Although triple-negative (hormone receptor–negative, HER2-negative) BCs can also have a spectrum of morphologies (including special types such as adenoid cystic carcinoma and metaplastic carcinoma), the majority of these IBCs are high-grade, with tumour cells displaying high N:C ratios and solid growth patterns. Some triple-negative BCs feature pushing borders, areas of geographical necrosis, and variable amounts of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes {23082819;16341146}. Cancers associated with germline BRCA1 mutations are frequently of this morphology {9701363}. Some contain a central scar and/or a large central acellular zone. Mitotic counts are typically very high (with proliferation indices often > 80%). DCIS is identified less frequently in the background of triple-negative cancers than in hormone receptor–positive tumours {17146782;16429394}.

As noted above, cancers with low percentages of ER-positive cells (and HER2 negativity) frequently have histological features more similar to those of high-grade triple-negative cancers {26846986}.

Histology of HER2-positive breast cancers

HER2-positive BCs are typically of intermediate to high histological grade, and they are frequently associated with background high-grade comedo DCIS. HER2-positive cancers usually have very pleomorphic nuclei and more-abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm than triple-negative cancers, sometimes giving cells a vaguely apocrine appearance. Proliferation is high, but lower on average than in triple-negative cancers (Ki-67 proliferation rates in the 20–60% range). The typical growth patterns are infiltrative cords or solid nests of cells, but single-file or gland-forming patterns are occasionally seen.

Differential diagnosis

IBC should be differentiated from neoplastic in situ lesions, sclerosing lesions (such as sclerosing adenosis and radial scar), and microglandular adenosis. Although most IBCs have an infiltrative growth pattern, more-nested patterns can mimic in situ lesions, such as DCIS. Conversely, DCIS involving a sclerosing lesion can mimic invasive carcinoma. Myoepithelial stains are essential for distinguishing between these lesions, with invasive lesions lacking myoepithelial cells at their periphery. However, the correct interpretation of myoepithelial staining is important, because stromal or vascular staining can mimic a myoepithelial cell layer in some cases. A nuclear myoepithelial stain (e.g. p63) in addition to a cytoplasmic stain (e.g. calponin or SMMHC) can be useful to help avoid pitfalls in interpretation.

Diagnostic difficulties can also be encountered with spindled forms of malignancy in the breast, where malignant phyllodes tumour, primary breast sarcoma, and other sarcomas may be in the differential. These are discussed in more detail in their corresponding sections.

Lastly, although rare, metastatic carcinoma to the breast should be considered when unusual growth patterns are present or there is a previous history or a clinical concern {28645508}. These are discussed in the section section on metastases to the breast.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry is typically performed on an IBC for one of the following reasons: (1) to distinguish invasion from mimics, (2) for more detailed diagnostic characterization (for example, confirming AR status in an invasive apocrine carcinoma), and (3) to test for biomarkers used in treatment decisions.

The immunohistochemistry panels useful for excluding mimics depend on the specific diagnostic differential and are discussed in more detail in the relevant entity-specific sections of this volume. Myoepithelial cell stains are commonly used when confirmation of an invasive process is needed, but there should be awareness that loss may be seen in some non-invasive entities, such as microglandular adenosis, and reduction seen in some sclerosing lesions and in apocrine metaplasia {21263240;26497122;36482276}.

For other diagnostic considerations, such as workup of breast spindle cell malignancies or possible non-IBC neoplasms, immunohistochemistry can be essential in determining the most likely diagnosis (see relevant sections for details). Immunohistochemistry may also assist in characterizing specific types or subtypes of BC.

Immunohistochemistry used for predictive or prognostic biomarker testing purposes requires adherence to specific testing and interpretation parameters and guidelines. The most common of these are ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 testing and PDL1 testing (see Biomarkers and predictive molecular assays in section Prognosis and prediction).

Tumour marker immunohistochemistry and molecular profiling strategies have been developed to assist in primary site location and clinical management of cancer of unknown primaries [[Greco AF. Cancer of Unknown Primary Site: A New Era of Practice-Changing Approaches to Diagnosis, Staging, and Precision Therapy. JCO Oncol Adv. 2024 Nov 12;1:e2400041(2024). doi:10.1200/OA-24-00041. ]]. Using such methodologies, the specific tumour type may be identified in as many as 92% of cancers of unknown primary, which may support the use of organ-specific tumour-type treatment strategies {39231374}.
