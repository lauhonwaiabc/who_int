3.1.6 Molecular classification

Intrinsic molecular classification of breast cancer

Breast cancer (BC) classification using cDNA microarray-based gene expression profiling (GEP) has shown four main BC molecular subtypes – luminal A, luminal B, ERBB2 (HER2)-enriched, and basal-like – that have associations with prognosis and response to treatment {10963602}. The PAM50 assay uses the expression of 50 genes to identify these intrinsic subtypes {22351696;24443618}.

In routine practice, ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 results from immunohistochemical testing can be used as an imperfect surrogate for the GEP-defined intrinsic subtypes as follows {20492696}: (1) luminal A–like (highly positive for ER and PR, HER2-negative, low proliferation), (2) luminal B–like (lower ER and PR than luminal A, high proliferation), (3) HER2-positive, and (4) ER-, PR-, and HER2-negative or triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). There is some heterogeneity within each of these groups noted by other more detailed profiling methods, and additional subgroups can be recognized. Molecular intrinsic classification by GEP is not currently essential for clinical decision-making, but it has helped solidify that there are distinct differences in outcomes for BCs with different underlying biology, and has created a foundation for the development of assays used in specific clinical scenarios as well as those integrating genomic and tumour immune microenvironment archetypes.

Molecular subtype / receptor-based clinical classification of breast cancers
Luminal breast cancer (ER-positive/HER2-negative)

Luminal BC originates from hormone-sensitive luminal progenitor cells and is usually classified histologically as an ER-positive invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST) or a hormone receptor–positive special type such as lobular (classic and other invasive lobular carcinoma patterns), tubular, mucinous, invasive cribriform, or micropapillary carcinoma, or invasive carcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation {18720457}.

Luminal A–like BC accounts for about 30–40% of BCs, is generally low-grade, and has high expression of hormone receptors and low proliferation. In contrast, luminal B–like BCs, which make up 20–30% of BCs, have a more-aggressive clinical profile, typically with higher grade and proliferation and lower expression of PR. Molecularly, luminal A–like tumours often exhibit mutations in PIK3CA (45%) and GATA3 (14%) and alterations in chromosome 16, whereas luminal B–like tumours have a higher frequency of TP53 mutations (29%) and fewer mutations in PIK3CA (29%) and GATA3 (15%), with gains in 8q and 17q and losses in 13q and 16q {23000897}.

Luminal A–like BCs have the best relative prognosis and slowest progression rates. They are typically sensitive to endocrine therapy and seldom benefit from chemotherapy. Luminal B–like BCs are more aggressive and often benefit from treatments beyond endocrine therapy alone, including the addition of chemotherapy and/or cell-cycle inhibitors {38101773}. Because luminal A–like and luminal B–like BCs may be challenging to distinguish based on traditional clinicopathological variables, validated commercial assays help to determine which luminal-like BCs might benefit from the addition of chemotherapy to endocrine therapy (see #51749Table 3.01, and Biomarkers and predictive molecular assays in section Prognosis and prediction).

Luminal BCs have a longer timeline to recurrence/progression than HER2-positive and triple-negative cancers, with late events often occurring decades after initial diagnosis. With disease progression and continued treatment, acquired endocrine resistance can occur by a variety of mechanisms, including (1) loss of ER, (2) selection of clones with ESR1 mutations, (3) deregulation of cell-cycle components, including ER regulatory proteins, and (4) crosstalk between ER and other growth factor receptor pathways {32289273;2612218;26271713}. Additional lines of therapy can often overcome this resistance {35584336}.

HER2-positive breast cancer

HER2-positive/HER2-enriched BCs account for about 8–20% of all BCs. Clinically, HER2-positive BCs often presents as a palpable mass or interval cancer at a younger age and outside of mammographic screening. They can be ER-positive or ER-negative and often have proliferation rates between those of luminal and basal-like BCs (see Triple-negative breast cancer, below). They are typically IBC-NST but can also be micropapillary or pleomorphic lobular carcinomas and some produce mucin. HER2-positive BC is driven by HER2 overexpression due typically to ERBB2 amplification, resulting in aggressive high-grade cancers {38101773}. These cancers have numerous genomic gains and losses, along with frequent mutations in TP53 (72%) and PIK3CA (39%) {23000897}. While most HER2-positive BCs have a HER2-enriched molecular GEP, they can also profile as luminal or basal, and differences exist within the HER2-enriched subtype that may account for different responses to treatment {25139534}.

HER2-positive BCs progress more rapidly than luminal cancers and are most likely to recur in the first 5 years after diagnosis. Prior to the advent of HER2-targeted therapy regimens, patients with these cancers had the worst overall survival. Because of their aggressive behaviour, chemotherapy combined with HER2-targeted therapies (often administered neoadjuvantly) can offer significant survival benefit to patients with this BC type (see Biomarkers and predictive molecular assays in section Prognosis and prediction). A variety of factors can influence treatment response in HER2-positive BC, including tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (see Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes in section Prognosis and prediction), but HER2-positive BCs have one of the highest rates of complete pathological response to neoadjuvant treatment, which is associated with excellent long-term outcomes {29788230;36344672;29233559}. Patients without a complete pathological response can be offered additional therapies to further reduce their residual risk of metastatic progression and an increasing number of options are also available in the metastatic setting that can significantly prolong survival in advanced cases.

Triple-negative breast cancer

TNBCs are aggressive and molecularly diverse forms of BC. They represent about 15% of all BCs but account for 30% of BC deaths {27888421}. Similar to HER2-positive BCs, TNBCs progress more rapidly than luminal cancers and recurrences typically occur within the first 5 years after diagnosis. Chemotherapy and immunotherapies (often administered neoadjuvantly) can offer significant survival benefit. TNBCs also have high rates of complete pathological responses to neoadjuvant treatment, with excellent long-term outcomes when achieved. Patients without a complete pathological response can be offered additional therapies to further reduce their residual risk of metastatic progression. While there is no targeted therapy specific for metastatic TNBC, there are an increasing number of treatment options including the use of antibody–drug conjugates.

These cancers are defined clinically by the absence ER, PR, and HER2 (CK5/6 and EGFR additionally infer a basal-like phenotype and AR positivity a luminal-androgen-like phenotype) {38101773}. TNBC is associated with a younger age, African American ethnicity, and a family history of BC, particularly with BRCA1 mutations. Most TNBCs are high-grade IBC-NST (including those with medullary-like growth pattern) and high-grade metaplastic carcinomas; however, low-grade forms with different outcome associations also exist. Distinct molecular subtypes are seen on molecular profiling within the TNBC group {28736315;21633166}. Although TP53 is frequently mutated, there are no somatic genomic alterations characteristic to TNBC. TNBC has a high tumour mutation burden for BC. High-grade forms of TNBC are more immunogenic than luminal tumours and the presence of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes may have prognostic and predictive implications {29233559} for immunotherapy.

Triple-negative low-grade tumours

While most TNBCs are high-grade with aggressive biology, there is also a disparate group of low-grade TNBCs that includes low-grade forms of metaplastic carcinomas, such as low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma and low-grade fibromatosis-like carcinoma, as well as salivary gland–like cancers, such as classic adenoid cystic carcinoma, low-grade mucoepidermoid carcinoma, secretory carcinoma, and acinic cell–like carcinoma. A distinct subset of low-grade invasive apocrine carcinomas are forms of TNBC, and very rare forms include tall cell carcinoma with reverse polarity and mucinous cystadenocarcinoma (see #52399Table 3.02). Despite their triple-negative phenotype, low-grade TNBCs generally have a more favourable outcome than usual TNBC and some may be adequately treated with surgical excision without any systemic therapy {28736315;34830849}. Low-grade TNBCs generally have quiet genomes and lack the recurrent TP53 mutations in conventional TNBC {28736315;34830849;34458945}, although rarely they can transform into high-grade TNBC, which may require more-aggressive treatment {39856067}.

Integrative cluster classification of breast cancer

The molecular classification of BC has been further enhanced by integrating genomic DNA copy-number alterations with GEP. Ten integrative clusters (IntClust) with distinct molecular features and clinical outcomes have been described, including a relatively pure HER2-positive group (IntClust 5), diverse luminal groups (IntClust 1, 2, and 3), and a basal-like group (IntClust 4 and 10) {22522925}.

The luminal IntClust groups enable more detailed distinction between typical-risk and high-risk ER-positive/HER2-negative cancers and their specific oncogenic drivers, such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway overactivation, FGFR1 signalling, MYC/ER signalling, and FGF / cyclin D1 activation. Patients with the ER high-risk IntClust profiles have similar outcomes to those with HER2-positive profiles. Similarly, distinct molecular subtypes of TNBC can be discriminated using combined RNA and DNA profiling that correlates with differences in clinical outcomes {39779850;22522925;30867590}. Investigation into how these IntClust profiles might be applied clinically is ongoing.

Mutation profiles of breast cancer

Genetic alterations and the subsequent dysregulation of signalling pathways involving hormones (ER, PR, AR), growth factors (HER2, FGFR1), cell-cycle regulation (cyclin D1, CDK4, CDK6, RB1, p53), and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS/RAF/MAPK pathways {23000897;22722201;22608084} underlie BC pathogenesis and progression, each of which are also associated with specific intrinsic subtypes. In BCs, there is a variety of known driver events, including copy-number alterations (e.g. ERBB2 and CCND1 amplifications) and deletions (e.g. CDH1, CDKN2A and/or CDKN2B, PTEN), as well as high-frequency substitution and indel mutations in genes such as TP53, PIK3CA, CDH1, AKT1, and GATA3. Also some BCs, such as TNBCs, can show a mutation signature of homologous recombination deficiency, owing to germline or somatic BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations or BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation {27135926}. This genetic heterogeneity underpins the variable clinical behaviour of BCs and their response to therapy. Mutation profiles have also been shown to differ between primary tumours and metastases, with disease progression often accompanied by mutations in genes associated with treatment resistance, such as ESR1 (associated with endocrine therapy resistance). Both genetic and epigenetic alterations can also have significant subclonal and spatial heterogeneity within and between tumour sites {34183353}.
