2.4.3 Classic adenomyoepithelioma

Definition

Classic adenomyoepithelioma (AME), encompassing benign and atypical forms, is a biphasic epithelial-myoepithelial neoplasm usually characterized by small glandular spaces lined by inner luminal epithelial cells and a proliferation of variably enlarged and clearly noticeable abluminal myoepithelial cells.

Related terminology

Acceptable: adenomyoepithelioma NOS.

Subtype(s)

Benign adenomyoepithelioma; atypical adenomyoepithelioma

Localization

Classic AME can arise in both the breast parenchyma and the retroareolar region {23627458}.

Clinical features

Classic AME predominantly affects postmenopausal women, with a peak age of > 60 years, but a wide age range is reported {23627458}, with rare occurrences in male patients {8389319;9275934}. AME usually presents as a palpable nodule, sometimes reaching a large size (as much as 100 mm). Nipple discharge can occur when large lactiferous ducts are involved {26823903}. Breast cancer screening programmes have led to the discovery of smaller lesions {20808695}. On rare occasions, AME can be associated with genetic syndromes, such as neurofibromatosis type 1 {19561236}, Lynch syndrome {38297508;35707990}, and Carney complex {35769184}.

Epidemiology

Classic AME is a rare breast tumour, but its true incidence is unknown {34877289}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The genetic drivers of AME vary according to ER status. PIK3CA hotspot mutations occur in both ER-positive and ER-negative AME and are present in > 50% of cases. AKT1 hotspot mutations are mostly associated with ER-positive AME, and highly recurrent (> 60%) HRAS p.Gln61 and p.Gly12/Gly13 hotspot mutations may be restricted to ER-negative AME {29739933;31094929;34496925}.

Macroscopic appearance

Classic AME usually presents as a circumscribed nodule with a well-defined outline. On rare occasions, cystic or papillary areas may be present {23627458}.

Histopathology

Classic AME is composed of glandular structures surrounded by a double cell layer: epithelial cells line the glandular lumen while myoepithelial cells form the outer cell layer. Myoepithelial cells in AME exhibit a wide range of morphological features. They often have clear glycogen-rich cytoplasm {6438900} and may display spindle, myoid, or plasmacytoid morphology {2844382}. At low-power magnification, AME typically presents a multilobulated contour with central sclerotic areas {16160470;17357195;2831705;2824328}. AMEs may rarely contain foci of collagenous spherulosis {14693844;20518417}. Small satellite nodules can occur at the periphery of the main tumour and should not be considered indicative of malignancy {16160470;17357195;2831705;2824328;26823903}.

AME exhibits various architectural patterns {1709559;2824328}, including tubular, spindle, lobulated, and papillary, often mixed within the same tumour.

Tubular AME is characterized by a relatively uniform admixture of small ductal structures lined by a luminal layer of epithelial cells surrounded by an outer layer of conspicuous myoepithelial cells {1709559;2824328}. Tubular AME can exhibit infiltrative margins in the absence of any cytological atypia; this type of AME is referred to as adenomyoepithelial adenosis by some authors {21048915}.

Spindle cell AME is defined by the prominent proliferation of spindle-shaped myoepithelial cells surrounding and compressing glandular structures lined by epithelial cells, which can sometimes be difficult to visualize {1709559;23627458}.

Lobulated AME consists of nests of neoplastic cells predominantly of myoepithelial type, surrounded by a dense hyaline sclerotic collagenous matrix material that probably reflects production of excessive basement membrane material {11284321}. The matrix may sometimes be myxoid and even chondroid, reminiscent of that seen in pleomorphic adenoma.

Papillary AME is AME with prominent intraductal papillary growth merging with features of classic papilloma, which is occasionally observed {2824328;23627458}. The papillary component may be observed in dilated ducts at the periphery of the main tumour nodule. The luminal cells may exhibit apocrine metaplasia, which can be identified on H&E staining and further confirmed by immunostaining with the GCDFP-15 antibody. In rare cases, squamous or sebaceous differentiation may be observed, serving as a diagnostic clue to the epithelial nature of the cells {16373236;1709559}.

Classic AME can be further subtyped into benign AME and atypical AME {33829532}. Benign AME may feature as many as 1.5 mitoses/mm2 (3 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area), with no cytological atypia of the component cells. Atypical AME is more mitotically active, with as many as 2.5 mitoses/mm2 (5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2), and exhibits mild to moderate cytological atypia in the epithelial component, myoepithelial component, or both {33829532}. The presence of atypical mitotic figures should raise suspicion for malignant transformation. Focal necrosis can be observed in atypical AME. Although rare, there have been reports of atypical AME presenting with nodal metastases {33829532}. Nonetheless, the incidence of nodal metastases is extremely low, and a diagnosis of atypical AME does not warrant a sentinel lymph node biopsy.

The luminal cells in AME may exhibit variable cytological atypia and form nests and compressed cords simulating carcinoma. In rare cases the extent and degree of epithelial atypia may warrant a diagnosis of ductal carcinoma in situ arising in an AME, which should be reported and managed as ductal carcinoma in situ {20604795;33080708}.

Limited data are available on preoperative core biopsy diagnosis {18239872;21373216;18524618}. AME should be differentiated from other benign and malignant breast lesions with a basal/myoepithelial phenotype. The diagnosis should be based on the classic biphasic epithelial-myoepithelial pattern, with careful attention focused on excluding other diagnoses, including intraductal papilloma, pleomorphic adenoma, and metaplastic breast carcinoma. When a papillary architecture predominates, AME may be difficult to distinguish from intraductal papilloma with myoepithelial cell hyperplasia. The myoepithelial cells in AME are more numerous, plumper, and larger than those seen in intraductal papillomas, normal lobules, and nodular adenosis. In the tubular pattern of AME, haphazardly arranged tubular structures predominate, forming a nodular lesion with prominent myoepithelial cells. The tubules are lined by cuboidal to columnar cells surrounded by hyperplastic myoepithelial cells often with clear cytoplasm, creating an appearance that can closely resemble epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma of the salivary glands. The tubules of tubular AME are larger and more irregular than those of microglandular adenosis. The increased number and size of myoepithelial cells in tubular AME allow it to be differentiated from tubular and sclerosing adenosis and from tubular adenoma.

Morphological overlap between adenoid cystic carcinoma and AME may occur. Although both lesions can express MYB, the MYB rearrangements typically found in adenoid cystic carcinoma are not found in AME {30003572}.

Immunohistochemistry may help to confirm the dual cell composition of AME. However, the myoepithelial cells in AME tend to stain in a variable and unpredictable pattern for the entire range of immunohistochemical myoepithelial cell markers. High-molecular-weight cytokeratins may show a unique and characteristic paradoxical staining pattern in AME, with diffuse positivity in the inner epithelial cells and negativity in the outer myoepithelial cells {25479938}. AME can be focally positive for ER and PR on immunostaining, mainly in the epithelial cells, but the diffuse strong pattern of staining typically encountered in low-grade invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) is not usually seen. In one series of 43 AMEs, a complete lack of hormone receptor expression was reported in 39% of tumours, variously associated with nuclear atypia, necrosis, and/or increased mitotic activity {29739933}, which may represent atypical AME.

Cytology

AME yields moderate to high cellularity with biphasic tissue fragments of uniform polygonal ductal cells in strands and occasional tubules interspersed with myoepithelial cells, which may be polygonal with cytoplasmic vacuoles or spindle, and collagen strands {16544319;29333189}. Stripped nuclei, which are usually spindle, spindle cells with clear or vacuolated cytoplasm, and plasmacytoid myoepithelial cells are present in the background. The nuclei of epithelial cells are ovoid with fine chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and smooth nuclear contours. Some myoepithelial nuclei have grooves or pseudoinclusions. Fragments of sclerotic stroma, some resembling collagenous spherulosis, may be present, as may myxoid stroma. Atypia in nuclei may raise the possibility of the spectrum of AME lesions, and marked intact cell dispersal with nuclear pleomorphism suggests carcinoma or a myoepithelial malignancy {16544319;17924402;21548117}. Atypia in myoepithelial cells, together with increased cellularity, may be an interpretive pitfall that can lead to a false positive diagnosis {21548117}. Immunohistochemical analysis on cell blocks may assist accurate diagnosis and categorization.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a neoplasm of biphasic appearance, with glandular structures lined by an inner layer of epithelial cells and an outer layer of prominent myoepithelial cells.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of classic breast AMEs pursue a benign clinical course, and complete surgical excision is curative. Tumours showing an extensive intraductal papillary component or satellite nodules are more likely to recur. Distant metastases from AMEs lacking significant cytological atypia or increased cell proliferation (< 2.5 mitoses/mm2, equating to < 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area) have been reported {27487934;16948523}. Molecular characterization of these rare metastasizing AMEs with benign histology is awaited. Considering these rare metastatic cases and the potential for malignant transformation {21307156;27494340;23030630}, classic AME may be better regarded as a neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential {26348644}. Molecular characterization of these rare metastasizing AMEs with benign histology is awaited, although whether they were comprehensively sampled or may have possessed atypical features is uncertain. Tumours with histological evidence of malignant transformation are discussed in section Malignant adenomyoepithelioma.
