1.3 Digital pathology and artificial intelligence

Diagnostic pathology, the cornerstone of cancer diagnostics, is gradually shifting towards a digital workflow. Digital technology, an umbrella term for computer-based products and solutions, has been used in pathology for many years. However, the recently introduced digital whole slide imaging technology has transformed and revolutionized many aspects of the field. The term digital pathology is frequently used to encompass both whole slide imaging technology and approaches based on machine learning / artificial intelligence (AI). As a branch of pathology informatics, digital pathology integrates virtual microscopy with computer-based tools. The core principles of digital pathology are described below.

Whole slide imaging

High-resolution scanners convert glass slides into high-resolution digital images, enabling seamless sharing and advanced analytical capabilities with easier archiving. A fully digital diagnostic workflow was first implemented in a few laboratories in 2015 and has since progressed and expanded steadily worldwide {31301690}. Digital diagnosis has been proven to be safe and non-inferior to microscopic diagnostics {28940580;27399211}. A digital workflow has many advantages, particularly when a digital archive is available. These include the ability to work remotely {32699117;32685256}, shorter turnaround times, accurate annotations and snapshots, the elimination of slide searching for meeting preparations, quicker revision of old cases and easier consultation with colleagues, multiple-observer viewing regardless of the location, improved tissue overview and tracking to ensure no tissue fragments are missed, automated linking of images with the laboratory information system to prevent mix-ups, integration with radiology systems, and enhanced digital teaching of students.

Image analysis

Image analysis employs integrated image analyses and AI-based algorithms to detect, quantify, and classify abnormalities in digital tissue images {37580849;34969036;31935669}. A key advantage of AI-based methods is their scalability, allowing them to process vast amounts of data in a relatively short time – potentially surpassing human capability in this regard. In the context of breast cancer, the clinical benefits of digital pathology span a wide range of applications, from cancer detection, subtyping, and grading, to predicting response to treatment or disease trajectory (e.g. patient survival probabilities {37968376}), and capturing molecular alterations, such as the detection of somatic driver mutations {39106449}.

A key part of AI is machine learning, which includes reinforcement, unsupervised, and supervised learning. Deep learning, a subset of AI based on convolutional neural networks, is particularly effective in digital pathology for handling complex data. Several large-scale AI foundation models trained on extensive datasets, enabling them to perform multiple tasks with minimal fine tuning, have been developed {39039250;38778098;39232164} [[Shaikovski G, Casson A, Severson K, et al. PRISM: A Multi-Modal Generative Foundation Model for Slide-Level Histopathology. arXiv:2405.10254 Preprint. 2024 May 16. doi:10.48550/arXiv.2405.10254. ]] [[Vaidya A, Zhang A, Jaume G, et al. Molecular-Driven Foundation Model for Oncologic Pathology. arXiv:2501.16652 Preprint. 2025 Jan 25. doi:10.48550/arXiv.2501.16652. ]]. These models, often based on deep learning architectures, utilizing a vision transformer model, support applications in natural language processing and multimodal learning. Many useful AI algorithms have progressed beyond their initial research phase and are now commercially available. Currently, breast pathology AI applications cover a broad spectrum of tasks encountered in the daily work of the breast pathologist, including biomarker stain quality control, detection of invasive cancer and ductal carcinoma in situ, identification of microcalcifications, detection of metastases in the lymph nodes, counting of mitoses to improve grading reproducibility, and objective assessment of the proportion of Ki-67–positive, ER-positive, and PR-positive cells, and ERBB2 (HER2) and PDL1 scores. However, no single tool currently covers all the elements of a breast pathology report.

Image classification has recently also extended to more-complex tasks, often referred to as advanced computational pathology {33204028;39829845}. AI-driven approaches have reaffirmed known associations between genotype and phenotype {37652006;33763651} and may be further developed to uncover novel prognostic features {37230111;37968376}. Research has demonstrated that AI can determine the molecular trait of driver gene mutations {35122049;35251119;35944502;38491101;39106449}, genome alterations such as tumour mutation burden {38778098}, and homologous recombination deficiency {36516847;39083703}. Additionally, AI is being applied to more specialized tasks, such as breast cancer subtyping {36251776;31226662;39393036} and predicting treatment responses {36896856;38238771;36658418}. Many of these have some form of certification, varying from the CE In Vitro Diagnostic (CE-IVD) mark to In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval. Further AI algorithms (e.g. for tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes {37608772} and lymphovascular invasion) are expected to become available in the near future. The adoption of AI-based tools also holds significant potential for workflow improvements. Combining the power of AI with other patient-related imaging, clinical, and omics data can create a powerful tool to enhance patient care. However, implementation still faces challenges, including the need to address or refine business models as there will be no direct reimbursement for AI use in most countries. Other challenges include scientific and regulatory hurdles, accountability, trust, economic impact, and environmental effects.
