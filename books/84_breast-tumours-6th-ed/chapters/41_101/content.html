4.1 Fibroepithelial tumours and hamartomas of the breast: Introduction

Mammary fibroepithelial tumours are biphasic neoplasms that exhibit proliferation of epithelial and stromal (mesenchymal) components. Neoplastic or malignant transformation in fibroepithelial lesions typically affects the stromal and not the epithelial component. Although epithelial neoplastic changes may occur in fibroepithelial lesions, they are classified separately and treated accordingly. The spectrum of entities among fibroepithelial tumours encompasses fibroadenomas (simple/conventional, cellular, juvenile, myxoid, and complex) and benign, borderline, and malignant phyllodes tumours, including the periductal stromal tumour. Although hamartomas are not strictly fibroepithelial tumours, they are circumscribed lesions with lobular glands and fibroadipose stroma, with some resemblance to fibroepithelial tumours, and are therefore discussed in this section.

Fibroadenomas occur in younger patients than phyllodes tumours do, and they are generally smaller. Owing to hormone sensitivity, fibroadenomas may undergo cyclical size change, rarely cause pain, and may also occasionally experience substantial enlargement during pregnancy, involuting after menopause. Phyllodes tumours are often symptomatic owing to their larger size and can grow rapidly over a short period of time. Hamartomas have a wide age and size range, occurring any time after puberty. Clinically asymptomatic fibroadenomas, phyllodes tumours, and hamartomas can be detected radiologically. Fibroepithelial tumours typically appear as rounded, lobulated masses on mammography, with coarse calcifications often observed in longstanding or involuting fibroadenomas. On ultrasonography, fibroepithelial tumours frequently appear hypoechoic, and phyllodes tumours often show internal acoustic shadows. The imaging features of hamartomas are variable owing to the mixture of fibrous, fatty, and glandular elements {7785651}.

Periductal stromal tumours share histological similarities with phyllodes tumours, exhibiting the biphasic proliferation with the presence of hypercellular and variably atypical, mitotically active stroma surrounding benign epithelium. However, periductal stromal tumours lack a distinct tumour outline or obvious frond-like architecture. The occurrence of more typical phyllodal patterns on recurrence suggests a close relationship between periductal stromal tumour and phyllodes tumour {12604890;25614463}. Consequently, this tumour is regarded as a subtype of phyllodes tumour. Hamartomas are composed of sequestered collections of haphazardly arranged normal lobular structures, stroma, and adipose tissue, often described as “breast within a breast” {25141109;12461066}.

Epithelial changes, including usual ductal hyperplasia, atypical ductal hyperplasia, lobular neoplasia, and ductal carcinoma in situ, and even invasive carcinomas, can be encountered in fibroepithelial tumours {15743740;26670744}. These changes are often thought to occur by chance and are unrelated pathogenetically to fibroepithelial tumorigenesis, as in phyllodes tumours where the stromal component is considered the primary biological driver of behaviour. Nevertheless, evidence suggests crosstalk between the epithelial and stromal compartments, particularly in phyllodes tumours. This is exemplified by consistently higher periepithelial stromal mitotic activity {1327488} and differential epithelial biomarker expression correlating to tumour grade {11920740;22820000}. Cytological assessment of fibroepithelial tumours was once routine, but its utility is limited by common pitfalls. Consequently, preoperative core biopsies have become the standard diagnostic approach {28554890;20526738;11827409;39947480}.

The concept that some phyllodes tumours may evolve from fibroadenomas is supported by molecular analysis {12210075;19276971;28721388;33727697}. MED12 mutations are frequently found in all types of fibroepithelial tumours {26437033;33727697;28721388}, with the MED12-mutant pathway probably playing a role in the progression of fibroadenoma to phyllodes tumour. Malignant phyllodes tumour may also develop de novo through the acquisition of genetic alterations targeting other cancer genes {29043292}.

The mainstay of treatment of fibroepithelial lesions is surgery, although asymptomatic fibroadenomas or hamartomas may be followed without intervention. Benign phyllodes tumours are usually excised. Several studies suggest a low recurrence rate even if margins are positive {16768772;26743469;28766221;36328344;35402210}. Therefore, current practice guidelines endorse surgical excision without specifically obtaining surgical margins for benign phyllodes tumours {39019058}. For borderline and malignant phyllodes tumours, complete excision with negative margins is recommended {24062207;29808288}. The role of adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the treatment of malignant phyllodes tumour has not been established {26768026}.

Classification of fibroepithelial tumours can be challenging owing to overlapping histological findings. Distinguishing the hamartoma from fibroadenoma, and cellular fibroadenoma from the benign phyllodes tumour, and discerning the different grades of phyllodes tumour are often diagnostic dilemmas {35523613}. Accurate grading of phyllodes tumours is important as it predicts biological behaviour, with malignant tumours possessing the potential to metastasize. With recent reports of metastases in phyllodes tumours that did not fulfil all WHO adverse histological criteria for malignancy {38934254;37694517}, there is a move towards revising and refining the diagnostic framework of malignant phyllodes tumours {40223225}.

Core biopsy interpretation of fibroepithelial tumours may also be another diagnostically problematic area that requires close clinicoradiological correlation with the triple approach in a multidisciplinary setting {39947480}.
