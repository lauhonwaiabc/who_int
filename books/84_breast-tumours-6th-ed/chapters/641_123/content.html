6.4.1 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a tumour showing neuroectodermal differentiation and is composed of epithelioid to polygonal cells with copious eosinophilic, distinctively granular cytoplasm.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: Abrikossoff tumour; granular cell myoblastoma; granular cell schwannoma; granular cell nerve sheet tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

As many as 8% of granular cell tumours arise in the breast {6246310}. The most common location is in the upper-inner quadrant near the supraclavicular nerve {15569210}. Skin and subcutaneous involvement is most common, although the breast parenchyma can also be involved.

Clinical features

Representing only 1 in every 1000 breast tumours, granular cell tumours are usually single, but multicentricity has been reported in as many as 18% of patients. These tumours mimic carcinoma clinically (irregular and firm masses) and radiologically (poorly defined or spiculated masses without microcalcifications). Lesions in the skin are firm and flesh-coloured to red, and they can cause skin retraction, nipple inversion, and (more rarely) pectoral fascia involvement. Exceptionally, granular cell tumours can show malignant change {9669341;32089415}. Colocalization with ductal carcinoma in situ has also been reported {12033967}.

Epidemiology

Granular cell tumours of the breast are rare neoplasms that usually arise in women, over a wide age range (19–77 years) {21732779;39153762}. Children and men are more rarely affected {30842135}. Presentation of the tumour has been described with coincidental breast carcinoma or associated with mastectomy scars {10782152}. In case series, the driver genes described as most frequently mutated in granular cell tumours are ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, and the fact that these genes map to the X chromosome may explain the higher prevalence in women {30166553;30597645}.

Etiology

There is limited information available on the etiology of granular cell tumour of the breast. Rarely, these tumours have been described in association with other conditions, including Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome {12833416}, neurofibromatosis type 1 {2166484}, Noonan syndrome {22915371}, and LEOPARD syndrome (multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, retardation of growth, and sensorineural deafness) {19054014}, but most cases, including multifocal presentations, are sporadic.

Pathogenesis

Genetic profiling has revealed frequent loss-of-function mutations in ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, encoding vacuolar ATPase H+ components that regulate endosomal pH. Much less frequently, genes encoding other vacuolar ATPase H+ components are mutated {30166553;30597645}. These mutations result in impaired vesicular acidification, altered distribution of endosomes, and massive accumulation of intracytoplasmic vesicles, in particular early and recycling endosomes. Although these alterations have been shown to be oncogenic, the mechanistic basis of ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2 loss-of-function mutations remains to be fully elucidated {30166553}.

The genomic profile of malignant granular cell tumours is characterized by a relatively low mutation burden, key inactivating mutations in ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, and occasional mutations in BRD7 (a tumour suppressor candidate) and GFRA2 (a receptor tyrosine kinase pathway gene) {37958362;27148567}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours are homogeneous and white or tan in colour, show regular or infiltrative borders, and can reach as much as 50 mm in diameter {15569210;11348331}.

Histopathology

Granular cell tumours have an infiltrative growth pattern, with poorly defined borders. The tumour is composed of sheets, clusters, and trabeculae of large, round to polygonal cells with abundant, eosinophilic, and distinctly granular cytoplasm. The cell borders are indistinct and appear syncytial. The centrally located nuclei are usually small, uniform, and hyperchromatic, and more rarely vesicular with prominent nucleoli. The finely granular cytoplasm results from massive accumulation of lysosomes, punctuated by larger intracytoplasmic granules surrounded by clear haloes (pustulo-ovoid bodies of Milian), which are often PAS-positive/diastase-resistant. Perineural and perivascular involvement is frequent {23908453}. Mitoses are generally scarce {20074934}. These tumours can elicit exuberant pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia when situated in the dermis {6246310}. Malignant granular cell tumour (which is exceedingly rare) is recognized by aggressive histological features, including significant nuclear pleomorphism, abnormal mitotic figures, a high mitotic count, prominent nucleoli, tumour necrosis, and an infiltrative growth pattern {9669341;32089415}.

The immunohistochemical profile of granular cell tumours includes strong and diffuse positivity for S100 and SOX10. CD68, CD63 (NKI/C3 clone), and NSE are also positive, most likely due to nonspecific reactivity with cytoplasmic lysosomes. Strong nuclear expression of TFE3 and MITF is common, whereas HMB45, melan-A, GFAP, keratins, and NFP are negative {26009539;17284958}. The Ki-67 proliferation index is typically low (< 2%) {2452781;15214825}.

The differential diagnosis includes non-neural granular cell tumours and other diverse tumours with granular cell change, including apocrine neoplasms, naevi, melanomas, fibrous histiocytomas, carcinomas, and alveolar soft part sarcoma.

Cytology

Smears are moderately cellular with sheets of polygonal epithelioid cells with abundant granular cytoplasm and round minimally pleomorphic nuclei. Dispersed single cells and stripped nuclei are usually found in a finely granular background {11348331;17924412;30589232}. The differential diagnosis includes apocrine carcinomas and histiocytic processes.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bland and large cells with granular cytoplasm, often with an infiltrative pattern; S100 is strongly and diffusely positive; must exclude other more-aggressive neoplasms showing granular cell change (melanoma, breast carcinoma).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Treatment is local excision {20074934}. Granular cell tumours of the breast have minimal risk of local recurrence, even when excised with positive margins {21732779}. The rare malignant form can give rise to metastasis, including distant dissemination. Features suggestive of malignancy include large tumour size (> 50 mm), cellular and nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, increased mitotic activity, tumour necrosis, and local recurrence {6246310}.
