2.2.1 Adenosis and sclerosing lesions: Introduction

Adenosis is defined as a relative increase in the number of acinar units in the terminal duct lobular units. It is a non-neoplastic proliferation, and the normal two-cell population and basement membrane are maintained.

Adenosis and benign sclerosing lesions are characterized by a lobulocentric proliferation of glands/acini that are lined by epithelial cells, surrounded by a myoepithelial cell layer. Benign sclerosing lesions (specifically radial scar and complex sclerosing lesion) also have a central sclerotic and/or elastotic nidus from which the glandular/acinar structures radiate {38923027}. Benign sclerosing lesions are often involved by epithelial hyperplasia, whereas in adenosis the acini are typically lined by a single layer of epithelial cells.

In the sixth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours series, infiltrating epitheliosis {26497122} is included with benign sclerosing lesions (see section Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion). This rare entity remains controversial, and the diagnosis should be rendered only if invasive carcinoma has been thoroughly ruled out with appropriate immunohistochemical stains.

Microglandular adenosis is different from other adenosis lesions in terms of both morphology and biological potential. It comprises a haphazard proliferation of small round glands, containing luminal secretions, lined by epithelial cells without an accompanying myoepithelial cell layer, but surrounded entirely by basement membrane. Although benign, microglandular adenosis may be associated with progression to invasive malignancy and may represent a non-obligate precursor of triple-negative breast carcinoma. A description is also provided of the morphological alterations in residual microglandular adenosis found in breast specimens obtained after neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
