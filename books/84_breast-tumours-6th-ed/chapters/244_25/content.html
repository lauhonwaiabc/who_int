2.4.4 Malignant adenomyoepithelioma

Definition

Malignant adenomyoepithelioma (M-AME) is an adenomyoepithelioma with carcinoma, in which the malignancy may arise from either the luminal epithelial or myoepithelial components, or from both cell types.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: adenomyoepithelioma with invasive carcinoma.

Not recommended: epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma; adenomyoepithelial carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Like AME, M-AME can affect any breast quadrant.

Clinical features

M-AME most frequently affects postmenopausal women, with a median age of 50 years {35459179;34458945}. No cases have been reported in young people and a single case of M-AME has been reported in a man {31651866}. Some patients report recent rapid increase in size of a longstanding mass {21307156}. Tumours arising in the context of neurofibromatosis type 1 {19561236} and Lynch syndrome {35707990} are on record.

Epidemiology

M-AME is rare, and no data on incidence have been reported {26838284}. In a series of 2078 consecutive primary breast carcinomas diagnosed by core needle biopsy, in patients > 70 years of age, 1 case of M-AME (0.048%) was found {18524618}. Current knowledge of M-AME is mainly based on single case reports and small series {34877289}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Genetic analyses of matched classic and malignant components of M-AME suggest that it arises from the malignant transformation of a pre-existing AME {27494340;29739933}, but the development of de novo M-AME cannot be ruled out. Limited genetic data on M-AME are available. The common genetic drivers of AME, namely HRAS p.Gln61 and p.Gly12/Gly13 hotspot mutations and PIK3CA mutations, are also found in M-AME {29739933;32355271;34496925;31094929}. HRAS p.Gln61 and p.Gly12/Gly13 mutations are associated with ER-negative AME with atypical histological features and are typically present in cases associated with carcinoma. Notably, HRAS NM_005343.4:c.182A>G p.(Gln61Arg) and p.Gly12 hotspot mutations have also been reported in epithelial-myoepithelial carcinomas of the salivary glands {30994537}. Homozygous CDKN2A deletions might be involved in the malignant transformation of ER-negative AME {29739933}.

In a study of a limited number of AME cases, paired M-AME and AME were found to share the same gain-of-function AKT1 variant. Additionally, the same NM_005343.4:c.35G>A p.(Gly12Asp) variant of HRAS was detected in both atypical AME and M-AME; however, no fusion drivers were identified {32355271}.

The TP53 mutations common in conventional ER-negative breast carcinoma have not been identified in M-AME {29739933;31094929}, except for 1 case {16634967}. None of 43 atypical/malignant AMEs in one series had ERBB2 amplification {29739933}.

Macroscopic appearance

M-AME exhibits varied macroscopic appearances, ranging from a multilobulated, well-defined mass resembling AME to a poorly defined mass with infiltrative borders {21307156;33829532}. The lesions are usually firm, rubbery, or fleshy in consistency, with associated cystic change and necrosis observed in more than half of all cases {26596263}. M-AME tends to present with larger tumours than those in AME {34458945}.

Histopathology

M-AME can present a wide spectrum of morphological features. In AME with malignant change of the luminal and/or myoepithelial component, the underlying histological appearance of an AME is recognized, with transition to carcinoma observed as cytological atypia, increased mitoses, and necrosis in the malignant component {27494340;23533258;16634967}. When the malignant transformation involves mainly the epithelial component, it can include features of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) and carcinomas of special types, including lobular carcinoma {9504856;7496592;9591734}. When the malignant transformation affects mainly the myoepithelial component, overgrowth of spindle or epithelioid myoepithelial cells predominates, with clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm, nuclear atypia, and frequent mitotic figures {7515373;27494340;21307156}.

Not uncommonly, the invasive carcinoma component is of the metaplastic subtype; spindle cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma, and matrix-producing carcinoma have been reported {9504856;7496592;21307156}. In these cases, an AME component can usually be identified by extensive sampling of the tumour. Rarely, tumours with an adenoid cystic carcinoma component have been reported {9504855;25056281;37392549}.

In rare cases, malignancy can affect both the epithelial and myoepithelial components {14736274;27494340;9591734}, giving rise to biphasic carcinomas composed of a mixture of malignant epithelial and myoepithelial cells, with the latter often being the predominant cell type.

At low-power magnification, M-AME shows a multilobulated or multinodular growth pattern. Rarely, a papillary architecture can be observed. The presence of a coexistent AME component may be easily appreciated in some cases, but in other cases the invasive carcinoma may dominate and obscure the AME component.

Mitotic count is increased in the malignant areas. The biphasic epithelial-myoepithelial nature, with dual cell types, is the key feature for the differential diagnosis with metaplastic carcinoma; immunohistochemistry with markers of epithelial and myoepithelial cell lineages can be useful {21307156}.

Mitotically active AME with cytological atypia can be difficult to distinguish from M-AME. AME exhibiting mild to moderate cytological atypia or as many as 2.5 mitoses/mm2 (5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area) but lacking other features of malignancy can be classified as atypical AME (as described in the previous section, section Classic adenomyoepithelioma). The use of Ki-67 in distinguishing atypical AME and M-AME has not been tested.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical studies using markers of epithelial and myoepithelial cell lineages may be useful in highlighting the biphasic epithelial-myoepithelial composition of the tumour {21307156}. However, it is important to recognize that M-AME may exhibit an aberrant immunoprofile, with a loss of clear distinction between the dual cell populations [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581. ]]. M-AME may be ER-positive or ER-negative, although malignant transformation of classic AME might be more frequently associated with ER-negative status. The ER status may be concordant in both the classic AME and malignant components, but an ER-negative carcinoma has been reported in association with an ER-positive AME {29739933}.

Immunohistochemical detection of the protein encoded by HRAS p.Gln61Arg is highly sensitive and specific and may be used to confirm origin from an AME – see Diagnostic molecular pathology below {31887226}.

Cytology

There are few case reports of M-AME in the cytopathology literature. Smears are highly cellular, with loosely cohesive tissue fragments of atypical epithelial cells simulating invasive breast carcinoma NST, surrounded by fragments of metachromatic matrix, highlighted by Giemsa staining, which may suggest the differential diagnosis from carcinoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma, where the matrix is often seen as spherules {21307156}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HRAS p.Gln61 and p.Gly12/Gly13 and PIK3CA hotspot mutations are found in M-AME {29739933;32355271;34496925;31094929}. The identification of HRAS p.Gln61 or p.Gly12/Gly13 hotspot mutations in a carcinoma of appropriate morphology supports origin from an AME {29739933;34496925;31094929;32355271}. Immunohistochemical detection of the protein encoded by HRAS p.Gln61Arg is highly sensitive and specific and provides a reliable surrogate of molecular testing {31887226}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant tumour arising in an AME, with the luminal or myoepithelial component (or both) being malignant.

Desirable: demonstration of biphasic components of AME by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

M-AME is staged according to the criteria adopted for invasive breast carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

The relatively low number of reported cases and short follow-up periods limit the available information on prognostic features in M-AME. Metastases may appear several years after primary diagnosis {27487934}.

The prognosis of M-AME may depend on the histological subtype and grade of the invasive disease. Given that this component is most commonly metaplastic carcinoma, the prognosis may be poor, with the lungs being the most common metastatic site {21307156}. However, tumours with a low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma component have shown a better outcome {7496592}.

Lymph node metastases are rare irrespective of the tumour size, and there are insufficient data available to suggest a requirement for axillary dissection {27494340;21307156;31578797;40115008;40100534}. The use of sentinel lymph node biopsy should be tailored to patient needs. Haematogenous spread seems to be more frequent than lymphatic spread, with the lungs and brain being the most common metastatic sites {1384377;7496592;10747327;35459179}. In a National Cancer Database (NCDB) study from the United States, the expected 5-year overall survival for all patients was 74.4%, and neither surgical technique, radiation, nor chemotherapy was associated with improved overall survival {31578797}. Therefore, the use of adjuvant therapy should be considered on an individualized basis.
