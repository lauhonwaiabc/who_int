Definition

Breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) is a mature CD30-positive T-cell lymphoma that arises in relation to a breast implant as an effusion confined by a fibrous capsule, and less commonly forming an invading mass.

Related terminology

Acceptable: seroma-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

BIA-ALCL is usually localized to the peri-implant space, but it can progress to involve surrounding breast parenchyma, chest wall soft tissues, or regional lymph nodes {29194092}.

Clinical features

The median time from implant placement to presentation is approximately 10 years (range: 2–32 years) {24323027;29302687}. Most patients present with a unilateral effusion around the implant; approximately 5% of cases are bilateral {24267734;24323027}. The overlying skin is usually unremarkable but can occasionally be erythematous. B symptoms are typically absent {30206414}. About 10–30% of patients present with a mass, and as many as 20% develop regional lymphadenopathy, most commonly in the axilla and supraclavicular fossa. Lymphadenopathy may be the first manifestation of disease {29194092}. Rarely, patients present with disseminated disease {24323027;34155351}. Effusion is best detected with ultrasonography, whereas tumour mass assessment, lymphadenopathy assessment, and follow-up are better done by PET {25073777}. Mammography is suboptimal for evaluation of periprosthetic effusion or mass.

Epidemiology

BIA-ALCL is a rare entity, and low incidence has been described in case series {32344893;30789476}. The average age at diagnosis of BIA-ALCL is 50 years (range: 21 to > 80 years). It arises almost exclusively in women, including rare cases in transgender women {29101436;29036941}. Although the vast majority of primary lymphomas of the breast are of B-cell lineage, BIA-ALCL is a site-specific lymphoma, and the most common lymphoma type associated with breast implants {24323027;29302687;26598546}.

Cases have been reported in > 51 countries, with most reported in the USA, Europe, and Australia {30789476}. Reported incidence rates vary worldwide, with the highest rates in the USA, Europe, and Australia, and much lower rates in Latin America and Asia {34651107}. Data from the population-based nationwide Dutch pathology registry showed that the prevalence of breast implants in women aged 20–70 years is 3.3%, rising slightly with age and often associated with textured-surface implants {29302687}. In another study, the prevalence of BIA-ALCL was found to be 30.54 per 100 000 implanted patients with textured implants (1 case per 3274 implanted patients) and 6.70 per 100 000 implanted patients with implants of any type (1 case per 14 925 implanted patients) {39331823}.

Etiology

One study reported that almost 100% of patients had received textured implants as opposed to those with smooth surfaces {28157769}. The saline or silicone filling of the implants does not appear to have a pathogenic role {24323027}. The suspected pathogenic triggers of lymphoma include a biofilm and the silicone shell, which is directly or indirectly immunogenic {27683157;20669503}, and shedding of silicone particles may elicit a host immune response. The bacterial biofilm around textured implants has been suggested as a trigger for immune reaction and lymphoma cell survival {26890506}, but more definitive studies are needed to establish its pathogenic role {38203524}. In 2019, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and national agencies of several countries acknowledged the association, which prompted the world’s largest breast implant manufacturer to issue a worldwide recall of textured implants. There were five case reports of BIA-ALCL arising in patients with Li–Fraumeni syndrome, suggesting that germline TP53 mutations are a predisposing factor {25974645;29370815;31392066;30546832}. It has also been reported that women with a history of breast cancer {38724638} and BRCA1 or BRCA2 germline mutations may have a higher risk of BIA-ALCL (1 in 1200) {32452517}, although these findings need independent validation {34651107}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of BIA-ALCL involves immunological and molecular factors. The immunological events that contribute to BIA-ALCL are probably multifactorial and include direct and/or indirect stimulation driven by silicone shell material, shed silicone particles, and periprosthetic bacterial biofilm {26890506;31316085;32414854}. Bacterial lipopolysaccharide antigens induce chemokines/cytokines, including IL-13, eotaxin, and IL-10, which induce a chronic T helper 17 (Th17) cell response more than a T helper 1 (Th1) cell response. A role for the T helper 2 (Th2) allergic inflammatory response {26890506;26979456;22286447;29689246;33146828} has also been suggested.

The JAK/STAT3 pathway is constitutively activated, which in as many as 60% of cases is associated with recurrent somatic mutations of JAK1, STAT3, or other genes {31774495;29921615;22791880;30206415;27198716}. Other genetic alterations include mutations of epigenetic modifiers involving notably KMT2C, KMT2D, CHD2, and CREBBP, as well as mutations in DNMT3A and TP53 (see #51738Table 7.01). Characteristic loss of chromosome 20 has also been detected in the majority of cases {33331925}, with loss of 20q13.12-q13.2 being highly specific for BIA-ALCL {33331925}, although various gains and losses of chromosome segments are common.

Gene expression profiling has demonstrated upregulation of hypoxia signalling genes probably resulting from local hypoxia, which can be highlighted immunohistochemically with staining of CAIX {32414854}. Amplification of CD274 (PDL1) at 9p24.1 has been seen in about one third of the cases, with overexpression of PDL1 in over half of the cases {31125630}. Like in other ALCLs, components of the T-cell receptor signalling pathway are downregulated {30206415}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic pathological assessment is best performed by en bloc resection of the intact capsule containing the implant and effusion. Because fluid collection responsible for swelling of the breast is a common presentation of BIA-ALCL, cytological examination of the effusion fluid surrounding the implant is critical for the diagnosis. Preparation of a cell block allowing for immunohistochemistry and molecular studies is strongly recommended {32045544}. Fluid can also be sent for flow cytometry. Fixation of the capsule on a flat surface allows for optimal orientation and subsequent on-edge sectioning. Generous sampling is recommended when a grossly distinct lesion is not identified, and any floating material or fibrinous material attached to the implant or capsule should be submitted for histological evaluation {31383966;32045544}. Tumour masses are suggested by the presence of variable capsular thickening or readily apparent tumorous lesions. Assessment of the capsular resection margins is warranted for complete excision of invasive lesions. Fragmented capsules or incomplete capsulectomy specimens may lead to recurrence or progression of disease {34155351}. As lymph node involvement by BIA-ALCL is commonly focal, lymph node excisional biopsy is recommended rather than FNA {32045544}.

Histopathology

The physiological reaction to implants is the formation of a fibrous capsule. Silicone fragments appear as refringent material that can migrate through surrounding fibrous tissue or to lymph nodes, causing a histiocytic reaction. The capsule is thickened and the luminal side shows necrosis, ghost cells, karyorrhexis, and scattered viable (< 10%) large, pleomorphic cells, usually arranged as a distinct layer, representing the early pathological stage of disease. In some cases, the only viable lymphoma cells are seen in the effusion/cytological specimen. The neoplastic cells are large, with moderately abundant amphophilic, eosinophilic, or clear cytoplasm and oval to lobated nuclei with small to prominent nucleoli; in about two thirds of cases, hallmark cells are identified. Karyorrhexis is common, and necrosis is usually extensive {29194092}. Upon progression, clusters of cells appear within or beyond the capsule and can infiltrate into surrounding soft tissue or breast parenchyma, usually associated with extensive necrosis {30206414}.

Lymph node involvement, when present, is usually sinusoidal and less frequently interfollicular, perifollicular, or diffuse. The infiltrate is best visualized with CD30 immunohistochemistry.

The immunophenotype is similar to ALK-negative ALCL, with uniform and strong expression of CD30 and usually negative staining for CD3, CD5, and CD7. The neoplastic cells commonly express CD4, CD43, CD25, IRF4 (MUM1), and GATA3, as well as cytotoxic markers (TIA1, granzyme B, and/or perforin). Approximately 20% of cases express surface T-cell receptor (either TCRαβ or TCRγδ) {30206414}. Rare CD8-positive or CD4/CD8 double-negative cases have been described {30206414;28715445}. Neoplastic cells almost constantly show nuclear expression of phosphorylated STAT3 even though STAT3 mutations occur only in a subset of cases {29921615;31774495}. PDL1 is expressed in a subset of cases. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER), LMP1, PD1, and ALK are negative.

Elevated levels of hypoxia-associated CAIX were detected in periprosthetic effusions of BIA-ALCL, but not in benign effusions {35437827}. IL-9, IL-10, and IL-13 are elevated in periprosthetic effusions of BIA-ALCL as compared with benign effusions, suggesting that IL-10 can be a helpful biomarker {37646570}. Expression of IL-13 and GATA3, as reported in some series, may suggest an allergic response {29689246}.

Differential diagnosis

Systemic ALK-negative ALCL can rarely involve the breast but does not localize to the capsule {30206414}. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, extranodal marginal zone lymphoma, and extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma in women with breast implants have been described with a similar anatomical distribution to that of BIA-ALCL {34155351}. Several cases of EBV-positive fibrin-associated large B-cell lymphomas have been described around breast implants {34226673;31753922;32110798}. These cases may be misdiagnosed as BIA-ALCL, underpinning the importance of complete workup of suspected cases of BIA-ALCL, including the demonstration of absence of EBV by EBER in situ hybridization, absence of T-cell markers using immunohistochemistry, and/or molecular TR gene rearrangement analysis. High-grade carcinomas such as those with medullary and pleomorphic patterns can be excluded through relevant immunohistochemical stains for epithelial markers.

Cytology

BIA-ALCL usually produces high cellularity with plentiful large single cells showing large, pleomorphic, and lobated nuclei that may be arranged in a horseshoe (hallmark cell), prominent nucleoli, and abundant basophilic (Giemsa) cytoplasm, which often contains vacuoles and blebs, in a background of degenerate tumour cells, macrophages, necrosis, and karyorrhexis {34623773;31348611;34798563;31173460;37149362}.

Diagnosis of BIA-ALCL is usually made initially on identification of atypical cells in periprosthesis implant fluid submitted for cytopathological examination. Preparation of a cell block is recommended to allow for H&E staining and immunohistochemical and molecular studies to be performed from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded material {32045544}.

Some clinical recommendations have stated that all such samples should be subject to CD30 and CD68/CD163 immunohistochemistry regardless of the initial cytopathological feature, while others recommend such investigations be restricted to samples which contain atypical cells, including possible macrophages and/or large atypical lymphoid blasts. If these atypical cells are CD30-positive and CD68-negative, the remaining panel for ALCL should be requested {31166611;28715445}. Periprosthetic effusions with high levels of CD30 by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) {30789639} or IL-9 by lateral flow assay {38913383} correlate with the presence of BIA-ALCL, while absent or negligible levels are characteristic of benign effusions.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Almost 80% of cases have monoclonal rearrangements of the TRG or TRB genes {29921615;30206414}. BIA-ALCL is consistently negative for ALCL-related gene rearrangements involving ALK, DUSP22, and TP63 {29921615;26598546}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a lymphoma restricted to the seroma surrounding a breast implant, or a tumour in association with a breast implant; large lymphoma cells; uniform strong expression of CD30; T-cell lineage, expression of one or more T-lineage–associated and/or cytotoxic markers.

Desirable: absence of ALK expression or ALK rearrangement; identification of lymphoma cells on the luminal side of the capsule on properly oriented sections.

Staging

A modification of the TNM system has been developed {26628470}. The pathological T staging is as follows: T1 (tumour confined at the luminal side of the capsule or tumour cells detected only in periprosthetic effusion), T2 (superficial infiltration of the capsule), T3 (deep infiltration of the capsule, often accompanied by chronic inflammatory cells), and T4 (throughout the capsule) {26628470;30206414}. The proportions of cases in some studies were T1, T2, T3, and T4 stage in 35.6%, 12.6%, 16.1%, and 34.5%, respectively {26628470;30206414}. In addition, lymph node involvement (N1) and distant disease (M1) may occur {26628470}.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year overall survival rate was 92% in one case series {24323027}. Surgery is the cornerstone of therapy, with complete excision of capsule and implant leading to a 5-year overall survival rate of 100% in patients with effusion only {24323027;26628470}. The 5-year overall survival rate is 83.7% for patients with disease beyond the capsule and 75% for patients with lymph node involvement {29194092}. By August 2020, it was estimated that 36 patients had died of BIA-ALCL. Incomplete surgery may lead to recurrence or progression of disease {26628470}.
