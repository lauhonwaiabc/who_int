3.2.10 Metaplastic carcinoma

Definition

Metaplastic carcinoma is a heterogeneous group of invasive breast carcinomas (IBCs) characterized by differentiation of the neoplastic epithelium towards spindle squamous cells and/or mesenchymal elements, including but not restricted to chondroid and osseous cells.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: carcinosarcoma; sarcomatoid carcinoma; carcinoma with pseudosarcomatous metaplasia; carcinoma with pseudosarcomatous stroma.

Subtype(s)

Low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma; fibromatosis-like carcinoma; spindle cell carcinoma; squamous cell carcinoma; metaplastic carcinoma with heterologous mesenchymal differentiation / matrix production

Localization

Metaplastic carcinomas can affect any anatomical area of the breast.

Clinical features

The clinical features of metaplastic carcinoma are similar to those of ER-negative IBC of no special type (NST); however, metaplastic carcinomas are more likely to present at an advanced stage {29330212}. Most tumours (85%) present with a palpable lump and are detected as a mass lesion on ultrasonography (100%) or mammography (78%). Microcalcifications are uncommon (17%) {27245135}; when present, they are often associated with ductal carcinoma in situ and/or osseous differentiation.

Epidemiology

Metaplastic carcinomas have been reported to account for 0.2–1% of all IBCs occurring in women with a median age of 63 years {29330212;25012264;24838367}. This variation in prevalence stems from the heterogeneity in definitions of metaplastic carcinoma used.

Etiology

The etiology of metaplastic carcinomas is multifactorial, and it appears not to differ from that of IBC-NST (in particular the triple-negative subtype).

Pathogenesis

Although metaplastic carcinomas of the breast constitute a heterogeneous group of tumours with distinctive morphological characteristics, the results of genetic studies (DNA) support a monoclonal origin of the various components of metaplastic carcinomas {20099298;15376261;28424200}. However, some authors support the concept of a late-step change of tumour de-differentiation rather than an origin from a basal-like stem cell {21462188}. It remains unknown whether somatic mutations cause the differentiation that allows for metaplastic carcinoma subtypes or whether it is a result of change in gene expression, for example through changes in gene methylation {25688406}. No specific pathognomonic mutations of metaplastic carcinomas have yet been identified.

Transcriptomically, these tumours are classified as basal-like or claudin-low subtype using the intrinsic gene classification {18720457;18815879;25412848;29214215;19435916;20813035} or as basal or mesenchymal-like using the classification proposed by Lehmann et al. {29214215;25412848}. Whole-exome and targeted sequencing analyses of metaplastic carcinomas have demonstrated that these tumours have complex landscapes of gene copy-number alterations and a repertoire of somatic mutations, including mutations affecting TP53, RB1, chromatin-remodelling genes (ARID1A and KMT2C), and genes related to the PI3K (PIK3CA, PIK3R1, and PTEN), MAPK (NF1, KRAS, and NRAS), and WNT (FAT1 and CCN6 [WISP3]) pathways {25688406;28153863;29946183;30350370}. Interestingly, there is evidence to suggest that TP53 mutations may be less frequent in spindle cell carcinoma and in low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma (LGASC) than in other forms of metaplastic carcinoma {28153863;30350370;29537649}. Compared with other forms of triple-negative breast carcinoma (TNBC), metaplastic carcinomas more frequently display mutations affecting PI3K pathway–related genes {28153863;29946183;30350370;29537649}, but at a frequency similar to that observed in luminal AR TNBC. Metaplastic carcinomas were also reported to harbour TERT promoter mutations in 25% of tumours in one study {29946183}.

Overexpression and mutations of EGFR and p63 immunopositivity have been reported in metaplastic carcinomas with squamous metaplasia and spindle cell morphology, but activating mutations in EGFR have not been identified {16739104}. Metaplastic carcinomas show a stem cell–like phenotype, with CD44 positivity and CD24 negativity {19435916}. Metaplastic carcinomas, as part of the TNBC group, are characterized by low expression of GATA3-regulated genes and genes that are responsible for epithelial–mesenchymal transition and cell–cell adhesion, and upregulation of vimentin and E-cadherin repressor molecules with reduction/loss of E-cadherin. These molecular features may account for the relative chemoresistance seen in these tumours {16739104}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of metaplastic carcinoma is often not distinctive, and the tumours can either present as well-circumscribed masses or display indistinct, irregular borders. Cystic degeneration may be seen in metaplastic carcinomas with squamous differentiation. Pearly, white to greyish, and glistening cut surfaces can be seen in areas of squamous and chondroid metaplasia, whereas the cut surface may be gritty in areas of osseous metaplasia. Compared with IBC-NST, metaplastic carcinomas tend to be larger, with a mean size of 39 mm, ranging from 20 to > 100 mm. Thorough sampling of the lesion is advised.

Histopathology

Metaplastic carcinomas constitute a group of tumours with morphologically distinct patterns and different outcomes, although there is often overlap. Given this heterogeneity a descriptive classification system is used. Metaplastic carcinomas can be monophasic (composed purely of one metaplastic component) or mixed, in which each component may display metaplastic features (e.g. squamous and/or spindle cells with a mesenchymal/matrix-producing component) or one or more metaplastic components may be admixed with IBC-NST. If more than one component is identified, it is suggested to note each component. Some IBC-NSTs may have a focal metaplastic component (< 10%) and this should be noted in the report.

Metaplastic carcinomas can be divided into low-grade (LGASC and fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma) and high-grade types.

Low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma

LGASC is characterized by well-developed glandular and tubular structures, with rounded rather than angulated contours, admixed with nests of squamous cells that may display squamous pearls and/or squamous cyst formation, arranged in a haphazard, infiltrative pattern in a spindle cell background. Cytological atypia is mild and mitotic activity is low. The tumour cells tend to form long, slender extensions at the periphery and infiltrate between normal breast structures, which is a useful diagnostic feature {37566996}. Clusters of lymphocytes are often observed at the periphery, sometimes in a cannonball pattern. Because of the favourable prognosis of this tumour {8434705}, it should be differentiated from other breast tumours. It should also be distinguished from syringomatous adenoma of the nipple {24382117} and the cellular phase of radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion {28451460;37566996}.

Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma

Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma of the breast is characterized by > 90% of the tumour being composed of bland spindle cells, with pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and slender nuclei with tapered edges and finely distributed chromatin, embedded in stroma with varying degrees of collagenization {32127014}. High nuclear grade is not seen in this tumour. The spindle cells may be arranged in wavy, interlacing fascicles, or form long fascicles with finger-like extensions infiltrating the adjacent breast parenchyma. Cords and clusters of plump spindled and more-epithelioid cells may be present {19579879;13679453;16224212;11474284;10326695}. Focal squamous differentiation may be seen. A gradual transition from plump cells to the spindle cell component is frequently observed. These tumours are almost invariably p63-positive; keratins are always expressed, occasionally focally and not uncommonly restricted to the plump spindle and epithelioid cells. Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma should always be considered as the main differential diagnosis of bland-looking spindle cell proliferations of the breast {26768028;11474284;34322734}. A diagnosis of fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma can be rendered based on the presence of any evidence of epithelial differentiation by histopathological and/or immunohistochemical analysis (see below). Because of the relatively favourable prognosis of this tumour type, it should be differentiated from the more common high-grade spindle cell metaplastic carcinoma.

Spindle cell carcinoma

Spindle cell carcinomas are characterized by a proliferation of atypical spindle cells that show varied architectural arrangements, ranging from long fascicles in a herringbone or interwoven pattern to short fascicles in a storiform (cartwheel) pattern {16538049;7275095;2473024}. Most often, a mixture of different patterns is observed. The cells may be elongated or plump. Nuclear pleomorphism is moderate to high and mitotic figures are usually easily identified. Foci of necrosis may be present. An inflammatory cell infiltrate is found in a proportion of tumours, often with lymphocytes and dendritic cells percolating through the tumour. Areas in which the neoplastic cells form small clusters with more-epithelioid morphology or display squamous differentiation may be observed. Spindle cell carcinoma may be pure or mixed with another metaplastic or conventional IBC-NST component. Heterologous elements are seen in some spindle cell carcinomas {25422911}.

It should be noted that this group of tumours shows a spectrum of differentiation ranging from basal epithelial to myoepithelial, and there is morphological overlap between these lesions and the rare malignant adenomyoepithelioma (see section Malignant adenomyoepithelioma) {33829532}. Metaplastic spindle cell carcinoma should always be considered as a main differential diagnosis of atypical/malignant-looking spindle cell proliferations of the breast {26768028}. A diagnosis of metaplastic spindle cell carcinoma can be rendered based on the presence of any evidence of epithelial differentiation by histopathological and/or immunohistochemical analysis (see below).

Squamous cell carcinoma

Pure metaplastic squamous cell carcinomas may present as a cystic lesion in which the cavity is lined by squamous cells with varying degrees of nuclear atypia and pleomorphism. The neoplastic cells infiltrate the adjacent stroma in the form of sheets, cords, and nests, with variable degrees of squamous differentiation, and they often elicit a conspicuous stromal reaction and prominent inflammatory infiltrates {2153044;5352811}. Several patterns can be observed. The acantholytic pattern of squamous cell carcinoma, characterized by the formation of irregular spaces lined by atypical squamous cells leading to a pseudoglandular or pseudoangiosarcomatous appearance, should be considered as a potential differential diagnosis with angiosarcoma {2431630}. Metaplastic squamous cell carcinoma may be pure, or mixed with other types of metaplastic carcinoma, usually of spindle cell carcinoma pattern, or with IBC-NST. For a diagnosis of primary squamous cell carcinoma of the breast to be rendered, primary cutaneous or metastatic squamous cell carcinoma from other sites (e.g. lung and cervix) must be ruled out.

Metaplastic carcinoma with heterologous mesenchymal differentiation / matrix production

Metaplastic breast carcinomas with mesenchymal elements are composed of mesenchymal components (including chondroid, osseous, rhabdomyoid, and occasionally neuroglial) or are associated with chondromyxoid matrix production. The majority are admixed with spindle cell or carcinomatous components which may display glandular/tubular differentiation, a solid growth pattern, and/or foci of squamous differentiation {19047898;2825549;2776108;2544506;2473024}. The mesenchymal components range from appearing differentiated with minimal atypia to exhibiting frankly malignant features that to some extent recapitulate the patterns found in true sarcomas of the soft tissues.

Tumours that appear to be purely mesenchymal also occur. The differential diagnosis of these tumours includes malignant phyllodes tumours, primary and metastatic sarcomas, and metaplastic carcinoma. Extensive sampling and application of extensive immunohistochemistry including a range of cytokeratins is required to ensure accurate diagnosis. In the vast majority of tumours, epithelial differentiation will be identified on further sampling and/or immunohistochemistry {33113154;34322734}. Gene sequencing for MED12 may help to identify malignant phyllodes tumours {39154782}. In some cases, the distinction between metaplastic carcinoma and sarcoma may not be possible and these tumours are best considered as metaplastic carcinoma for management purposes {23084962}.

Immunohistochemistry

The vast majority (> 90%) of metaplastic carcinomas lack expression of ER, PR, and ERBB2 (HER2) {16842242;29330212;28029685;24838367}. The majority express keratins, usually high-molecular-weight cytokeratins, at least focally, p63, and EGFR {16842242;28029685}. The identification of epithelial differentiation in metaplastic breast carcinomas requires the use of a panel of immunohistochemical markers including a range of keratins – in particular AE1/AE3 and MNF116 (positive in 75–85%); 34βE12, CK5/6, and CK14 (positive in 70–75%); and p63 (positive in 77%). Low-molecular-weight cytokeratins, such as CK8/18, CK7, and CK19, are positive in a lower proportion of tumours (36–61%). Myoepithelial markers, including SMA, CD10, and maspin, are also frequently positive (in 50–70%). Metaplastic carcinomas are negative for CD34 and often lack expression of desmin (in 18%) and SMMHC (in 11%) {28029685}. E-cadherin may be aberrantly expressed within squamous foci {30350370}; β-catenin may also be aberrantly expressed {20693983}. It should be noted that metaplastic carcinoma with an LGASC pattern may occasionally have a biphasic composition: the inner layer in gland-like structures is positive for luminal and occasionally basal cytokeratins, and the outer layer displays varying levels of expression of basal cytokeratins and p63, with staining ranging from complete to incomplete/absent {22446941;20453835}.

Cytology

Metaplastic squamous cell carcinoma produces keratinous debris and squamous cells showing varying degrees of nuclear atypia, and the differential diagnosis is a metastatic squamous cell carcinoma. Fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinomas yield limited material and the differential diagnosis with similarly rare fibromatoses is challenging. Mixed metaplastic carcinomas show a variable mix of atypical spindle cells, squamous carcinoma cells, a component of adenocarcinoma cells, and chondroid, osteoid, or other heterologous elements {21791899;21244696}. The specific cytopathological diagnosis of metaplastic carcinoma may not be possible in all cases because of selective sampling of various pathological elements.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant squamous or spindle cell elements, and/or features of mesenchymal differentiation / matrix production.

Desirable: coexisting ductal carcinoma in situ or IBC-NST; expression of cytokeratins and/or p63.

Staging

Metaplastic carcinomas are staged similarly to other types of IBCs (see section Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction).

Prognosis and prediction

Lymph node metastases are found significantly less frequently in metaplastic breast cancers than in IBC-NST of similar size and grade {24838367}. As for other TNBCs, metaplastic carcinoma may have a distant pattern of metastases, preferentially affecting the brain and lungs, in the absence of regional lymph node metastases. There are no predictive markers of therapeutic response supported by level 1 evidence for patients with metaplastic carcinoma. Retrospective analyses have suggested that specific subtypes probably have distinct outcomes {25422911;20153509}. Fibromatosis-like carcinoma and LGASC are associated with more indolent behaviour despite the triple-negative phenotype {26348644;19579879;8434705}. Within the group of high-grade metaplastic carcinomas, spindle cell, mixed spindle and squamous, and squamous cell carcinomas appear to have a worse prognosis than matrix-producing metaplastic carcinoma {25422911;20153509}. A higher number of morphologies within mixed metaplastic carcinomas has also been correlated with a worse outcome {30350370}. The prognostic value of histological grade in metaplastic breast carcinomas is uncertain.

The 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year overall survival rates for patients with metaplastic breast carcinoma are 77% {29330212}, 62% {24838367;16469754}, and 53% {20585866}, respectively. No difference in 5-year survival was found between hormone receptor–positive and hormone receptor–negative metaplastic carcinomas {24838367}. There is evidence that the rare forms of HER2-positive metaplastic carcinoma may be associated with a better outcome than triple-negative metaplastic carcinomas {29330212}. Radiotherapy provides a survival benefit {20585866}. As a group, metaplastic breast carcinomas are reported to have lower response rates to conventional adjuvant chemotherapy and a worse clinical outcome after chemotherapy than other forms of TNBC {17009109;20143153;16258085;16469754}.
