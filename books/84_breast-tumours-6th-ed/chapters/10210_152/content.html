10.2.10 The polygenic component of breast cancer susceptibility

Several breast cancer susceptibility genes have been identified in which germline pathogenic variants substantially increase the risk of developing breast cancer (i.e. by 2-fold to 20-fold). These genes include ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, CDH1, CHEK2, PTEN, PALB2, RAD51C, RAD51D, and TP53 {33471991;33471974}. Together they explain about 20–25% of the familial breast cancer risk.

Over the past two decades, > 200 independent common genetic variants (mostly SNPs) associated with breast cancer risk have been identified {29059683;29058716;32424353}. These risk variants are associated with modestly increased risks (as much as 1.3-fold per allele). Unlike pathogenic variants in risk genes associated with high penetrance, these variants are common in the population and are often referred to as low-penetrance alleles. These so-called low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles have mostly been identified through genome-wide association studies in which SNPs are genotyped in women affected by breast cancer (cases) and women unaffected by breast cancer (controls). Because the breast cancer risk associated with each of these variants is small, large numbers of subjects are required for genome-wide association studies; consequently, the establishment of large research consortia has been pivotal to the continued identification of these variants. The most recent and largest breast cancer genome-wide association study, from the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC), measured 11.8 million SNPs for > 130 000 cases and 110 000 controls. The replicated common low-penetrance loci to date have been estimated to explain 18% of the familial risk of breast cancer. However, it is estimated that these represent only 44% of the familial breast cancer risk that can be explained by the polygenic component of breast cancer susceptibility {29059683}.

Most genome-wide association studies have been conducted in populations of European ancestry. Although many loci are common across ancestries, the contribution of common variants discovered to date is smaller in other ancestries {39753771}. A few loci were first identified in Asian studies though they have subsequently been replicated in Europeans {22383897;23411593}. Associations may differ by population owing to difference in allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium patterns or interactions with lifestyle factors. This highlights the need for larger genome-wide association studies in non-European populations, in particular African populations, where there is a higher frequency of triple-negative breast cancer.

Breast cancer subtypes

Risk variants in many of the susceptibility genes show subtype-specific associations, with the majority (including BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51C, and RAD51D) being more strongly associated with ER-negative and/or triple-negative disease {35084436}. ATM and CHEK2 variants are more strongly associated with ER-positive disease while TP53 variants are more strongly associated with ERBB2 (HER2)-positive breast cancer.

The majority of the common, low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles are associated with both ER-positive and ER-negative disease. Approximately one third are more strongly associated with ER-positive than ER-negative breast cancer, while a smaller proportion (~15%) are more strongly associated with ER-negative disease {29059683;29058716;31911677}. This demonstrates that ER-positive and ER-negative breast cancers share a common genetic etiology, but with some subtype specificity.

Low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles associated with ER-negative breast cancer are also associated with breast cancer risk in BRCA1 pathogenic variant carriers – a finding consistent with the observation that the majority of BRCA1 pathogenic variant carriers develop ER-negative disease {22144499;25919761}. BRCA2 pathogenic variant carriers more commonly develop ER-positive disease, and the low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles that are associated with overall breast cancer are also associated with breast cancer risk in BRCA2 pathogenic variant carriers {25919761}.

Although most breast cancers are of ductal histology, there have also been efforts to identify low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles that specifically predispose individuals to other histological subtypes. One low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility allele at 7q34 specifically associated with lobular breast cancer has been identified {24743323}. The 313 polygenic risk score (PRS, see below) is more strongly associated with lobular than ductal breast cancer. No low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles have been identified that are specifically associated with ductal carcinoma in situ, and the PRS is similarly associated with ductal carcinoma in situ, indicating that ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive ductal carcinoma have a common genetic etiology {26884359}.

Few of the low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles have been definitively associated with other cancer types. A variant in on 5p15, rs10069690, close to TERT, is associated with increased risk of triple-negative breast cancer, as well as increased risk for ovarian, lung, thyroid, gastric, and renal cell carcinomas, but a decreased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, prostate cancer, and pancreatic cancer {36768147;39192222}. Other variants in this region are associated with multiple other cancers, illustrating a general pattern whereby variants in the same region are associated with different cancers, perhaps owing to tissue-specific regulation of the same target genes.

A low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility allele at 19p13, associated with triple-negative breast cancer, is also associated with ovarian cancer {27601076}.

Genome-wide heritability analyses have indicated genetic correlations with breast cancer for both lung and colorectal cancer, in addition to ovarian cancer, indicating that some breast cancer–related SNPs associated with these other cancers may be identifiable {30683880}.

Breast cancer–specific survival

The potential for germline genetic information to add to the routinely collected tumour-derived prognostic factors has been investigated in numerous settings. The possible association between common genetic variation and breast cancer–specific survival has been examined in candidate gene studies {24112814} and pooled analyses {25890600;25897948}. These studies have identified and further evaluated numerous common genetic variants nominally associated with outcome, but few have been replicated and/or reached genome-wide significance. In the largest genome-wide association studies to date, involving 96 661 women with breast cancer and 7697 breast cancer–specific deaths, no loci reached genome-wide significance for association with breast cancer–specific survival, overall or by subtype {30787463}. The 313 SNP PRS (see below) has been shown to be associated with good prognostic features and better survival, but this association disappears after adjustment for known prognostic factors {36689693}. Specific analyses examining germline associations with outcome related to specific treatments have also been inconclusive, perhaps owing to limited power {39971965;34407845}.

Assigning causation to genetic variation

Genome-wide association studies take advantage of the linkage disequilibrium structure of the human genome and identify common variants that are likely to tag a genomic region associated with the disease. These region-tagging common genetic variants are associated with breast cancer risk, but they are unlikely to be the cause of the genetic susceptibility. Identification of the causal variants, target genes, and mechanisms of action requires a combination of detailed analysis of association data with in silico analysis and functional experiments.

Fine-mapping analysis of > 150 risk regions has revealed that many loci harbour multiple independent risk signals (as many as 11) {31911677;39753771}. The majority of loci lie in regulatory regions, with only a minority lying in coding sequences. There is a strong enrichment for binding sites for transcription factors including ESR1, FOXA1, GATA3, TCF7L2, and E2F1. The likely target genes, identified predominantly through conformational capture, fall into a variety of pathways, including developmental process pathways (including mammary development), cycle-cell and DNA damage control, and immune function. There is a strong enrichment for breast cancer somatic driver genes (e.g. MAP3K1). The underlying mechanisms, however, remain poorly understood.

Polygenic risk scores

The magnitude of risk associated with each individual low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility allele is low, but these risks appear to combine multiplicatively, so that the breast cancer risk associated with carrying a large proportion of these alleles can be substantial. A PRS, which is calculated as a weighted sum of the number of risk alleles carried by an individual, can now be used to identify women at high (or low) risk, even in the absence of a high-risk pathogenic variant {23109704}. The most widely validated PRS, based on 313 variants, is associated with a relative risk of about 1.6 per standard deviation in women of European ancestry. Women in the highest 1% of the PRS distribution are at 4.37- and 2.78-fold risks of ER-positive and ER-negative disease, compared with women in the middle quintile of the PRS, while women in the lowest 1% are at 0.16- and 0.27-fold risks, respectively {30554720}. The 313 SNP PRS is also predictive of risk in women of Asian and African ancestry though the association is weaker {32737321;33769540;34347061}. The addition of further risk-associated alleles into the PRS is anticipated and likely to further improve breast cancer risk stratification in the general population, providing additional support for the utility of the PRS in the preventive setting {32620889}.

The 313 SNP PRS has also been shown to be predictive of breast cancer risk among carriers of BRCA1, BRCA2, and CHEK2 germline pathogenic variants {32665703;27711073}, and this information may be utilized to inform clinical management. The data for risk modification for other risk genes are currently sparse. The PRS is also predictive of contralateral breast cancer risk, both in the general population and in pathogenic variant carriers {33022221;34113011}. It is associated strongly with breast cancer in men (though the associations for some loci differ from those for female breast cancer, with some loci, for example on 14q, much more strongly associated with male breast cancer) {32785646}.

Several breast cancer risk prediction models that make use of various clinical variables, such as family history, reproductive history, and breast density, are currently applied in the clinical setting: these include the Breast and Ovarian Analysis of Disease Incidence and Carrier Estimation Algorithm (BOADICEA), the BRCAPRO model, the Breast Cancer Risk Assessment Tool (BCRAT), and the International Breast Cancer Intervention Study (IBIS/Tyrer-Cuzick) risk assessment tool. Incorporating PRS into these models substantially improves risk prediction {30643217}; in particular the version of BOADICEA incorporating the PRS has been extensively validated both in the general population and in pathogenic genetic variant carriers {36162852;38834293}. Some of the risk factors incorporated into these risk models are also hereditary traits; in particular, mammographic density is an important breast cancer risk factor that is incorporated in several models {25342443;25862352} as it is strongly heritable and about 20% of the known low-penetrance breast cancer susceptibility alleles are associated with mammographic breast density. Nevertheless, the PRS and mammographic breast density, and other genetic and lifestyle factors, are strongly and independently predictive of risk, and risk stratification is greatest when all factors are considered jointly {30643217}.
