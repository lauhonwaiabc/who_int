3.4.4 Neuroendocrine carcinoma

Definition

Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) is a malignant epithelial neoplasm, composed of small cells with high mitotic and proliferation indexes, most expressing neuroendocrine differentiation markers. SCNEC may occur either in a pure form or combined (mixed) with any other carcinoma histotype.

Large cell NEC (LCNEC) in the breast is invariably classified as invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) with neuroendocrine differentiation.

Related terminology

Acceptable: small cell carcinoma.

Not recommended: poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma; large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Localization

No specific data are available.

Clinical features

There are no notable or specific differences in presentation from other high-grade breast carcinomas. Clinical syndromes related to hormone production have not been documented. Patient age ranges from 43 to 70 years {15976350;10976697}. Tumour size ranges from 13 to 50 mm (mean: 26 mm) {10976697}. Approximately 40% of small cell carcinomas have nodal metastasis {25853028}. In three population-based studies of patients with primary SCNECs of the breast {25853028;19563623;25885914}, 70–81% had localized disease (local or regional) and 19–30% had distant metastasis.

Epidemiology

Primary NECs of the breast are extremely rare. SCNECs account for approximately 0.1% of all breast cancers {25853028} and for 3–10% of extrapulmonary small cell carcinomas {19563623;25885914;21618245}. LCNECs, regarded as invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation, are extremely rare.

Etiology

There are no data on the etiology of NECs, but they probably have the same etiology as other high-grade breast carcinomas. No link with cigarette smoking has been reported.

Pathogenesis

Of the nine SCNECs of the breast reported by Shin et al. {10976697}, 3 cases were associated with invasive, poorly differentiated duct carcinoma, and ductal carcinoma in situ was seen in 7 tumours – 5 were of the small cell type in ducts and 2 were of the ductal type with high nuclear grade. These findings suggest that SCNECs result from a specific line of differentiation of mammary cancer stem cells towards neuroendocrine / small cell–type differentiation, which can take place at the in situ stage or later (at the invasive stage), rather than resulting from the malignant transformation of specific neuroendocrine cells in the normal breast tissue. In a way akin to lung SCNECs, breast SCNECs display TP53 and RB1 somatic genetic alterations in as many as 75% and 19% of cases, respectively {27329168}. Another study showed co-alterations of TP53 and RB1 in 86% of SCNECs {35590107}. Data on the pathogenesis of invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation are lacking (ex LCNEC).

Macroscopic appearance

Primary NECs have no gross features distinct from those of other types of high-grade breast cancer. They can present with poorly circumscribed, fleshy, white to tan cut surfaces and focal areas of necrosis.

Histopathology

Histologically and immunohistochemically, NECs are morphologically indistinguishable from their counterparts in the lung {29661717;26960398}. Primary breast SCNECs are poorly differentiated, high-grade cancers (according to the Nottingham histological grading system), displaying an infiltrative growth pattern, and are composed of densely packed, fairly uniform, small, dark hyperchromatic cells that have a high N:C ratio, scant cytoplasm, inconspicuous nucleoli, and poorly defined cytoplasmic borders. In high-grade invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation (ex LCNEC), the cells show visible cytoplasm and highly pleomorphic nuclei with variably coarse chromatin. Common features of both SCNEC and invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation are high numbers of mitotic figures and focal areas of necrosis (see section Invasive breast carcinoma of no special type). An in situ component with the same cytological features may be detected. Lymphatic tumour emboli are frequently encountered {15976350;10976697}. A mixed cancer comprising SCNEC breast carcinomas or high-grade invasive breast carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation and other histological types can be observed {36310301}.

The differential diagnoses of primary NEC include metastatic small cell carcinoma of non-mammary origin (most commonly from the lung), Merkel cell carcinoma, lymphoma, and melanoma. The presence of an associated ductal carcinoma in situ or conventional-type mammary carcinoma component confirms the primary nature of the tumour. SCNEC should also be differentiated from metaplastic carcinomas or invasive breast carcinoma NST with basaloid cells and an increased N:C ratio and solid-basaloid adenoid cystic carcinoma. High-grade invasive breast carcinoma NST with extensive neuroendocrine differentiation should be distinguished from metastatic LCNEC of non-mammary origin (e.g. from the lung).

Immunohistochemical analysis shows consistent staining for cytokeratin markers but variable staining for neuroendocrine markers, with more than two thirds of cases staining positively for chromogranin and synaptophysin. PGP9.5, CD56 (NCAM), and NSE are positive in the majority of cases. CD56 is not a specific neuroendocrine marker and its positivity is not diagnostic of neuroendocrine differentiation. SCNECs are positive for BCL2 and negative for ERBB2 (HER2) {10976697}. ER positivity has been reported in 30–50% of cases {10976697;27329168} and PR positivity in a smaller proportion, but other authors did not identify hormone receptor expression in SCNEC {18357385}. TTF1 expression in SCNEC of the breast has been reported, but not diffuse strong nuclear staining as seen in small cell carcinoma of the lung {20661411;19578049}. GATA3 expression has been reported in NEC of the breast but not in metastatic NECs, including those from the lung {27125358}.

Cytology

FNA of SCNEC shows the presence of malignant cells with small cell features, which may display nuclear moulding, dense or finely granular or salt-and-pepper chromatin, and scant delicate cytoplasm. Chromatinic smearing and apoptosis are present. Necrosis is frequently present in cytological samples of NEC.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: small cells with high mitotic and proliferation indexes; high-grade tumour; exclusion of metastatic origin.

Desirable: coexisting ductal carcinoma in situ and/or invasive breast carcinoma of other type.

Staging

NECs are staged similarly to other types of invasive breast carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

SCNEC is significantly associated with a worse prognosis than other breast carcinomas with neuroendocrine differentiation {25399232}. The outcomes of patients with breast SCNEC were superior to those of patients with stage-matched small cell lung carcinoma, except in patients with distant disease {25853028}. It was reported that radiation therapy is not associated with a significant difference in survival for patients with either localized or regional disease {25853028}. Localized SCNEC is usually managed similarly to usual NST breast cancer. In the metastatic SCNEC setting, regimens that are implemented in small cell lung cancer are usually attempted. There is some evidence that ER-positive SCNEC may respond to hormone therapy {22424945}.
