5.2.2 Nipple adenoma

Definition

Nipple adenoma is a benign epithelial proliferative lesion of the breast featuring ductal/tubular structures involving the superficial duct orifices of the nipple, the surrounding stroma, and often the contiguous overlying epidermis.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: nipple duct adenoma.

Not recommended: florid papillomatosis of the nipple; erosive adenomatosis of the nipple; papillary adenoma of the nipple; papillomatosis of the nipple; superficial papillary adenomatosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Nipple adenoma involves the superficial portion of the nipple and is often contiguous with the epidermis.

Clinical features

Most nipple adenomas are detected when of small size owing to changes in the skin. If not excised, nipple adenomas may grow for many years {3006526;38526685} and form large masses {24716784;38526685}. Clinically, nipple adenoma presents as a small skin papule or nodule in 50% of cases, often with erythema, scaling, and erosion of the skin {3006526;37272622} due to replacement of the epidermis by glandular epithelium (hence the term “erosive adenomatosis”). The resulting bleeding and/or exudative crust may cause discomfort and is sometimes described as nipple discharge. Epidermal involvement may mimic the clinical appearance of mammary Paget disease. Mammography cannot usually identify the lesion owing to its superficial location and small size. Ultrasound may detect a circumscribed mass for larger adenomas, with or without cystic components {23341397;27647201;26548327;37272622;35915844;35242682}, and MRI may show nipple enhancement {16365591;26548327;37272622;35915844;35242682}, but the findings are often nonspecific.

Epidemiology

Nipple adenoma is rare, and information on epidemiology is mainly based on single case reports or small series. It occurs in female patients and rarely in male patients across a wide age range (5 months to 89 years). It is most commonly described in women in their fifth decade of life {3006526;37272622}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Twelve of 24 nipple adenomas (50%) in one series had activating PIK3CA mutations {27441415}, including one tumour with PIK3CA NM_006218.4:c.3140A>G p.(His1047Arg) and KRAS NM_004985.5:c.183A>C p.(Gln61His) mutations. Two tumours harboured a BRAF NM_004333.6:c.1799T>A p.(Val600Glu) mutation (also known as BRAF V600E) {27441415}, a molecular alteration also common in tubular adenoma of the skin (66% of cases) and syringocystadenoma papilliferum (50–64% of cases) {35158743}.

Macroscopic appearance

Nipple adenomas are rubbery, white to grey, and range from 20 to 40 mm {37272622;38526685}.

Histopathology

Nipple adenoma forms a nodular mass in the superficial aspect of the nipple, often in continuity with the overlying epidermis. On low-power examination it often appears wedge-shaped and symmetrical. It consists of tubular elements lined by glandular epithelium surrounded by myoepithelium. The glandular epithelium often replaces the squamous epithelium at a duct orifice, causing erosion, haemorrhage, and inflammation of the skin surface. Nipple adenoma may arise from and incorporate lactiferous ducts, and shows various combinations of sclerosing adenosis, papilloma, and usual ductal hyperplasia; the latter occasionally shows focal central necrosis and an architectural arrangement of the cells that may mimic ductal carcinoma in situ. Superficial keratin cysts and squamous metaplasia may be seen. Nipple adenomas are benign, but there are rare reports of ductal carcinoma in situ involving an adenoma, or invasive carcinoma contiguous with an adenoma {8532696;3006526;4350340;11091632;4284848;20408826;5123518}. Rarely a nipple adenoma harbours atypical ductal hyperplasia {37272622}. A case of low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma arising in a nipple adenoma has been reported {31705759}.

Differential diagnosis

Nipple adenoma may mimic invasive carcinoma clinically, as both may present with palpable ulcerative haemorrhagic masses {29431468}, but they can be distinguished on morphological grounds, supported by immunohistochemical studies in difficult cases. Nipple ulceration frequently raises clinical concern for mammary Paget disease, but the histological features of the latter are absent. Usual ductal hyperplasia is commonly seen in nipple adenoma and may raise the differential diagnosis of ductal carcinoma in situ. Immunohistochemical identification of myoepithelial cells around the ducts and tubules, and heterogeneous CK5/6 and ER expression in the epithelium support a benign proliferation {20463570}. Syringomatous tumour occurs in the nipple but consists of small epithelial nests and cords growing around the lactiferous sinuses and into the smooth muscle of the areola without involvement of the epidermis. Intraductal papillomas occur deeper within the lactiferous sinuses and do not involve the overlying skin.

Cytology

Nipple adenoma can be assessed by FNAB or brush cytopathology when it ulcerates. FNAB produces moderately cellular smears consisting of small to predominantly large ductal epithelial cell tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells and bare bipolar nuclei, macrophages, and haemosiderophages {26011103}. The clinical presentation and the cytopathological presence of isolated epithelial cells and/or coagulative necrosis often result in a categorization of atypical or suspicious {24975720;25585841}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nodular lesion composed of ductal/tubular structures involving the superficial duct orifices of the nipple and its surrounding stroma, contiguous with the overlying epidermis, with preserved myoepithelial cells.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry to identify a preserved myoepithelial cell layer and a heterogeneous cell population in problematic cases.

Staging

Nipple adenoma is a benign lesion and is not staged.

Prognosis and prediction

Nipple adenomas may recur if not completely excised. Limited surgery, including Mohs micrographic surgery or cryosurgery, may be used to preserve the nipple {27045746;24975720;28960299;27345934}. The subsequent risk of developing breast cancer does not appear to be elevated beyond that associated with other proliferative breast lesions.
