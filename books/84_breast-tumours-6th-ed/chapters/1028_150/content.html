Definition

Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants in STK11 and is associated with mucocutaneous melanin pigmentation, gastrointestinal polyposis, and a high lifetime risk of gastrointestinal and extraintestinal cancers, including pancreatic, breast, and gonadal tumours.

Localization

The polyps in PJS typically occur in the small intestine (in 95% of patients with PJS). About 25% of patients have polyps in the colon and stomach {26721304}. The second most common organ site for malignancy in women after the colorectum is the breast, with a cumulative breast cancer risk of as much as 54% at age 65 years (see #48430Table 10.12) and a mean age at diagnosis of 37 years {16707622}.

Clinical features

Presenting symptoms include abdominal pain, intestinal bleeding, anaemia, and intussusception, which typically manifest in the first two decades of life {17311531}. If present, the characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation allows diagnosis of asymptomatic patients in familial cases, but the characteristic Peutz–Jeghers polyps constitute the main clinical hallmark.

PJS is associated with a moderate or high risk of a range of malignancies, with an overall risk of any cancer by the age of 70 years of 81% {16707622;11113065;3587280}. Well-documented extraintestinal tumours include carcinomas of the breast and pancreas and otherwise rare gonadal lesions, including sex cord tumours with annular tubules of the ovary and Sertoli cell tumours of the testis.

Epidemiology

The incidence of PJS is estimated at roughly one tenth that of familial adenomatous polyposis, with an estimated incidence of 1 case per 50 000–200 000 births {25645574}. PJS onset may occur at any age and the risks of gastrointestinal and extragastrointestinal cancer are high {36517957}.

Etiology

PJS is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, but approximately 45% of patients with PJS are de novo cases. A germline variant resulting in loss of function in the tumour suppressor gene STK11 (formerly LKB1), encoding a serine/threonine kinase, can be found in > 90% of patients with PJS. Approximately 80–85% of alterations are STK11 point mutations and small intragenic insertions or deletions, including splice site variants {17924967;15608654}. The remaining cases result from larger rearrangements such as exon to whole gene deletion or partial duplication of STK11 {20301443}. There is no clear evidence of other genetic causes of PJS or genotype–phenotype associations. Patients with somatic mosaicism have rarely been reported {32504210}.

Pathogenesis

The direct precursor to gastrointestinal cancer in patients with PJS remains unknown {16344569}. Peutz–Jeghers polyps are probably an epiphenomenon to the cancer-prone condition and not obligate malignant precursors {16344569;21940722}. There are no data on the pathogenesis of breast cancer in patients with PJS.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance is not known to be at variance with that of sporadic breast cancer.

Histopathology

There are few data on breast cancer phenotypes in PJS. Still, the minimal data suggest that breast cancers in PJS are likely to be of intermediate-high grade, ER-positive, and ERBB2 (HER2)-negative {30689838}. Gastrointestinal Peutz–Jeghers polyps have been described in detail in the Digestive system tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO Classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). http://publications.iarc.fr/579 ]].

Cytology

The cytology is not known to be at variance with that of sporadic breast cancer.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Sequencing and copy-number analyses can identify causative STK11 germline variants in most patients with PJS, although diagnosis is typically made based on the clinical phenotype.

Staging

The staging is the same as for sporadic breast cancer.

Prognosis and prediction

In families where an STK11 pathogenic variant has been identified, predictive testing should be offered to at-risk family members. The prognosis for patients with PJS is now mainly determined by the risk of malignancy, and increased cancer mortality has been shown in PJS {21205875}. Patients with PJS should be surveilled to prevent gastrointestinal complications and to detect cancer of the colon, stomach, small bowel, pancreas, breast, ovary, uterus, cervix, and testes {25645574}.

The risk of breast cancer in women with PJS is within the same range as for women affected by BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathogenic variants (40–85% lifetime risk for breast cancer). Therefore, consensus opinion surveillance recommendations by groups that contain breast cancer experts include monthly breast self-examination starting at age 18 years, biannual clinical breast examination starting at age 25 years, annual breast MRI from ages 25–29 years, and mammography with consideration of tomosynthesis (3D mammography) alternating every 6 months with breast MRI with contrast from ages 30 to 75 years {35487791}. The option of prophylactic mastectomy might be discussed on a case-by-case basis, factoring in family history. In patients with PJS, referral to a breast cancer specialist for management of breast cancer surveillance is reasonable, and a multidisciplinary approach including a breast surgeon is recommended when prophylactic mastectomy is being considered. Specific data for breast cancer outcomes in patients with PJS are not available.
