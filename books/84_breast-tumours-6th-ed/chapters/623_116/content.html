6.2.3 Atypical vascular lesions

Definition

Atypical vascular lesions are atypical vascular proliferations on irradiated skin that are often small and multiple.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: atypical vascular proliferation, atypical postradiation vascular proliferation.

Not recommended: benign lymphangiomatous papules {10564386}.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The localization is strictly limited to previously irradiated skin. There is a strong predilection for the breast or the anterior chest wall after radiation treatment in the setting of breast-conserving therapy or as an adjunct to mastectomy for breast cancer {7801888;16006792;17357996;18551753;24943869}. Any anatomical site may be affected, depending on the underlying reason for irradiation and the area to which radiation has been administered {11859204;16006792}. The diagnosis of atypical vascular lesion typically requires the presence of a clinically detected skin lesion.

Clinical features

Atypical vascular lesions present as small, brown to erythematous papules and rarely plaques measuring about 5 mm {16006792;18551753}. They are frequently multiple at presentation and typically occur within 3–6 years after radiation treatment. The postradiation latency period may be as short as 1 year.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology is determined by the underlying reason for radiation treatment, the vast majority of described cases being women in their late sixth to early seventh decade of life who have received radiation treatment for breast cancer {16006792;17357996;18551753}. The occurrence of these lesions after radiation has been described with similar frequency in patients who have undergone either breast-conserving therapy or total mastectomy {16006792;30251277}.

Etiology

Atypical vascular lesions are directly associated with radiation treatment. The median total administered radiation dose is 50 Gy {16006792}.

Pathogenesis

Atypical vascular lesions are thought to arise secondary to lymphatic obstruction resulting from radiotherapy, with subsequent dilation of superficial vascular lumina {24943869;37121782}. Their precise pathogenesis remains unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

Atypical vascular lesions are papular and often erythematous.

Histopathology

Atypical vascular lesions are relatively circumscribed, often with somewhat wedge-shaped outlines. The proliferation is typically centred in the superficial dermis to mid-dermis, with rare extension into the deep dermis, but the subcutaneous tissues are typically spared. There are two histopathological subtypes with overlapping histological features. The more common lymphatic pattern is composed of irregularly shaped, thin-walled vascular channels with a branching and anastomosing growth. Lymphatic valve-like structures may be evident. The vessels are lined by a single layer of endothelial cells, which may show hobnailing and nuclear hyperchromasia. True cytological atypia or endothelial cell multilayering is not observed. Rare examples are characterized by more-dilated vascular spaces or focal dissecting growth through the dermal collagen bundles. Histologically, the vascular pattern resembles microvenular haemangioma or hobnail haemangioma with thin capillary channels lined by flattened to hobnail endothelium. This form often lacks a lobular growth pattern and may exhibit extravasated red blood cells or haemosiderin deposition. Individual lesions may show composite features of both vascular and lymphatic patterns.

The endothelial cells express the endothelial cell markers CD31, ERG, and variably CD34. The lymphatic pattern also usually expresses podoplanin (recognized by D2-40). In contrast, the vascular pattern is typically negative for podoplanin, but there is a layer of surrounding pericytes as highlighted by SMA staining {18551753}. Unlike most secondary cutaneous angiosarcomas, atypical vascular lesions are reported to lack immunoreactivity for PRAME {37477762;39152799}.

Atypical vascular lesions lack MYC amplification as demonstrated by FISH, and there is no MYC overexpression by immunohistochemistry, unlike in most secondary cutaneous angiosarcomas {20949568;21909081;22121953;24457083}. These are important ancillary tools in diagnostically challenging cases.

Cytology

The diagnosis of atypical vascular lesions is by core needle biopsy or surgical pathology.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: superficial dermal-based growth; lesional circumscription parallel to the epidermis; narrow vascular spaces lined by a single layer of hobnail endothelial cells; lack of infiltrative growth, cytological atypia, and multilayering.

Desirable: no MYC amplification by FISH; no MYC overexpression by immunohistochemistry in diagnostically challenging cases.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The disease may be complicated by local recurrences or the development of further atypical vascular lesions in the radiation field over time. Progression to angiosarcoma is exceedingly rare and remains controversial, because the vast majority of atypical vascular lesions have a benign clinical course {16006792;18551753;24943869;32043616;36137938}. The diagnostic biopsy must be representative of the underlying lesion to avoid missing an underlying angiosarcoma owing to sampling error {17572278}.
