2.2.2 Sclerosing adenosis

Definition

Sclerosing adenosis is a lobulocentric proliferation of benign glandular structures distorted by sclerosis, arising from the terminal duct lobular unit.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Sclerosing adenosis shows no characteristic location or laterality.

Clinical features

Sclerosing adenosis is a frequent incidental microscopic finding in breast specimens removed for other indications. Many lesions are associated with microcalcifications and/or architectural distortion detected mammographically, which leads to core needle biopsy {28357084}. On occasion, large areas of adenosis may present as a non-palpable, image-detected mass lesion {15074558;26838282;29436376}.

Epidemiology

Sclerosing adenosis is described in 10–30% of women {31554551} and in a wide age group, but it most commonly occurs in women in the third or fourth decade of life {15074558;29436376}.

Etiology

No specific factors have been identified that are etiologically linked to sclerosing adenosis.

Pathogenesis

Sclerosing adenosis is a non-neoplastic proliferation, and there are no known molecular or genetic abnormalities.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance depends on the size and histopathological features of the lesion. Because most adenosis lesions are small, they are usually grossly inapparent. On rare occasions, they may form a firm, rubbery, grey mass, called nodular adenosis or adenosis tumour. They can be associated with microcalcifications, which, if abundant, may impart a gritty texture on the cut surface {15074558;26838282}.

Histopathology

Sclerosing adenosis is characterized by enlarged lobular units showing a proliferation of small glandular/acinar structures that are compressed or distorted by stromal collagen {15074558;26838282}. The acini are small, round to somewhat angular, and generally uniform in size. Microcalcifications are common in sclerosing adenosis and may be present in either sclerotic stroma or glandular lumina. The epithelial cells are small and usually cuboidal to columnar and have centrally located nuclei. Myoepithelial cells at the periphery of the acini are usually easily identified by their hyperchromatic spindle-shaped nuclei {26838282}. The ratio of epithelial to myoepithelial cells varies. In some cases, epithelial cells appear more atrophic, with preferential proliferation of myoepithelial cells. In others, both the epithelial and myoepithelial cell components become hyperplastic. Both the epithelial and myoepithelial components may show metaplastic changes. Examples of sclerosing adenosis in which the epithelial cells show apocrine morphology are referred to as apocrine adenosis (see section Apocrine adenosis and apocrine adenoma).

Sclerosing adenosis is the most common benign breast lesion misdiagnosed as malignant {38923027}. The amount of intralobular stromal collagen varies and results in compression of the acini, which may confer a growth pattern that mimics infiltration. Glandular compression and distortion are more prominent in the centre of the lesion and may result in luminal obliteration, resulting in a solid or cord-like growth pattern {26838282}. Sclerosing adenosis may extend into adipose tissue, but the lobulocentric growth pattern is maintained. The finding of this lobulocentric growth pattern on low-power microscopic examination is very useful in establishing the diagnosis.

Atypical epithelial cell proliferations and carcinoma in situ of both ductal and lobular types may involve sclerosing adenosis and can cause diagnostic problems. In particular, carcinoma in situ involving sclerosing adenosis may mimic invasive carcinoma, and this may be particularly problematic in small samples (e.g. core needle biopsy samples) {16540734;23486609;11521220}. Identification of the lobulocentric growth pattern of the underlying sclerosing adenosis and the presence of myoepithelial cells around ducts are useful in establishing the correct diagnosis. Immunohistochemical staining to highlight myoepithelial cells may be required to confirm the in situ nature of the process. In difficult cases the use of at least two myoepithelial cell markers, with a combination of nuclear and cytoplasmic staining, is helpful.

Although rare, sclerosing adenosis may be present around nerves, which may mimic perineural invasion by invasive carcinoma {11521220}. Appreciation of the benign cytological features and the presence of myoepithelial cells around the glandular structures assists in the distinction from invasive carcinoma.

The tubular pattern of sclerosing adenosis, which comprises a haphazard proliferation of elongated benign tubules, sometimes extending into fat, differs from classic sclerosing adenosis by the lack of obvious lobular architecture and wider separation of tubules. It may also be mistaken for invasive carcinoma, particularly when involved by an atypical proliferation {8540608}.

Cytology

The cellularity is usually low with portions of lobules showing crush artefact, distorted tubules showing epithelial cells with bland nuclear chromatin, myoepithelial cells and interposed basement membrane and sclerotic stroma, and small cohesive tissue fragments of ductal epithelial cells with myoepithelial cells. Bare bipolar nuclei may be scant. Nuclear cytopathological atypia is absent {22706864}. The differential diagnosis includes intraductal papilloma and normal lobules.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: lobulocentric architecture; proliferation of small, compressed glandular or acinar structures distorted by stromal collagen; presence of basement membrane and retained myoepithelial cell layer around glandular or acinar structures.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry (e.g. p63, calponin, SMMHC) for further identification of myoepithelial cells.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Sclerosing adenosis is associated with a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in the risk of developing subsequent breast cancer, which is similar to the level of risk associated with other proliferative lesions without atypia {24510013;25838080;28798985}. The diagnosis of sclerosing adenosis is not an indication for surgical excision. If there is an atypical epithelial proliferation, clinical and imaging correlation and multidisciplinary assessment are required to inform subsequent management.
