7.2.2 Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) not otherwise specified (NOS) is a diffuse neoplasm of large B lymphocytes lacking features of other defined types of large B-cell lymphoma.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: primary breast diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Primary breast DLBCL arises in and is confined to the breasts with or without involvement of regional lymph nodes. Both breasts are equally affected; 2–5% of patients present with bilateral involvement. Approximately 60–70% of patients present with localized, stage IE disease, and the remaining patients have regional, usually axillary lymphadenopathy at diagnosis {18636016;29697005;35402679}.

Clinical features

The median patient age is in the sixth or seventh decade, but there is a wide age range and > 95% of cases arise in women {17932394;29423097;29697005}. Most patients present with a painless, palpable mass growing in size. Regional lymphadenopathy occurs in a subset of patients. In about 10% of patients, the lesion is detected by screening mammography {18636016;29423097}. B-type symptoms occur in 5% of patients {23712546;29697005} and skin manifestations are rare.

Epidemiology

Primary breast DLBCL represents < 1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas and about 2% of all extranodal lymphomas {17932394;28376147;29423097}. Its incidence has increased over the last four decades {28376147}. In the USA, the incidence is highest in Native Americana, Alaskans, and Asian/Pacific Islanders, and is low in African Americans {29423097}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

There are no known specific risk factors. Using gene expression profiling, DLBCL-NOS can be divided into germinal-centre B-cell (GCB) and activated B-cell (ABC) cell-of-origin subtypes. The mutation profiles of these subtypes overlap, but some mutations are preferentially associated with each subtype {28985567;29666115}. More-granular molecular subgroups with different biological and prognostic significance have been defined {29641966;29713087;32187361;32289277} but are not yet being used for therapeutic decisions. In most studies of primary breast DLBCL, 50–90% of cases are of ABC or non-GCB type {15492762;18636016}. Chromosomal translocations involving MYC, BCL2, and BCL6 occur less often than in systemic DLBCL. Cases with MYC and BCL6 (without BCL2) rearrangements are considered a genetic subgroup of DLBCL-NOS or high-grade B-cell lymphoma NOS (based on morphology).

The mutation landscape of DLBCL-NOS is fairly well characterized, but few data exist for primary breast DLBCL. MYD88 and CD79B mutations are common, in approximately 50% and 33% of cases, respectively {26752547}. Targeted sequencing on 18 specimens showed mutations in PIM1 (50%); MYD88 (39%); CD79B, PRDM1, or CARD11 (17% each); and KMT2D, TNFAIP3, or CREBBP (11% each) {29262531}. The available data suggest that PIM1, MYD88, CD79B, and CARD11 mutations activate the NF-κB pathway {29262531} and promote immune evasion, both hallmarks of the MCD/C5/MYD88 molecular subgroups of DLBCL {29641966;29713087;32187361;32289277}. Primary breast DLBCL biology bears a resemblance to lymphomas arising in immune privileged sites {38635786;37706649}.

Macroscopic appearance

Median tumour size is 30–40 mm (range: 10–200 mm). Primary breast DLBCL tends to be well circumscribed, unlike systemic DLBCL involving the breast {17932394;18636016}.

Histopathology

Morphology

Primary breast DLBCL diffusely replaces breast parenchyma and is composed of large lymphoma cells that can resemble centroblasts or less often immunoblasts, or rarely can be anaplastic. The neoplastic cells can preferentially involve breast lobules, imparting a nodular appearance. Lymphoepithelial lesions are uncommon {18636016;22569408}.

Immunophenotype

The lymphoma cells are positive for B-cell markers, including CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a, and PAX5 {15492762;18636016;22569408}. Most cases are positive for surface or cytoplasmic immunoglobulin. Ki-67 staining reveals high proliferation, with a median Ki-67 proliferation index of 70–80% {15492762;22569408}. ABC-type/non-GCB-type tumours usually express IRF4 (MUM1) and FOXP1, and GCB-type tumours are positive for CD10, BCL6, GCET1, and/or LMO2. BCL2 is often positive. MYC can be expressed in highly proliferative neoplasms, but usually not uniformly or brightly unless MYC is rearranged. The neoplastic cells are usually negative for CD30, and pan–T-cell antigens. There is no evidence of EBV infection {18636016;22569408}.

Differential diagnosis

Other types of DLBCL must be excluded. Systemic DLBCL-NOS often involves the breast(s) and presents as a breast mass {18636016;29423097}. In one study, the ratio of primary to secondary involvement by DLBCL was approximately 1:1 {18636016}. These patients have a history of disease and/or concurrent DLBCL-NOS elsewhere. DLBCL with MYC and BCL6 (without BCL2) rearrangements rarely occurs as primary breast lymphoma and systemic DLBCL-NOS needs to be excluded. A rare form of EBV-positive large B-cell lymphoma can arise in association with breast implants and is probably closely related to fibrin-associated large B-cell lymphoma {34226673}. A starry-sky pattern and high proliferation rate (Ki-67) with absence of BCL2 expression and presence of MYC rearrangement distinguishes Burkitt lymphoma from primary breast lymphoma.

Cytology

Smear preparations and cell blocks are highly cellular with dispersed intermediate to large atypical lymphoid cells in a monotonous pattern that is diagnostic of a large cell lymphoma. Occasionally, DLBCL can produce scattered loosely cohesive tissue fragments of large atypical cells mimicking carcinomas.

The cytopathological diagnosis of DLBCL by FNAB is a well-established, safe, efficient methodology and is a valid diagnostic approach, but the subtyping of the lymphoma requires appropriate application of core needle biopsy and ancillary tests including flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, cytogenetics, and increasingly molecular pathology applied in an integrated multidisciplinary diagnostic approach {16339755;16222689;23999127;31352987;32294049;29130993}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular studies are usually not required to establish the diagnosis. Primary breast DLBCL usually carries monoclonal IGH rearrangements. T-cell receptor genes are in germline configuration.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: restriction to the breast with or without regional lymph node involvement; diffuse proliferation of large atypical lymphoid cells; B-cell lineage; exclusion of other defined types of large B-cell lymphoma.

Desirable: cell-of-origin subtyping; reporting of isolated MYC, BCL2, or BCL6 rearrangements or dual MYC and BCL6 rearrangements.

Staging

The Lugano classification for staging has been adopted by the ninth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification {25113753}. Staging of patients with bilateral primary breast DLBCL is controversial (stage IE vs IV).

Prognosis and prediction

There is no role for surgical excision; patients are treated with systemic immunochemotherapy, often with involved-field radiotherapy {17932394;30302857}. The 5-year overall survival and event-free survival rates in various studies have been 70–80% and 60–65%, respectively. Relapse occurs in a subset of patients, not uncommonly after 24 months {29697005;30302857}; 10–15% of cases relapse in the same or the contralateral breast, and 15–25% of cases relapse in the CNS {17932394;29697005;30302857}.

The International Prognostic Index (IPI) is useful for prognostication {17932394;24467658}. The cell of origin (GCB vs ABC) is of unproven prognostic value but can guide therapy, particularly at the time of relapse. More-refined molecular subtyping of DLBCL-NOS {29641966;29713087;32187361;32289277} and stromal signatures have prognostic power within the cell-of-origin subtypes or independent of the subtype distinction {19038878;31530861}.
