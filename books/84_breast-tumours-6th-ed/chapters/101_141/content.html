10.1 Genetic tumour syndromes of the breast: Introduction

In this section, we discuss the genetics of familial predisposition to breast cancer and some of the established and emerging genes that are a source of discussion within genetics clinics. BRCA1 and BRCA2 have now been well established, and PALB2 is also considered an important predisposition gene; all three would be considered for testing in patients suspected to have a familial predisposition. However, many other genes (ATM, CHEK2, etc.) have limited evidence for their use in routine testing – mostly due to limited data on penetrance and frequency in the population and a lack of data on ethnic variations. In part because of these limitations, variants of unknown significance (VUSs) are commonly encountered in these genes, making patient management a challenge. Nonetheless, most panels would also include ATM, BARD1, CHEK2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, and TP53 {33471991}.

There is now increasing interest in multigene breast cancer susceptibility and the use of polygenic risk scores; these topics are covered in the multigene section at the end of this section (see section The polygenic component of breast cancer susceptibility). #50964Table 10.01 lists details of the familial syndromes covered in this section, as well as details of some additional syndromes of relevance to breast cancer that are not covered in this volume because breast cancer is not the major feature of the syndrome; most of these syndromes are covered in detail in other volumes of this series.

Recent technological advances in high-throughput and large-scale sequencing testing, which is increasingly including both tumour and non-tumour (normal) comparisons, have enabled the identification of germline variants that may identify underlying hereditary syndromes that otherwise would not have been recognized. This has been exemplified in patients with a variety of heterogeneous tumours when universal tumour–normal sequencing is performed on a large institutional patient population {28873162}. In addition, whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded specimens is feasible and potentially applicable in a routine clinical environment {29388947}, suggesting a path forwards beyond targeted sequencing. There is growing awareness of the ethical considerations of tumour sequencing {30125358}.

Screening for familial predisposition can also impact the pathologist. Clinical, morphological, and immunohistochemical features of tumours can be identified by pathologists as being potentially related to some hereditary syndromes. For example, hereditary syndromes associated with BRCA1 and BRCA2 germline pathogenic variants tend to be related to bilateral tumours arising mostly at a young age, and in the case of BRCA1-associated syndrome, to well-demarcated tumours composed of solid nests and a lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, prominent nuclear atypia, a high grade, and high mitotic activity. A panel of immunohistochemical markers has been proven to be useful in discriminating between BRCA1/BRCA2-related and sporadic cancers {29979200}.

Cowden syndrome has been associated with molecular apocrine differentiation using expression profiling {20712882}; however, a recent study of patients with germline pathogenic PTEN variants did not have a specific morphological phenotype {39567325}.

Specific breast cancer subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer are an additional indicator of potential underlying germline variants associated with homologous recombination deficiency. For example, pathogenic variants in BRCA1 occur in 14.8% of patients with triple-negative breast cancer {30257646}, whereas none of the carriers of ATM, RAD51D, CHEK2, or PALB2 pathogenic variants have a family history. This concept of testing triple-negative breast cancer patients irrespective of family history or patient age therefore emphasizes the importance of breast cancer subtyping through detailed morphological and immunohistochemical analysis. Identifying patients with high-risk variants in cancer susceptibility genes by family history alone is not sufficient {39858629}.

Patients with homologous recombination deficiency, which is commonly associated with germline and/or somatic variants in BRCA1 and/or BRCA2, show sensitivity to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors {30181424}. The emerging use of immunohistochemistry for markers like RAD51 to evaluate prediction for PARP inhibition in breast cancer is therefore intrinsically linked with the further merging of the disciplines of pathology and genetics {30377213}. Pathologists can assist in the identification of these patients by knowing the situations in which these biomarkers are best applied and the context of their interpretation.
