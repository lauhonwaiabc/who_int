Definition

Acinic cell–like carcinoma is an invasive breast carcinoma composed of granular and clear epithelial cells, some of which contain intracytoplasmic zymogen granules, with various architectural patterns ranging from microglandular to solid.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: acinic cell carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Acinic cell–like carcinoma can affect any breast quadrant, with no site predilection.

Clinical features

Breast acinic cell–like carcinoma usually affects adult women (aged 20–80 years). A single case affecting the male breast has been reported {11091630}. The clinical presentation is similar to that of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST).

Epidemiology

Breast acinic cell–like carcinoma is a rare type of invasive breast carcinoma, originally described by Roncaroli et al. in 1996 {8865856} and then subsequently better delineated by Damiani et al. in 2000 {10963383}. Approximately 70 cases of women aged 35–80 years have been described in the literature {28043647;30305896;33827325;35003741;35512568;35894298;36579416;36828363;37809983;38292630;38298310;39049123;39413470}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Breast glandular epithelium can rarely show acinic-like differentiation {19207954} that may explain the development of acinic cell carcinoma. One case arose in a patient with a germline BRCA1 mutation {23374397}. Breast acinic cell–like carcinomas show a DNA copy-number and mutation landscape similar to that of triple-negative breast carcinomas of conventional histology or diagnosed in association with microglandular adenosis (MGA), harbouring mutations of TP53, PIK3CA, KMT2D ERBB4, ERBB3, NEB, BRCA1, MTOR, CTNNB1, INPP4B, FGFR2, and MLH1 {28972583;26011570;25688711;27713419;31361912;36332545;36828363;38304172;39286022;39389890}. A clonal relationship has been demonstrated between MGA and coexisting acinic cell–like carcinoma {27713419}. The acinic cell–like carcinoma and high-grade components of mixed carcinomas were found to be clonally related, with additional somatic mutations in the high-grade component in 1 case {26011570}. Acinic cell–like carcinoma of the salivary gland has been associated with a recurrent t(4;9)(q13;q31) genomic rearrangement that results in NR4A3 overexpression {30664630;31094928}. However, this rearrangement and NR4A3 overexpression has not been identified in acinic cell–like carcinoma of the breast {36828363;39934983}. Similarly, the mutation profiles of breast acinic cell–like carcinomas differ from those of acinic cell carcinomas of the salivary glands {25688711}. Thus, acinic cell–like carcinomas of the breast appear to be more closely related to MGA and triple-negative breast cancers than they are to acinic cell carcinomas of the salivary gland.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of acinic cell–like carcinoma is the same as that of invasive breast carcinoma NST, being characterized by firm, irregular nodules, ranging from 10 to 71 mm {28043647;30305896;33827325;35003741;35512568;35894298;36579416;36828363;37809983;38292630;38298310;39049123;39413470;36332545}. One case arising within a fibroadenoma has been reported {24859396}.

Histopathology

At low magnification, acinic cell–like carcinoma can show a great variety of architectural patterns, ranging from a microglandular proliferation similar to that seen in MGA to more solid areas that may have central necrosis. Tumours lacking clear cytoplasm or eosinophilic granules arising in a spectrum of MGA, atypical MGA, and tumours considered by some to be MGA-like carcinoma may form part of a constellation of MGA-like growth pattern tumours, which includes acinic cell–like carcinoma. The two architectural patterns (MGA-like and solid areas with necrosis) frequently merge together. Owing to the high variability in architectural patterns, diagnosis is based primarily on recognition of the cytological features. At least some of the neoplastic cells have abundant, variably eosinophilic and basophilic granular cytoplasm, imparting a variegated appearance {27067778}. PASD staining reveals intracellular, eosinophilic granules that range in appearance from fine to coarse. lntracytoplasmic granules are clearly evident on ultrastructural examination {10963383}. Eosinophilic granules imparting the appearance of intestinal Paneth cells are uncommon in the breast, but when present in carcinomas, Paneth-like cells most commonly suggest acinic cell–like carcinoma {21163678}. The cytoplasm is sometimes clear. The nucleus is centrally located and atypical, with distinct nucleoli. The neoplastic cells show various degrees of atypia. Cellular atypia and mitotic figures are more prominent in the solid areas. Ductal carcinoma in situ of high nuclear grade can be present. Acinic cell–like carcinoma may be mixed with invasive carcinoma NST or metaplastic carcinoma {26011570;25688711}. After neoadjuvant chemotherapy, acinic cell–like carcinoma can exhibit areas identical to those seen in classic and atypical MGA {33649459}.

Breast acinic cell–like carcinoma is usually triple-negative, although rare ER-positive and/or PR-positive cases have also been reported {36332545}. The neoplastic cells are positive for markers of serous differentiation, such as lysozyme and α1-antichymotrypsin. In addition, positivity for S100, EMA, and low-molecular-weight keratins is a consistent feature. High-molecular-weight keratins (e.g. CK5/6) can also be detected in a subset of cases. Focal positivity for GCDFP-15 can be seen {36332545;39389890;39413470}. AR immunoreactivity has been documented {24859406;36332545;39389890;39413470}.

The differential diagnosis includes a wide range of breast tumours, including high-grade invasive carcinomas. Differential diagnosis is based on cytological features, especially the presence of intracytoplasmic granules, and markers of serous acinar differentiation. Acinic cell–like carcinomas with bland nuclear morphology can raise suspicion of secretory carcinoma of the breast, but acinic cell–like carcinoma lacks the ETV6::NTRK3 translocation that is typical of secretory carcinoma {18462362}. MGA also enters into the differential diagnosis of low-grade acinic cell–like carcinoma, and it is difficult to delineate the boundaries between atypical MGA and acinic cell–like carcinoma in some cases. Basement membrane stains are positive around the glands or nests of MGA whereas EMA is commonly negative. Lack of markers of serous differentiation may also assist.

Cytology

Breast acinic cell–like carcinomas, as defined in this edition, are extremely rare, and the FNAB findings have not been reported. There has been one FNAB published report of a single case of acinic cell carcinoma, which showed hypercellularity, with cells showing intracytoplasmic coarse granules {22734799}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Breast acinic cell–like carcinoma can be included in the triple-negative breast cancer group because it is most often negative for ER and PR, and ERBB2 amplification has not been demonstrated. In contrast to acinic cell carcinomas of the salivary glands, acinic cell–like carcinomas of the breast do not show t(4;9)(q13;q31) rearrangement and NR4A3 overexpression, and exhibit a molecular profile that is more similar to that of conventional triple-negative breast cancers and carcinomas arising in association with MGA. Absence of NR4A3 expression by immunohistochemistry is a useful diagnostic adjunct {36828363}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: MGA-like growth pattern; eosinophilic and basophilic granular cytoplasm.

Desirable: PASD-positive intracytoplasmic granules; positivity for S100, EMA, lysozyme, and α1-antichymotrypsin.

Staging

Acinic cell–like carcinoma is staged according to the criteria adopted for other types of breast carcinomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Because of the small number of cases reported with follow-up information, knowledge about the prognosis of acinic cell–like carcinoma is still limited {28043647}. The available data indicate that acinic cell–like carcinoma is a triple-negative carcinoma with intermediate aggressive potential. Approximately one quarter of published cases had axillary lymph node metastasis. Acinic cell–like carcinoma mixed with high-grade invasive carcinomas is more aggressive. A few cases with distant metastases have been reported, including to lung, liver, bone, peritoneum, and meninges. Nevertheless, most of the reported patients are alive with no evidence of recurrence 3–184 months after the diagnosis (mean: 42 months), especially in pure acinic cell–like carcinoma without a high-grade component {28043647;30305896;33827325;35003741;35512568;35894298;36579416;36828363;37809983;38292630;38298310;39049123;39413470}. Most of the reported patients underwent chemotherapy and radiotherapy in addition to surgery, but it has also been proposed that pure low-grade tumours may not benefit from systemic chemotherapy {34830849}. The available molecular evidence supports the contention that acinic cell–like carcinoma may be a precursor of more-aggressive forms of triple-negative breast carcinomas {26011570}.
