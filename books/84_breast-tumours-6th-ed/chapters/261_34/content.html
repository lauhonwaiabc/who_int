2.6.1 Non-invasive lobular neoplasia: Introduction

The term “lobular neoplasia” (LN) refers to the entire spectrum of atypical epithelial lesions originating in the terminal duct lobular unit and characterized by a proliferation of generally small, non-cohesive monomorphic cells, with or without pagetoid involvement of terminal ducts. The designations “atypical lobular hyperplasia” and “lobular carcinoma in situ” (LCIS) are widely used to describe the variable extent of involvement of individual lobular units {2986821;1748429}. The term “lobular neoplasia” does not encompass pleomorphic and florid LCIS.

The first clear clinicopathological description using the term “lobular carcinoma in situ” was published in 1941 {19970575} to describe the morphological similarities between the cells of what is now known as classic LCIS and those of an adjacent invasive lobular carcinoma. LCIS, in a way analogous to ductal carcinoma in situ, was thought to be a precursor of invasive lobular carcinoma. For the next several decades, patients with LCIS were routinely treated with mastectomy. In the late 1970s, Haagensen suggested the designation “lobular neoplasia” for the full spectrum of lesions encompassing atypical lobular hyperplasia and classic LCIS, based on his belief that LN represented a benign proliferation as only a risk factor for subsequent breast carcinoma in either breast {209887}. Although the term itself has not been universally adopted, the conceptual model of LN led to a paradigm shift in the way affected patients were clinically managed. By the 1990s, the practice of non-surgical management of patients with LN was fully adopted, and it continues today, with the exception of pleomorphic and florid LCIS subtypes.

Some of the important issues with LN relate to the appropriate nomenclature, the lack of specific mammographic abnormalities, its role as a risk indicator or a non-obligate precursor, conflicting ideas in the literature regarding its biological and clinical significance, personalized breast cancer surveillance in the case of LN diagnosis, the risk of development of invasive carcinoma, and the best clinical approach for the management of patients diagnosed with LN by core needle biopsy.

The clinical management of florid and pleomorphic LCIS is also a subject of debate.
