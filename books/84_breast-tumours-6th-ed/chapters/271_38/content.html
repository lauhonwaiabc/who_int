2.7.1 Ductal carcinoma in situ

Definition

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a non-invasive proliferation of cohesive neoplastic epithelial cells exhibiting a range of nuclear grades and architectural patterns confined to the mammary ductal-lobular system.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: intraductal carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

DCIS frequently shows one or more histological architectures (see Histopathology, below) but these are not considered discrete subtypes. For management, DCIS can be divided into different grades and receptor phenotypes.

Localization

DCIS can arise either in the breast or in accessory breast parenchyma.

Clinical features

In the pre-mammographic era, DCIS presented as a palpable mass, nipple discharge, or mammary Paget disease (see section Mammary Paget disease). Although these presentations still occur, as many as 85% of DCIS cases are now detected mammographically, typically as calcifications, and are impalpable. The mean patient age at diagnosis is 50–59 years. The disease is most often unilateral, but about 22% of women with DCIS in one breast develop either in situ or invasive carcinoma in the contralateral breast {1365682}. Both high- and low-grade DCIS are detectable on mammography, with high-grade DCIS classically presenting with linear, pleomorphic, or branching calcifications, and low-grade disease presenting with granular and amorphous patterns of calcifications. On MRI, DCIS (in particular high-grade DCIS) may appear as a non–mass like enhancement with delayed peak enhancement profiles. Although the sensitivity of MRI for DCIS is lower than that for invasive breast carcinoma (IBC), it is higher for high-grade DCIS than with mammography. Nevertheless, owing to access and costs, MRI is not widely used. Ultrasound changes are often subtle and nonspecific but DCIS is sometimes visualized as a mass. If a non-mass lesion is accompanied by calcifications or architectural distortion, it is likely to be malignant, and a biopsy is recommended {38243857}.

Epidemiology

Before the time of widespread mammographic screening, DCIS was infrequently identified, accounting for only 2–3% of palpable breast cancers. Because DCIS is now a predominantly radiologically identified lesion, it is most common in geographical areas with population-based breast cancer screening mammography programmes. As a result of mammographic diagnosis, the apparent incidence of DCIS in the United States has increased from 1.87 cases per 100 000 person-years in 1973–1975 to 32.5 cases per 100 000 person-years in 2005. DCIS is most commonly diagnosed in women aged > 50 years {20071685}, with 1 case identified per 1300 screening mammograms {32155777}.

Etiology

The risk factors for DCIS and IBC are similar, indicating a common etiology; germline mutations in hereditary breast cancer predisposition genes, family history, nulliparity, late age at first childbirth, late menopause, and elevated body mass index after menopause are all associated with an increased risk of DCIS, as are postmenopause hormone replacement therapy and high mammographic breast density {20956818}. There is some evidence that molecular events differ by race/ethnicity {39223670}.

Pathogenesis

The increase in lifetime risk of DCIS for a BRCA mutation carrier versus a non-carrier is estimated to be about 6-fold {23184082}. Similar associations of increased risk have been reported for 76 known IBC predisposition loci with DCIS {26884359}. The prevalence of germline BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations among women diagnosed with DCIS is similar to that among women diagnosed with IBC {15728167}, although this is likely to have changed with the uptake of prophylactic mastectomies.

The evidence for DCIS as a precursor of IBC includes series demonstrating high genomic concordance between synchronous DCIS and IBC {30284611}. Further, apart from a higher frequency of ERBB2 amplification in DCIS, many genes have similar mutation profiles in DCIS as in IBC, with frequent alterations in PIK3CA, GATA3, and TP53, the latter largely restricted to high-grade disease {28338653}. Similar to invasive carcinomas, there is evidence that DCIS lesions of low and high nuclear grade follow different pathways, have different molecular and genetic profiles, and show different rates of progression to IBC. Of note, the 3-tiered grading system for DCIS does not indicate progression from low, through intermediate, to high nuclear grade (although an admixture of grades may be seen). High levels of genomic instability are seen in DCIS of high nuclear grade, whereas DCIS of low nuclear grade shows fewer genomic alterations {17549568}. Distinctive chromosomal changes similar to those observed in grade 1 and grade 3 IBCs are seen in DCIS of low and high nuclear grade, respectively. DCIS cases with low nuclear grade are characterized by frequent 16q loss and 1q gain, whereas cases of DCIS with high nuclear grade often show gains of 5p, 8q, 17q, and 20q; amplifications of 11q13, 17q12, and 17q22-q24; and loss of 8p, 11q, 13q, and 14q {10398097;15297420}.

Methylation of genes seen in IBC is also reported in DCIS and is associated with features such as high nuclear grade, ERBB2 amplification, and negative ER and PR status {25331261;21710692}, and the same is true for genomic gains and losses {21547576}. However, none of the genomic changes identified in DCIS have yet been consistently associated with invasive progression or recurrence risk, and therefore none have proven clinical utility {26078038}. There is some evidence that progression of DCIS to IBC may involve an altered relationship between DCIS cell copy number and the immune microenvironment (e.g. as assessed by quantification of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes) {28611201}.

Macroscopic appearance

DCIS is rarely identified clinically or macroscopically as a mass, except for some papillary subtypes, which may present as well-defined, pale, soft masses. Most DCIS detected by mammography cannot be detected visually, and there is no consistent macroscopic appearance. Identification may require specimen (and specimen slice) X-ray for localization and tissue sampling. On cut sections, DCIS with prominent comedo-type necrosis may exude pale material from the ducts within poorly defined fibrous areas. A small proportion of high-grade DCIS may be extensive enough and have such an abundance of intraluminal necrosis or associated stromal reaction that it presents as multiple areas of round, pale comedonecrosis or a firm, gritty mass.

Histopathology

DCIS is characteristically a unifocal disease, limited to a single duct system, but it can extend into the lobules (lobular involvement). Reports of high frequencies of multifocality may represent misinterpretation of the 3D duct structures when examined histologically in two dimensions. High-grade DCIS typically grows in a continuous pattern; however, skip lesions as large as 10 mm may be seen in low-grade disease {7831530}, adding to the difficulty in assessment of focality, lesion size, and distance to surgical margins.

DCIS is highly heterogeneous histologically, biologically, genetically, and clinically (in terms of both diagnosis and behaviour). Classification systems have varied over time. Historically, DCIS was classified according to architectural pattern and categorized as solid, cribriform, micropapillary, or papillary types (for more information on the papillary type, see section Papillary ductal carcinoma in situ). Some systems also included comedo DCIS as an architectural type (although comedonecrosis can be seen in association with a range of architectural patterns), whereas others use the presence or absence of comedonecrosis in combination with nuclear grade for categorization. Comedonecrosis is seen as sheets of granular eosinophilic material within the duct lumen with associated karyorrhectic/nuclear debris. Necrosis has been stratified by the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) into central (comedo), where the central portion of an involved ductal space is replaced by an area of expansive necrosis detectable at low magnification, or focal (punctate), where small foci or single-cell necrosis (≤ 10%) that is indistinct at low magnification is present {35869801}. Nevertheless, comedonecrosis is poorly defined and the proportion of a duct lumen diameter with necrosis required for a diagnosis of comedonecrosis varies widely, even among breast pathologists {30980039}.

The use of cytonuclear morphology is recommended as the primary feature for histological grading of DCIS {19123730} [[Royal College of Pathologists Internet. London (UK): Royal College of Pathologists; 2019. Cancer datasets and tissue pathways. Available from: https://www.rcpath.org/profession/guidelines/cancer-datasets-and-tissue-pathways.html. ]]. Thus, DCIS is categorized as being of low, intermediate, or high nuclear grade. In general, there is greater homogeneity of nuclear grade than of architectural pattern {9306942}; however, it is not unusual to observe grade variation within DCIS, a reflection of intratumoural genetic heterogeneity {23890733}.

DCIS of low nuclear grade is composed of small, monomorphic cells, typically growing in a cribriform, micropapillary, or (less often) solid pattern and involving more than two complete spaces (or measuring > 2 mm; see section Atypical ductal hyperplasia). Low-grade solid DCIS may have microrosettes with small luminal spaces. Micropapillary and cribriform patterns are commonly admixed, but micropapillary DCIS in particular may be extensive {2535928}. The nuclei are uniform in size and shape, with a regular nuclear outline. The nuclei in DCIS of low nuclear grade are 1.5–2 times the size of an erythrocyte {19123730}. Mitotic figures are rare. Calcification is commonly seen in secretions within the luminal spaces and may be psammomatous. Necrosis, particularly comedo-type necrosis, is uncommon, but it does not preclude the diagnosis.

DCIS of intermediate nuclear grade is composed of cells that show moderate variability in size, shape, and polarization. The nuclei have variably coarse chromatin and sometimes have prominent nucleoli. Mitoses may be present. Necrosis (either punctate or comedo) may be seen. Calcifications may be present in secretions and/or in necrotic material.

DCIS of high nuclear grade is composed of large, atypical cells, most commonly with a solid architecture. However, cribriform and micropapillary patterns may be seen. Uncommonly, a single layer of large, highly atypical cells lines spaces in flat DCIS of high nuclear grade (previously called the pleomorphic subtype of clinging DCIS). Polarization around luminal spaces is minimal. The nuclei are large and typically pleomorphic, with irregular contours, coarse chromatin, and often prominent nucleoli. In high-grade DCIS, the nuclei are > 2.5 times the size of an erythrocyte in diameter {19123730}. Mitoses are usually conspicuous. Central comedonecrosis bearing calcification is often present but is not mandatory for the diagnosis.

Reporting of DCIS should follow the ICCR dataset and include core pathological parameters {35869801}.

Mammary Paget disease

High-grade DCIS may present as mammary Paget disease of the nipple (see section Mammary Paget disease), when it extends from the subareolar ducts along the basement membrane into the basal layers of the epidermis. Underlying high-grade DCIS is virtually always identified if carefully sought; occasionally invasive carcinoma is also identified, although less frequently now than in the historical literature. The epidermis, which may be hyperkeratotic and parakeratotic, contains an infiltrate of single (or clusters of) large pleomorphic high-grade DCIS cells. Occasionally some acinar structures may be seen. Immunohistochemistry can be helpful for confirmation of the diagnosis; the DCIS cells are positive for CK7, ERBB2 (HER2), and GATA3 {26944832;18240020}.

Other patterns

Uncommonly, DCIS is composed of apocrine, neuroendocrine, signet-ring, or clear cells. The neuroendocrine form often has a spindled cell pattern, at least focally. Squamous forms are described but are rare. There are few data on the prognostic impact of cytological morphologies, but these are also graded and reported histologically like the common architectural patterns.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of DCIS includes usual ductal hyperplasia (UDH) and atypical ductal hyperplasia at one end of the spectrum and invasive and microinvasive carcinoma at the other. DCIS of intermediate nuclear grade, in particular the neuroendocrine type, which commonly shows a spindle cell pattern, may resemble florid UDH. Immunohistochemistry demonstrates a heterogeneous pattern of reactivity with basal cytokeratins (e.g. CK5 and CK14) in UDH, whereas these are uniformly negative in neuroendocrine DCIS. ER is strongly and uniformly positive in neuroendocrine DCIS, whereas hormone receptors usually show a mosaic pattern in UDH. Gynaecomastoid-type UDH may mimic low-grade micropapillary DCIS, but it is formed of more-rudimentary micropapillae. Like other UDH, the gynaecomastoid type lacks the nuclear uniformity of low-grade DCIS, and the nuclei, which may be somewhat pyknotic, are aligned along the periphery of the micropapillae.

Atypical ductal hyperplasia is a microfocal, low-grade atypical epithelial proliferation partially involving no more than two membrane-bound spaces (or equivalent, i.e. ≤ 2 mm). Typically, although part of the spaces have the cytological and architectural features of low-grade DCIS, they are insufficient in extent for this diagnosis; elsewhere in the spaces, there is often admixed columnar cell change or UDH. Solid DCIS, in particular of low or intermediate nuclear grade, may be difficult to distinguish from lobular carcinoma in situ (see section Lobular carcinoma in situ).

DCIS may involve the lobular units, or it may extend into sclerosing lesions (e.g. sclerosing adenosis); both presentations may mimic invasive carcinoma. An inflammatory and fibrotic reaction can be observed around ducts bearing DCIS of high nuclear grade, but it is especially common in association with lobular involvement. Careful examination for small, irregular (rather than rounded) clusters of cells in the stroma should be undertaken to exclude (micro)invasion, particularly if there is a prominent lymphocytic infiltrate {29394917}. Myoepithelial cell markers and/or basement membrane markers can be helpful to demonstrate an intact myoepithelial and basement membrane layer around involved lobules or sclerosing adenosis, but they may be difficult to assess, particularly in some cases of high-grade DCIS with an altered myoepithelial cell immunophenotype; an absence of reactivity for myoepithelial cell markers does not unequivocally distinguish DCIS from invasive carcinoma {18936688}. Therefore, the use of more than one myoepithelial cell marker is recommended. Basement membrane markers might also be used in these cases, but the interpretation is even more challenging and impractical. The outline of the cell clusters is a valuable feature, with small invasive clusters having irregular contours. Invasive cribriform carcinoma, a low-grade invasive carcinoma (see section Invasive cribriform carcinoma) is a close mimic of DCIS, but it has the irregular, infiltrative growth pattern of invasive disease, with desmoplastic stroma and an absent myoepithelial layer. How best to classify lesions that have the histological appearance of DCIS but in which no myoepithelial cells can be demonstrated around tumour cell nests is an unresolved issue. While the presence of surrounding basement membrane shown by immunohistochemistry may be used to support the diagnosis of DCIS, both the absence of myoepithelial cell markers and demonstrable basement membrane should not result in a diagnosis of invasive carcinoma in the absence of a recognizable pattern of invasive carcinoma. This is similar to the recommendations provided for encapsulated (see section Encapsulated papillary carcinoma) and solid papillary carcinomas (see section Solid papillary carcinoma [in situ and invasive]).

In rare cases, large tumour emboli may occlude and distend the lymphovascular spaces and mimic DCIS. In contrast to DCIS, the tumour emboli are haphazardly distributed and are devoid of myoepithelium and basement membrane, and no inflammation or reactive changes are present in the surrounding stroma. Immunohistochemical stains for myoepithelial markers are negative, whereas endothelial markers may highlight residual endothelial cells lining the lymphovascular spaces.

Cytology

DCIS cannot be consistently distinguished from IBC on cytopathology. Its nuclear cytology can be low-, intermediate-, and high-grade and the cells can assume variable architectural patterns, for example, solid cribriform and micropapillary lacking myoepithelial cells and the bare bipolar nuclei in the background. Differential diagnosis of low-grade DCIS includes UDH and low-grade carcinoma, papillary DCIS, and solid papillary carcinoma. High-grade DCIS often has plentiful dispersed cells, a variable number of small to large epithelial tissue fragments showing solid, cribriform, micropapillary, and comedo architecture, and high nuclear grade {16780019}. Features that suggest a high-grade DCIS or high-grade DCIS component include low epithelial cellularity, necrosis, and calcifications {9360042}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A variety of biomarkers have been assessed in DCIS. Surrogate immunoprofiles of intrinsic subtypes have been investigated; the luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, and basal-like molecular subtypes are all seen in DCIS. Of note, the frequency of these subtypes differs between DCIS and invasive disease; for example, HER2 overexpression is more common in DCIS than in IBC, particularly in high-grade DCIS {18681955}, although the exact percentage of DCIS cases that express HER2 is still unclear {24202240}.

ER and PR

ER assessment is recommended by the ICCR to determine the benefit of endocrine therapy as adjuvant chemoprevention (depending on surgery undertaken), whereas PR testing is considered optional. There is little evidence regarding the optimum methodology for scoring immunohistochemical assays of hormone receptors in DCIS. In general, techniques have been extrapolated from those used for IBC, and similar cut-off points (e.g. > 1% DCIS cell nuclei) are applied, but this approach is pragmatic rather than evidence-based. The distribution of receptor expression in DCIS is similar to that in IBC; about 75% of cases are ER-positive {18681955}. However, there is insufficient evidence to determine whether there is an association between levels of receptor expression and response to hormone therapy in DCIS, although one randomized clinical trial has shown an increased local recurrence risk of in situ (but not invasive) carcinoma in ER-negative DCIS {33727261}. There is no indication for assessing HER2 status in DCIS.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: proliferating, cohesive neoplastic epithelial cells, confined to the mammary ductal-lobular system, exhibiting a range of nuclear grades and architectural patterns.

Desirable: in difficult cases, pragmatic interpretation of multiple myoepithelial markers for diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

The breast cancer–specific survival of women with DCIS is extremely long {30092498;30059817}. Recent reports highlight that DCIS patients aged > 50 years have a risk of dying from all causes comparable to that of the general female population {28375855}. The grade of DCIS is associated with breast cancer–specific survival {30059817}. Breast cancer deaths after a diagnosis of DCIS may be due either to undetected invasive carcinoma at the time of diagnosis or to recurrence as invasive disease; women who develop recurrence as invasive disease have a significantly worse outcome than those with recurrence as pure DCIS (which does not affect breast cancer–specific survival) {21398619;30092498}. It has, however, been shown that not all invasive carcinoma (18%) arising after DCIS is clonally related and may represent new disease {35681052}, which partly accounts for the later and continuing invasive recurrences that occur on long-term follow-up.

A variety of factors have been associated with increased risk of recurrence after breast-conserving surgery, including younger patient age, large lesion size, high-grade disease, certain architectural patterns, comedonecrosis, HER2 positivity, and positive margins {34380636;33727261;39801240}. However, the relative importance of these factors (and their possible interactions) remains unclear, especially those for invasive disease as study designs have not differentiated between this and recurrent DCIS. Nonetheless, algorithms for the incorporation of a number of features to predict local recurrence risk have been proposed but are not very widely used {26059650}. To date, no individual molecular prognostic marker has been consistently demonstrated to be of clinical value in predicting the behaviour of DCIS – in particular in identifying cases at risk of development of recurrence as clinically significant (i.e. invasive) disease {30092498}. Nevertheless, six prognostic factors for invasive disease have been identified in a meta-analyses but with relatively low hazard ratios, the highest being 1.84 for detection by palpation {31023696}.

Globally accepted robust and reproducible biomarkers for the prediction of recurrence in DCIS have been hampered by the large numbers required for a well-powered analysis along with prolonged follow-up, to account for heterogeneity and confounding clinicopathological factors and studies. Randomized clinical trials have assessed the benefits of adjuvant hormone therapy in the management of patients with DCIS {21145284;11498833}, but there is also real-world evidence that such treatment reduces the risk of ipsilateral recurrence {30092498}. In the National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project (NSABP) B-24 study of patients treated with wide local excision and radiotherapy, this benefit was restricted to ER-positive disease {22393101}. Adjuvant hormone therapy also reduces the risk of contralateral breast cancer {21145284;22393101}. Results for PR are generally similar, but less significant, and the role of PR testing in DCIS is unclear. Furthermore, addition of a brief course of trastuzumab to radiation therapy in high-grade HER2-positive DCIS did not result in markedly improved reduction of ipsilateral breast recurrence in a large phase III clinical trial {33739848}.

Randomized clinical trials and large national databases consistently show an approximately 50% reduction in local ipsilateral recurrence in patients receiving breast radiotherapy. No established predictive factors for radiotherapy benefit have been identified. Even when small (≤ 25 mm) DCIS lesions of low or intermediate nuclear grade are managed with complete excision without radiotherapy, there remains a steady increase in local recurrence rate over time (14.4% at 12 years; 7.5% invasive disease) {26371148}. Therefore, the selection of patients who may not require breast radiotherapy is currently done largely on a case-by-case basis rather than on the basis of a guideline or protocol. Molecular assays for the prediction of radiotherapy response have been proposed but are not yet fully validated or widely used globally {27704370;33821346;33830392}. Initial, short-term results of a recent randomized clinical trial suggest that active monitoring may not be inferior to guideline-concordant care for women with low-risk DCIS diagnosed by core needle biopsy. However longer follow-up is needed to determine if active monitoring will be sufficient for these patients {39665585}.
