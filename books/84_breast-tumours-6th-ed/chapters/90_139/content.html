9.0 Metastases to the breast

Definition

Metastases to the breast are malignant tumours in the breast originating from an extramammary organ or site.

Localization

Metastases may involve the skin, axillary region, nipple, or any breast quadrant. Based on an early autopsy series, the upper-outer quadrant was the most common metastatic site {4337956} but similar data were not reported in more-recent datasets from the era of modern mammographic screening.

Clinical features

Clinical findings in patients with primary breast cancer and metastases to the breast may overlap. The majority (as many as 77%) of patients with metastases to the breast have metastatic disease involving other sites {4337956;17582626;23174933}. Metastases to the breast occur across a wide age range, with most series reporting a median age of 51–54 years {35447141;17582626;23174933}. Metastasis to the breast may be the first sign of an extramammary primary tumour (6–51%) {35447141}, or occur on average 12 months after the primary tumour diagnosis (range: 0–269 months) {17582626}. Female patients are more frequently affected than male patients {37708072;18042689;23174933;27337944;17582626}. In paediatric patients, the most common metastases are from rhabdomyosarcoma (exclusively alveolar), with rare case reports of neuroblastoma, medulloblastoma, and Ewing sarcoma {27820124;37899598}.

Metastatic tumours may present clinically, either as a rapidly growing, painless, firm, palpable mass(es) (35–70%) {4337956} and/or radiographically (14–70%) {35447141;17582626}. They are usually solitary lesions (75–85%), but can be multifocal (10%), bilateral (25%), or diffuse, with axillary involvement in 25–48% of patients {23174933;26838286;27337944}. When diffuse, metastases may mimic inflammatory breast cancer owing to lymphatic spread {22734030;18311479;31660096;19292804;29194708}.

The findings on mammography and ultrasonography are nonspecific. Most (75–85%) metastases appear as a single well-circumscribed tumour, but may rarely (5%) present as a heterogeneous, poorly defined, hypoechoic, irregular, stellate mass(es) without posterior acoustic shadowing {26293973}. Microcalcifications are uncommon but may be seen in as many as 10% of cases, including metastases from high-grade serous carcinomas, thyroid carcinomas, or mucinous gastrointestinal carcinomas {10759349;17582626}. On MRI, metastases are most commonly isointense with increased rapid enhancement.

Owing to their rarity, it is common for metastases to the breast to be misdiagnosed as primary breast carcinoma because of the absence of clinical history {17582626}, an undiagnosed non-breast primary malignancy, and/or overlapping histological and immunohistochemical features. The vast majority (98%) of metastases are correctly identified when the clinical history is known and available to the pathologist, but only a minority (36%) are correctly identified in the absence of known or available clinical history {35447141}. For instance, metastatic melanoma may be misdiagnosed as primary triple-negative breast carcinoma, and metastatic serous carcinoma may be misdiagnosed as primary ER-positive micropapillary carcinoma.

Epidemiology

The breast is an uncommon location for metastases and only 0.7% of metastatic tumours were reported in the breast in a large autopsy series {18517275}. Metastatic non-mammary tumours to the breast account for 0.2–1.4% of breast malignancies in case series {26838286}, with a recent slight increase due to increased pathologist awareness and improved radiographic detection and treatment of stage IV disease {32396659}. The majority of bilateral carcinomas are independent primaries, as only a minority (10–23%) demonstrate a molecular genetic relationship {28921573;33236215}. The majority of extramammary neoplasms described to involve the breast are haematological neoplasms {39518009;4337956}. These include diffuse large B-cell, follicular, and marginal zone lymphomas; myeloma; and small lymphocytic lymphoma / chronic lymphocytic leukaemia.

Metastases from extramammary solid tumours account for 0.2–1% of breast tumours in case series {23174933;27337944;11745279;32396659}. As a group, carcinomas (including lung, gynaecological, gastrointestinal tract, renal, prostate) explain the majority of extramammary metastases to the breast {23174933}. However, melanoma is the single most common extramammary solid tumour to metastasize to the breast. Sarcomas, mesothelioma, and neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) may also rarely metastasize to the breast.

Etiology

The etiology varies according to the metastatic tumour and its site of origin.

Pathogenesis

Metastasis to the breast from extramammary malignant tumours is predominantly via haematogenous spread but may occur via lymphatics. Although older individual case reports have suggested that lung adenocarcinoma can metastasize to the breast via retrograde axillary lymphatic drainage, the evidence is limited {26464732}.

Macroscopic appearance

There are no specific macroscopic features. A grossly pigmented appearance may be seen in metastatic melanoma. The majority of metastatic lesions are solitary, firm, round to oval, and well circumscribed, but they may be multiple.

Histopathology

The presence of the following histological features may suggest a non-breast primary: macronucleoli, nuclear inclusions, and cytoplasmic pigment in melanoma; cellular dyscohesion and cytological features of low- versus high-grade lymphomas; speckled chromatin pattern in NETs; columnar cells and garland-pattern cellular necrosis in colorectal adenocarcinomas; and papillary or micropapillary architecture and psammomatous calcifications in serous carcinomas. Metastasis to the breast should be considered in the setting of a high-grade, triple-negative carcinoma that lacks an associated in situ component, and in the setting of a low- to intermediate-grade adenocarcinoma that is ER-negative. When metastases are gland forming, the distinction from breast carcinoma may be made by ER immunohistochemistry owing to expected positivity of luminal tumours, with the notable exception of Müllerian tumours. The latter may be a pitfall due to ER positivity as well as overlapping morphological features such as micropapillary and papillary patterns or adenosquamous components. If available, the H&E slides of the primary non-breast tumour and metastases in the breast should be compared.

Awareness of any unusual clinical, radiological, or pathological features is essential for recognition. Discordance between morphology and immunohistochemistry should be further investigated. Metastatic carcinomas may invade the surrounding tissue displacing ductular lobular units without prominent desmoplasia/elastosis and may be associated with extensive lymphatic invasion. The presence of carcinoma in situ is characteristic of primary breast carcinoma but may rarely be misinterpreted owing to metastatic carcinoma involving ducts (via canalicular spread) or lymphatics {33593410;11172202;16819337}.

Owing to lack of specificity of any single immunohistochemical stain, a panel approach to immunohistochemistry should be used to exclude breast origin, which is more straightforward in ER-positive than ER-negative tumours. Immunostains with the highest sensitivity for ER-positive breast carcinomas are TRPS1 (> 95%) {39243111;33011748} and GATA3 (80%) {36649695}, followed by mammaglobin (50–77%) {36980510} and GCDFP-15 (40–70%) {21411781;17145637}, each with varying specificity {35461734}. Immunostains with the highest sensitivity for triple-negative breast carcinomas are TRPS1 (90%) followed by SOX10 (80%) and GATA3 (66%) {23260325;30468800;38647255}. TRPS1 is a marker initially reported as sensitive in both ER-positive and triple-negative breast tumours {39518009;38173281}, but recent data have cast doubt on its specificity. Positivity in as many as 70% of tubo-ovarian, 71% of endometrial {39264831;38357982;37352847}, and 27.4% of bladder carcinomas {38052269} as well as a lower percentages of other tumours {39518009} has been reported. TRPS1 labelling in malignant phyllodes tumours {35063444} and breast angiosarcomas {38902365} has also been documented.

The aforementioned markers of breast origin lack discriminatory power in skin adnexal sweat gland tumours and have limited clinical utility. Targeted panels should be selected based on the differential, such as melanoma (S100, SOX10, HMB45, melan A, MITF), lung adenocarcinoma (TTF1 and napsin A), Müllerian tumours (WT1, PAX8), prostatic adenocarcinoma (PSA, P501S, NKX3-1), and gastrointestinal tract adenocarcinomas (CK20, CDX2) {24711169}.

It is important to be aware of overlapping and aberrant staining patterns (see #49738Table 9.01), some of which are strong and diffuse (e.g. SOX10 in triple-negative breast carcinomas) and some of which are weak and focal (e.g. napsin A). Melanoma may rarely label for cytokeratins {33934372}. Breast carcinomas may label for SOX10 (10% across subtypes; 66% of triple-negative tumours) {23260325}, S100 (57%) {8121183}, melan A (18%) {18949364}, WT1 (< 10% of breast cancers, especially with micropapillary and mucinous features) {18469795}, PAX8 (higher expression with antibody clone MRQ-50 [41%] vs. BC12 [5%]) {39105782;31335489;35417734}, NKX3-1 (27.1% in lobular carcinomas) {24996432}, napsin A (13%) {26842346}, and rarely (< 5%) PSA {20863373} and TTF1 {21107096}, the latter seen in primary breast small cell carcinomas.

Cytology

The features depend on the malignancy that is the source of the metastasis, and awareness of the patient’s clinical history is essential. On occasion, the breast metastasis may be the initial presentation of a malignancy, such as metastatic melanoma, lymphoma, or lung carcinoma {18446623}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnostic molecular pathology is related to the metastatic tumour type.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The essential and desirable diagnostic criteria vary according to the primary tumour.

Staging

The assigned stage pertains to the primary tumour, which should be staged according to the current (ninth) edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, Oâ€™Sullivan B, et al., editors. Prognosis and prediction

Patients with metastases to the breast often have disseminated disease and poor prognosis, with reported median survivals ranging from 10–12 months to 22.4 months (range: 0.4–192.7 months) {23174933;17582626;17615059}. With early detection and improved systemic therapy, the prognosis for stage IV disease has improved slightly and is anticipated to continue to do so {17615059}. In the era of targeted molecular-based therapy and immunotherapy, it is critical to recognize and distinguish metastases to the breast from primary breast carcinomas owing to vastly different treatment courses.

Tumour marker immunocytochemistry and molecular profiling strategies have been developed to assist in primary site location and clinical management of carcinoma of unknown primary tumours in general [[Greco AF. Cancer of Unknown Primary Site: A New Era of Practice-Changing Approaches to Diagnosis, Staging, and Precision Therapy. JCO Oncol Adv. 2024 Nov 12;1:e2400041(2024). doi:10.1200/OA-24-00041. ]]. Using such methodology, the specific tumour type can be identified in as many as 92% of cases of carcinoma of unknown primary, which will support the use of organ-specific tumour-type treatment strategies {39231374}.
