3.2.3 Microinvasive carcinoma

Definition

Microinvasive carcinoma of the breast is an invasive breast carcinoma ≤ 1 mm.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: carcinoma in situ with microinvasion.

Subtype(s)

Various morphological subtypes of invasive breast carcinoma may be identified that are ≤ 1 mm. Microinvasive carcinoma is usually of no special type (NST) with a minority being of special type, including lobular (as many as 8.6%), tubular or cribriform, mucinous, and micropapillary {27030304;25981971;26818833;21415700}. Microinvasive carcinoma is also heterogeneous with regard to molecular subtype and corresponding immunohistochemically defined surrogate classifications, including the luminal A–like, luminal B–like, ERBB2 (HER2)-overexpressing (non-luminal), and triple-negative categories {25981971;26013173}. The molecular and immunohistochemical subtypes of microinvasive carcinoma typically reflect those of the associated carcinoma in situ (CIS) {27402610}.

Localization

There are no specific features.

Clinical features

Microinvasive carcinoma has no specific clinical features. It is not palpable and is generally identified during microscopic assessment of CIS {19026501}. The clinical and/or imaging features are those of the associated CIS component.

Epidemiology

Data relating to the incidence of microinvasive carcinoma are subject to bias owing to variations in the availability of population-based mammographic screening, differences in definitions over time, sampling error, and the relatively high rate of misdiagnosis {10716157}. Although theoretically microinvasive carcinoma is an intermediate but necessary step in the progression of CIS to invasive carcinoma, this occurrence is rarely captured histologically. The incidence of microinvasive carcinoma is related to the grade and size of the ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) lesion and therefore varies widely in the literature. In one series, the risk of microinvasive carcinoma was 3% in DCIS lesions measuring < 5 mm, 6% in DCIS ranging from 6 to 10 mm, and 23% in DCIS lesions ≥ 50 mm {2162238}. In a recent multivariate analysis of DCIS with microinvasion, the presence of multiple foci of microinvasion was reported to be significantly associated with DCIS > 20 mm {33344258}. Although some data suggest that 8–9% {28165014;29119353} or even > 20% {29963116} of CIS lesions may have microinvasion, most suggest that it is less common. The incidence of microinvasion in the Sloane Project was 4.6% (512 of 11 285 DCIS lesions) {36224403}. Microinvasive carcinoma accounted for 1.4% of resected invasive breast cancers in a single institution series of 12 152 cases {25981971}.

Etiology

The etiology of microinvasive carcinoma is the same as that of invasive breast carcinoma.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of microinvasive carcinoma is largely unstudied (owing to the limited size and low incidence of the lesion), but it is believed to be similar to that of invasive breast carcinoma, of which microinvasive carcinoma is an early manifestation. In addition to tumour-associated factors resulting in the breakdown of the myoepithelial / basement membrane barrier, the inflammatory response may also contribute {27030304}. The molecular pathology of microinvasive carcinoma is unknown and difficult to study. It may reflect the earliest changes related to invasion. On the basis of the different molecular phenotypes observed, microinvasive carcinoma does not appear to have unique biological characteristics {25981971}.

Macroscopic appearance

Microinvasive carcinoma does not have specific macroscopic features itself and will exhibit the macroscopic features of any associated background abnormality, usually CIS, which ranges from mass forming to grossly inconspicuous. The most common association is with high-grade DCIS, which may form a distinct mass or appear as a poorly defined firm area. CIS with associated microcalcification may have a gritty consistency; rarely, ducts containing DCIS with abundant luminal comedo-like necrosis may extrude caseous material on cut section.

Histopathology

Microinvasive carcinoma is the earliest morphologically recognized form of invasive carcinoma with a dominant CIS component, most commonly high-grade DCIS {25981971;18493870}. Microinvasive carcinoma is less commonly observed in association with classic, florid, and pleomorphic lobular CIS {21415700;18493870;26818833;33824462;30489319} and, rarely, with Paget disease of the nipple {22895267}. Microinvasive carcinoma should only be diagnosed when clear evidence of invasion is seen in the form of one or more distinctly separate foci, none of which measures > 1 mm. Some earlier definitions mandated the presence of invasion into the non-specialized (interlobular) stroma {18493870}, but this is no longer a requirement.

Microinvasive carcinoma consists of tumour cells arranged singly or in small angulated clusters with haphazard infiltration of the stroma, which typically demonstrates reactive changes (e.g. mononuclear infiltrate, oedema, and desmoplastic changes). The vast majority are carcinomas NST, but a small minority are special type {27030304;25981971;26818833;21415700;3824462;30489319}.

The interobserver reproducibility in diagnosing microinvasive carcinoma on H&E alone is poor. In one series, experts disagreed on a considerable number of cases with complete agreement on H&E sections in only 18% (9 of 50 cases) {26818833}. In another series, only 20% of 109 cases in which microinvasion was diagnosed or suspected were actually confirmed to be microinvasive carcinoma on review {10716157}. Because the foci are small, they may cut through on deeper levels or subsequent immunohistochemistry, which may also affect interobserver reproducibility. Peer review of these cases may be of value.

The use of immunohistochemistry to demonstrate the absence of myoepithelial cells in a focus of microinvasive carcinoma may assist diagnosis and improve reproducibility {26818833}. However, myoepithelial cell staining may be diminished or virtually absent in some benign {20463570;22339683;29197112} and DCIS lesions {29197112;18936688}. Phenotypic differences in the myoepithelial cells surrounding DCIS relative to normal ducts and lobules {26818833} support the use of more than one myoepithelial cell marker when evaluating these cases, such as a combination of a nuclear (e.g. p63) and cytoplasmic (e.g. calponin, SMMHC) marker. Cytokeratin immunohistochemistry to highlight low-volume infiltrating tumour cells may also be of value.

Microinvasive carcinoma may be misdiagnosed in lesions with overlapping histological features. Overdiagnosis tends to occur owing to CIS involving normal lobules, CIS involving sclerosing adenosis, entrapment and distortion of epithelium in sclerosing lesions, artefactual needle track displacement of carcinoma cells (especially from papillary lesions), and cautery or crush artefact {36707635}. Microinvasive carcinoma may be underdiagnosed owing to sampling error in cases with dense lymphocytic infiltration around ducts and lobules involved by CIS {36707635}. Tertiary lymphoid structures around DCIS are more common in DCIS with microinvasion and a particularly careful assessment for microinvasion is warranted in these cases {27402610}.

The presence of microinvasive carcinoma in DCIS is associated with larger DCIS extent, higher nuclear grade, and the presence of comedonecrosis {36224403}. A significant subset of microinvasive carcinoma in association with DCIS is multifocal, reaching 34.5% and 35.1% in two series, respectively {25981971;33344258}, and an average of three foci per DCIS case in another {12828606}. If any invasive focus is > 1 mm, the focus is designated invasive carcinoma and staged accordingly, and the presence of additional foci of microinvasive carcinoma noted. It is recommended to indicate if the microinvasive carcinoma is multifocal since this may be associated with worse disease-free survival {33344258} and may influence adjuvant therapy recommendations. In some centres, the number of foci of microinvasion is recorded as an indication of microinvasive lesion burden. In surgical resection specimens, if there are multiple foci of microinvasion in close proximity, a pragmatic approach would be to apply the same recommendation used for staging multifocal established invasive carcinomas and regard deposits < 5 mm apart as part of a single invasive tumour deposit. Although there is only limited evidence, this is a practical approach which would classify close proximity multifocal microinvasion (< 5 mm apart) as an established invasive carcinoma, base the invasive tumour size measurement on the maximum dimension of the region with two or more microinvasive foci, and stage accordingly {33344258;37296968}. There should be caution in applying this rule if there is very minimal infiltration, and examination of further deeper sections should be considered.

As microinvasive carcinomas may carry a risk, albeit low, of metastatic spread {17081752}, assessment of hormone receptor and HER2 status should be performed where possible.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma ≤ 1 mm.

Desirable: coexisting carcinoma in situ.

Staging

Microinvasive carcinoma is defined as pT1mi, regardless of the histological subtype, CIS extent, or number of microinvasive foci [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. ]] [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017. ]] {28094848}. The general rule of rounding tumour size to the closest millimetre does not apply at the boundary of microinvasive carcinoma; i.e. a 1.1 mm carcinoma is classified and staged as invasive disease, pT1a [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. ]].

Closely associated foci of microinvasive carcinoma (≤ 5 mm apart) should be summated and staged according to overall size. In the post-neoadjuvant setting, microinvasive carcinoma is a very rare category to use.

When foci are suspicious but indefinite for microinvasive carcinoma, as per the standardized TNM staging practice [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. ]]} [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, Oâ€™Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. ]] {28094848}, it is recommended that the case is diagnosed as CIS and classified as pTis.

Prognosis and prediction

The rate of nodal involvement associated with microinvasive carcinoma varies in the published literature and depends on whether sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed, the extent of lymph node examination (e.g. additional levels and/or immunohistochemistry), and how lymph node positivity was defined {17081752;24238761}. In a series of 2609 patients with DCIS and microinvasive carcinoma in whom sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed, identified by the US National Cancer Database, only 2.9% had axillary nodal metastases {32430750}. Axillary nodal metastases have been observed in 1% and 7% of resections after core biopsy diagnoses of DCIS with suspected or definite microinvasion, respectively {31342378;31147995}.

The prognosis of microinvasive carcinoma is controversial, probably secondary to differences in diagnosis and incidence. Microinvasive carcinoma has long been believed to be equivalent to DCIS, and some series support this notion {29963116;26013173}. Other recent publications suggest that the prognosis is closer to that of small invasive cancers {29119353;27577080}, with shorter disease-free and locoregional recurrence-free survival in patients with DCIS and microinvasion than in those with DCIS alone {36427119}. Although with significant limitations, retrospective reviews of the SEER Program dataset report 20-year cancer-specific mortality of 6.9% – 9.65% in patients with DCIS and microinvasive carcinoma {29119353;28165014}. In the Sloane Project series, all ipsilateral DCIS recurrences and the majority (71.4%) of subsequent ipsilateral invasive recurrences were of high histological grade compared with only 30.4% of invasive carcinomas developing after DCIS without microinvasion (p = 0.02). Distant metastasis and breast cancer mortality were higher in patients with microinvasive carcinoma than in those with DCIS alone (1.2% vs 0.3%, p = 0.01; 2.1% vs 0.8%, p = 0.005) {36224403}. However, a 1.3% difference in overall survival may not be clinically significant {36707635}. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found that overall survival and distant metastasis–free survival tended to be shorter in patients with DCIS with microinvasion than in patients with DCIS alone, but the difference was not statistically significant. There is also some evidence that patients with HER2-positive microinvasive disease not receiving chemotherapy with anti-HER2 therapy have a worse outcome {27577080}, but data are limited.
