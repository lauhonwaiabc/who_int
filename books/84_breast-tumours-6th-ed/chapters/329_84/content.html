3.2.9 Invasive apocrine carcinoma

Definition

Invasive apocrine carcinoma is characterized by large cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and enlarged nuclei with prominent nucleoli, resembling apocrine sweat glands.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: carcinoma with apocrine differentiation.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The localization is similar to that of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST).

Clinical features

The clinical features are similar to those of IBC-NST. Invasive apocrine carcinoma usually presents as a firm, poorly circumscribed mass. Microcalcifications may be visible on mammography, particularly when associated with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS).

Epidemiology

Invasive apocrine carcinoma is rare, constituting about 1% of all breast carcinomas {27259012}. Current knowledge is based on case reports and small series; patients tend to be older than those with IBC-NST {29870876;28445946;30240079;23245877}.

Etiology

Most invasive apocrine carcinomas are sporadic. Some carcinomas in patients with germline PTEN mutation (Cowden syndrome) may have apocrine morphology {20712882}.

Pathogenesis

There is no definitive information regarding the pathogenesis of invasive apocrine carcinoma. The relationship between apocrine metaplasia, atypical apocrine adenosis, and invasive apocrine carcinoma remains controversial. Similar genetic alterations in papillary apocrine metaplasia and adjacent DCIS and/or invasive carcinoma with apocrine morphology raise the possibility that apocrine metaplasia may be a non-obligate precursor of a subset of invasive apocrine carcinomas {11141494}. Reports of early oncogenic events in apocrine metaplasia and apocrine adenosis, including overexpression of the ERBB2 (HER2) and MYC (c-myc) oncoproteins, without alterations of the corresponding genes, may also support this hypothesis {10861572;14965710;14965711}.

Overall, invasive apocrine carcinomas have heterogeneous gene expression profiles {18720457} but they constitute a more homogeneous group when analysis is restricted to carcinomas with apocrine morphology, a triple-negative phenotype {26939876}, and a molecular apocrine signature (molecular apocrine or luminal AR IBC) characterized by AR upregulation without ER activation {16491124;15897907;20712882;21741601;23663520}. However, tumours with this profile have not been clearly shown to be directly equivalent to invasive apocrine carcinoma as defined based on morphology.

Invasive apocrine carcinomas harbour mutations of TP53 and the PIK3CA/PTEN/AKT pathway genes {26208846;32504034;36870308;37394589}. TP53 mutations appear to be less common in triple-negative breast carcinomas (TNBCs) with apocrine differentiation, which display a high mutation frequency in PIK3CA and other PI3K signalling pathway–related genes, similar to luminal-type breast carcinomas, with potential prognostic and therapeutic implications {26939876;31822498;37310016}. Mutations within the MAPK pathway (e.g. in KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF) are less common {28027454;26208846;32504034;36870308}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance is nonspecific and similar to that of IBC-NST.

Histopathology

The tumour cells have abundant granular, eosinophilic, or vacuolated cytoplasm with distinct cell borders {3717305;15695073}. The nuclei are enlarged and round to oval with prominent nucleoli, and with variable nuclear atypia and mitotic activity {3717305;18248561;20208479}. The predominant growth pattern is solid, but any IBC-NST architectural pattern may be observed. DCIS with apocrine morphology usually accompanies the invasive carcinoma {23771415}.

Invasive apocrine carcinomas express GCDFP-15 {7850069} and AR {9521507}, also found in apocrine metaplasia. There is no specific receptor profile; the most common is ER, PR, and HER2 negativity and AR positivity, which represents a distinctive group of TNBCs (for further reference see Triple-negative breast cancer in section Molecular classification) {23245877;27259012}. However, AR activation may also be associated with HER2 overexpression and/or ERBB2 amplification {29382369;20208479;22944625;22039288}. GATA3 is expressed in 90% of carcinomas with apocrine morphology {25544392} and in > 70% of AR-positive TNBCs {27184484}. Unlike in most breast carcinomas, TRPS1 may be negative in invasive apocrine carcinoma {35413381;36870308;37074839;39243111}.

Differential diagnosis

Apocrine metaplasia may have an inconspicuous to absent myoepithelial cell layer. Variation in nuclear size is < 3-fold {18184276;21263240}.

Apocrine atypia / apocrine DCIS involving a sclerosing lesion may mimic invasive apocrine carcinoma {10658905}. Immunohistochemical staining for myoepithelial markers may be helpful in this setting {34537861}.

Granular cell tumours have abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm, lack nuclear atypia, are ER-negative, and do not express any keratins. They are strongly and diffusely positive for CD68 and S100 {20074934}. In contrast, invasive apocrine carcinomas show only focal and weak positivity for S100.

Oncocytic carcinomas of the breast are very rare and may show positivity for GCDFP-15 and HER2; they are usually ER-positive {21111455}.

Apocrine morphology is also observed in some special subtypes of invasive carcinoma, particularly in pleomorphic invasive lobular carcinoma {1592388} and rarely in mucinous and micropapillary carcinoma, all of which are best classified as those special types with apocrine differentiation. Rare carcinomas composed of apocrine cells (positive for GCDFP-15) with prominent vacuolated or foamy cytoplasm and resembling histiocytes have been described {7726365}. Most of these tumours show loss of E-cadherin and may represent a subtype of invasive lobular carcinoma with apocrine differentiation {12089723;21398688}. Carcinomas with histiocytoid apocrine cells may be confused with histiocytic proliferations; immunohistochemical staining for cytokeratins and CD68 can be useful in distinguishing these lesions. The differential diagnosis of tumours and lesions of the breast with eosinophilic granular cells is summarized in #48279Table 3.07.

Cytology

Invasive apocrine carcinoma shows high cellularity with abundant single cells and small dyscohesive tissue fragments. The cells are large with abundant vacuolated to densely eosinophilic cytoplasm and marked nuclear atypia with prominent nucleoli. The differential diagnosis includes atypical apocrine adenosis involving a mass-forming sclerosing lesion and degenerative changes in the epithelium of apocrine cysts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No specific diagnostic molecular test is available.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: apocrine morphology in > 90% of tumour cells; no distinct other special type morphology (e.g. invasive lobular, mucinous, or micropapillary carcinoma).

Desirable: GCDFP-15 and AR positivity; no specific receptor profile, but most invasive apocrine carcinomas are ER-negative and PR-negative; ER-negative, PR-negative, HER2-negative, and AR-positive tumours form a distinct subgroup of TNBC.

Staging

Invasive apocrine carcinoma is staged according to the TNM classification system.

Prognosis and prediction

The results of clinical follow-up studies are often contradictory owing to the use of different criteria for the diagnosis of invasive apocrine carcinoma {23771415}. Recent studies have reported a worse clinical outcome in patients with invasive apocrine carcinoma than in those with IBC-NST {28445946;23245877}; however, in an analysis of SEER Program population-based data, the outcome improved after adjustment for demographic and clinicopathological features {28445946}. Some studies identified better overall survival and breast cancer–specific survival for patients with AR-positive, triple-negative invasive apocrine carcinomas than for those with other triple-negative tumours {29344276;29573977;29870876;29429597}, whereas other studies found no differences {23098574;23416046}.

Information on the pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy is very limited. One group reported partial or complete pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in about 30–50% of patients with invasive apocrine carcinoma {30240079}. However, in another series, the response of these tumours to neoadjuvant treatment was poor, but the patients had a good prognosis nonetheless {22277312}. Similarly, in a study evaluating subtypes of TNBC defined by gene expression profiling, the pathological complete response rate for luminal AR cancers after neoadjuvant chemotherapy was only 10%, but the tumours behaved clinically more like ER-positive carcinomas with a favourable outcome {23948975}.

The prognostic and predictive role of TILs in invasive apocrine carcinomas has not been widely explored {30425121;35027319}. Although several studies indicate lower levels of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes in invasive apocrine carcinomas, the current evidence is inconsistent {33739790;34066060;35800740;37782562;38960391;35444182}.
