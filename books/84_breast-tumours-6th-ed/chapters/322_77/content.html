3.2.2 Invasive breast carcinoma of no special type

Definition

Invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST) is a large and heterogeneous group of IBCs that cannot be classified morphologically as any of the special histological types.

Related terminology

Acceptable: invasive breast carcinoma NOS; invasive ductal carcinoma.

Biomarker-defined subtypes

ER-positive, ERBB2 (HER2)-negative; ER-positive, HER2-positive; ER-negative, HER2-positive; ER-negative, HER2-negative; triple-negative (ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-negative)

IBC-NST has no unique localization features beyond those of all IBCs.

Histopathology

General histological features

The preferred term for this heterogeneous pattern of IBCs is “of no special type” as they do not necessarily show any ductal/glandular formation {1757079}. While many have associated ductal carcinoma in situ, there is no definitive evidence that all arise from the terminal duct lobular unit. The histopathological features of IBC-NST vary considerably from tumour to tumour and can vary within the same tumour.

The tumour margins can be highly infiltrative (permeating the surrounding stroma and disrupting the normal lobular units) or continuously pushing with an expansive pattern of growth. Architecturally, the tumour cells may be arranged in cords, clusters, and/or trabeculae, but others are characterized by a predominantly solid or syncytial infiltrative pattern with little associated stroma. In a proportion of tumours, glandular differentiation may be apparent as tubular structures with central luminal spaces. Occasionally, areas with single-file infiltration or targetoid features are seen, but the cells lack the cytomorphological characteristics of invasive lobular carcinoma, are generally more pleomorphic than in lobular carcinoma, and typically show membrane E-cadherin expression, although the latter may be at least partially aberrant in high-grade tumours {30760857}. A wide range of mitotic and apoptotic activity can be seen. Similarly, a spectrum of histological grade is observed (see Histological grading in section Prognosis and prediction for detailed information on grading).

The stromal component is also extremely variable. There may be a highly cellular fibroblastic proliferation, a scant element of connective tissue, or marked hyalinization. Foci of elastosis may be present in a periductal or perivenous distribution. Focal necrosis may be present and is occasionally extensive with secondary formation of cyst-like cavities. In a minority of tumours, mostly with a medullary pattern (see below), a distinct tumour-associated lymphoplasmacytoid infiltrate can be identified.

In the majority of cases, associated ductal carcinoma in situ is present and may be extensive. Coexistent ductal carcinoma in situ is usually of the same nuclear grade as the invasive carcinoma and this may be helpful in histological grading {8951484}.

Approximately one quarter of IBC-NSTs show angiolymphatic/lymphovascular invasion {8144141}. Tumour emboli are more commonly seen within lymphatic rather than vascular spaces but it may be impossible to confirm the nature of the involved space and these terms are applied to mean either lymphatic or blood vessel tumour emboli. Such emboli can be observed throughout the tumour but only extratumoural angiolymphatic/lymphovascular invasion is routinely assessed and reported in breast cancer. This should not, however, be included in the tumour size. True involvement should be differentiated from tissue retraction due to suboptimal fixation.

Mixed invasive breast carcinoma of no special type

Not infrequently, IBCs contain a mixture of both IBC-NST and a special subtype. If the special subtype makes up between 10% and 90% of the cancer, the term “mixed IBC-NST and special subtype carcinoma” is used. For this type of mixed IBC-NST and special subtype, it is recommended that both elements present are reported, ideally as well as the overall percentage of the special subtype – for example, “mixed IBC-NST and invasive lobular carcinoma (30% lobular)”. Since the biomarker status can be distinct, the biomarker status of both components should be reported, potentially requiring reassessment on the excised tumour if both are not present in prior biopsy. The histological grade of the components may also differ; the higher grade should be specified in the pathology report, but both grades may be recorded. Cancers with < 10% special subtype should be classified as IBC-NST, with the focal specialized subtype described in the report as a comment. Cancers with > 90% specialized subtype should be classified as the special subtype.

Some tumours show indeterminate morphological features where distinction between IBC-NST and invasive lobular carcinoma cannot be reliably made despite the application of immunohistochemistry, including staining for E-cadherin and p120. These tumours can be called IBC-NST with “lobular features / lobular growth pattern”.

Morphological patterns of invasive breast carcinoma of no special type

The umbrella term IBC-NST includes a range of histological patterns, some of which were previously regarded as special subtypes. For example, oncocytic, lipid-rich, glycogen-rich clear cell, and sebaceous carcinomas are extremely rare tumours without sufficient clinical evidence available for their designation as special tumour subtypes, and their specific histomorphology is now considered to be part of the spectrum of differentiation seen in IBC-NST. Similarly, carcinoma with medullary pattern, invasive carcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, carcinomas with pleomorphic and choriocarcinomatous patterns, and tumours with melanocytic features are currently considered to be morphological patterns of IBC-NST regardless of the extent of differentiation/pattern, and the 90% rule for special subtype is not applied to tumours showing any of these patterns.

Medullary pattern

Cancers historically described as medullary carcinoma, atypical medullary carcinoma, or carcinoma with medullary features were previously recognized as a specific special type of well-circumscribed breast cancer with high histological grade, pushing margins, syncytial architecture with no glandular structures, regions of necrosis, a prominent tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) infiltrate, and a high proliferation rate, but a better clinical outcome than other size- and stage-matched high-grade cancers. As a diagnostic category, medullary carcinoma has suffered from poor interobserver reproducibility and an overlap in features with carcinomas that have basal-like molecular and histological profiles as well as carcinomas associated with BRCA1 mutations. The recognition of the prognostic importance of TILs in high-grade invasive breast cancers appears to explain the good prognosis of these cancers {19286369} and the quantity of TILs can be reported if clinically relevant. Cancers with these features belong to the immunomodulatory subgroup of triple-negative breast cancer, mainly characterized by a high level of expression of immune-related and inflammation genes {25208879;21633166;27083182;17417968;30075151}. Carcinomas with a medullary pattern are thus now considered to represent one end of the spectrum of the TIL-rich IBC-NSTs rather than a distinct morphological subtype and the term “IBC-NST with medullary pattern” can be used.

Invasive carcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation

Some degree of neuroendocrine differentiation, as determined by histochemical or immunohistochemical analysis, occurs in 10–30% of IBC-NSTs {24589259;30140036}. Currently this finding has no known clinical relevance as a standalone feature, and routine staining of IBCs for neuroendocrine markers is not recommended. These cancers are typically hormone receptor–positive and HER2-negative {28884749}. Neuroendocrine differentiation is more commonly observed in some special breast cancer types, in particular mucinous carcinomas and solid papillary carcinomas (occurring in 20% and 72%, respectively). Outside of the context of neuroendocrine differentiation in IBC-NSTs, recognized special breast cancer types, and the very rare small cell carcinoma, it is uncertain that primary neuroendocrine neoplasm of the breast constitutes a specific entity (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction). When a breast tumour shows distinct neuroendocrine morphology, supported by immunohistochemistry, and is histologically different from solid papillary carcinoma and mucinous carcinoma, metastasis from another primary site, for example the gastrointestinal tract or lung, should be considered {37485220;36243799}. (See section Neuroendocrine neoplasms)

Other very rare patterns

These include IBC with osteoclast-like stromal giant cells and pleomorphic, choriocarcinomatous, melanotic, oncocytic, clear cell lipid-rich, clear cell glycogen-rich, and lymphoepithelial patterns.

Osteoclastic giant cells can be found in the stroma of IBC of any histotype {8200630}, often associated with an inflammatory, fibroblastic, hypervascular stroma, with extravasated erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and monocytes along with mononuclear and binucleated histiocytes, some containing haemosiderin. The carcinomatous part of the lesion is most frequently a well-differentiated or moderately differentiated IBC, but all the other histological subtypes have also been observed (most commonly invasive cribriform carcinoma).

The pleomorphic pattern is a rare pattern of high-grade IBC-NST characterized by a proliferation of pleomorphic and bizarre (sometimes multinucleated) tumour giant cells constituting > 50% of the tumour cells in a background of carcinoma, sometimes with metaplastic (spindle and squamous) differentiation {10849092;20013346}.

A choriocarcinomatous pattern is extremely rare but may be associated with elevated levels of serum hCG {6692294}; however, as many as 60% of IBC-NSTs have been found to contain hCG-positive cells {59853;4374964}.

A very few case reports have described tumours that appear to represent combinations of IBC and melanoma, and in some there appeared to be a transition from one cell type to the other {10524531;8952560;9195577}, which has been described as a melanotic pattern. The presence of melanin in breast cancer cells should not be construed as evidence of melanocytic differentiation, because pigmentation of carcinoma cells with melanin can occur when breast cancers invade the skin and involve the dermoepidermal junction {615831}. Similarly, focal expression of melan-A has been reported in 18% of breast cancers {18949364}. However, most melanotic tumours of the breast represent metastases from melanomas originating in extramammary sites.

Very rarely, breast carcinomas demonstrate oncocytic differentiation with cells containing abundant, brightly eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm, due to high numbers of mitochondria {28454314;21509527}. However, strong positive mitochondrial immunohistochemical staining (> 50% of the tumour cells) has been reported to be present in 19.7% of 76 consecutive invasive carcinomas of the breast {21111455}. The nuclei are round and centrally located, with prominent nucleoli {28043647}. These cancers often display chromosomal gains of 11q13.1-q13.2 and 19p13, changes frequently seen in oncocytic tumours of the kidney and thyroid {21509527}. Because breast carcinomas with eosinophilic granular cytoplasm are not rare, the term “IBC-NST with oncocytic carcinoma pattern” should be reserved only for lesions with typical morphology and appropriate immunohistochemical and/or ultrastructural characteristics. These tumours should be distinguished from IBCs with apocrine differentiation, which are typically hormone receptor–negative and AR-positive.

IBC-NST may very occasionally have conspicuous cytoplasmic vacuoles or a clear cell appearance. This may be due to a lipid-rich pattern with neutral lipids in the cytoplasm and irregular nuclei, typically with moderate to severe atypia and abundant mitoses. Alternatively, the clear cytoplasm may be due to abundant glycogen (glycogen-rich clear cell pattern). The clear or finely granular cytoplasm contains PAS-positive, diastase-sensitive glycogen. Because intracytoplasmic glycogen can be observed without a substantial clear cell appearance and because clear cell morphology can be caused by substances other than glycogen, both features are required for the diagnosis of this pattern of IBC-NST. As well as primary breast carcinoma, non-primary tumours with clear cell histology in the breast (e.g. metastatic clear cell carcinoma of the kidney) should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. The prognosis of these rare patterns of IBC-NST is not clear and not evidently different from more typical forms {18442419;24400866}. IBC-NST may also display a sebaceous pattern in which the tumour cells have abundant, often vacuolated, cytoplasm {16944238;8532397;19261098;1324221;20432752;10692180}. In a pure form, originating in the breast, rather than in the cutaneous adnexal sebaceous glands, this is extremely rare.

Other very rare breast carcinomas include lymphoepithelial carcinoma / lymphoepithelial-like carcinoma of the breast, which shows the same features as in other organs but appears not be associated with EBV infection {11135047}. The carcinoma cells are typically pleomorphic with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and prominent intermixed lymphoid cells. While some have suggested that this is a form of lobular carcinoma {12193205} with no membrane expression of E-cadherin {12193205}, this remains unclear. The differential diagnosis includes IBC-NST with medullary pattern and lymphoma.

Cytology

The features of IBC-NST vary with the histopathological grade but smears are usually highly cellular. Low-grade IBC-NST tends to show a mix of relatively cohesive, small, crowded epithelial tissue fragments including tubular as well as larger 3D fragments, and a lesser number of single dispersed cells showing mild to moderate nuclear atypia. High-grade carcinomas show smaller dyscohesive tissue fragments and plentiful dispersed single cells with large and pleomorphic nuclei with prominent nucleoli. Necrosis can be present in high-grade carcinomas. The medullary pattern shows poorly cohesive small sheets of highly pleomorphic cells with prominent nucleoli and a background of lymphocytes and plasma cells {31112942}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma with features of breast epithelial origin by morphology, with or without immunohistochemistry; features characteristic of pure special subtypes, if present, constitute < 10% in pure IBC-NST; in mixed IBC-NST and special type carcinoma, between 10% and 90% of the lesion is of special subtype.

Staging

See Staging in section Prognosis and prediction.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognostic characteristics of IBC-NST are similar to or slightly worse than the overall group of IBC, with a 10-year survival rate of 65–78% {1607149}, compared with about 80% for breast cancer in general {14557212;21761358;28863134;26376021}. Prognosis is profoundly influenced by the conventional clinicopathological variables of patient age, tumour size, histological grade, and lymph node stage, and by predictors of therapeutic response, such as ER, PR, and HER2 status (see section Prognosis and prediction) {18490649;25585328;22180017}.

The Ki-67 proliferation index and AR status have been shown to have prognostic value in some studies {24273215;28151718}. Approximately 70–80% of IBC-NSTs are ER-positive, and 12–20% of primary carcinomas are HER2-positive.

The management of IBC-NST is related to these prognostic and predictive characteristics. In general, the same prognostic and predictive concepts apply as for other IBCs.
