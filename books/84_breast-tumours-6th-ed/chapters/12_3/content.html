1.2 Small diagnostic samples

In current clinical practice, obtaining small biopsy samples via FNAB, core needle biopsy (CNB), or vacuum-assisted biopsy (VAB) is the primary diagnostic step in the evaluation of breast lesions after clinical and imaging evaluation. Any results must be correlated with the clinical and imaging findings {38061151;38199146;17013595;32025985}, and this triple assessment underpins the basis for subsequent patient management {38061151;38199146}, which may include (1) a return to normal screening, (2) active surveillance with imaging, (3) additional tissue sampling, (4) preoperative systemic therapy, or (5) surgical excision {38061151;38199146;37330436;29773220;17013595;32025985}. In low socioeconomic settings where imaging is not readily available, FNAB may be the only diagnostic tool for palpable breast lumps and is correlated with clinical findings only.

The use of classification systems and standardized reporting is helpful. Radiological findings are reported according to classification systems such as the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) (#52730Box 1.02) {17164080}. Reports of histopathological findings should adhere to established terminology {29773220;38061151;38199146}. In some countries, the B-classification system is used for categorizing lesions according to their biological potential (#50015Box 1.03).

Any image guidance method may be used, with preference for ultrasound guidance when the breast (or axillary) lesion is clearly visible via that modality or is palpable. Ultrasound is particularly advantageous for mass-forming lesions {1648757;17013595;32025985}. Stereotactic mammographic guidance is mostly used to target microcalcifications and architectural distortion {1648757;32025985}. MRI-guided biopsy is used when the imaging target is not visible by other modalities {9169689;18641246;32025985}.

Small-gauge needles, defined as 22 gauge or smaller, are used for FNAB {9237187}. For CNB tissue sampling, narrow-gauge core needles (most often 14 gauge) can be used successfully for masses, with larger-gauge needles and VAB devices used for microcalcifications, architectural distortions, and non–mass enhancing lesions {32025985}. Given the widespread availability of CNB and VAB, FNAB is less frequently used today in some regions of the world {32025985}. However, it remains a viable option for palpable masses, for resource-constrained settings where rapid diagnosis utilizing rapid onsite evaluation is advantageous for patient care (#50016Table 1.02). Advantages and disadvantages of each of these methods are summarized in #52733Table 1.03.

For breast CNB and VAB, step-sections from paraffin blocks with two to three H&E-stained levels are sufficient for most diagnoses. Additional levels should be obtained when a pathological correlate for the imaging target is not identified on initial sections. Depending on the laboratory’s volumes, case mix, and workflows, most centres cut and retain a number of unstained slides for ancillary studies for tumour subtyping and biomarkers should they become necessary.
