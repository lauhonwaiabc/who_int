6.5.1 Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia

Definition

Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia (PASH) is a benign stromal myofibroblastic proliferation forming anastomosing slit-like spaces.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

PASH shows no preferential location within the breast.

Clinical features

PASH can occur in both sexes and is commonly seen as an incidental finding in breast tissue. Palpable and/or mass-forming PASH (referred to as nodular or tumoural PASH) presents as a painless, unilateral firm or rubbery mass that can have circumscribed or indistinct margins. In rare instances, PASH can present as rapid diffuse enlargement of both breasts. When visible on mammography, PASH occurs as a mass or developing asymmetry without calcifications; by sonography it appears as a hypoechoic mass and by MRI as either a postcontrast well-delineated mass or an area of non–mass enhancement {36778653}. In male patients, PASH most often occurs as a palpable mass or nodule and is usually associated with gynaecomastia {9659260;7544764}. PASH with stromal giant cells, in the background of gynaecomastia, may be associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 {11407482}, but this appearance is not specific for this syndrome {25586494}. PASH can occur in accessory breast tissue along the milk line {16096415;39664127}.

Epidemiology

Current knowledge of PASH relies on single case reports and no epidemiological data are available. PASH is most commonly seen in women in their fourth to sixth decades of life, but it has also been described in postmenopausal women receiving hormonal therapy, in men, and in children {19606909;22583194;39365427}.

Etiology

In premenopausal women and prepubertal boys, PASH is driven by hormonal imbalances. PASH is believed to be an aberrant response of myofibroblasts to endogenous or exogenous hormones – most likely progesterone, because the staining intensity of PR is greater than that of ER {1989462;20586640}. Breast enlargement due to gynaecomastia with PASH occurs in patients with HIV/AIDS and has been associated with highly active antiretroviral therapy {12100117;12603389;27891161}. The breast swelling in these patients can regress after an alteration in treatment {27891161}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis for PASH is not known and it does not have characteristic molecular features.

Macroscopic appearance

When mass forming, PASH occurs as a well-circumscribed, unencapsulated, firm rubbery mass of 10–120 mm {7872425}, and in some cases it has a multinodular appearance. The cut surface is tan-white and homogeneous.

Histopathology

PASH is a proliferation of myofibroblasts involving interlobular and intralobular stroma. Anastomosing, slit-like channels, devoid of red blood cells and lined by spindle cells (which can resemble flattened endothelial cells), are present in dense stroma. The spindle cells have bland cytology and lack mitotic activity or atypia. PASH is present as the stromal component of gynaecomastia (in particular florid gynaecomastia), in juvenile hypertrophy, and in macromastia during pregnancy. PASH may also be seen in the stroma of fibroadenomas and phyllodes tumours.

The term “fascicular PASH” refers to the formation of cellular bundles of myofibroblasts arranged in a fascicular pattern {7872425}, lacking slit-like spaces and merging with conventional PASH. Fascicular PASH can mimic myofibroblastoma, but it is differentiated by the presence of typical breast epithelial elements within the borders of the tumour. The stromal cells of PASH show positive immunohistochemical staining for CD34, actin, desmin, and hormone receptors, with PR positivity observed more frequently than ER positivity {7872425;1989462}. The stromal cells of PASH are negative for endothelial markers (CD31, ERG, podoplanin [recognized by D2-40]), cytokeratins, and S100.

PASH must be distinguished from angiosarcoma as well as other spindle cell lesions {28676174;36482275;35976671}. True blood-filled anastomosing vascular channels lined by malignant endothelial cells are the key feature of angiosarcoma.

Cytology

FNAB of PASH is nonspecific, producing hypocellular smears with stromal fragments composed of a myxoid matrix and a small number of spindle cells, and scant similar spindle cells in the background. Lobular units and small ductal epithelial tissue fragments are usually present {15880710;10578994}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bland spindle cell myofibroblasts; an anastomosing network of slit-like channels; dense acellular collagenous stroma.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry is negative for endothelial markers, if needed; the stromal changes surround ducts and often involve lobules.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

PASH need not be excised when identified in a core biopsy, provided there is histology–imaging concordance. Although local recurrences of PASH have been reported, it is unclear whether excised PASH has truly recurred or whether new PASH foci have arisen in a hormone-rich environment {20708765}. The clinical implications of atypical PASH are unknown owing to its rarity.
