2.2.3 Apocrine adenosis and apocrine adenoma

Definition

Apocrine adenosis is a lobulocentric proliferation of benign glandular structures lined by luminal apocrine cells and distorted by stromal sclerosis.

Apocrine adenoma is a well-delineated benign tumour characterized by a proliferation of round and oval glandular structures lined by apocrine cells, encircled by inconspicuous myoepithelial cells, with little intervening stroma.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: sclerosing adenosis with apocrine metaplasia; nodular adenosis with apocrine metaplasia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Terminal duct lobular units

Clinical features

Apocrine adenosis is usually an incidental microscopic finding but may be detected mammographically because of associated microcalcifications or on MRI as non–mass-like enhancement {18171412}.

Apocrine adenoma may present as a solitary, painless, freely mobile, well-defined, palpable nodule. In 1 case, the size minimally fluctuated with the menstrual cycle and was also associated with a white nipple discharge {2362943}. On imaging, apocrine adenoma usually presents as a well-circumscribed mass, which is hypoechoic on ultrasound. On both clinical and radiological examination, the presentation is similar and indistinguishable from a fibroadenoma.

Epidemiology

Apocrine adenosis occurs most frequently in the third or fourth decade of life. The expected frequency of apocrine adenosis on core biopsies is not well established but appears to be < 1–2% {18171412;27649945}. The mean age in a large case series was 59 years {22288965}.

In contrast to apocrine metaplasia, which commonly occurs in the setting of fibrocystic changes, apocrine adenoma appears to be extremely rare with only a handful of cases described in the literature. However, since there is no size criterion to discriminate a collection of metaplastic apocrine ducts from a fully developed apocrine adenoma, the latter is probably underrepresented in the literature. It predominates in female patients with occasional case reports in male patients {14567753}. The age at presentation ranges from 14 years (1 male patient and 1 female patient) to 72 years.

Etiology

No specific etiological factors have been identified that are linked to apocrine adenosis.

Whether apocrine adenoma is a de novo tumour or a hyperplastic lesion, such as nodular sclerosing adenosis with superimposed apocrine metaplasia, is debatable.

Pathogenesis

Apocrine change is the most common type of metaplastic change in the breast {18171412}. Regarding the origin of apocrine cells, most agree that breast ductal epithelial cells undergo metaplasia around puberty to become apocrine cells, similar to those seen in the axilla, areola, and perineum. This is supported by a transition from normal cuboidal ductal epithelium to an intermediate form that culminates in the fully mature apocrine cell, influenced by androgens and activin. The latter may explain the few cases of apocrine adenoma that have been described in male patients. An alternative theory for the origin of the apocrine cells is embryological entrapment from cutaneous apocrine glands during development. Some refute both theories by arguing that apocrine cells are normal constituents of breast epithelium.

Given the low prevalence of apocrine adenosis and apocrine adenoma in the literature, the pathogenesis of these lesions has not been well studied. Loss of heterozygosity has been identified in apocrine proliferations in several loci (1p, 3, 11q, 16, 17q) {11425268}. Comparative genomic hybridization has shown losses and gains in apocrine adenosis, with accumulation through apocrine ductal carcinoma in situ and apocrine carcinoma {11141494}. Molecularly, the few described cases are diploid, but some may be tetraploid. Studies performed on the more common entities of apocrine metaplasia and apocrine adenosis have reported an elevated risk of subsequent breast cancer and possibly premalignant potential {7560201}. Abnormal oncoprotein and apoptosis-related protein expression associated with higher proliferation rates have been described in some cases, as well as loss of heterozygosity and allelic imbalance, suggesting a possible oncogenic potential for selected metaplastic apocrine lesions that may have genetic instability {20123450}.

Macroscopic appearance

Apocrine adenosis is usually grossly inapparent. In some cases, a rubbery grey mass may be appreciated on gross examination. Owing to the association with microcalcifications, the cut surface may be gritty {18171412}.

On gross examination, apocrine adenomas are well-circumscribed nodules which may be focally cystic, ranging in size from 3 to 17 mm.

Histopathology

Apocrine cells have abundant eosinophilic, granular, or foamy cytoplasm, a supranuclear vacuole, and an enlarged nucleus with even chromatin and a single prominent nucleolus.

Apocrine adenosis (sclerosing adenosis with apocrine change) is characterized by a lobulocentric proliferation of acini lined by apocrine cells, with an attenuated peripheral myoepithelial cell layer, in turn surrounded by basement membrane. The acini are distorted by stromal sclerosis. Variation in nuclear size (between apocrine cells) should be < 3-fold {34537861}.

Atypical apocrine adenosis refers to apocrine adenosis with superimposed cytological atypia, based on the recognition of ≥ 3-fold variation in nuclear size. Apoptosis and mitotic activity are occasionally observed {18171412;25106711;27684975}.

Apocrine adenoma comprises a well-circumscribed, compact proliferation of round and oval glands lined by luminal apocrine cells with a peripheral, attenuated, myoepithelial cell layer. The glands may be cystically dilated or form delicate papillary fronds. The apocrine cells are evenly spaced, columnar or cuboidal in shape, and with apical luminal blebs (snouts) and decapitated eosinophilic secretory luminal debris. The nuclei are basally located, bland appearing, round to oval shaped, and with uniform chromatin and a single punctate nucleolus. Rarely, microcalcifications, atypia, and mitoses may be present.

Ultrastructurally, the granular cytoplasm that characterizes apocrine cells is attributed to abundant endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria, intermediate filaments, and secretory vesicles. The cytoplasm may also contain supranuclear golden-brown granules that are positive with PASD and Prussian blue (iron) staining.

Apocrine cells are typically ER-negative, PR-negative, and AR-positive. They also express EMA, CK8/18, CD10, and three proteins found in cyst fluid, GCDFP-15 (PIP), GCDFP-24 (apo-D), and GCDFP-44 (ZAG).

Differential diagnosis

Florid apocrine adenosis and atypical apocrine adenosis may be mistaken for invasive carcinoma, particularly on core needle biopsy. Immunohistochemical myoepithelial cell markers are useful in reaching the correct diagnosis {27684975}, but awareness that the myoepithelial cell layer may be attenuated or even partly lost in benign apocrine lesions is important to avoid overdiagnosis of malignancy {22339683;21263240}.

Complex fibroadenoma may contain foci of apocrine metaplasia and is distinguished from apocrine adenoma by its preserved fibroepithelial architecture, prominent stroma, and focal rather than dense proliferation of apocrine cells. Tubular adenoma is composed of closely packed tubules and is architecturally similar to apocrine adenoma, but lacks apocrine metaplasia. Apocrine adenoma is differentiated from ductal adenoma by the lack of a thick duct wall and diffuse rather than focal apocrine metaplasia. It is discriminated from prominent apocrine metaplasia, seen as part of the spectrum of fibrocystic change, by its well-demarcated outline. Lastly, it is differentiated from atypical and malignant apocrine proliferations by the lack of cytological and architectural atypia and necrosis.

Cytology

The diagnosis of apocrine adenosis cannot be made specifically on FNAB, but smears are cellular with apocrine cells seen singly and in small tissue fragments with possible fragments of lobules. The apocrine cells have granular, eosinophilic, or foamy cytoplasm, central round nuclei with mild to moderate variation in size, pale chromatin, and one prominent nucleolus. The background contains few to rare bipolar naked nuclei. Features that might suggest atypia include a 3-fold variation in nuclear size and prominent nucleoli {18171412;25106711;27684975}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For apocrine adenosis: a lobulocentric proliferation of benign glandular structures, distorted by stromal sclerosis, lined by apocrine cells (sclerosing adenosis with apocrine change) For apocrine adenoma: a well-delineated, benign tumour characterized by a proliferation of round and oval tubular structures, lined by luminal apocrine cells, with a peripheral attenuated layer of myoepithelial cells, and little background stroma.

Desirable:

PASD positivity of apocrine cells and immunohistochemical expression of EMA, CK8/18, and AR, with ER and PR negativity.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Subsequent breast cancer risk associated with a diagnosis of apocrine adenosis appears to be in the order of 1–2-fold. Albeit with limited data, atypical apocrine adenosis does not appear to behave as a high-risk precursor lesion {22288965}.

Based on the few cases described in the literature, apocrine adenoma is considered benign and stable, further supported by low Ki-67 scores (range: 0.7–1.3%) {20123450}. After core biopsy diagnosis confirmation, apocrine adenoma can be managed like fibroadenoma. No recurrences or reports of malignant transformation have been noted.
