6.2.4 Postradiation angiosarcoma

Definition

Postradiation angiosarcoma is a malignant tumour of endothelial differentiation of the skin or rarely of the breast parenchyma occurring secondary to irradiation.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: postirradiation angiosarcoma.

Not recommended: postradiation haemangiosarcoma; postradiation lymphangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lesions occur in the skin of the chest wall or scars within the irradiation field of previously treated breast carcinoma {15166670;16288486}.

Clinical features

Because postradiation angiosarcomas occur in patients treated for breast cancer, the age range at diagnosis reflects that specific cohort. Tumours arise in the radiation field with a mean latency period of 5–6 years {15166670;16006792}, with rare cases presenting after a shorter latency period of 1–2 years. They typically present as solitary or multiple cutaneous erythematous to violaceous patches, plaques, papules, or nodules, occasionally with ulceration {16006792;23327731}. Rarely, they may present as a rash or subtle skin thickening. Historically, angiosarcoma also arose in the setting of chronic lymphoedema (Stewart–Treves syndrome).

Epidemiology

Postradiation angiosarcoma is a tumour, described to occur at an older patient age than primary angiosarcoma of the breast (a median of 70 years vs 40 years) {18813119;16288486}. As the frequency of breast-conserving therapy for breast carcinomas has increased over time, the incidence of postradiation angiosarcoma has also increased (3-fold between 1992 and 2016 in the SEER database) by as many as 30 cases per 100 000 person-years {37725702}.

Etiology

This type of angiosarcoma is most commonly seen in the setting of breast-conserving surgery with radiation therapy {15166670;16006792}. Less frequently, it occurs in the setting of mastectomy and adjuvant radiation therapy. Angiosarcoma is the most common radiation-induced sarcoma of the breast {11955733;15981282}.

Pathogenesis

Ionizing radiation has been theorized to induce the development of angiosarcoma by several mechanisms, including direct damage of DNA, leading to genomic instability and mutation of cancer-related genes; prolonged cellular stimulation during repair of radiation-induced ischaemia; and/or radiation-induced chronic lymphoedema, which increases vascular growth factors {27128306;29249796;31448482}. Amplification of the MYC oncogene (8q24) is the most recurrent and characteristic alteration found in postradiation angiosarcoma {24457083;21909081;22121953}; this aberration is not identified in atypical vascular lesions and is rare in primary angiosarcoma {22383169}. In addition, postradiation angiosarcomas harbour more frequent FLT4, CRKL, HRAS, and KMT2D alterations than primary angiosarcomas {37350195}. This mutation landscape, which is distinct from those of other sarcoma types, is characterized by upregulation of various vascular-specific receptor tyrosine kinases. FLT4 (5q35, also known as VEGFR3) is seen coamplified with MYC in a subset of cases {26735859}. Mutations in KDR (VEGFR2) and PLCG1 (another member of the VEGFR2 signalling pathway) have also been identified {26735859;24633157}; FLT4, KDR, and PLCG1 alterations appear to be mutually exclusive. Truncating mutations in PTPRB, a tyrosine phosphatase specific to vascular endothelium that inhibits angiogenesis, have also been reported in a subset of angiosarcomas {24633157}.

Macroscopic appearance

On cut surface, the lesions vary from haemangioma-like to solid masses, showing haemorrhagic and necrotic central areas and more spongy, vascular features towards the periphery. The extent of the tumours cannot be reliably determined on gross examination.

Histopathology

Postradiation angiosarcomas can be diffuse or multifocal, and they are predominantly dermal-based tumours with variable infiltration into the subcutaneous tissues {7801888}. Infrequently, tumours extend into the deeper breast parenchyma. Although these tumours display a heterogeneous appearance, most have a vasoformative growth pattern. Well-differentiated neoplasms are composed of irregular, angulated, and variably dilated vascular channels, often with a sieve-like morphology {15166670}. The neoplastic channels are lined by ovoid cells with hyperchromatic or vesicular nuclei and often prominent small nucleoli. Lesions with intermediate morphology show intraluminal papillary growth or multilayering, with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei and increased mitotic figures. Poorly differentiated tumours show a solid growth pattern, composed of either sheets of epithelioid cells or short fascicles of spindle cells {12775509;15166670}, associated with prominent mitoses, blood lakes, and necrosis.

Epithelioid angiosarcomas are more common in postradiation angiosarcomas than in primary tumours; they have a solid appearance, with sheets of large atypical epithelioid to polygonal cells with ovoid vesicular nuclei, large nucleoli, and relatively abundant cytoplasm. Because vasoformation can be limited, epithelioid angiosarcoma may be confused with carcinoma. Less-common patterns include the capillary lobule pattern and the radiation dermatitis–like pattern {16121049;28169467}. The former consists of a lobular proliferation of atypical endothelial cells architecturally resembling haemangioma, but with an infiltrative growth pattern. The latter consists of scattered hyperchromatic cells associated with haemorrhage to thin, worm-like vascular channels lined by atypical endothelial cells. Despite heterogeneous morphology, the use of grading is not recommended; all cases are considered high-grade, with most following a highly aggressive course {18813119}.

Diffuse and strong immunoreactivity is seen with both CD31 and ERG, along with variable expression of CD34 and podoplanin (recognized by D2-40). Focal keratin and rarely EMA expression can be seen, especially in the epithelioid pattern {23689692}. Tumours also show strong nuclear expression of MYC, due to the consistent MYC gene amplification {24457083;21909081;22121953}. Radiation-associated angiosarcomas may demonstrate loss of H3 p.Lys27me3 (H3K27me3) {28983701}, whereas this marker is retained in the endothelial cells of benign and atypical vascular lesions. Most secondary angiosarcomas show strong immunoreactivity to PRAME, in contrast to atypical vascular lesions {37477762;39152799}.

Angiosarcomas may be associated with preceding or synchronous postradiation atypical vascular lesions in the skin and subcutis of the breast. In challenging cases, immunohistochemistry or FISH can be performed, as postradiation atypical vascular lesions are MYC-negative {21909081}.

Cytology

The features are described in section Primary angiosarcoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

High-level amplification of MYC at 8q24 is a consistent hallmark of radiation-induced and lymphoedema-associated angiosarcomas of the breast, present in > 90% of cases {20008140;20949568;26735859}. The high-level amplification of MYC, typically defined as > 100 copies, in the form of homogeneously staining regions or multiple focal amplicons on FISH, can be used to exclude challenging atypical vascular lesions {20949568;21909081}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: previous irradiation of the field, usually after a time interval of > 3 years; predominantly dermal to subcutaneous growth, with rarer involvement of breast parenchyma; infiltrative growth pattern, with dissection of adipose tissue and lobular stroma; vasoformative growth with at least focal cytological atypia.

Desirable: MYC overexpression by immunohistochemistry; MYC amplification by FISH.

Staging

Angiosarcomas of the breast are staged using the soft tissue sarcoma system, with pT stage dependent on tumour size.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour size has been shown to be a prognostic factor for angiosarcoma {30820717;38722225}. There is a high rate of local recurrence, with a median recurrence-free survival time of < 3 years {15166670;16006792}. Multiple recurrences are common. Common distant metastatic sites include the lungs, contralateral breast, skin, liver, and bone {15166670}. Axillary lymph node metastases are rare {35426390}. Median overall survival was recently reported as 42 months, with 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival being 80%, 42%, and 34%, respectively {37158932}.

The use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy is increasing, with partial to complete pathological responses reported {34173294;38722225;39016208;39476458}. In one recent study, good responders had a significantly improved disease-specific survival, and patients with MYC-amplified tumours receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy showed better disease-free survival compared with patients with MYC-amplified tumours not receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy {38722225}. No standard guidelines exist for the use of adjuvant radiation or systemic therapy. Some reports demonstrate improved survival with adjuvant chemotherapy {29249796;32889893;34409543;38833974}, while others do not {36538913;39016208}.
