2.3.1 Tubular adenoma

Definition

Tubular adenoma is a benign well-circumscribed tumour composed of a dense proliferation of closely approximated round and oval tubular structures composed of bilayered ductal and myoepithelial cells, with little background stroma.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: fibroadenoma, pericanalicular subtype (with prominent epithelial proliferation).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Tubular adenoma can arise anywhere in the breast {24926447}.

Clinical features

Tubular adenomas are slow-growing neoplasms that may be detected 2–12 months after inception {24926447}. On physical examination, they present as palpable, solitary, painless, freely mobile, well-defined masses. On imaging, they usually present as a well-circumscribed mass. On ultrasonography, they are hypoechoic with mild posterior acoustic resonance. MRI shows heterogeneously enhancing, T2-hyperintense, oval masses with persistent kinetics {38141235}. Fibroadenoma and tubular adenoma share similar clinical, radiological, cytological, and immunohistochemical findings; therefore, tubular adenomas may be mistaken for fibroadenoma preoperatively, but they can be distinguished by histopathology. In rare cases, tubular adenomas may exhibit rapid enlargement. Infrequently, they may show suspicious findings on imaging (Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System [BI-RADS] 4) {29449780}, including clustered high-density calcifications {38141235}; other suspicious features include older patient age (> 48 years), a firm consistency, restricted mobility, poorly defined margins, large size, and skin ulceration {24926447}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of tubular adenoma ranges from 0.13% to 2.9% {24926447;26516321}. These tumours usually occur in young premenopausal women of childbearing age (15–49 years), with 90% of cases occurring in women < 40 years of age {24926447}. Tubular adenomas are rare before menarche or after menopause {16684324;11029792;11972869}.

Etiology

Tubular adenomas arise from terminal duct lobular units. The exact etiology is unknown, but given the incidence in women of reproductive age, it is most likely related to reproductive hormones. No clear association with pregnancy or oral contraceptive use has been described.

Pathogenesis

The overlap in clinicopathological features between tubular adenoma and fibroadenoma, their occasional coexistence, and their combined features in an adenoma {30150335} suggest that they share common histogenesis {25775939}, with the stromal component dominating in fibroadenoma and the epithelial and myoepithelial cells in tubular adenoma. The few cases of tubular adenoma that have been studied using next-generation sequencing show a varied mutation spectrum. One study showed MED12 alterations in 9 of 18 tested tubular adenomas, with 7 of those at the codon 44 hotspot, as in fibroadenomas and other fibroepithelial lesions {26109290}. In contrast, other series characterized mutations only in MET and FGFR3 {27438523}. Tubular adenomas in patients with Cowden syndrome demonstrate loss of PTEN expression by immunohistochemistry {38000681}. One potential explanation for this discrepancy may be that tubular adenomas resemble the pericanalicular subset of fibroadenomas, which have a lower prevalence than intracanalicular fibroadenomas of MED12 exon 2 mutations. In light of this divergent molecular data, despite the similarities in clinical, radiological, cytological, and immunohistochemical findings between tubular adenoma and fibroadenoma, as well as their occasional admixture and association, their histogenetic relationship remains uncertain {27438523;38000681}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumour size is generally 10–75 mm (average: 30 mm), with rare giant forms (as large as 200 mm {31585326}) reported in the literature. Like fibroadenomas, tubular adenomas are usually smoothly rounded, unencapsulated, firm tumours that have a solid homogeneous or micronodular rubbery cut surface. However, they tend to be softer than fibroadenomas and the cut surface is yellow to tan-brown in colour rather than white as in fibroadenomas.

Histopathology

Low-power views show a sharply demarcated, compact proliferation of small, uniformly round and regular tubular structures. High-power views reveal tubules lined by a double layer of inner ductal luminal cells and an outer layer of attenuated myoepithelial cells. Rare nucleoli and mitoses are seen. The lumen is usually empty but can occasionally contain mucin or PAS-positive eosinophilic proteinaceous material. Unlike in fibroadenomas, the intervening stroma is sparse, mainly consisting of a delicate fibrovascular network and possibly containing a few scattered lymphocytes. Consequently, no compression of the tubules due to fibrosis is evident. Infarction has rarely been described (in 2.4% of cases) {22383931}. Fibroadenoma and tubular adenoma can be definitively distinguished only on histopathology, the key features being the prominent adenosis-like epithelial proliferation and the minimal stroma in tubular adenoma.

Cytology

Tubular adenomas show moderate to highly cellular smears with cohesive small tubular tissue fragments and larger tissue fragments containing multiple tubules in a loose stroma with a small number of bare bipolar nuclei in a clean background. The main differential diagnosis is fibroadenoma, which features larger, irregular, often 3D epithelial tissue fragments, plentiful bare bipolar nuclei, and larger, often myxoid stromal fragments {36516736}. Fibroadenomas may show occasional tubular epithelial tissue fragments.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a well-circumscribed tumour composed of a dense proliferation of closely approximated round and oval tubular structures with little background stroma; tubules composed of bilayered ductal and myoepithelial cells.

Desirable: immunohistochemical evaluation for confirmation of the dual cell population by myoepithelial markers (p63, p40, SMA, calponin) and luminal markers (EMA, CK19, CK8/18), if needed.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Tubular adenomas are benign neoplasms with no increased risk of malignancy {38141235}. However, rare case reports describe their association with ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive ductal carcinoma {22700460}.
