2.5.3 Papillary ductal carcinoma in situ

Definition

Papillary ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is composed of filiform arborizing fibrovascular cores lined by neoplastic ductal epithelium, devoid of myoepithelium and contained within central or peripheral ducts with retained myoepithelium at the periphery.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: papillary carcinoma in situ.

Not recommended: intracystic papillary ductal carcinoma; encapsulated/solid papillary carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Papillary DCIS is often associated with DCIS having other architectural patterns, and it may involve central or peripheral ducts. Papillary DCIS in central/subareolar ducts may be associated with encapsulated papillary carcinoma.

Clinical features

Papillary DCIS occurring in peripheral ducts tends to be clinically occult and is usually detected mammographically owing to associated microcalcifications, or rarely as a circumscribed or scattered nodularity on other imaging modalities (see the Clinical features subsection in section Ductal carcinoma in situ). DCIS with papillary, cribriform, and micropapillary patterns often coexists with encapsulated papillary carcinoma, especially in the retroareolar location. While in the past, encapsulated papillary carcinoma and adjacent DCIS were classified together as intracystic papillary DCIS, this terminology is not recommended (see also section Encapsulated papillary carcinoma). It has been reported that papillary DCIS accounts for approximately 3% of DCIS cases {25471940}.

Epidemiology

Papillary DCIS represents approximately 1.5% of all DCIS cases in the SEER registry 2013–2017 and tends to be more common in postmenopausal women [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program Internet. SEER Cancer Statistics Review. Cancer of the Female Breast (In Situ). Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2019 cited 2025 Jun 25. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/archive/csr/1975_2017/browse_csr.php?sectionSEL=4&pageSEL=sect_04_table.26 ]]. Papillary DCIS is also the most common type of DCIS in men {9827718}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Papillary DCIS shows the same genetic alterations as DCIS of similar nuclear grade but different architectural patterns (see section Ductal carcinoma in situ).

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of papillary DCIS in a subareolar location is difficult to distinguish from that of encapsulated papillary carcinoma (see section Encapsulated papillary carcinoma); the two lesions may coexist. Peripherally located papillary DCIS is usually not apparent on macroscopic examination.

Histopathology

Papillary DCIS consists of an intraductal neoplastic epithelial proliferation lining arborizing fibrovascular cores devoid of myoepithelium. Unlike encapsulated and solid papillary carcinomas, papillary DCIS shows an intact myoepithelial layer at the epithelium–stroma interface of the involved ducts. The epithelial proliferation may partially or completely fill the spaces between the papillae, creating solid, cribriform, micropapillary, spindled, or compact columnar patterns that may obscure the underlying papillary architecture. Papillary DCIS often constitutes one of several architectural patterns in a case of DCIS, but rarely it may be the only pattern, especially in the retroareolar location. Compared with other types of DCIS, papillary DCIS is less commonly associated with comedonecrosis {25471940}. Like all types of DCIS, papillary DCIS is graded on the basis of the nuclear atypia of the neoplastic epithelium; the majority are of low or intermediate nuclear grade, with some cases of high-grade papillary DCIS reported {25471940}. Although most papillary DCIS lesions consist of a single, uniform cell population, papillary DCIS with a dimorphic pattern has been described {8056421}. In addition to neoplastic ductal cells, this type features a second population of epithelial cells with abundant clear cytoplasm (globoid cells) that resemble myoepithelial cells but have the same degree of nuclear atypia as other neoplastic epithelial cells {8056421}.

The differential diagnosis of papillary DCIS includes papilloma, papilloma with atypia, atypical adenomyoepithelioma, and encapsulated papillary carcinoma {21084964;21753694}. Solid papillary carcinoma in situ, a special subtype of papillary DCIS, is described separately (see section Solid papillary carcinoma (in situ and invasive).

The differential diagnosis of dimorphic papillary DCIS includes papilloma with DCIS and atypical adenomyoepithelioma. Immunohistochemical staining for myoepithelial antigens, high-molecular-weight cytokeratins (CK5/6 and CK14), ER, and neuroendocrine antigens may be used to distinguish between these lesions (see #47567Table 2.04). Low-grade and intermediate-grade papillary DCIS typically show diffuse nuclear positivity for ER and no expression of CK5/6 or CK14 in the neoplastic epithelial component; however, high-grade papillary DCIS may show a different pattern.

Cytology

Papillary DCIS is rarely diagnosed specifically on FNAB cytopathology and has a differential diagnosis of usual ductal hyperplasia, atypical ductal hyperplasia, encapsulated papillary carcinoma, solid papillary carcinoma, and low-grade invasive carcinoma. Features that suggest papillary DCIS in the usually cellular smears are micropapillary tissue fragments with thin-necked bulbous-tipped papillary projections lacking myoepithelial cells, smaller papillary tissue fragments that lack myoepithelial cells and show relatively uniform ductal-type cells, and marked dispersal of single often columnar cells showing no or mild nuclear atypia. The nuclear grade is low to intermediate. Foamy histiocytes may be present in the background, but bare bipolar nuclei are scant or absent.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

See section Ductal carcinoma in situ.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a neoplastic proliferation of epithelial cells covering delicate arborizing fibrovascular cores devoid of myoepithelium but contained within a duct with a surrounding myoepithelial layer.

Desirable: immunohistochemical demonstration of an absence of myoepithelium along the fibrovascular cores but the presence of myoepithelium at the periphery of the tumour-containing duct.
