2.2.5 Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion

Definition

Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion is a benign lesion with fibroelastosis and entrapped glandular structures, with or without proliferative epithelial lesions. Radial scars are small lesions, typically with a stellate configuration, whereas complex sclerosing lesions are larger (often > 10 mm) and/or more disorganized.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: radial scar; radial sclerosing lesion; benign sclerosing ductal proliferation.

Not recommended: sclerosing papillary lesion; scleroelastotic scar; stellate scar; non-encapsulated sclerosing lesion.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Not applicable

Clinical features

On breast imaging, the irregular stellate configuration may mimic an invasive carcinoma; the presence of a radiolucent centre is reported as indicative of radial scar as opposed to carcinoma. Rarely, the lesion is of sufficient size to produce a palpable mass. Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion may be multiple and is frequently bilateral {28813198;25578683;20306826}.

Epidemiology

In case series, described occurrence varies depending on the mode of detection. In current practice, these lesions are most often identified by mammography {30390667;39209731}; the rate of radial scar detection by core biopsy has increased after the introduction of digital breast tomosynthesis with no significant increase in the upgrade rate at surgical excision {30242581}.

Etiology

No specific factors have been identified that are etiologically linked to radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion.

Pathogenesis

PIK3CA hotspot mutations were detected in 64% of radial scars {24193002} and 75% of radial sclerosing lesions {28451460} in two separate studies. A study of infiltrating epitheliosis also yielded a high frequency of PIK3CA hotspot mutations and a PIK3R1 mutation in 1 case {26497122}. The landscape of somatic genetic alterations found in radial scar, complex sclerosing lesion, and infiltrating epitheliosis suggests that they represent a morphological spectrum of related lesions.

Macroscopic appearance

These lesions may be undetectable on gross examination or may be of sufficient size to produce an irregular area of firmness that can exhibit yellow streaks reflecting the elastotic stroma. The gross appearance may be indistinguishable from that of an invasive carcinoma {30390667;28813198;25578683;20306826}.

Histopathology

Radial scar has a zonal stellate configuration, whereas complex sclerosing lesions are larger and more disorganized. Central dense hyalinized collagen and elastosis (sometimes marked) are seen. Entrapped in the central fibrous tissue are small, irregular, benign tubules. A two-cell layer is retained, although this layer may not always be visible on H&E staining, because the myoepithelial cell layer is occasionally attenuated. Around the periphery of the lesion, there are various degrees of ductal dilatation, usual ductal hyperplasia, and apocrine metaplasia.

In larger complex sclerosing lesions (often > 10 mm), several of these components appear to combine and then converge with intermingling areas of sclerosing adenosis and small, frequently sclerosing micropapillomas and various patterns of epithelial proliferation {30390667;28813198;25578683;20306826}.

Infiltrating epitheliosis is a rare complex sclerosing lesion of the breast, characterized by infiltrating ducts surrounded by a scleroelastotic stroma. The ducts contain a florid epithelial proliferation with the characteristic morphology of usual ductal hyperplasia. This epithelial hyperplastic process appears to flow or infiltrate into the surrounding stroma; of note, myoepithelial cells may be discontinuous or absent around the most-peripheral glands {20306826;26497122;39403834;22461653}. No long-term follow-up information on infiltrating epitheliosis is available and this diagnosis should be rendered with extreme caution.

Cytology

Radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion shows a mix of features of epithelial hyperplasia and fibrocystic change: predominantly large epithelial tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells, smaller epithelial tissue fragments, and bare bipolar nuclei, as well as apocrine sheets and histiocytes in a proteinaceous background, which may dilute the bare bipolar nuclei. Nuclear features in the epithelial cells, whether in tissue fragments or seen as single intact cells, are bland, with generally small nucleoli. Occasionally, stromal tufts and tubular epithelial tissue fragments may be seen {10458501}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: characteristic lobulocentric architecture; presence of a myoepithelial cell layer and basement membrane around the tubular structures; dense hyalinized stroma with elastosis.

Desirable: demonstration of myoepithelial cells by immunohistochemistry (e.g. p63, calponin, SMMHC) if needed.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Despite suggestions (especially in the radiology literature) that these lesions may be premalignant, there is no evidence to support this contention. The results of two long-term follow-up studies suggest that any apparent risk is related to the various patterns of associated intraductal hyperplasia. An update analysis of one of these studies showed that radial scar is associated with an increased risk of breast cancer even after adjustment for histological category of benign breast disease (relative risk: 1.74), whereas a meta-analysis suggested that the magnitude of risk is more modest (relative risk: 1.45). Atypical hyperplasia and carcinoma (both in situ and invasive) may involve or arise in association with radial scar / complex sclerosing lesion, particularly in lesions measuring > 6 mm, which are more often mammographically detected and found in women aged > 50 years. The management of radial scar and complex sclerosing lesion detected by mammography remains controversial {28215506;29532340;27371970;26967341}. Lesions with associated epithelial atypia detected on core needle biopsy have a risk of associated malignancy and should be excised. Data indicate that lesions that are radiology–pathology concordant without atypia may not require excision {38845392}. A rare association between complex sclerosing lesion and metaplastic carcinoma, in particular low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma, has been described {28813198;26967341;20506884;21073820}.
