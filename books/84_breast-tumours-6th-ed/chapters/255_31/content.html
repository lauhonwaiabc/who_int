2.5.5 Solid papillary carcinoma (in situ and invasive)

Definition

Solid papillary carcinomas (in situ and invasive) are tumours characterized by a solid growth pattern with delicate fibrovascular cores. They frequently show neuroendocrine differentiation and are biologically indolent.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: neuroendocrine breast carcinoma in situ; spindle cell ductal carcinoma in situ; neuroendocrine ductal carcinoma in situ.

Not recommended: endocrine ductal carcinoma in situ; solid papillary carcinoma with invasion.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Any part of the breast may be affected, but the central/subareolar area is affected most commonly.

Clinical features

Solid papillary carcinoma may present as a palpable breast mass, an imaging abnormality, and/or a bloody nipple discharge. On mammographic examination, it can appear as a rounded, circumscribed mass, and on ultrasound as a solid well-defined hypoechoic or heterogeneous mass. Coexisting stromal distortion suggests an associated invasive component.

Epidemiology

Solid papillary carcinoma is described to account for < 1% of cases {35096664} with an incidence in a Japanese case series of 1.1–1.7% {17587241}. It occurs primarily in postmenopausal women, mainly during the seventh decade of life or later {7573685;21753694}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Solid papillary carcinomas have relatively simple genomes with few copy-number aberrations, including 16q losses, 16p gains, and 1q gains {22025283}. PIK3CA mutations are found in 43% of cases. The rare cases that have been studied are classified as luminal B by PAM50 subtyping {25041824}. When compared with invasive breast carcinomas of no special type (NST) of the same grade, solid papillary carcinomas display lower expression levels of genes related to proliferation, cell adhesion, migration, and movement, suggestive of a less invasive phenotype. Genes related to neuroendocrine differentiation, including RET, ASCL1, and DOK7, are expressed at significantly higher levels in solid papillary carcinomas than in encapsulated papillary carcinomas {25041824}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross examination may show a well-circumscribed, tan-pink soft mass.

Histopathology

Solid papillary carcinomas are characterized by expansile nodules with a solid growth pattern and inconspicuous, delicate fibrovascular cores. The proliferation is composed of a monotonous population of round to spindle-shaped epithelial cells with (usually) mild to moderate nuclear atypia and a variable mitotic count. The cells have eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and may contain mucin vacuoles. Signet ring–cell or plasmacytoid features may be prominent. Nuclear palisading around the fibrovascular cores is common. Extracellular mucin production can also be present; when prominent, mucinous carcinoma needs to be excluded {7573685}. Indeed, there is a recognized association with type B mucinous carcinoma. Neuroendocrine differentiation may also be appreciated, especially when there is an associated mucinous component or an invasive component with neuroendocrine differentiation {38958708;36645462}.

Solid papillary carcinoma in situ is diagnosed when the nodules have rounded, well-circumscribed contours and a distribution pattern consistent with an in situ process, regardless of whether a myoepithelial cell layer is present around the periphery of the nodules {17927587}. Solid papillary carcinoma in situ with invasion is diagnosed when solid papillary carcinoma is associated with strands or large clusters of tumour cells with an irregular contour and an infiltrative growth pattern, often invasive breast carcinoma NST. On occasion, these irregular clusters are present within pools of extracellular mucin at the periphery of the lesion, recognizable as mucinous carcinoma {7573685;17587241}. The invasive component may also be of other special histological types, such as lobular, cribriform, or tubular carcinoma {16625097}. In rare cases, the majority of the lesion shows features of invasive disease (invasive solid papillary carcinoma) composed of tumour nodules devoid of myoepithelium with ragged contours creating a geographical jigsaw pattern within a desmoplastic stroma {27017244;17587241}. Fat infiltration by irregular solid papillary nests and vascular invasion may also be seen in these invasive cases.

The neoplastic cells do not express high-molecular-weight cytokeratins. They are strongly and diffusely ER-positive, PR expression is variable, and ERBB2 (HER2) is not overexpressed. The Ki-67 proliferation index is usually low or intermediate {27017244;16625097;22025283}. Neuroendocrine differentiation is common and can be demonstrated by synaptophysin, INSM1, and chromogranin expression {17587241}.

The differential diagnosis includes papilloma with florid usual ductal hyperplasia, particularly if the hyperplasia completely obliterates the spaces between papillae. In such cases, the hyperplastic epithelial cells in papilloma express high-molecular-weight cytokeratins and show heterogeneous positivity for ER, whereas the cells of solid papillary carcinoma lack expression of high-molecular-weight cytokeratins and show strong, diffuse ER positivity. A myoepithelial cell layer is present along the fibrovascular cores and at the periphery of intraductal papilloma. Other considerations include encapsulated papillary carcinoma, in which the rounded tumour nodule possesses a fibrous capsule and the neoplastic epithelium between the papillae displays a predominantly cribriform arrangement, and invasive lobular carcinoma with solid papillary morphology, in which the tumour cells have the cytological and immunophenotypical features of lobular neoplasia {27116593}.

Cytology

Solid papillary carcinoma shows high cellularity with a large number of dispersed single cells showing mild to moderate nuclear enlargement and variable columnar cytoplasm, highly discohesive epithelial tissue fragments varying from small to large and lacking myoepithelial cells, and distinctive branching thin fibrovascular tissue fragments with few attached epithelial cells. Papillary tissue fragments with fibrovascular cores covered in multilayered crowded columnar epithelial cells may be present. Bare bipolar nuclei are absent {17580353}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For solid papillary carcinoma in situ: expansile nodules composed of a monotonous population of round to spindle-shaped epithelial cells with mild to moderate nuclear atypia and delicate fibrovascular cores; distribution consistent with carcinoma in situ regardless of the presence of myoepithelial cells For solid papillary carcinoma in situ with invasion: a solid papillary carcinoma in situ with associated recognizable invasive component (different morphologies) For invasive solid papillary carcinoma: tumour nodules composed of cells with solid papillary carcinoma cytomorphology devoid of myoepithelium with ragged contours creating a geographical jigsaw pattern within a desmoplastic stroma.

Desirable:

Neuroendocrine differentiation as demonstrated by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Solid papillary carcinoma in situ is staged as pTis (DCIS), whether a myoepithelial cell layer is identified or not.

Solid papillary carcinoma in situ with invasion is staged according to the size of the invasive component only.

Invasive solid papillary carcinoma is staged according to the size of the invasive component.

Prognosis and prediction

Solid papillary carcinoma has an excellent prognosis. Spread to axillary lymph nodes is reported rarely in cases of solid papillary carcinoma in situ with invasion (3–5%) and metastasis to distant sites is exceptional {27017244;38958708}.
