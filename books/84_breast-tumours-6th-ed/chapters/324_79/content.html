3.2.4 Invasive lobular carcinoma

Definition

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is an invasive breast carcinoma most often composed of dyscohesive monotonous cells that are dispersed or arranged in single-file linear cords with minimal or no stromal reaction.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: infiltrating lobular carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

A high rate of multicentricity (i.e. multiple tumours in different quadrants of the same breast) and multifocality (i.e. multiple tumours in the same quadrant) has been reported in some studies {2153007;6277027;23490334;18629634}, but this has not been found in other series based on clinical {8630916} or radiological {1438749} analysis. An incidence of contralateral tumours, particularly synchronous tumours, of 5–19% has been reported, which is higher than for invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), albeit that remains controversial {15084238;7602346;10548312;4337952;18458044;8156495}.

Clinical features

ILC may present as a palpable breast lump but may also be impalpable owing to its tendency to infiltrate breast stroma and adipose tissue without eliciting a stromal reaction. This may lead to more-advanced disease at presentation compared with other types of breast cancers. Similarly, while ILC may be visualized on mammography, as distortion or as a spiculated mass, it may not be easily visualized on all imaging views and may be mammographically occult. Microcalcifications are infrequent. Mammography has a lower sensitivity for the detection of ILC (57–89%) than of IBC-NST, with false negative rates as high as 19% {1984294;8273634;1438749;38730592}. ILC is therefore overrepresented in women with interval cancers. The detection of ILC has improved with the use of digital breast tomosynthesis, particularly in women with dense breasts {27210245;30627022;37938385}. Ultrasonography is more sensitive than mammography (78–95%), but may underestimate tumour size {9930776;11259968;10767184;9571945;15293298;15708454;19168843}. Interestingly, in ILC with CDH1 non-truncating mutations, there is greater discrepancy between radiological and pathological size evaluation {39025406}.

MRI is more effective in diagnosing ILC, in particular multifocal and multicentric lesions, and is included in most guidelines for evaluation of disease extent following a diagnosis of ILC on core needle biopsy {18389253;37504323} [[National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Early and locally advanced breast cancer: diagnosis and management (NG101). London (UK): NICE; 2025 Feb 19. Available from: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng101. ]], but may lead to false positives and overestimation of tumour size {20657229;15024602}. Contrast-enhanced mammography is an alternative to breast MRI {23220903} with improved sensitivity for ILC compared with standard mammography. Contrast-enhanced mammography offers higher specificity than breast MRI, but slightly lower sensitivity owing to weaker enhancement of ILC than IBC-NST {33178931}. For distant staging, 18F-FDG PET/CT has limited sensitivity for ILC owing to its often low metabolic activity, but may be useful in detecting distant metastases, particularly in patients with suspected advanced disease {26294295}. Whole-body MRI is emerging as a promising tool for systemic staging, offering high sensitivity for bone and soft tissue metastases, which are more frequent in ILC than in IBC-NST {35384456;30312335}.

Epidemiology

ILC is the most common special type IBC and represents 10–15% of all IBCs {1607149;12636465;16175185;8630916;9365176;9544955;36239760;39128018;32725525}. At diagnosis, women with ILC are older than women with IBC-NST {39128018;36239760;15084238;16175185;18035533;8630916;9365176}. The incidence of ILC relative to that of IBC-NST appears to have increased since the 1980s {12636465;10861434} and has also increased in Hispanic and Black women {39837694}. This has been at least partially attributed to the use of postmenopause hormone replacement therapy {19850989}. ILC is uncommon in male patients {29092024}.

Etiology

Germline mutations in the CDH1 gene, encoding E-cadherin, are very rare (0.2–0.5%) and are associated with an increased risk of hereditary diffuse gastric cancer and ILC with a risk for ILC as high as 42% {29929997}. Germline mutations in BRCA2 are associated with a risk of ILC, and germline mutations in ATM, PALB2, and CHEK2 are associated with a moderate risk {34672684}. Germline BRCA1 is significantly less frequent in patients with ILC {34672684}.

Other risk factors for the development of ILC include lifestyle and hormonal influences, as for other forms of BC, and are discussed in section Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction.

Pathogenesis

The characteristic lack of cohesiveness and invasive growth pattern of lobular carcinoma cells {19927317;27524621} appears to be due to loss of function of the E-cadherin transmembrane protein. The extracellular domain of functional E-cadherin forms homotypic interactions with E-cadherin on adjacent cells, facilitating the formation of cell–cell adhesions via adherens junctions. The intracellular domain of E-cadherin interacts with α-, β-, and p120 catenins, linking the adherens junctions to the actin cytoskeleton {39821030}. Most ILCs display loss of heterozygosity of chromosome 16q, which encompasses the CDH1 gene locus at 16q22.1 {7682767;26451490;26926684;19877120;20570624;18473330;19284381}, and as many as 71% of ILCs harbour deleterious mutations affecting CDH1 {26451490;26926684;19877120;20570624;39025406}. CDH1 inactivation may also result from fusion genes, deletions, and non-coding variants {39106449}. Loss of function of the CDH1 gene causes cytosolic translocation of p120 catenin (p120) {17325485}. In a minority of tumours without CDH1 genetic alterations, alterations affecting CTNNA1 (the gene encoding α-catenin) or other components of the cadherin–catenin family have been observed {29774524;38347189}.

Notably, CDH1 mutations may be found in ILC with retained E-cadherin and β-catenin expression, and/or membranous expression of p120 catenin {39025406;38641322}. Conversely, ILC showing E-cadherin deficiency, by immunohistochemistry, may lack CDH1 genetic alterations. Nonetheless, identification of a CDH1 pathogenic genetic alteration strongly suggests the diagnosis of ILC as these alterations are extremely rare in non-lobular breast cancers {37925055}.

Other recurrently altered genes in ILCs include PIK3CA (45%), RUNX1, TP53, TBX3, PTEN, FOXA1, MAP3K1, ERBB2 (3.9–5% in primary ILCs), and ERBB3 {27161491;26451490;26729235;26926684;27135926;27284958;26907767}. Genomic differences have been observed between classic ILC and other patterns of ILC {30641862;22354696;34359596;29669935;31907376;26926684;33441174}. ERBB3-mutated ILCs are more frequently ERBB2 (HER2) low {38428737}. ERBB2 mutations are observed more frequently in grade 3 or ER-negative ILCs and in metastatic ILCs (as many as 28%) {33441174;30520184;34359596;26451490;27602491}. Additional genetic alterations enriched in metastatic ILC, compared with primary tumours, include mutations in ESR1, TP53, FAT1, NF1, AKT1, and ARID1A; copy-number alterations (PTEN or NF1 deletion, CYP19A1 amplification); higher tumour mutation burden; and enrichment in APOBEC mutation signatures {30820448;33083532;30423024;32943456}.

As many as 85% of ILCs are classified as luminal A subtype by gene expression profiling, but have distinct transcriptomic profiles when compared with ER-positive IBC-NST {26451490;26926684;19877120;20570624}, but some ILCs are classified as luminal B and rarely as HER2-enriched or basal-like, using gene expression profiling {19877120;20837693;27161491;26451490} and immunohistochemical studies {22399188;19204205;21865043;21452022}. Distinct subgroups of ILC have been identified at the gene expression level by clustering analyses, labelled as “reactive-like”, “immune-related”, and “proliferative” in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) cohorts, the biological and clinical significance of which is not yet fully determined {26451490;26729235}. ILCs have significantly lower levels of stromal tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) than IBC-NST {29471435} and the tumour microenvironment is also enriched in M2 macrophages {36927792}. Cancer-associated fibroblasts in ILC have molecular specificities when compared with those in IBC-NST, after adjustment for age and grade {26715212}.

Macroscopic appearance

ILCs may present as irregular and poorly delimited tumours that can be difficult to identify and measure macroscopically. It is not infrequent to detect greater tumour extent microscopically, emphasizing the need for thorough sampling to include the breast tissue beyond the macroscopically visible tumour. ILCs tend to be larger than IBC-NSTs {8630916;8156495;9544955;39128018;36239760}.

Histopathology

The architectural pattern of classic ILC is characterized by a proliferation of cells that lack cohesion and appear individually dispersed or arranged in single-file or double-file linear cords that invade the breast parenchyma and adipose tissue {4345605;21022022;229072;5775975;4337952} without a stromal reaction. These infiltrating cords frequently demonstrate a concentric pattern around residual normal ducts. There is often little or no host reaction or disturbance of the background architecture.

The cells have a thin rim of cytoplasm and appear rather small, albeit larger than normal breast epithelial cells. They may have intracytoplasmic lumina {1916690}, often harbouring a central mucoid inclusion, simulating a “target” in the cytoplasm.

All ILCs should be graded using the Nottingham system, which has been shown to have greater prognostic significance than differences in morphology, such as the pleomorphic pattern {17929165}. Most classic ILCs (~76%) are grade 2, whereas grade 3 ILCs are mostly of non-classic type {18704988;17653904;23238630;36482273;36239760;8630916}.

Atypical lobular hyperplasia and lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) are seen in association with ILC in 58–98% of tumours {17325484;2153007;7076138;5915941}. Lymphovascular invasion is observed in < 15% {39864363;34864349}. ILCs demonstrate fewer TILs than ER-positive, HER2-negative IBC-NST with a median of 5% and with only 15% showing > 10% TILs. ILCs with TILs > 5% have shown a poorer outcome {29471435;32404955}.

ILC can show special architectural patterns – with the most frequently reported being solid, alveolar, and trabecular – and/or special cytological features, including signet ring–cell forms, apocrine, histiocytoid, and pleomorphic {18704988;34359596}. The different architectural or cytological patterns are frequently intermingled within the same tumour {18704988;17929165}. Special cytological features may be seen in classic ILC or in ILC with special architectural patterns.

The solid pattern is characterized by dyscohesive cells of lobular morphology, organized in sheets {166034;34359596}. The alveolar pattern features cells arranged in aggregates of ≥ 20 cells {3940412} that may be separated by thick or thin collagenous septa. When the alveoli are separated by thin septa and appear incomplete in places, the distinction from the solid pattern may be difficult and the term “solid/alveolar” could be used in such situations. A variation of the solid pattern presents with solid papillary architecture, which is well delimited peripherally with a pseudocapsule but often associated with satellite foci of ILC at the periphery of the tumour {34359596;27116593}. Pathologists should be aware of this solid papillary ILC entity to avoid confusion with other papillary lesions, particularly in small biopsies. The trabecular pattern is characterized by typical ILC cells arranged in cords that are two to three cells thick and may mimic IBC-NST {22399188}.

ILC tumour cells may have signet ring–cell features, with large intracytoplasmic vacuoles that displace the nuclei peripherally. They may be histiocytoid, with relatively bland, uniform nuclei, single small eosinophilic nucleoli, and ample granular or foamy cytoplasm; or they may be apocrine, with eosinophilic cytoplasm and prominent eosinophilic nucleoli {2472346;37525567}. Of note, it may be difficult to distinguish histiocytoid from apocrine differentiation, and ILCs with histiocytoid and apocrine features are both typically ER-negative and AR-positive {21398688;34537861}. The prognostic relevance of these cytological features is a matter of debate but is likely to be superseded by histological grade and biomarker profile.

Pleomorphic ILC is characterized by its grade 3 nuclear pleomorphism {1592388}. Pleomorphic ILC usually shows a higher number of mitoses than classic ILC and is frequently associated with pleomorphic LCIS. Pleomorphic ILC may also display apocrine differentiation {39654376} and express lower levels of ER and PR {10672061}. The pleomorphic morphology is unlikely to have prognostic value in itself {17929165} with overall histological tumour grade being of superior prognostic relevance.

A small proportion of ILCs contain small, well-formed glands or tubules {39748709}. While these lesions have been described as tubulolobular carcinomas {924431;9199642}, some ILCs with tubules show membranous expression of E-cadherin, whereas others are E-cadherin–negative but demonstrate a switch to expression of P-cadherin {32572153} re-establishing adherens junctions. It has also been proposed that tumours formerly categorized as tubulolobular carcinoma that express E-cadherin are better classified as IBC-NST, whereas those lacking E-cadherin expression are considered as ILC with tubular elements {40049332}. Further evidence is required before any change in terminology can be adopted and, at present, it is recommended that tumours with this morphology with a lack of E-cadherin expression are regarded as true tubulolobular carcinomas. For those tumours exhibiting E-cadherin–negative ILC elements with E-cadherin–positive tubular elements, classification as mixed ILC and tubular carcinoma should be considered.

ILC with extracellular mucin {35477749;36482279} is a recently described entity, often associated with lymph node metastases (> 50%), and more frequently presenting unusual features for ILC (grade 3, tumour necrosis, sometimes HER2 positivity). Extracellular mucin may be focal. Tumour cells often contain intracellular mucin and present a diffuse signet ring–cell appearance; a solid architecture may also be observed. It is important to distinguish ILC with extracellular mucin, which has a poor prognosis, from invasive mucinous carcinomas, which typically have a favourable prognosis.

Biomarker profile

ILCs are ER-positive in as many as 96% and PR-positive in as many as 81% of tumours {36239760;39128018;15084238;18035533;8630916}. ERBB2 gene amplification and HER2 protein overexpression are generally rare in classic ILC (< 5%) but may be observed in as many as 23% of grade 3 ILCs {15084238;1676930;18035533;9495354;19284381;33441174;11117786;18473330;39889242;25283075}. The proportion of ER-positive, HER2-negative ILCs that are HER2-low ranges from 40% to 70% {38428737;37017812;38509112}. Triple-negative ILC accounts for 1.8% to 13.3% of ILCs {35119530;36239760;34217971;25283075}. Patients with triple-negative ILC tend to be older and have worse outcomes than patients with triple-negative IBC-NST and hormone receptor–positive ILC {33753865;34217971}. The mean Ki-67 proliferation index is lower in triple-negative ILC than in triple-negative IBC-NST tumours (16.6% versus 38.9%; p < 0.001). AR positivity is more common in triple-negative ILC than in triple-negative IBC-NST (94.7% versus 50.0%; p < 0.001) {33753865;9355973;18704988}.

The loss of expression of the cell–cell adhesion molecule E-cadherin seen on immunohistochemistry is observed in 80–90% of ILCs {26768029;11190811;25283075}. However, E-cadherin may be observed in as many as 23.5% of ILCs {26768029;20871222}. In ILCs that express E-cadherin, the staining is observed in a variable proportion of tumour cells and is usually aberrant, i.e. present in the cytoplasm or, if membranous, is incomplete or fragmented, sometimes with a bead-like appearance {8383197;39025406;9496256;7682767;8256859;7534041;20871222;18473330;18379416}. In occasional tumours with classic ILC morphology that display strong E-cadherin membranous staining, the use of immunohistochemistry to evaluate other molecules in the cadherin–catenin complex, such as p120 catenin and β-catenin, can be useful {17325485;20871222;15077190}. The use of immunohistochemistry to assist the diagnosis of ILC improves pathologist concordance and reduces the level of morphological misdiagnoses {34889530;17325485}.

Differential diagnosis

ILC with a predominant trabecular architecture should not be misclassified as IBC-NST with a Nottingham score 3 for tubules. The presence of atypical lobular hyperplasia or LCIS, dyscohesive cells, linear or single-cell infiltration of tumour cells, circumferential infiltration of tumour cells around normal ducts, infiltration of adipose tissue without a stromal reaction, uniform monomorphic nuclei, and intracytoplasmic “targetoid” vacuoles is suggestive of ILC. Triple-negative basal-like or poorly differentiated IBC-NST can show E-cadherin loss by immunohistochemistry owing to epithelial–mesenchymal transition {18156431;30760857}.

ILC with histiocytoid morphology may be cytologically bland, and may be confused with histiocytes, fat necrosis, or (if additionally apocrine-appearing) granular cell tumour {21398688}. The use of cytokeratins (pancytokeratin AE1/AE3, CK7, and CK8/18) and AR immunohistochemistry may aid in the differential diagnosis.

ILC with a predominantly solid pattern may be misinterpreted as lymphoma or neuroendocrine neoplasm.

ILC with a prominent component of signet-ring cells should be distinguished from a metastatic signet ring–cell carcinoma from the gastrointestinal tract. Immunohistochemistry using antibodies to GATA3, TRPS1, CK7, ER, PR, GCDFP-15, E-cadherin, and p120 catenin may help to establish an intra-abdominal tumour as metastatic ILC {17325485;26465236;25544392;39243111}.

Cytology

Classic ILC produces low cellularity, scattered small dyscohesive sheets, and predominantly small single cells showing small mildly pleomorphic nuclei, indistinct nucleoli, and eccentric cytoplasm often containing a single vacuole. In some tumours, including those with special patterns, the cellularity will be high with similar cells. Pleomorphic ILCs are characterized by greater cellularity, small dyscohesive tissue fragments, and single cells showing marked nuclear pleomorphism and hyperchromasia, prominent nucleoli, and high mitotic activity, more closely resembling the cytopathological characteristics of IBC-NST grade 3 {1398644;7992580;9100538;27489086}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Systematic molecular testing of CDH1 status is not routine in clinical practice and its value remains to be determined.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: dyscohesive, monotonous cells; individually dispersed or arranged singly or in single-file linear cords or other architectural patterns; minimal or no stromal reaction; low- to intermediate-grade nuclei in classic ILC and high-grade nuclei in pleomorphic ILC.

Desirable: presence of atypical lobular hyperplasia / LCIS; ER positivity and HER2 negativity; loss of membranous E-cadherin expression.

Prognosis and prediction

Despite the favourable prognostic features of ILC (low-grade, ER positivity, HER2 negativity, and low proliferation), recent studies of a large number of patients with ILC with long follow-up showed that patients with ILC have an elevated risk of late recurrence and death compared with patients with IBC-NST {39715246}. ILCs were more likely to be of larger size (> 50 mm) and more-advanced tumour stage (stage III/IV), with more lymph node positivity than IBC-NST at presentation, and a tendency to occur in older patients {15084238;24504379;27233940;36239760;39128018;18035533;18458044;2803932;10752679}.

Histological grade is an independent predictor of outcome in patients with ILC {15605082;11520101;8522285;17929165;36482273}. In particular, a high mitotic count is associated with a worse prognosis {17929165}. The 21-gene recurrence score is reported to have prognostic and predictive significance in patients with ILC {35137951}. Patient survival did not differ within a defined genomic risk group between ILC and IBC-NST in the MINDACT trial {39864363}. Low rates of pathological complete response have been observed in patients with ILC treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, reported in only 7% of the cases {24529560;22508812;24177758}, possibly suggesting a superior role for neoadjuvant endocrine therapy. However, this relative resistance to cytotoxic therapy may be related to the molecular characteristics and the lower proliferation rate of ILC cells rather than to the morphology {22961065;28628772;24529560}.

Metastases from classic ILC in lymph nodes may be inconspicuous, and even a significant number of single cells distributed in a buckshot pattern may be missed on H&E staining alone {28877063}. However, as pN1mi and pN0i+ axillary lymph node statuses have no impact on survival, it is not recommended to analyse axillary sentinel lymph nodes using cytokeratin immunohistochemistry {21247310}.

The metastatic pattern of ILC differs from that of IBC-NST with a higher frequency of metastases to bone, skin, gastrointestinal tract, uterus, meninges, and ovary, diffuse serosal involvement, and infiltration of visceral fat, whereas metastases to the lung are more frequent in IBC-NST {8156495;9365176;15084238;23863867;8297656;1653879;8630916;28877063;8211676;14746850;6331484;39626345}. Metastases to the eyelid have been reported and are composed of histiocytoid/apocrine cells {4350331;34537861;28108967}. The frequency of multiple metastases is higher in patients with ILC than in those with IBC-NST {14746850}.
