3.2.8 Invasive micropapillary carcinoma

Definition

Invasive micropapillary carcinoma is an invasive breast carcinoma composed of small, hollow, or morula-like clusters of malignant cells with an inside-out growth pattern, surrounded by clear spaces.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: micropapillary carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Invasive micropapillary carcinoma shows no predilection for a particular quadrant.

Clinical features

Invasive micropapillary carcinoma usually presents as a palpable mass. Mammographically, the tumour appears as a dense, irregular mass with indistinct margins. Microcalcifications are identified in nearly 50% of cases {19542384;37858400}. Sonographic examination shows a hypoechoic or occasionally isoechoic mass, often with an irregular shape and a non-circumscribed margin {37858400}. On MRI, the tumour usually appears as an irregularly shaped mass with heterogeneous enhancement {23901311;37858400}. Axillary lymph node metastases are present in more than two thirds of patients at diagnosis {22493629;24913934}.

Epidemiology

Pure invasive micropapillary carcinoma is rare, accounting for 0.9–2% of all invasive breast carcinomas (IBCs) {29072365;10667424;25953688;8302807}. Mixed forms are more frequent, and areas with a micropapillary pattern are found in as many as 7.4% of all IBCs {17050071;14717664;11431712}. Invasive micropapillary carcinomas usually occur in female patients but may affect male patients {19542384;15887800;18484683}. The mean patient age at presentation of both pure and mixed forms overlaps with the age reported for ER-positive IBCs of no special type (NST) {17050071;10674270;8302807;11431712}.

Etiology

There are no specific etiological factors for invasive micropapillary carcinoma apart from those currently known for breast cancer in general. See section Epithelial tumours of the breast: Part 2: Introduction.

Pathogenesis

No specific pathogenetic mechanisms or highly recurrent molecular alterations have been identified. Invasive micropapillary carcinomas are luminal A or B carcinomas by gene expression profiling {18720457}. Array comparative genomic hybridization analysis has detected recurrent gains of 8q, 17q, and 20q; deletions of 6q and 13q {19479727;12152162}; and a constellation of copy-number alterations different from those of grade- and ER-matched IBC-NSTs {19479727;18484683}.

MYC, CCND1, and FGFR1 amplifications are reported in 33%, 8%, and 17% of tumours, respectively {18484683}. The limited data available reveal a spectrum of mutations similar to those found in luminal B IBC-NSTs, with recurrent mutations in PIK3CA, TP53, GATA3, MAP2K4, and NBPF10 {24395524;26907767}.

Mutations in genes involved in cell polarity, cell shape, and ciliogenesis have been detected in single cases {24887297} but no recurrent fusion genes have been identified {24395524;24887297}. Deregulated expression of genes related to apicobasal polarity (in particular, high levels of LIN7A expression), cell adhesion, and cell migration has been reported {26887652}. In vitro data showed that increasing EMA (MUC1) expression decreases intercellular adhesion and adhesion between cells and extracellular matrix {11745478;8730100;7698991}, suggesting that EMA expression on the stroma-facing surface of the cell membrane might account, at least in part, for the detachment of the cells from the stroma {15154007}.

The non-micropapillary component of mixed micropapillary carcinomas harbours genetic alterations similar to those found in the micropapillary areas {19479727;18484683}.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis of tumour clusters in paired primary invasive micropapillary carcinomas and axillary lymph node metastases found that multiple lymph node foci arise from a single subclone of the primary tumour, often characterized by high-frequency copy-number loss of PRDM16 and IGSF9 and copy-number gain of ALDH2 {35013309}.

Arginine methylation modification is involved in the metabolic reprogramming of invasive micropapillary carcinoma. Overexpression of PRMT3 was found to be associated with tumour proliferation, metastasis, and poor clinical prognosis {37301537;36637351}. Spatial transcriptome analysis has revealed extensive metabolic heterogeneity within invasive micropapillary carcinomas, with SRBP1 (encoded by SREBF1), a key molecule in lipid metabolism, strongly associated with the metastasis of invasive micropapillary carcinomas {34799559}.

Data are limited on the molecular alterations underpinning invasive carcinomas composed predominantly (> 50%) of micropapillary clusters suspended in tight pools of stromal mucin. A study of 5 such cases identified molecular profiles intermediate between mucinous carcinomas and invasive micropapillary carcinomas and no TP53, PIK3CA, GATA3, or MAP3K1 mutations {30843622}. A study comparing the molecular alterations in mucinous carcinoma with micropapillary features (MPMC) and pure mucinous carcinomas found that both have a low mutation burden {32358590}. However, mutations in TTN, GATA3, and PIK3CA were more common in pure mucinous carcinomas, while GATA3, TP53, and SF3B1 were recurrently mutated in MPMCs. Recurrent 6q14.1-q27 losses, and 8p11.21-q24.3 gains occurred in both tumours; however, MPMCs harboured 17q and 20q gains as well as 17p losses, while pure mucinous carcinomas displayed gains at 6p. PI3K/AKT, mTOR, ERBB, and focal adhesion pathways were deregulated more frequently in MPMCs than in pure mucinous carcinomas {32358590}. These findings raise the possibility that invasive carcinomas composed predominantly (> 50%) of micropapillary clusters in tight mucin pools may be more akin to invasive micropapillary carcinomas than mucinous carcinoma, but this issue remains controversial.

Macroscopic appearance

Not clinically relevant

Histopathology

In pure invasive micropapillary carcinoma, > 90% of the tumour consists of hollow or morula-like aggregates of cuboidal to columnar neoplastic cells. The micropapillary clusters are devoid of fibrovascular cores and are immersed in a spongy stroma consisting of clear empty spaces around the cell clusters loosely separated by a delicate stromal meshwork of inconspicuous fibroblasts and connective tissue. The clear spaces may mimic lymphatic vessels but are not lined by endothelium. The micropapillary clusters typically show reverse polarity, with the apical pole of the tumour cells at the periphery of the clusters facing outwards (towards the clear stromal spaces around the clusters) rather than inwards (towards the centres of the clusters). In H&E-stained sections, this pattern may be appreciated as cells at the periphery of the clusters having focal cytoplasm facing the surrounding clear space, or secretions and blebs at the outer cell membrane; electron microscopy has revealed the presence of microvilli on the cell surface facing the stroma {7808965}. The cells have a cuboidal to columnar shape; the cytoplasm is eosinophilic, dense, or finely granular. Apocrine features may be present. Nuclear grade is often intermediate or high, with variable nuclear pleomorphism. Pure invasive micropapillary carcinomas may be associated with multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells, which may be as large as the micropapillary clusters {26370736}. About 75% of pure invasive micropapillary carcinomas are of Nottingham histological grade 2 or 3 {19479727;18484683;23763700;29403084;28418916}. Calcifications are common and may be psammomatous.

Invasive carcinomas composed predominantly (> 50%) of micropapillary clusters in stromal mucin have been described. In the first reported series of invasive micropapillary carcinomas {7808965}, two tumours were described as colloid-like. Other groups have reported invasive carcinomas with > 50% of the tumour composed of micropapillary clusters in tight pools of mucin loosely separated by a delicate meshwork of fibroblasts and collagen fibres {25953688;29063656;30843622}. Whether these tumours are primarily mucinous or micropapillary carcinomas is debated, and only limited molecular data are available. One group found intermediate molecular features in a series of 5 cases {30843622} but another group concluded that these tumours are unrelated to mucinous carcinoma {32358590}. Most studies have reported that the clinical behaviour of these tumours is worse than that of pure mucinous carcinoma {25953688} and, although the precise classification of these tumours is not fully resolved, pathologists need to be aware of these tumours (see section Mucinous carcinoma).

Immunohistochemistry

Most pure invasive micropapillary carcinomas are ER-positive and PR-positive {28418916;23763700;19479727;18484683}, but a triple-negative phenotype has been reported in 15–20% of tumours {29228910;24921921;26200840}. ERBB2 (HER2) overexpression and amplification are reported in a variable proportion {18484683;19479727;20444748;23763700}. HER2 immunoreactivity may yield intense staining of the entire cell membrane (score: 3+) or weak to moderate staining in a U-shaped basolateral pattern that spares the luminal aspect of the cell membrane. Carcinomas with a basolateral HER2 staining pattern should be regarded as having equivocal expression (score: 2+) and reflexed to in situ hybridization testing {29846122;28752841}. It has been suggested that intense continuous basolateral HER2 staining in invasive micropapillary carcinoma qualifies as HER2 3+ {31974492;33212478} but this assessment is not endorsed by medical organizations. Expression of basal keratins is uncommon {18484683}.

EMA is expressed on the luminal aspect of the cell membrane in well-differentiated IBC-NST; however, in invasive micropapillary carcinoma, EMA is expressed on the stroma-facing surface of the cell membrane, documenting reverse polarity. Of note, a similar staining pattern of EMA indicative of reverse polarity has also been described in some mucinous carcinomas {24439232}. Immunohistochemical staining for EMA may be used to distinguish true reverse polarity from possible artefactual stromal retraction in IBC-NST. In invasive micropapillary carcinoma, the expression of basolateral adhesion proteins (E-cadherin, p120) in a mutually exclusive pattern with EMA is also evidence of reverse polarity {27862202;15198358}. Invasive micropapillary carcinoma of the breast is positive for GATA3 and TRPS1 {39243111}.

Differential diagnosis

In the absence of coexistent ductal carcinoma in situ, the possibility should be considered of metastasis to the breast or axillary lymph nodes by an invasive carcinoma with a micropapillary/papillary growth pattern that is primary at another site (e.g. ovary, fallopian tubes, peritoneum, lung). Notably, metastatic serous carcinoma of Müllerian origin is usually ER-positive but does not express GATA3, while it is PAX8-positive. TRPS1 is usually expressed in breast carcinomas but may also be expressed in carcinomas primary at other sites (including 50% of serous ovarian tumours) {38647255} and positive TRPS1 staining needs to be interpreted with caution and in conjunction with other more specific markers.

Cytology

Invasive micropapillary carcinoma is usually admixed with IBC-NST. It shows high cellularity, small, crowded tissue fragments, and single dispersed cells which have a high N:C ratio and highly pleomorphic nuclei {17676481;9990283}. The tissue fragments are irregular in shape and may be seen to fit together in a jigsaw pattern and have a blush of mucin at their margin (Giemsa stain).

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FISH for ERBB2 should be performed as necessary (see Histopathology, above).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive tumour clusters with micropapillary architecture with reversed cell polarity, set in clear spaces in > 90% of the tumour.

Desirable: clean staining for EMA (MUC1) and sialyl Lewis x (sLex) lining the stroma-facing border of the cell clusters {33893728}.

Staging

The staging is the same as for other types of IBC.

Prognosis and prediction

Peritumoural lymphovascular invasion and axillary lymph node metastases are significantly more frequent in invasive micropapillary carcinomas than in IBC-NSTs {16196516;30064485;15505530;20304650;23763700}. Accordingly, invasive micropapillary carcinoma is associated with a worse prognosis than unselected IBC-NSTs of the same size. However, the micropapillary histological type does not seem to add independent information on the risk of disease recurrence or overall survival when compared with IBCs matched for patient age, tumour size and grade, peritumoural lymphovascular invasion, immunohistochemically defined molecular subtype, and number of positive lymph nodes {23763700}. A meta-analysis including 1888 invasive micropapillary carcinomas across 14 studies revealed a higher rate of locoregional recurrence in patients with invasive micropapillary carcinoma than in patients with IBC-NST; however, there were no statistically significant differences in overall survival, disease-specific survival, or distant metastasis–free survival {29228910}.

More recently, a study of predictive models in patients with invasive micropapillary carcinoma found age, T stage, N stage, ER status, adjuvant radiotherapy, and type of surgery to be independent prognostic factors for overall survival and cancer-specific survival {36933963}. These parameters were used to construct nomograms specific for invasive micropapillary carcinoma, which showed higher clinical value than traditional TNM tumour staging by decision curve analysis {36933963}.
