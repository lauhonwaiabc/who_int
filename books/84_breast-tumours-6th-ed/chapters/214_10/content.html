2.1.4 Atypical ductal hyperplasia

Definition

Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) is an epithelial proliferative lesion with cytological and architectural features similar to those of low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) but less developed in architecture, degree of terminal duct lobular unit involvement, and contiguous extent.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: atypical intraductal hyperplasia.

Not recommended: atypical hyperplasia (to be used only if ductal vs lobular cannot be determined).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ADH typically occurs in the terminal duct lobular units. It can also occur within lesions such as papilloma and fibroadenoma or rarely within major lactiferous ducts.

Clinical features

ADH is most frequently diagnosed in women undergoing screening mammography in whom microcalcifications are identified, or less commonly as an incidental finding in tissue sampled for other reasons. There are no radiological features that help distinguish the calcifications associated with ADH from those present in other lesions, such as columnar cell change/hyperplasia, flat epithelial atypia, or low-grade DCIS. Proliferative lesions in general, including ADH, may also account for non–mass enhancement on breast MRI. ADH itself does not present as a mass, although other lesions in which ADH may be found can present as a mass (e.g. intraductal papilloma, fibroadenoma).

Epidemiology

The frequency of ADH diagnoses increased with the introduction of mammographic screening but has remained steady in more-recent decades. ADH now accounts for 2–14% of diagnoses in breast biopsies within screened populations {23225449;19098204;25603785}. In case series, ADH is more frequently reported in women < 60 years old {23225449;25603785} and with dense breasts {25603785}.

Etiology

Because ADH is an estrogen-driven process, the factors that are associated with the development of ER-positive invasive breast carcinomas may also be associated with an increased risk for the development of ADH {24480577}.

Pathogenesis

Studies of loss of heterozygosity in ADH and low-grade DCIS have shown similar genetic abnormalities, providing evidence that these are related clonal processes {7560165}. The current model of the pathogenesis of breast cancer places many of the high-risk lesions, including ADH, in the low-grade pathway to luminal/ER-positive invasive disease {18223478}. ADH is thought to be a very early neoplastic step in the pathway to low-grade DCIS and ER-positive, low-grade invasive ductal carcinomas {20500230}. Allelic imbalances are seen at similar frequencies in ADH and low-grade DCIS, and there are similar recurrent regions with loss of heterozygosity, including loci on 1q, 16q, and 17p {11745672;23348384;18936904;16604511}.

Macroscopic appearance

ADH is not macroscopically identifiable.

Histopathology

ADH is an intraductal proliferation with cytological and architectural features similar to those of low-grade DCIS, but with partial involvement of ductal spaces and/or uniform involvement of limited extent. ADH most commonly involves the terminal duct lobular units and can involve ducts and/or acini. Microcalcifications are often seen in association with ADH. The cytological features include evenly spaced monotonous cells containing rounded nuclei with dense chromatin. No intermediate-grade or high-grade nuclear features or necrosis are seen. The cell borders are well defined. In some examples of ADH, the lesional cells are admixed with the non-uniform cell population of usual ductal hyperplasia or arise in association with other benign proliferations.

The architectural features include rigid bridges, bars, and arcades of uniform thickness; bulbous micropapillae (with narrow bases and broad tips); and a cribriform pattern. Solid and pagetoid patterns of ADH are less common and can be difficult to distinguish from classic lobular carcinoma in situ; in particular, focal ADH with pagetoid pattern undermining residual native epithelium should not be interpreted as evidence of low-grade DCIS. When duct spaces are uniformly involved by an intraductal proliferation with the cytological and architectural features of low-grade DCIS, the distinction between ADH and DCIS is based on size/extent criteria. According to Page et al. {2986821}, complete involvement of two or more duct spaces qualifies as DCIS and lesser involvement is considered ADH. Tavassoli and Norris {2297643} proposed that lesions ≤ 2 mm in contiguous extent be classified as ADH. ADH in a papilloma is diagnosed if the extent of the atypical intraductal proliferation is ≤ 3 mm. These thresholds are arbitrary and should be used as general guidelines, and they can be used in combination. Because these systems were developed on the basis of findings in excisional biopsies, these criteria should be applied with caution. The WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board recommends a conservative approach when lesions of limited extent are identified, particularly in core needle biopsies, in which the entire lesion may not be visualized. Since the diagnosis of ADH is quantitative, some guidelines recommend the use of the term “atypical intraductal epithelial proliferation, AIDEP” to designate those lesions diagnosed on a limited core biopsy / diagnostic vacuum-assisted biopsy and reserve the term ADH for lesions diagnosed on surgical excision or vacuum-assisted excisions [[Non-operative Diagnosis Working Group of the UK National Coordinating Committee for Breast Pathology. G150. Guidelines for Non-Operative Diagnostic Procedures and Reporting in Breast Cancer Screening. London (UK): The Royal College of Pathologists; Aug 2021. ]]. Because ADH represents part of a spectrum of atypical architectural patterns ranging from flat epithelial atypia to low-grade DCIS, its diagnosis can be problematic. Diagnostic agreement rates for biopsies with ADH are low (40–60%) compared with the much higher agreement for high-grade DCIS, invasive carcinoma, and benign breast lesions {1463092;26999810;25781441}. Variability in diagnosis may be resolved by consensus or second reviews {27398812;26999810;27334105;24511905;27481892;24948027;21532546}. The presence of non–low grade nuclei precludes the diagnosis of ADH regardless of the extent of the lesion.

The cells of ADH are typically strongly and diffusely positive for ER and do not express high-molecular-weight cytokeratins such as CK5/6. This staining pattern is similar to that seen in other lesions in the low-grade breast neoplasia pathway (including flat epithelial atypia and low-grade DCIS). In contrast, the cells of usual ductal hyperplasia show heterogeneous staining for both ER and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins. The latter is usually mosaic and should be differentiated from the more diffuse expression of high-molecular-weight cytokeratins seen in high-grade basal DCIS, an uncommon form of DCIS.

Cytology

It is not possible to diagnose ADH on FNAB, because the combination of cytopathological features, architecture, and size requirements is required for the diagnosis, the latter two requiring a surgical pathology specimen. The differential diagnosis includes usual ductal hyperplasia and low-grade DCIS. Most cases are most likely categorized as atypical.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Cytological features: low-grade monotonous cells similar to those of low-grade DCIS, with uniform, round, evenly spaced nuclei and distinct cell borders Architectural features: rigid bridges, bars, or arcades of uniform thickness; OR bulbous micropapillae (with narrow bases and broad tips); OR a cribriform pattern (should not be just a flat pattern) Extent: partial involvement of multiple spaces; or uniformly involved duct spaces but measuring ≤ 2 mm in contiguous extent (≤ 3 mm in a papilloma).

Desirable:

Cells uniformly ER-positive and CK5/6-negative or CK14-negative.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

ADH is associated with a 3- to 5-fold increased risk of developing invasive breast carcinoma in either breast, although some studies have found the risk to be higher in the ipsilateral breast {9870852;2986821;17563394;17154175;16034008}. The absolute risk of breast cancer after a diagnosis of ADH is approximately 1% per year for at least 25 years, with a mean latency period before the development of cancer of 8–12 years after diagnosis {24480577;25551530}. The risk slowly increases over several decades and has been shown (in some but not all studies) to increase with the number of ADH foci {27352219;26565738;25551530}. Hormonal agents such as tamoxifen significantly reduce the future risk of invasive disease {25551530;22622807}.

For ADH identified on core needle biopsy, the reported upgrade rate to DCIS or invasive breast carcinoma has been variable; in more-contemporary series with careful radiological–pathological correlation, the rate is 10–20% {27538687;28474262;30318287;20970167;19098204;30771862;30611396;30591993;29410346;26291517;25529860;24680949;23598968}.

International guidelines for management of B3 lesions, including ADH, have been developed {29773220;37330436;38061151} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Breast Cancer Screening and Diagnosis. Version 2.2025. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2025 cited 2025 Mar 28. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/pdf/breast-screening.pdf. ]]. These recommend surgical diagnostic excision or vacuum-assisted excision for adequate sampling and exclusion of more-advanced disease. Surgical excision may be spared in select patients with ADH of limited extent on core biopsy or when almost all mammographic microcalcifications have been removed during the diagnostic procedure {36905438;36180303;27573525}. Most current guidelines also recommend mammographic follow-up although the duration and interval vary. Recent data from the UK Sloane Project for atypia have questioned the need for additional imaging follow-up {38302129} with evidence suggesting that patients with atypia, including ADH, can be managed via routine breast screening {38265306}.
