2.3.2 Lactating adenoma

Definition

Lactating adenoma is a benign breast nodule, diagnosed during pregnancy or breastfeeding, that is composed of aggregates of terminal duct lobular units showing lactational change.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: tumour of pregnancy; nodular lactational hyperplasia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lactating adenoma occurs predominantly in the breast, and may be bilateral or multifocal. Rarely it occurs along the milk line from the axilla to the groin.

Clinical features

Lactating adenoma usually presents as a soft, painless, palpable, solid, mobile, discrete mass, without nipple or skin changes. Infarction can occur in 5% of cases {28491154}, and may result in enlargement, pain, tenderness, skin changes, and suspicious imaging findings. Lactating adenoma is usually < 50 mm, but it has the potential for rapid enlargement (as much as 250 mm) {10819069}. Termination of breastfeeding usually leads to spontaneous regression within 1–5 months, but rare antepartum {26855073;24946360;32348915} and postpartum {28269736;10819069} enlargement has been described that can result from infarction {28491154}. Mammography usually shows a well-circumscribed, hypodense or isodense mass, but lactating adenoma can rarely present as a partially obscured or asymmetrical mass. The typical sonographic features are of a smooth, homogeneously hypoechoic, solid, ovoid mass with its long axis parallel to the chest wall {31867197}. Additionally, posterior acoustic enhancement and an echogenic pseudocapsule may be seen {28491154}. Owing to the milk fat, lactating adenoma may appear hyperechoic or radiolucent. MRI shows non-enhancing septa, characterized as low suspicion, which correspond to the connective tissue between lobules {33220595}. Sonography is the preferred mode of diagnosis {33304009} owing to the increased density of the pregnant/lactating breast, but distinction from fibroadenoma can be challenging. Confirmatory tissue diagnosis, which is achieved with biopsy/excision, is indicated if the mass rapidly enlarges and has worrisome sonographic findings, such as posterior shadowing, irregular margins, and heterogeneous echotexture {31867197}.

Epidemiology

Lactating adenoma usually affects women aged 19–34 years, with most cases occurring in the third decade of life. Despite its name, lactating adenoma occurs more frequently during the third trimester of pregnancy (and rarely in the first or second) than after childbirth. As the most prevalent breast lesion in pregnant and postpartum women {28491154}, it accounts for approximately 70% of all core biopsies in this population {8592313}. However, not all lactating adenomas undergo biopsy/excision, and the true incidence is therefore unknown.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

It remains unknown whether lactating adenoma arises de novo or is a hyperplastic lesion, a pre-existing fibroadenoma {33220595}, or a tubular adenoma with superimposed physiological changes. Lactating adenoma and tubular adenoma are considered two ends of a spectrum, because both are characterized by florid epithelial proliferation with minimal stroma. In fact, the histological and ultrastructural changes of lactating adenoma in the first and second trimester resemble those of tubular adenoma. The few cases of lactating adenoma that have been studied using next-generation sequencing failed to show any mutations, including the MED12 exon 2 mutations frequently seen in fibroadenomas {27438523}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lactating adenoma is a well-circumscribed, unencapsulated, lobulated solid mass, with a firm (but softer than fibroadenoma or tubular adenoma) to rubbery, greyish-tan to yellow cut surface. Necrosis may be seen in infarcted tumours.

Histopathology

Lactating adenoma is a well-circumscribed proliferation of closely packed hyperplastic secretory lobules separated by sparse delicate connective tissue, which may be oedematous {3193299;24946360}. Lactating adenomas in the first and second trimester resemble tubular adenomas because they are composed of round to irregular oval acinar structures, but with variability in the size and shape of the glands. After the second trimester, the tumours mature and are composed of epithelial cells arranged in variably sized ductuloalveolar spaces. This network is lined by cuboidal or hobnail-shaped cells containing granular or vacuolated clear cytoplasm, as well as luminal eosinophilic secretions (milk), both of which are oil red O–positive and contain α-lactalbumin {3193299}. The cells have small round nuclei, with no atypia and occasional mitoses; binucleated cells are common. Identical cytological and ultrastructural changes are seen in surrounding breast tissue. Fibroadenoma may also show secretory activity, but it is distinguished from lactating adenoma by its preserved fibroepithelial architecture, prominent stroma, and focal rather than dense proliferation of cells with secretory activity.

Cytology

Abundant milky background with fine fat vacuoles in a finely granular proteinaceous secretory material is present, with small cells with central round nuclei, single small nucleoli, and wispy, vacuolated cytoplasm seen singly and in larger 3D acinar cell tissue fragments. Stripped nuclei may also be seen. In some cases there may be larger cells with macronucleoli, and correlation with the clinical information of pregnancy and lactation assists in the diagnosis {24976078}. Occasionally cases have been reported where the findings have obscured a malignancy {31041174}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a well-circumscribed proliferation of closely packed hyperplastic secretory lobules with a myoepithelial layer, separated by sparse delicate connective tissue; bland-looking epithelial cells containing vacuolated or granular cytoplasm and small nuclei with pinpoint uniform nucleoli.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Lactating adenoma is characterized by slow growth and may regress spontaneously. Studies with follow-up ranging from 6 months to 14 years (average: 3.4 years) have shown no progression to cancer, but simultaneous occurrence with breast cancer has been described in some cases {37787331}. In 1 case, invasive carcinoma occurred at the site of a previously excised lactating adenoma. In another, transition from lactating adenoma to carcinoma was noted. Importantly, the hormones that prime breast development during pregnancy, such as estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin (especially progesterone and prolactin), also play a role in carcinogenesis. Therefore, it is unclear whether lactating adenoma is capable of malignant transformation, is a risk factor, or happens to coincidentally occur adjacent to malignancy.
