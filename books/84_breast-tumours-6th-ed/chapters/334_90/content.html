3.3.4 Secretory carcinoma

Definition

Secretory carcinoma is an invasive carcinoma composed of epithelial cells with intracytoplasmic secretory vacuoles and extracellular eosinophilic, bubbly secretions, arranged in a variable architecture and frequently associated with ETV6::NTRK3 fusion.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: juvenile breast carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In women, most secretory carcinomas arise near the nipple or in the upper-outer quadrant, but any region of the breast may be involved, including ectopic breast tissue {4285563;6445777;11570920;22157932;28548128}. In men and children, most secretory carcinomas arise in the subareolar region {4285563;22157932;28548128}.

Clinical features

Secretory carcinoma typically presents as a slow-growing, firm, painless, mobile mass, sometimes with nipple discharge {6445777;22157932}. The radiographical features of a circumscribed lobulated mass may mimic those of benign lesions such as fibroadenoma {18499854}.

Epidemiology

Secretory carcinomas account for < 0.05% of all invasive mammary carcinomas and occur predominantly in women {22494666;34084719}. Although initially described in children, most cases present between the fourth and seventh decades of life (range: 3–91 years) {22157932;28548128;26291510;14691916;22832018;19011601;1992979;39259928;34282256}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Secretory carcinomas are characterized by a t(12;15)(p13;q25) translocation, which results in an ETV6::NTRK3 fusion gene that is present in the in situ and invasive components, indicating that this is an early event in tumorigenesis {28548128;19011601;12450792}. In breast, ETV6::NTRK3 is highly specific to secretory carcinoma {15101049}, yet it can be detected, albeit rarely, in non-secretory breast carcinomas {35102630}. ETV6::NTRK3, which was initially identified in congenital infantile fibrosarcoma {9462753} and cellular mesoblastic nephroma {9823307}, encodes a constitutively activated chimeric tyrosine kinase that signals through the RAS/MAPK and Pl3K pathways to drive cellular transformation and oncogenesis {11751416}. ETV6::NTRK3 has also been identified in mammary analogue secretory carcinomas arising in other sites, such as the salivary glands, thyroid, and skin {27631515;27282352;20410810}. The shared morphological, immunophenotypic, and genetic features of secretory breast carcinomas with analogous tumours at other sites suggest a common pathogenetic origin irrespective of location for these lesions. Notably, ETV6::NTRK3 expression has also been documented in other tumour types, including papillary carcinoma, leukaemia, and glioma {30333516}. This fusion is therefore unique in being expressed in tumours derived across multiple cell lineages. Other than NTRK3, NTRK1 fusions have been reported in three secretory carcinomas of the breast {30707464;35102630}. Other than this alteration, secretory carcinomas are relatively quiet genomically, with very low mutation burdens and only occasional chromosomal gains and losses {28548128}, even in cases with axillary lymph node metastasis {35158121}. Notably, recent reports indicate that TERT promoter mutation may be associated with aggressive biological behaviour in breast secretory carcinomas {36585729;31012486;29623306}.

Small molecule inhibitors (larotrectinib and entrectinib) that target NTRK3 and other NTRK family members have recently been developed and shown promising efficacy in the treatment of patients with TRK fusion cancers {29466156;29986850}, including breast secretory carcinoma {29623306}.

This highlights the need for rapid and reliable molecular confirmation of ETV6::NTRK3, which has routinely been achieved by RT-PCR using ETV6 and NTRK3 primers, including in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumour blocks {10895816}, and by FISH using either ETV6 break-apart probes or 5′ ETV6 and 3′ NTRK3 convergence probes {15101049}. The use of a pan-TRK monoclonal antibody in immunohistochemistry identified NTRK (NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3) fusions in 20 of 21 cases in one study {28719467} but was less effective in detecting fusions involving NTRK3 in a second study {30171197}, indicating that more-reliable NTRK antibodies will be necessary in order for immunohistochemistry to be implemented as a biomarker for NTRK3 inhibitors in secretory breast carcinoma.

Next-generation sequencing methods, including hybrid-based capture for multiple specific fusions {25801821}, RNA-based panel sequencing for specific expressed transcripts lacking introns {25384085}, and methods that integrate RNA sequencing with whole-genome or whole-exome sequencing {26556708}, are likely to be combined with immunohistochemistry for routine detection of ETV6::NTRK3 for diagnostic and therapeutic assignment.

Macroscopic appearance

Secretory carcinomas are typically solitary, firm, circumscribed masses with grey-white or yellow-tan cut surfaces. Multifocal tumours have been described {26291510;14691916}. The mean tumour size is about 20 mm, but size ranges from 3 to 160 mm {37897648;6445777;22157932;28548128;26291510;14691916;22832018;19011601;7435774;2925177}.

Histopathology

Secretory carcinomas are composed of polygonal tumour cells with eosinophilic granular or vacuolated cytoplasm and round to oval nuclei arranged in microcystic/honeycomb, solid, tubular, or papillary growth patterns. Microcystic areas can simulate thyroid follicles and may merge to form small solid sheets. Most tumours show mixed patterns. Intracytoplasmic and extracellular eosinophilic or amphophilic secretions are consistently present and stain positively with PAS, mucicarmine, and Alcian blue. Nuclear pleomorphism is almost always mild or moderate, and mitotic activity is low. The vast majority of secretory carcinomas are therefore grade 1 or 2 by Nottingham grading, with high-grade tumours being extremely rare {26291510}. The tumour stroma is often sclerotic. An in situ component may be present; it is typically cribriform or solid with low or intermediate nuclear grade {28548128;14691916;19011601} and may be identified with the use of myoepithelial markers.

Immunohistochemically, the tumour cells typically express CEA (polyclonal), S100, mammaglobin, SOX10, MUC4, and TRPS1, often in a strong and diffuse pattern {22157932;28548128;26291510;19011601;1992979;33110239;38262580;39243111}. Most tumours express the basal markers CK5/6 and/or EGFR, although this expression may be focal {28548128;19011601}. GATA3, CK8/18, KIT (CD117), and vimentin may also be positive. Most cases are triple-negative for ER, PR, and ERBB2 (HER2) {28548128;26291510;19011601}, but weak ER and PR expression is not uncommon {39730784;37897648;34282256;31119054;39243111;39389890}. The Ki-67 proliferation index is variable but often < 20% {22157932;26291510;14691916}. Pan-TRK immunohistochemistry serves as a novel surrogate marker for NTRK family (NTRK1, NTRK2, or NTRK3) gene rearrangements across various tumour types. In breast secretory carcinoma, the pan-TRK clone EPR17341 demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity by typically showing either a diffuse pattern or at least focal, intense nuclear staining, making it a valuable diagnostic aid {31498178;37586834;36759438}.

Rare cases with apocrine differentiation {38929541} or fibrosarcomatous features {33342011} have been reported in breast secretory carcinomas. The differential diagnosis may include carcinoma with apocrine differentiation (usually AR-positive), acinic cell–like carcinoma {18462362}, cystic hypersecretory carcinoma in situ {24121179}, mucoepidermoid carcinoma {30380176}, and tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity {27913435}, especially in cases with limited biopsy material. Detection of ETV6::NTRK3 fusion can be used to support the diagnosis of secretory carcinoma, as needed.

Cytology

FNAB smears show variable cellularity with tissue fragments and relatively cohesive sheets of cells with granular or variably vacuolated cytoplasm, characteristic large intracytoplasmic vacuoles containing proteinaceous material, round nuclei with homogeneous chromatin, and scant mitoses. Similar proteinaceous material is seen in the background and signet ring–like cells may be present raising a differential of mucinous carcinoma {35156343;15584046;2822366}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Secretory carcinoma is characterized by pathognomonic ETV6::NTRK3 gene fusion {12450792}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: polygonal tumour cells with eosinophilic granular or vacuolated cytoplasm and round to oval nuclei arranged in microcystic/honeycomb, solid, tubular, or papillary growth patterns with intracytoplasmic and extracellular secretions.

Desirable: a triple-negative or weakly ER-positive and PR-positive immunoprofile; pan-TRK immunohistochemistry; FISH showing ETV6 rearrangement or identification of ETV6::NTRK3 by next-generation sequencing.

Staging

Secretory carcinoma is staged according to the guidelines for invasive carcinomas of the breast. Most secretory carcinomas present as pT1 or pT2 tumours. Axillary metastases are reported in about 20–35% and typically involve no more than three nodes {6445777;22157932;28548128;22494666;26291510}. Distant metastases are extremely rare {6445777;26291510;2925177;15963235;11106125;34162406;31012486} with lung and liver as the most common sites of involvement {34162406;31012486}.

Prognosis and prediction

Secretory carcinomas generally have an indolent clinical course, especially in children and young adults, and even in patients with axillary nodal metastases {4285563;6445777;22494666;19011601}. Nevertheless, some tumours may be more aggressive and manifest with late recurrences {2925177;34282256}. Early recurrence has been reported in 1 case {34176200} and rare deaths have been reported {6445777;26291510;2925177;15963235;34162406}. Systemic treatment is generally not advised without pathologically confirmed lymph node metastasis {34830849}. New targeted therapy from emerging TRK inhibitors has shown promising efficacy in recurrent or metastatic diseases {31012486;29623306;29233640}. Population-based studies have revealed high 5-year (94–96%) and 10-year (91–93%) cause-specific survival rates {22494666;34282256}.
