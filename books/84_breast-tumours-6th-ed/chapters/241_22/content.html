2.4.1 Epithelial-myoepithelial tumours: Introduction

Epithelial-myoepithelial tumours of the breast constitute a heterogeneous group of biphasic neoplasms composed of cells with epithelial and myoepithelial cell phenotypes. The benign epithelial-myoepithelial tumours include pleomorphic adenoma and classic adenomyoepithelioma (AME). The malignant epithelial-myoepithelial tumours include adenoid cystic carcinoma, and malignant AME in which the malignant component may be derived from luminal epithelium, myoepithelium, or both. Adenoid cystic carcinoma is described separately (see << 3.3.3>>Adenoid cystic carcinoma).

AME of the breast was first described by Hamperl in 1970 {4323195}. Breast AMEs demonstrate a variety of histological patterns {23627458;16160470;1709559;33829532}, and many display benign clinical behaviour {27494340;23627458;2844382}. However, there have been reports of local recurrence and metastasis, and these tumours can therefore be considered to have uncertain malignant potential {39389890}. Rare AMEs exhibit malignant transformation {23627458;27494340;30982573}. The morphological heterogeneity of breast AME is further supported by recent genetic data. ER-positive AMEs frequently display PIK3CA or AKT1 activating mutations, whereas ER-negative tumours often harbour HRAS mutations {29739933;31887226}.

The morphological resemblance of AME to epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma of the salivary gland has been noted in previous reports {6318584;9769134;20306833;2447854}, although AME is not a recognized entity in the salivary gland. In the fifth edition Breast tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581. ]], the term “adenomyoepithelioma” was retained in consideration of the typically indolent course of these tumours after excision and the concern about potential overtreatment if the terminology was replaced with “carcinoma” as in “epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma” {16160470}. However, it was acknowledged that some tumours may exhibit malignant change, and the terminology of malignant AME was recommended for such cases, with a description of the corresponding malignant component to guide appropriate therapy.

The WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board for the sixth edition considered introducing the morphology-based classification of AME and malignant AME proposed by Rakha et al. {33829532} but eventually opted against it, as supporting evidence regarding the clinical significance of this approach is not yet available. Therefore, the classification of epithelial-myoepithelial tumours of the breast outlined in the fifth edition is retained in the sixth edition, but a few clarifying comments have been added, as follows.

Classic AME includes benign AME and AME with focal cytological atypia (atypical AME). Given rare reports of distant metastases and the potential for malignant transformation even in histologically benign-appearing lesions, classic AME is best regarded as a tumour of uncertain malignant potential. If ductal carcinoma in situ arises in an AME, it is reported as such and managed as ductal carcinoma in situ.

Malignant AME encompasses cases with malignant epithelial and/or myoepithelial components and recognizable AME-like architecture. Cytological atypia is moderate to marked and necrosis is frequent. The outline of the lesion is at least focally irregular, associated with stromal desmoplasia and an inflammatory response. In some cases, malignant AME may take the form of a specific subtype of invasive carcinoma (e.g. metaplastic spindle cell carcinoma arising in an AME). The use of the term epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma for breast tumours with epithelial and myoepithelial malignant components is no longer endorsed.

The use of immunohistochemistry to highlight the dual epithelial and myoepithelial cell nature of AME can aid in distinguishing AMEs from other benign and malignant lesions of the breast. Because myoepithelial cells in epithelial-myoepithelial tumours can be identified through immunohistochemistry, it is crucial to understand the range, sensitivity, and specificity of the antibodies that may be used for their detection. #48373Table 2.02 provides a list of such antibodies, including their cross-reactivities. It is recommended to use a panel of myoepithelial markers (e.g. SMMHC and p63), rather than relying on a single marker. Some myoepithelial antigens are more labile and may be lost owing to suboptimal fixation. Markers such as SMA, although less myoepithelial specific, are more robust and can be helpful in assessing myoepithelial cells in poorly fixed specimens. The choice of antibodies to myoepithelial cells may also depend on the lesion being evaluated, because some antibodies may be less helpful than others in certain cases. For example, p63 is useful for distinguishing in situ from invasive carcinoma, with myoepithelial cells being retained in the former and lost in the latter {38012539}, but is not as useful as high-molecular-weight cytokeratins for corroborating a diagnosis of usual ductal hyperplasia. Myoepithelial cells in breast lesions such as AME, benign sclerosing lesions, and ductal carcinoma in situ can also be immunophenotypically different from normal myoepithelial cells at the epithelial–stromal interface of normal ducts and lobules {20463570;18936688}. Even normal myoepithelial cells in the acini differ from those in the terminal and interlobular ducts, with the former having less-developed myofilaments {11250738} and displaying distinct staining patterns.
