3.4.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction

Neuroendocrine differentiation in breast carcinomas was first described in mucinous carcinomas by Feyrter and Hartmann in 1963, as an invasive carcinoma morphologically similar to intestinal carcinoids based on positive silver staining. The first case series was published in 1977 under the term “primary carcinoid of the breast”. Later, the presence of neurosecretory granules was demonstrated using electron microscopy and modified silver staining, and these cases were referred to as argyrophilic carcinomas {6183185}. This was followed by demonstration of positive chromogranin A staining in 1985 {4025508}, which provided evidence of neuropeptide production in breast carcinomas.

Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) of the breast was endorsed as a distinct entity in the third edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours series, in the 2003 volume Pathology and genetics of tumours of the breast and female genital organs [[Tavassoli FA, Devilee P, editors. Pathology and genetics of tumours of the breast and female genital organs. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2003. (WHO classification of tumours series, 3rd ed.; vol. 4). https://publications.iarc.who.int/4. ]], and defined on the basis of the criteria of Sapino et al. {11762336}. Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the breast were regarded as tumours of epithelial origin, with morphology similar to that of gastrointestinal and pulmonary NETs, expressing a neuroendocrine marker (specifically chromogranin or synaptophysin) in at least 50% of the total invasive tumour cell population.

However, in the fourth edition of the WHO classification of breast tumours, NECs were included under the category “carcinomas with neuroendocrine features”. These tumours were described as exhibiting to some extent morphological features similar to neuroendocrine differentiation. Breast carcinomas with neuroendocrine features were classified into (1) “well-differentiated NETs”, which included low- and intermediate-grade tumours, and (2) “poorly differentiated NEC / small cell carcinomas”. This classification also acknowledged the existence of a third category, a subset of breast carcinomas with neuroendocrine differentiation as determined by morphological, histochemical, and immunohistochemical analysis. These included invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), as well as special types such as solid papillary carcinoma and cellular mucinous carcinoma.

A 2018 expert consensus statement by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and WHO {30140036} proposed adopting the term “neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN)” to encompass all tumours with predominant neuroendocrine differentiation, including both well-differentiated and poorly differentiated forms. Morphology and the expression of markers of neuroendocrine differentiation were recognized as key features defining these neoplasms at any specific anatomical site.

The fifth edition Breast tumours volume of this series acknowledged that NENs of the breast are rare and poorly defined. Apart from sporadic cases of small cell carcinoma, analogous to its pulmonary counterpart, the definition of NENs in the breast varied widely, resulting in variable incidence ranging from < 0.1% to as high as 20% {24589259;11895486;26114478} depending on the different diagnostic criteria applied. There is overlap between NENs and breast carcinomas that may show neuroendocrine differentiation such as IBC-NST, solid papillary carcinoma, and cellular mucinous carcinoma. These lesions are distinct breast neoplasms and should not be classified as NET or NEC.

These changes over the years in the classification and diagnostic criteria for neuroendocrine-differentiated cancers have puzzled pathologists and created confusion in the clinical management of these patients {36862258;36670280}.

The editorial board of this sixth-edition Breast tumours volume agreed that the 2018 proposed unified classification of NEN is difficult to apply to the breast. Apart from solid papillary carcinoma and cellular mucinous carcinoma, which are distinct special type breast cancers, as well as the rarely encountered small cell carcinoma, it is uncertain if true primary NENs exist in the breast {35133666}. Studies evaluating the prognostic relevance of neuroendocrine differentiation in IBC have yielded variable results and suggest a need for refining the 2018 proposed unified classification of NEN {36243799}. As there is currently no conclusive clinical relevance of neuroendocrine differentiation, routine staining of IBC-NST for neuroendocrine markers is not recommended.

When a breast tumour shows distinct neuroendocrine morphology, resembling a neuroendocrine tumour (previously referred to as carcinoid tumour), and is histologically different from solid papillary carcinoma and mucinous carcinoma, with neuroendocrine differentiation supported by adjunctive immunohistochemistry, metastasis from another primary site such as the gastrointestinal tract and lung needs to be ruled out {37485220;36243799}.

Thus, a recommended algorithm for a diagnostic approach towards breast tumours displaying neuroendocrine morphology is shown in #56422Fig. 3.XXX.

This algorithm considers that invasive breast cancers with neuroendocrine morphology do exist. Among these, solid papillary carcinoma and cellular mucinous carcinoma are special tumour types (see section Solid papillary carcinoma (in situ and invasive) and section Mucinous carcinoma). Invasive carcinomas whose morphological features strongly suggest neuroendocrine differentiation, but which are negative for or demonstrate focal and weak neuroendocrine marker expression, must be classified as IBC-NST.

If neuroendocrine marker expression is moderate to strong, or if it is diffuse and weak but unequivocal, these tumours should be classified as IBC-NST with neuroendocrine differentiation. Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma is likely to belong to this group of tumours. IBC-NST with neuroendocrine differentiation is described as a morphological pattern of IBC-NST in this edition.

Primary NETs of the breast are extremely rare and can be diagnosed only if they present with the typical morphology of a NET, similar to other anatomical sites, and display strong, diffuse expression of neuroendocrine markers. It is essential that metastatic NET, solid papillary carcinoma, and invasive carcinoma NST with neuroendocrine differentiation have been excluded.

Finally, the algorithm considers primary small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) to be a specific cytomorphological growth pattern of invasive breast cancers with neuroendocrine morphology, provided that metastasis to the breast has been ruled out. Primary SCNEC is extremely rare in its pure form, but it can coexist with IBC.
