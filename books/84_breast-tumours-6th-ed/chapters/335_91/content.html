3.3.5 Mucoepidermoid carcinoma

Definition

Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) is an invasive carcinoma composed of mixed mucinous, intermediate/basaloid, and squamoid/epidermoid neoplastic cells arranged in solid and cystic patterns.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

MEC can affect any breast quadrant; when arising in the retroareolar region, it can cause nipple discharge {28043647}.

Clinical features

Breast MEC usually arises in adult women ranging in age from 29 to 86 years {22135628;38062474}.

Epidemiology

MEC is quite frequent in the salivary glands, but it rarely affects the breast, with < 60 cases reported to date {22135628}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Almost all reported breast MECs have been included among the triple-negative breast carcinomas, lacking ER and PR expression and ERBB2 (HER2) amplification; low-positive ER has been seen in a minority of cases {29390541;30380176}. Only 1 case has been reported with HER2 amplification {38062474}. MAML2 gene rearrangements, initially identified in low-grade salivary gland MEC {27068311;27340278}, have been recently reported in cases of low- to intermediate-grade breast MEC {19200580;32362174}, including the t(11;19)(q21;p13) translocation resulting in CRTC1::MAML2 fusion {30380176;38243319} and rarer t(15;19)(q21;q26) resulting in CRTC3::MAML2 fusion {37422157;39398906}. Identical rearrangements have also been identified in cutaneous and breast hidradenoma, a related benign adnexal neoplasm {27914681;32853960;33325313;37422157}. Of note, MAML2 fusions have not been reported in high-grade cases of breast MEC.

Macroscopic appearance

MEC can present as a solid nodule or a cystic lesion. The reported size ranges from 6 to 110 mm {22135628}.

Histopathology

Similar to its salivary gland counterpart, breast MEC can show a wide range of histological features, spanning from low-grade to high-grade lesions {22135628;14634807;28043647}. Grading can be performed by applying the salivary gland or the breast grading systems, with similar results {14634807}. Low-grade MEC more frequently shows a cystic component. Cystic spaces are lined by mucous cells intermingled with eosinophilic cells. Solid areas show neoplastic cell nests with a peripheral layer of basaloid cells gradually merging in groups of epidermoid cells and mucous cells. High-grade MECs are more frequently solid, and they show the same cell composition as their low-grade counterparts. In high-grade MEC, cytological atypia is present, mitotic figures are numerous, and necrosis can be present. An intraductal component can be present {14634807;24166608;37422157}. True keratinization with squamous pearl formation is not a feature of MEC of any grade.

Immunohistochemistry is helpful in identifying the different cell types. Specifically, high-molecular-weight keratins (e.g. CK14) and p63 stain basaloid and epidermoid cells, whereas low-molecular-weight keratins (e.g. CK7) stain the mucous cells {14634807;2480681;24166608}. This gives rise to a characteristic zoning pattern of positivity similar to that observed in MEC of the salivary glands {14634807;12021929}. Breast MEC retains the GATA3, TRPS1, and mammaglobin positivity observed in other breast tumours {30380176;37422157}. Limited data demonstrate that S100 protein, SOX10, and MUC4 are variably positive in breast MECs {30380176;37422157}. MECs are typically triple negative in phenotype, though weak ER expression may be encountered.

The differential diagnosis includes secretory carcinoma, as both demonstrate relatively bland tumour cells, prominent secretory material, and partially overlapping immunophenotypes. MEC includes a subset of p63-positive tumour cells, in contrast to secretory carcinoma. Secretory carcinomas typically harbour ETV6::NTRK3 fusions, in contrast to the MAML2 gene rearrangements of MEC. MEC with macrocystic architecture of one to a few cell layers may be mistaken for simple cysts on limited sampling. MEC can mimic the heterogeneous cell population of usual ductal hyperplasia, with bland cytology, irregular microcystic spaces, and positive CK5/6 expression. Metaplastic squamous cell carcinoma should be distinguished from MEC, which lacks true keratinization. Cutaneous and breast hidradenoma share overlapping morphology and identical molecular alterations with low-grade MEC. Differences in cell composition, growth pattern, and immunophenotype may be useful in the differential diagnosis {37422157;35331622}.

Cytology

The cytopathological features have rarely been reported and are similar to those observed in the corresponding salivary gland lesions. Low-grade carcinomas show cellular smears with tissue fragments and sheets of intermediate and epidermoid cells with scattered goblet cells and glandular cells with single vacuoles, mild nuclear pleomorphism and rare focal keratinization in low-grade tumours. High-grade carcinomas show intermediate basaloid and squamous cells with marked nuclear atypia and pleomorphism and often minimal glandular differentiation. Cystic degeneration may occur and FNAB may only yield thin mucinous background, which should be considered atypical {16930336}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Non–high-grade MECs often harbour the t(11;19)(q21;p13) translocation resulting in CRTC1::MAML2 fusion or the rarer t(15;19)(q21;q26) resulting in CRTC3::MAML2 fusion, which are not seen in other primary breast carcinomas. Tumours harbouring the CRTC1::MAML2 or CRTC3::MAML2 fusion have been recently reported to demonstrate positive LINC00473 in situ hybridization {40107452}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: presence of basaloid, epidermoid, and mucous cells; absence of true keratinization.

Desirable: molecular tests to confirm the genetic aberrations in MEC (FISH showing MAML2 rearrangement or identification of CRTC1::MAML2 or CRTC3::MAML2 by next-generation sequencing).

Staging

MEC is staged according to the criteria adopted for other types of breast carcinomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Grading is the most important histopathological prognostic factor for breast MEC. Grading can be performed using the criteria usually applied to breast cancer, as well as those for MEC of the salivary glands {14634807;22135628;30380176}; both grading systems are based on architecture, cellular atypia, and mitotic count. Similar to its salivary gland counterpart [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Head and neck tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 9). https://publications.iarc.who.int/629. ]], high-grade MEC of the breast shows solid architecture, nuclear anaplasia, and a high mitotic count, often associated with necrosis and lymphovascular infiltration {22135628}.

According to recent reviews of the literature {32362174;34830849}, no low- or intermediate-grade MEC has been associated with patient mortality. Only 1 reported case of low-grade MEC recurred as high-grade disease, but the patient was alive and well at last follow-up {12440816}. Rare instances of axillary lymph node metastasis with low-grade MEC similarly show relatively indolent behaviour {29390541}. In contrast, high-grade MECs showing axillary and/or distant metastases have been lethal {22135628}. One patient only with recurring, high-grade MEC showing HER2 amplification and treated accordingly was alive with no evidence of disease 61 months after follow-up {38062474}. Further studies are needed to better define the pathogenesis and behaviour of high-grade MEC.
