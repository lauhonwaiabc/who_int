2.5.2 Intraductal papilloma

Definition

Intraductal papilloma is a benign breast lesion arising within a duct, composed of papillary projections with fibrovascular cores, covered by an epithelial and myoepithelial layer.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: for central papilloma: large duct papilloma; major duct papilloma; for peripheral papilloma: microscopic papilloma.

Not recommended: papillomatosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Intraductal papilloma can occur on any part of the breast parenchyma, including ectopic breast tissue on the milk line and benign epithelial inclusions within axillary lymph nodes {24525508}. Many intraductal papillomas occur centrally in large ducts near the nipple.

Clinical features

Central papillomas mainly occur in perimenopausal women {34734332;33106592;31429678;33876349} and are usually solitary. Peripheral papillomas may be multiple and typically affect slightly younger patients [[Ryska A, Torgersen FA. Intraductal Papilloma. In: Sapino A, Kulka J, editors. Breast Pathology. Encyclopedia of Pathology. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020. pp. 180â€“5. doi:10.1007/978-3-319-62539-3_4711. ]]. Serous or serosanguinous nipple discharge is more characteristic of central papillomas; peripheral papillomas seldom cause such symptoms. Both central and peripheral papillomas may present as palpable masses, though this clinical scenario is less common for peripheral papillomas.

Mammography usually shows a round or oval well-circumscribed opacity without – or rarely with – microcalcifications. Ultrasound examination reveals a solid-cystic mass or a small mass within a dilated duct. Colour Doppler ultrasonography may highlight the vascular pedicle. Galactography shows an intraluminal filling defect or duct dilatation {18929044}. Ductoscopy allows for direct tissue sampling and duct excision {16417642}. On MRI, intraductal papillomas appear as circumscribed enhancing masses or may have irregular contours {30945398}. MRI was found to be superior to galactography in diagnosing intraductal papillomas among patients having negative ultrasound and mammography {34039866}. Peripheral papillomas are usually clinically occult, small (< 5 mm) lesions that are discovered incidentally on imaging as peripherally located microcalcifications or small masses {34734332}.

Epidemiology

Intraductal papillomas (with or without atypia) were described in 5–10% of benign breast biopsies from different cohorts {16723843;28296665}. Incidence reported in case series ranges from 2% to 3% {37197642}. Patients present over a wide age range, but most are between 30 and 50 years old {16723843;12673557;33462367}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most intraductal papillomas are monoclonal epithelial proliferations with activating point mutations in the PIK3CA/AKT1 pathway {19898424;33106592}. PIK3CA mutations are commonly found in intraductal papillomas without or with atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) but not in intraductal papillomas with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or carcinomas arising in association with intraductal papillomas {19898424;33462367}. The most prevalent copy-number changes are chromosome X loss (partial or full; 13%), followed by 16q loss (8%), and 7q loss (8%) {32195332}.

Macroscopic appearance

Large, central intraductal papillomas may be visible as well-circumscribed tumours with papillary fronds attached to the wall of a dilated duct. The size of central papillomas varies from a few millimetres to several centimetres. Focal necrosis or haemorrhage may be present, particularly in larger lesions. Smaller lesions are usually grossly occult unless they are associated with other findings.

Histopathology

Intraductal papillomas are characterized by an arborescent structure. The fibrovascular cores are usually broad and sometimes oedematous; finer cores may also be seen. The finger-like projections are covered by a bilayer of myoepithelium and epithelium, the latter usually consisting of cuboidal or columnar cells. Areas of adenosis, usual ductal hyperplasia, and/or apocrine metaplasia may be present. Mitotic figures are absent to rare. Apart from the commonly associated apocrine metaplasia, squamous, clear cell, osseous, chondroid, and mucinous metaplasia may rarely occur [[Ryska A, Torgersen FA. Intraductal Papilloma. In: Sapino A, Kulka J, editors. Breast Pathology. Encyclopedia of Pathology. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020. pp. 180â€“5. doi:10.1007/978-3-319-62539-3_4711. ]]. Occasionally, collagenous spherulosis is present. Rarely, myoepithelial cells may undergo hyperplasia within the intraductal papilloma {34734332}.

The fibrovascular cores and the duct wall may become sclerotic leading to entrapment of epithelium mimicking invasion. However, the myoepithelial cell layer remains continuous within these sclerotic areas and recognition of this finding, with the use of immunohistochemistry if necessary, is crucial to avoiding overdiagnosis. As reduced expression of some myoepithelial cell antigens may occur in benign sclerosing lesions, it is recommended to use a panel of immunohistochemical markers {20463570}. Intraductal papillomas with prominent sclerosis are often reported as sclerosing papillomas.

Intraductal papillomas with ADH and DCIS are characterized by a focal population of monotonous cells with cytological and architectural features of low-grade ductal neoplasia. Peripheral papillomas more often harbour such atypical foci {34734332}. The assessment of epithelial atypia is facilitated by considering the space between adjacent fibrovascular cores as equivalent to a duct space and applying the criteria used for the interpretation of intraductal proliferations. ADH in an intraductal papilloma is defined as an atypical area of < 3 mm in extent. Intraductal papilloma with ADH can also be reported as atypical papilloma. If the atypical area is ≥ 3 mm, DCIS in an intraductal papilloma should be diagnosed. Myoepithelial cells are absent in these foci, whereas the atypical epithelial cells are strongly and diffusely ER-positive and do not express high-molecular-weight cytokeratins (cytokeratins 5, 6, and 14). Foci of conventional apocrine metaplasia in an intraductal papilloma may also lack or show decreased myoepithelial cells and do not express high-molecular-weight cytokeratins, but should not be regarded as concerning unless apocrine atypia is present {22339683}. Importantly, when epithelial proliferations with intermediate or high nuclear grade atypia are seen, the diagnosis of DCIS within a papilloma should be made regardless of extent {12673557}. Papillomas with DCIS must be distinguished from papillary DCIS and encapsulated papillary carcinoma (see section Papillary ductal carcinoma in situ and section Encapsulated papillary carcinoma). Foci of lobular neoplasia may be present in an intraductal papilloma. Immunohistochemical demonstration of loss of E-cadherin or β-catenin and aberrant (cytoplasmic) expression of p120 can be used to highlight these areas. Intraductal papilloma with lobular neoplasia should be reported as such and is not classified as atypical papilloma.

On core needle biopsy, features of intraductal papilloma (as described above) are often readily recognized, even in fragmented specimens. However, the limited sample may cause diagnostic difficulty, especially in the case of sclerosing papillomas or papillomas with epithelial hyperplasia or atypia. Myoepithelial immunostaining may be helpful in highlighting the underlying papillary architecture and establishing the presence or absence of myoepithelial cells. At diagnostic core needle biopsy and vacuum-assisted biopsy, if the papillary fragments are unequivocally benign (presence of broad fibrovascular cores, myoepithelial cells around the entire lesion and along the fibrovascular cores, and usual epithelial hyperplasia confirmed by morphology and, if needed, immunohistochemistry), a diagnosis of intraductal papilloma is rendered. It has been suggested that if the whole lesion is removed by vacuum-assisted biopsy and lacks atypia, surgical excision can be avoided. In a large series of breast intraductal papillomas without atypia, no progression was detected on active surveillance {33876349}. Diagnoses of intraductal papilloma with ADH or DCIS, however, require surgical excision {34734332;38061151;34039866;34008998}. The presence of atypical lobular hyperplasia in intraductal papilloma in core biopsy – provided there is radiology–pathology concordance – may not warrant surgical excision {33106592;37863800}.

Intraductal papilloma must be distinguished from papillary DCIS, encapsulated papillary carcinoma, and solid papillary carcinoma (see next sections). Adenomyoepitheliomas may have papillary architecture and can closely mimic intraductal papilloma, but the diagnosis of adenomyoepithelioma should be restricted to cases showing a biphasic cell population with prominent expansion and proliferation of the myoepithelial component. Fibroepithelial lesions with pronounced polypoid stromal architecture may also cause differential diagnostic difficulty in fragmented biopsy specimens. Other differential diagnostic considerations include tall cell carcinoma with reversed polarity, hidradenoma papilliferum, and nipple adenoma.

Cytology

Intraductal papillomas produce moderate to high cellularity with large cohesive tissue fragments with complex or stellate papillary fibroelastotic stromal cores and attached hyperplastic ductal epithelial tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells and regular nuclei, reflecting the wide range of surgical pathology of papillomas {17115436;17636490}. Similar cohesive epithelial tissue fragments of varying size and dispersed often columnar epithelial cells with small bland nuclei are present in variable numbers. Apocrine sheets, histiocytes, and haemosiderophages are present in the proteinaceous background that may dilute the typical bipolar nuclei. Reasonable degrees of accuracy in differentiating benign papilloma from the spectrum of more malignant papillary lesions have been achieved by centres with considerable experience in FNAB cytopathology {35341114;22236744}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular studies are not relevant for routine diagnosis and assessment of intraductal papillomas.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraductal papillary growth; presence of epithelial and myoepithelial cell layers along the fibrovascular cores; lack of cellular atypia.

Desirable: identification of myoepithelial cells within the lesion and at the periphery of the duct, with immunohistochemistry if necessary.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with intraductal papilloma carry a 2- to 3-fold relative risk increase of developing breast carcinoma {16723843;35718581}. Multiple papillomas are associated with a relative risk of breast cancer that is higher than that for proliferative fibrocystic lesions but lower than for ADH / atypical lobular hyperplasia {8674001;12673557;33462367}. With atypical papillomas (papillomas with ADH), the risk of subsequent invasive carcinoma is reported to be 5- to 7.5-fold, with the risk to the ipsilateral breast and bilateral breasts differing between studies {8674001;16723843}. The risk of subsequent carcinoma and local recurrence associated with atypical papillomas is obscured by the frequent concurrent presence of ADH or DCIS within the surrounding breast parenchyma {8674001;12673557;16723843}.
