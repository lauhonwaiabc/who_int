4.0.0.5 Parathyroid carcinoma

Definition

Parathyroid carcinoma is a malignant neoplasm originating from parathyroid parenchymal cells.

ICD-O coding

8140/3 Parathyroid carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.1 & XH7JQ0 Adenocarcinoma of other endocrine glands or related structures & Parathyroid carcinoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Parathyroid carcinoma can arise in any of the normal anatomical locations of parathyroid glands or in anatomical sites where developmental parathyroid tissue may be identified, including the neck, thyroid, retro-oesophageal space, thymus, mediastinum, and pericardium {27313750; 30455668; 32248393; 33857367}.

Clinical features

Most patients with parathyroid carcinomas present with symptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism {1518393; 15287039; 22290780; 31111352; 31708875}; however, extremely rare examples have been reported in the setting of chronic renal failure–related tertiary hyperparathyroidism {8640028; 8909528; 15275889; 21247853; 27664600; 31142320}. Symptomatic patients have renal disease with nephrolithiasis and/or bone disease {26163537}. Other signs and symptoms of hypercalcaemia (including weakness, vomiting, constipation, nausea, polyuria, polydipsia, pancreatitis, arrhythmias, and neurological manifestations) can also occur {26177319; 31708875}. Patients with hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome (HPT-JT) can also manifest associated characteristics features (see section Hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome).

Biochemical investigations

Laboratory tests usually show significantly elevated levels of PTH (usually > 3 times and often 10 times the upper limit of normal) and calcium (> 3 mmol/L or > 12 mg/dL, or 3–4 mg above the upper limit of normal) {1518393; 15287039; 21861982; 21937626; 22290780; 31111352}. Rare examples of biochemically non-functioning parathyroid carcinomas {18194932; 21937626; 26350418; 28726134; 33810359; 33910359} have been reported, occurring in up to 3% of parathyroid carcinomas in clinical series {21937626}. A third-to-second generation PTH assay ratio > 1 has been shown to help predict the risk of malignancy with a high sensitivity (~83%) and specificity (range: 97–100%) {18782355; 20519352; 24423313}. Symptomatic hyperparathyroidism in association with a palpable neck mass (often > 30 mm) requires exclusion of parathyroid carcinoma {21861982; 26177319}.

Imaging

Structural (e.g. ultrasonography, CT) and functional (e.g. 99mTc sestamibi scan) imaging studies are often used in the preoperative workup of any patients with hyperparathyroidism. Imaging findings concerning for parathyroid carcinoma include the presence of invasive growth and/or calcification on ultrasound, or the suspicion of metastatic disease on 99mTc sestamibi scan or CT) {26177319}. However, most parathyroid carcinomas are diagnosed by histopathological examination of a parathyroidectomy specimen or during the workup of a metastatic carcinoma. However, a fraction of patients with parathyroid carcinoma can manifest with recurrent laryngeal nerve paralysis with vocal cord dysfunction and hoarseness, invasion into adjacent anatomical structures (either on imaging studies or intraoperative documentation), or distant metastasis at the time of their clinical workup {11323564; 21937626; 29344163; 31708875}.

Epidemiology

Parathyroid carcinoma accounts for < 1% of primary hyperparathyroidism. The reported incidence of parathyroid carcinoma has increased in parallel to the increasing rate of parathyroid surgery in North America {17372919}, Finland {28362521}, Australia {22295377}, China {29344163}, and the Republic of Korea {33738545} over the past decade(s). Epidemiological data indicate that these tumours affect 5.73 (USA, 2000–2003), 6.6 (Republic of Korea, 2016), and 10.4 (Finland, 2010–2013) individuals per 10 million population per year {17372919; 33738545; 28362521}. It is unclear whether increased laboratory screening for hypercalcaemia {26163537} and the integration of ancillary biomarker studies {22295377} have contributed to this observation.

Parathyroid carcinoma can occur at any age, including in the paediatric population {1518393; 21937626; 22290780; 26077914; 31111352; 31708875; 3216840; 33738545; 33815520}. The mean age at the time of diagnosis is 51–57 years in most clinical series {1518393; 21937626; 22290780; 25456964; 26077914; 31111352; 31708875; 33738545}. Compared with people in other ethnic groups, non-Hispanic White people in the USA had a disproportionately higher incidence of parathyroid carcinoma in the National Cancer Database (NCDB) {25456964}. Although an equal or not significantly different sex distribution is reported in most cohorts {10430265; 1518393; 26077914; 29344163; 31111352}, a slightly decreased M:F ratio has been noted in some studies {22290780; 28362521; 31708875; 33738545; 33815520; 21937626}. Parafibromin-deficient parathyroid carcinomas are reported to have a male predilection {30923346; 31945198} and are more frequent in younger patients {29324469; 31945198}.

Etiology

CDC73 mutation, associated with HPT-JT, dramatically increases the lifetime risk of parathyroid carcinoma {24402736; 32468209}. Given its rarity, there are few studies on the etiology of sporadic parathyroid carcinoma. An association with previous irradiation to the head and neck region has been reported, although this phenomenon is also reported for parathyroid adenomas {3179662}.

Pathogenesis

Parathyroid carcinoma can occur sporadically, but it is also a feature of familial hyperparathyroidism syndromes, most notably occurring in 10–15% of patients with HPT-JT as well as in rare forms of familial isolated hyperparathyroidism {12434154; 18436011; 19529956; 23293331; 24823466; 25444225; 29324469}. When parathyroid carcinomas arise in the setting of HPT-JT, inactivating germline mutations or deletions of CDC73 are found in the majority of kindreds {12434154; 14585940; 14715834}. Moreover, 40–80% of sporadic parathyroid carcinomas harbour inactivating CDC73 mutations in addition to frequent loss of heterozygosity of the CDC73 locus at chromosome 1q25-q31 {12960210; 14585940; 14694525; 15531515; 16006799; 17468190}. The CDC73 mutations are frequently germline, indicative of de novo mutations or undetected HPT-JT {14585940; 15531515; 21360064}.

CDC73 encodes parafibromin, a nuclear tumour suppressor belonging to PAF1C (a multiprotein unit that interacts with RNA polymerase II to facilitate mRNA elongation and posttranscriptional processing) {15632063; 15923622; 19908240}. Parafibromin exhibits antiproliferative and proapoptotic properties, partly through the epigenetic regulation of the oncogenes CCND1 (encoding cyclin D1) and MYC (c-myc) oncogenes and partly through interactions with the WNT pathway effector β-catenin {16630820; 18987311; 19906718}. The majority of CDC73 mutations in parathyroid carcinomas are truncating or frameshift alterations (or alternatively, missense mutations in conserved regions), and loss of parafibromin immunoreactivity is observed in CDC73-mutated cases {14585940; 15475453; 16931959; 17639063}. Premature truncating mutations often lead to an abolished nuclear localization signal, providing an explanation for the absence of nuclear parafibromin expression in CDC73-mutated parathyroid carcinomas {16116486; 16931959; 16964291}. Moreover, CDC73 mutations abolishing the nucleolar localization signal have also been reported, and these rare cases might display an exclusive loss of parafibromin nucleolar expression, suggesting a nucleolar tumour-suppressor role for parafibromin in addition to the established nuclear properties {17923126; 20304979; 21221636; 24842573}. CDC73-mutated parathyroid carcinomas may display an increase in genomic instability, highlighted by augmented levels of copy-number alterations, an increased tumour mutation burden, and poorer patient outcomes, as compared with CDC73-wildtype cases {32574388}.

Other reported genomic alterations in sporadic parathyroid carcinomas include loss of TP53 and RB1 alleles, although mutations in these genes are rarely found {7906387; 8200932; 30788456}. Amplifications of CCND1 (encoding cyclin D1) have been demonstrated in subsets of cases {28352668}. Unlike in parathyroid adenoma, MEN1 mutations are only rarely reported in parathyroid carcinoma {17555500; 30788456; 31309300}. TERT promoter mutations are also rarely reported in parathyroid carcinoma {25876648; 31309300}. Parathyroid carcinomas recurrently have mutations in genes with potential implications for targeted molecular therapies, including PTEN, NF1, KDR, and PIK3CA {30373905; 31309300}. Previously unrecognized DICER1 mutations were recently reported in a series of advanced parathyroid carcinomas {31309300}.

From an epigenetic standpoint, parathyroid carcinomas display aberrant methylation of several cancer-specific genes, including the WNT pathway regulator APC and the cell-cycle regulators CDKN2A and CDKN2B {21638518; 21078161}. Levels of 5-hydroxymethylcytosine have been found to be extensively reduced in parathyroid carcinomas, suggestive of a universal reduction in demethylation events across the genome in parathyroid carcinoma {26973719}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross examination findings vary considerably among tumours. Smaller tumours may be indistinguishable from parathyroid adenomas. However, most parathyroid carcinomas are large, poorly circumscribed masses and are variably encapsulated, weighing 1.5 g to > 50 g {13149082}. The mean tumour size range is 30–49 mm {22290780; 28362521; 31708875}, but smaller tumours (e.g. 11 mm) have been reported {31734935}. Tumours may be grossly adherent to the thyroid gland and/or adjacent structures. The cut section of the tumours is often firm and tan to pink, with a lobular appearance attributable to band-forming fibrosis {4693587; 26177319; 26163537}.

Histopathology

The histopathological diagnosis of a parathyroid carcinoma is restricted to parathyroid tumours that show at least one of the following findings: (1) angioinvasion (vascular invasion), (2) lymphatic invasion, (3) perineural invasion, (4) local malignant invasion into the adjacent structures/organs, or (5) regional or distant metastasis. The submission of a complete parathyroidectomy specimen is required in the diagnostic workup of a parathyroid neoplasm. Evaluation of multiple levels and the use of ancillary tools can assist the diagnostic assessment.

Like in other endocrine neoplasms, angioinvasion is typically assessed at the tumour periphery or the tumour capsule, but not within the tumour substance. The demonstration of angioinvasion is a highly reliable criterion of malignancy {20221704; 22290780; 22434247; 23001705; 26177319; 31734935} and has prognostic significance {20221704; 22290780; 22434247; 28362521; 31708875}. Angioinvasion often involves small veins and is characterized by tumour cells invading through a vessel wall and/or intravascular tumour cells admixed with thrombus/fibrin {23001705; 22290780; 26177319}. When the latter is subtle, the fibrin–platelet complex can be highlighted using CD61 or Martius Scarlet Blue histochemistry. Vascular endothelium can be highlighted using ERG, CD34, and CD31. Lymphatic invasion can be highlighted using D2-40. Perineural invasion is often a microscopic finding, but gross invasion into large nerve bundles (e.g. recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion) can also occur and can be noted in intraoperative findings.

Most parathyroid carcinomas lack a true fibrous capsule. The tumours are variably cellular and are often subdivided by broad bands of fibrous connective tissue extending from the peritumoural pseudocapsule. Fibrous bands are present in most parathyroid carcinomas {4693587; 26177319; 30455668}; however, their presence alone is not diagnostic of malignancy {8338192; 17021134; 26177319; 28061891; 33740321}. In addition, focal extension into a fibrotic band or cellular nests in a connective tissue component overlying the external border of the lesion does not qualify for malignancy. The latter becomes critical in parathyroidectomy specimens that were subjected to previous manipulations (e.g. FNAB, PTH washout, ethanol injection) or in parathyroids from patients with longstanding secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism {9023564; 17021134; 17887929; 26177319; 28061891; 31708875; 33740321}. The upper parathyroid glands in particular may be embedded in the pseudocapsule or parenchyma of the thyroid gland, and this should not be mistaken for true invasive growth {21742652; 30455668; 31708875}.

Cytological atypia can be variable in parathyroid carcinomas. Some tumours may show marked pleomorphism with coarse chromatin and prominent macronucleoli, but others that are enriched in chief cells with micronucleoli and no marked atypia are indistinguishable from their benign counterparts with the exception of their invasive growth. CDC73-mutated parathyroid carcinomas commonly have eosinophilic cytoplasm with perinuclear clearing {29324469}. Other morphological correlates of CDC73-mutant parathyroid neoplasms include larger tumours with encapsulation, diffuse sheet-like growth, nuclear enlargement, arborizing vasculature, and microcystic change {29324469}, as well as high proliferative rates {30923346}.

In the parathyroid, an increased mitotic count is defined as > 5 mitoses/10 mm2 {8338192}. Increased mitotic count can occur in secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism–related multiglandular parathyroid disease and in parathyroid adenomas with degenerative changes {4693587; 7211756; 26177319}. However, the identification of even a single atypical mitotic figure and/or coagulative tumour necrosis should alert the pathologist to the possibility of parathyroid carcinoma. A triad of coagulative necrosis in association with increased mitotic activity (> 5 mitoses/10 mm2) and prominent macronucleoli is worrisome for malignancy {8338192}. It is desirable to assess such tumours using ancillary biomarkers and to evaluate multiple levels to help identify invasive growth.

Like for other neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), assessment of the proliferative activity of all parathyroid carcinomas by recording a formal Ki-67 proliferation index and mitotic count using standardized approaches is now recommended {32687943}.

Immunohistochemistry

Unlike parathyroid adenomas, carcinomas are associated with immunohistochemical loss of expression of parafibromin, APC, E-cadherin, p27, BCL2, MDM2, and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine; positivity for PGP9.5, galectin-3, and TERT; p53 overexpression; and an increased Ki-67 labelling index (often > 5%) {10078919; 12605367; 16112008; 16931959; 19017757; 19885612; 20473645; 22290780; 23001705; 25002250; 26177319; 27490759; 28642344; 29626276; 30923346; 31708875}. Among these, loss of nuclear parafibromin expression has been regarded as a reliable biomarker of malignancy in the appropriate morphological setting {16931959; 17639063; 23001705; 30361844; 30923346; 31734935; 33269427}. Loss of parafibromin expression, termed parafibromin deficiency, can be seen in any tumour, benign or malignant, in the setting of germline CDC73 mutation (HPT-JT) {29324469}. However, it has been proposed that a lower threshold for invasive growth may be appropriate in parafibromin-deficient parathyroid tumours {29324469}. Nucleolar loss of parafibromin has also been reported in some parathyroid carcinomas {21221636; 30903444; 30923346}, and the significance of this finding requires further somatic/germline CDC73 mutation analysis in individual cases. A panel approach and diagnostic nomograms combining multiple biomarkers (e.g. parafibromin, PGP9.5, Ki-67, and galectin-3) have also been suggested to be helpful {12605367; 16112008; 19017757; 25002250; 27490759; 29626276; 31734935; 33642204}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses of parathyroid carcinoma include parathyroid adenoma with postbiopsy reactive changes {17021134; 17887929; 28061891; 33740321}, secondary/tertiary parathyroid hyperplasia with increased proliferative activity and fibrosis (discussed in section Parathyroid hyperplasia), atypical parathyroid tumour (see section Atypical parathyroid tumour), and parathyromatosis {17559137; 33394375}.

Parathyromatosis represents foci of parathyroid tissue that often grow around the site of a former surgery or biopsy due to an inadvertent implantation of lesional parathyroid tissue, and can also be related to the persistence of developmental remnants {1985539; 2307452; 33394375; 33740321}. The locally infiltrative growth of parathyromatosis may make it indistinguishable from parathyroid carcinoma; however, biomarker studies, markedly elevated serum calcium and PTH levels, a palpable neck mass, and (more importantly) the presence of vascular or perineural invasion can help distinguish parathyroid carcinoma {17559137; 26177319; 33394375}.

Atypical parathyroid tumours that show coagulative tumour necrosis, atypical mitotic figures, or loss of parafibromin may represent parathyroid carcinomas that are not captured using the conventional strict criteria. Therefore, it may be helpful to perform parafibromin immunohistochemistry in atypical parathyroid tumours, because parafibromin-deficient atypical parathyroid tumours have an increased risk of recurrence or metachronous disease in other glands and would warrant long-term follow-up {29324469}.

Some parathyroid carcinomas display follicular growth with variable luminal proteinaceous material that can simulate a follicular thyroid neoplasm. Rarely, the tumours can demonstrate a carcinosarcomatous growth {23493876} that can mimic various other neoplasms. Most parathyroid carcinomas show diffuse sheet-like or trabecular (rather than acinar) growth. The trabecular pattern may be mistaken for a poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma or medullary thyroid carcinoma, especially when parathyroid carcinomas involve the thyroid gland {30455668; 31708875; 32146582}. In addition, parathyroid carcinomas composed exclusively of oncocytic cells can mimic an oncocytic thyroid carcinoma. In difficult cases, immunohistochemical markers to confirm parathyroid origin are usually definitive {29626276}.

With the exception of non-functioning disease, PTH is often expressed in parathyroid carcinomas. However, PTH expression alone is not specific for parathyroid neoplasms, because other NENs can (rarely) show aberrant PTH expression {29626276}. The latter may be of significance when assessing biopsy specimens from metastatic tumours {27529763}. Parathyroid carcinomas are positive for the transcription factors GCM2 {21164298} and GATA3 {25046229}. As GCM2 immunohistochemistry is not widely used in most laboratories, positivity for PTH and GATA3 can be used to confirm parathyroid origin {29626276}.

Rare paragangliomas {20237987} and metastases (especially of breast origin) {28875280} have been identified in parathyroids and can be mistaken for parathyroid neoplasms. GATA3 alone is also not specific to parathyroid, because NENs (including paragangliomas and pituitary neuroendocrine tumours [PitNETs] and other carcinomas (notably from the breast) can be positive for GATA3 {23939152; 30390035; 32193825; 32303954}.

Calcitonin and CGRP reactivity should not be relied upon completely to distinguish medullary thyroid carcinoma from parathyroid, because these proteins can also be expressed in parathyroid proliferations, including parathyroid carcinomas {30903444}. Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) reactivity and diffuse monoclonal CEA expression can help identify medullary thyroid carcinoma {29744727}. It is important to recognize that C-terminus–specific PAX8 antibodies are negative in NENs; however, polyclonal PAX8 can show variable staining in parathyroid tumours {29626276}.

Parathyroid carcinomas are typically positive for cytokeratins and chromogranins, like other epithelial NENs {29626276}, but often lack INSM1 {32813226}.

Cytology

Smears show the same features as those of parathyroid adenoma, although it has been suggested that more prominent nucleoli, mitoses, and (possibly) a papillary-solid pattern may suggest a carcinoma, whereas nuclear atypia is seen in both lesions {28217943}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular profiling of a parathyroid neoplasm may be used to assist the diagnosis of malignancy {21638518; 22733721; 23764768; 33269427}. Screening for germline CDC73 variants should be considered for all parathyroid carcinomas, particularly parafibromin-deficient tumours {21167377}. Patients with advanced disease may benefit from somatic mutation analyses to search for personalized actionable targets {31309300}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a parathyroid neoplasm with one of the following features: (1) angioinvasion (vascular invasion), (2) lymphatic invasion, (3) perineural invasion, (4) local invasion into adjacent structures/organs, (5) histologically confirmed metastasis.

Desirable: ancillary biomarkers including but not limited to Ki-67 (MIB1), parafibromin, and galectin-3 immunohistochemistry; germline and somatic genotyping.

Staging

Staging of parathyroid carcinoma is based on the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system.

Prognosis and prediction

Older age, tumour size > 32 mm, completeness of tumour excision, high-risk clinicopathological findings (angioinvasion, local gross invasion into neck organs, nodal and distant metastases), loss of parafibromin expression, and galectin-3 positivity have been considered risk factors in parathyroid carcinomas {10430243; 16000846; 20221704; 21937626; 22434247; 31111352; 31708875; 31734935; 31945198}.

Locoregional recurrence and/or distant metastasis (e.g. to lung, liver, bone) occurs in 25–60% of parathyroid carcinomas {20221704; 25444314; 22290780; 31111352; 31708875; 31734935}. Late distant metastasis (after 10 years) has been reported {31111352}. The 20-year cumulative incidence of distant metastasis is reported to be about 40% in a large series from MD Anderson Cancer Center {31111352}.
