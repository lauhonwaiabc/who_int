2.0.0.1 Pituitary tumours: Introduction

The pituitary gland has two major components: the anterior pituitary, which is a neuroendocrine structure comprising six hormone-secreting cell types and sustentacular folliculostellate cells within a richly vascular stroma, and the posterior pituitary, which is composed of pituicytes (modified glia of hypothalamic origin) supporting the axons of hypothalamic neurons that secrete hormones into the bloodstream. The tumours that arise from this gland reflect these cell types and their embryonic origin {34017065} [[Asa SL, Perry A. Tumors of the pituitary gland. Rockville (MD): American Registry of Pathology; 2020. (AFIP atlas of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 1).]]. Additionally, tumours in the sella turcica can arise from the meninges and stromal elements; occasional germ cell tumours and haematopoietic neoplasms involve this gland, and metastases to the pituitary occur.

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs)
Evolution of the nomenclature

Tumours of adenohypophysial hormone–secreting cells have been traditionally classified as pituitary adenomas; however, these tumours may be locally invasive and can metastasize. There are no morphological features that distinguish locally aggressive and metastatic lesions from indolent ones {34017065}.

Adenohypophysial cells contain ultrastructural neurosecretory granules and express neuroendocrine proteins such as synaptophysin, chromogranin A, CD56, and INSM1; therefore, they are members of the family of neuroendocrine cells. Consistent with the WHO / International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) proposal to unify the approach to neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) {30140036}, a change in nomenclature has been suggested {28264912}, and these neoplasms are now recognized as PitNETs {33866514; 34017065}. As a transition from the fourth edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs, in which the term “pituitary adenoma” was used, in this fifth edition, we use the term “PitNET/adenoma” to indicate that “pituitary adenomas” still can be used but “PitNET” is preferred.

This change in nomenclature is accompanied by a change in coding that aligns with other NETs. PitNETs are like well-differentiated appendiceal NETs that have a low frequency of metastatic spread, but they imply much greater morbidity and mortality. This change will obviate the need for distinguishing pituitary carcinomas on the basis of discontinuous spread or distant metastasis. It remains to be seen whether there are primary poorly differentiated high-grade malignancies that qualify for the WHO/IARC definition of neuroendocrine carcinoma (small cell [SCNEC] or large cell [LCNEC] neuroendocrine carcinomas) {30140036}. The current WHO classification encourages the use of the term “metastatic PitNET” with subtype information (e.g. metastatic densely granulated corticotroph tumour) instead of “pituitary carcinoma”. There is no single clinical feature, morphological parameter, or biomarker that can help accurately determine the risk of metastatic spread in PitNETs {22822048; 33433883; 34017065}. No widely accepted grading systems have been defined for PitNETs. The spectrum of pituitary mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) includes PitNET in association with pituicyte-family tumours {34129177} or craniopharyngiomas {18769335; 23344977}.

The new PitNET classification

PitNETs reflect their derivation from six adenohypophysial cell types with multiple morphological subtypes of each tumour type. The molecular mechanisms responsible for the differentiation of these various cells types has been carefully studied during development and tumorigenesis {8077321; 8530610; 8964846; 11290323; 11557786; 11796836; 11910101; 12415254; 16932287; 30390035}. It has become clear that there are three families of cells determined by the expression of three transcription factors: PIT1, TPIT, and SF1. The PIT1 family is complex, giving rise to four terminally differentiated cell types that are known to be fluid, fluctuating from one to another in different physiological conditions. Each adenohypophysial cell type can give rise to multiple subtypes, and almost any tumour can be clinically non-functioning or may give rise to clinical and biochemical evidence of hormone excess. Some PitNETs are composed of less differentiated cells leading to an immature differentiation status as seen in immature PIT1-lineage tumours (formerly known as silent subtype 3 adenomas, then as poorly differentiated PIT1-lineage PitNETs) {26743473; 28766057}. There are also null cell tumours that fail to demonstrate terminal differentiation along SF1, TPIT, or PIT1 lineages {25403448; 29434339; 31401793}. Multiple PitNETs may develop in the same gland, creating collision tumours of multiple cell lineages {30215160}.

Detailed histological subtyping of a PitNET on the basis of cell lineage, cell type, and related characteristics provides critical clinical information for risk stratification, because different PitNET subtypes have different therapeutic responses {24144285; 26177314; 29434339; 32813227; 34017065}. Hormone immunohistochemistry alone can sometimes be misleading because of nonspecific or diffusion-type staining, emphasizing the importance of staining for pituitary transcription factors to ensure accurate tumour characterization {32813227}. The distinction of multiple synchronous PitNETs of distinct lineages absolutely requires staining for transcription factors {30215160}. Therefore, the current WHO classification of PitNETs is based on routine staining for transcription factors (PIT1, SF1, TPIT, GATA3, and ERα) and antibodies against adenohypophysial hormones (ACTH, growth hormone, prolactin, TSH-β, LH-β, FSH-β, and the α-subunit of glycoprotein hormones), as well as other biomarkers including but not limited to low-molecular-weight keratins (e.g. CAM5.2, CK8/18) {29434339; 29453601; 32813227; 34017065} (see #28646Table 2.01). Like for other NETs, the Ki-67 proliferation index should be assessed using either manual counting or automated image analysis nuclear algorithms {29434339; 34017065}. The term “PitNET NOS” can be used when pathologists do not have access to ancillary tools to enable subtyping, but such cases should be referred to centres that can perform accurate subtyping. Although routine SSTR immunohistochemistry is not required for the management of patients with PitNETs, immunoreactivity for SSTR2 and SSTR5 has been shown to predict responsiveness to somatostatin analogues {28176162; 30346538; 32375119}.

Epidemiology of PitNETs

Like other NETs {18565894}, PitNETs that were once considered rare are increasingly diagnosed. Approximately 20% of so-called normal pituitary glands harbour an incidental radiologically detectable lesion {8451394; 15274075}. Multiple population studies from the last 15 years have identified a prevalence of 78–116 cases per 100 000 people {16968795; 19650784; 19968031; 26423473}. Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) data from 2011–2015 estimate that surgically resected sellar tumours have an incidence of 4.12 cases per 100 000 person-years {30445539}. In the Republic of Korea, the incidence in cases per 100 000 person-years was 3.5 for non-functioning pituitary tumours, 1.6 for lactotroph tumours, 0.5 for somatotroph tumours, and 0.2 for corticotroph or thyrotroph tumours {32949324}. Prolactinomas causing amenorrhoea-galactorrhoea syndrome are the most common functioning PitNETs {25084775; 32741475}; these are usually sparsely granulated lactotroph tumours that are treated medically, and most are therefore not captured in cancer or surgical statistics. Surgical resection is performed for 56% of clinically diagnosed PitNETs {16968795}. Among those resected surgically, > 40% are hormonally inactive mass lesions of SF1 lineage {29434339}, approximately 15% are of TPIT lineage, and about 30% are of PIT1 lineage, with more than half of those giving rise to growth hormone excess {7893282; 16968795; 19650784; 29434339}.

Etiology and pathogenesis of PitNETs

The pathogenesis of PitNETs remains largely enigmatic, and continued investigation is still warranted. Some cases are familial, and the genes implicated have shed some light on the development of these tumours (see #28647Table 2.02) Sporadic tumours have, in limited cases, been shown to have mutations that affect the signalling pathways regulating hormone production (see #28648Table 2.03) {33433883}, but the majority of PitNETs have no known driver mutations {33433883}. Instead, epigenetic alterations are implicated in the silencing of tumour suppressor genes, mainly those involved in chromatin remodelling and cell-cycle regulation {33433883}.

In select cases, application of molecular immunohistochemistry tools may be used to demonstrate global loss of menin (MEN1), p27 (CDKN1B), and SDHB protein (encoded by SDHB) to guide further germline screening for multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 and type 4 and succinate dehydrogenase–deficient tumour syndromes {22170724; 23933118; 24625421; 29453601; 33805450; 30421319}. The diagnosis of a sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour in a young patient should prompt genetic testing for AIP-related pathogenesis (familial isolated pituitary adenoma [FIPA] syndrome) {23371967}; however, AIP immunohistochemistry is not recommended, because it has poor sensitivity in the detection of AIP mutations since this protein is epigenetically downregulated in sporadic tumours {25658813}. At present, it appears that the overall utility of MAX immunohistochemistry in routine clinical practice is limited, and it is not recommended as a screening test of MAX mutation {33367756}.

Other anterior pituitary tumours
Craniopharyngiomas

The anterior pituitary derives from the oral ectoderm, and tumours reflecting this with squamous and adamantinomatous morphology are known as craniopharyngiomas. Molecular studies have clearly identified the basis for the morphological distinction between these two entities; papillary craniopharyngiomas harbour BRAF p.V600E mutations that can be detected with the monoclonal VE1 antibody, whereas adamantinomatous subtypes have CTNNB1 mutations leading to nuclear β-catenin immunoreactivity {29453601; 32965631}.

Pituitary blastoma

Very rare primitive tumours composed of a mixture of adenohypophysial neuroendocrine cells, primitive Rathke cleft cells, and primitive folliculostellate cells are known as pituitary blastomas. These tumours are a hallmark of DICER1 syndrome {24839956; 33644822}.

Posterior pituitary and hypothalamic neoplasms
Pituicytomas

The tumours known as pituicytomas have long been recognized as neoplasms of pituicytes {10716149; 20586639; 23887161}. However, spindle cell oncocytomas {12170092} and granular cell tumours were of unknown cytogenesis at the time of their initial description, and tumours with ependymal morphology were thought to be unusual ependymomas arising in the sella turcica. However, the normal posterior pituitary contains subtypes of pituicytes that correspond to these tumours: light and dark spindle cells, as well as oncocytic, granular, and ependymal pituicytes {7188885}. Therefore, it is no surprise that thoughtful analyses {18992914}, biomarker expression {19525896; 23887161; 24242699}, and methylation profiling {29539639} have shown that these various neoplasms represent a spectrum of a single nosological entity and can be classified as subtypes of pituicytoma. The transition to the recognition of new terminology is proposed in this WHO classification. However, patient demographics and clinical outcomes appear to vary between these tumours, warranting their recognition as distinct subtypes despite the presence of overlapping features in some cases.

Neuronal neoplasms

The existence of gangliocytomas in the sella has been known for many years; these may be the source of adenohypophysiotropic hormones and they may be associated with PitNETs. The spectrum of neuronal tumours has recently been expanded by the recognition of hormonally active neurocytomas of hypothalamic differentiation {30379651; 31635149}.

Grading and staging of primary sellar neoplasms

The prognosis of patients with sellar tumours depends on tumour type, proliferative capacity, and extent of disease. Currently there are no grading or staging systems for these tumours, but these are needed. In designing these systems, the following should be considered.

Grading

In many NETs, grade is defined by proliferative rate. However, this has not proved valuable alone in the pituitary. Instead, prognosis varies by tumour type {17961741; 29067608; 29453601; 30481158; 33852418}, and certain tumour types are recognized as more aggressive (high-risk PitNETs), including immature PIT1-lineage tumours {16316952; 19170710; 26743473}, Crooke cell tumours {14508394; 25635886; 32145418}, null cell tumours {31401793; 32480370; 25403448}, and biochemically non-functioning (silent) corticotroph tumours {10981760; 25216855}. In addition, sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours {16118335; 20688896; 21722151; 23288882; 25129651} and acidophil stem cell tumours are often aggressive and resistant to medical therapies {199135; 6261917; 16995577}. Sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours often manifest with invasive large tumours {30035294; 34135861}.

If complete surgical resection is not possible, the probability of recurrence and time to clinically relevant regrowth correlate with radiological tumour doubling time {24423330}. The predictive value of mitoses (conventional or phosphohistone H3–assisted count), Ki-67 labelling index, and p53 staining in this scenario remains unproven {19687686; 31037500}.

Staging

Persistent disease after surgical resection depends on tumour size and extent of invasion {25732637; 27663264}. Multiple efforts have been made by surgeons and radiologists to define the extension of tumours into adjacent neuroanatomical regions; namely, the Knosp classification defines the degree of lateral extension into the cavernous sinus {8232800} and the Hardy classification outlines the degree of extrasellar and vertical extension into suprasellar regions [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Knosp grade ≥ 3 cavernous sinus invasion predicts a need for repeat operations, radiotherapy, and/or medical therapy {28323946}. Despite efforts to predict cavernous sinus invasion on MRI, the precise confirmation of invasion frequently requires surgical and/or histological examination. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}. There is a need to reach a multidisciplinary consensus on the staging of these tumours, so that predictive and prognostic scores can be developed and applied to individual cases.
