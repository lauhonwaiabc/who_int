6.2.1.3 Adrenal cortical adenoma

Definition

Adrenal cortical adenoma is a benign epithelial neoplasm of adrenal cortical cells.

ICD-O coding

8370/0 Adrenal cortical adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2F37.Y & XH3DV3 Other specified benign neoplasm of endocrine glands & Adenoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: adrenocortical adenoma.

Not recommended: oncocytoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Adrenal cortical adenoma is usually a unilateral and solitary mass arising in the adrenal gland; however, bilateral and multifocal adenomas can occur {33534120}. Ectopic tumours also occur {31467854; 31127594}.

Clinical features

Adrenal cortical adenomas may be hormonally active with associated clinical manifestations, or they may be non-functioning (silent) and may be discovered incidentally on imaging studies performed for other reasons {31672302}.

Primary aldosteronism (Conn syndrome) is primarily a laboratory diagnosis that requires confirmation of the plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio followed by aldosterone suppression testing {26125435; 29594118; 34008000}. It is the most common cause of secondary hypertension {26125435}. Not all affected patients have hypokalaemia. Affected patients may present with headaches, palpitations, weakness, polydipsia, and polyuria with hypertension and hypokalaemia/normokalaemia, and they have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease {26125435}. Bilateral adrenal gland involvement occurs in 60–70% of cases of primary aldosteronism {26125435; 31672302}. Bilateral manifestations are enriched in bilateral diffuse zona glomerulosa hyperplasia and bilateral aldosterone-producing microadenomas (< 10 mm) {33534120; 26610199}. The distinction of bilateral aldosterone excess from unilateral disease (often due to adenoma or microadenoma) is important in deciding on surgical intervention {28420172; 31672302; 26934393}. Adrenal venous sampling is the historical standard in the lateralization of aldosterone excess {26934393}, but the optimal approach is still debated {28420172}. Some patients may have concurrent primary aldosteronism and Cushing syndrome {29069700; 22863749; 29770148}. Mild autonomous cortisol secretion in adrenal cortical adenomas may pose diagnostic challenges, and a subset of these can simulate a non-functioning cortical adenoma {31672302; 29594418; 33258172}. Therefore, dynamic endocrine tests {31672302; 33258172; 25425660} and functional imaging studies (including but not limited to iodocholesterol [NP-59] studies) {33190101; 33596032} are useful in this distinction.

In patients with cortisol-producing adrenal cortical adenomas, clinical features of cortisol excess include central obesity, rounded facies, hirsutism, poor wound healing, skin striae, weight gain, proximal muscle weakness, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis, and susceptibility to infections {25425660}. Growth failure is an important paediatric manifestation {25425660}.

Virilization or feminization is extremely rare in adrenal cortical adenomas {16302588; 9364358; 29310349; 29426273; 30674304}. This diagnosis requires exclusion of an adrenal cortical carcinoma. The diagnosis of adenoma is made with caution in this functional group. These tumours may also cosecrete glucocorticoids, similarly to some adrenal cortical carcinomas {29310349}. Testosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas are more common in children {16302588; 9364358; 29310349; 29426273}, but rare examples have been reported in adults {30674304}. Oncocytic adrenal cortical adenomas may cosecrete testosterone and cortisol {29426273; 29310349}.

Non-functioning adrenal cortical adenomas are often discovered incidentally, with the increased use of imaging studies. Large tumour size, invasiveness, heterogeneity, lipid content, and the rate of washout after intravenous contrast on CT imaging studies are important factors that are considered in the surgical management of a non-functioning adrenal cortical neoplasm {31672302}. For instance, lipid-rich adrenal cortical neoplasms discovered incidentally are less worrisome and may be observed; however, large (> 40 mm) and lipid-poor tumours or those with heterogeneous radiographic features are of greater concern and are therefore subjected to surgery. FDG PET-CT imaging has been suggested to discriminate benign from malignant adrenal lesions, with a maximum standardized uptake value of ≥ 4.6 being suggestive of malignancy. However, lower values are not specific for adenoma {32914407}, and false positive or inconclusive results have been observed in some cases including black adenomas {29499515}.

Epidemiology

Approximately 5% of the general population have one or more adrenal cortical nodule(s) that are usually solitary and unilateral but can be multifocal or bilateral {21618498; 16699294; 20969508}. Adrenal cortical adenomas are the most common adrenal cortical tumour.

Etiology

Genetic factors and environmental factors contribute to the etiology of adrenal cortical neoplasms. Tobacco use has been shown to be associated with an increased risk of benign and malignant adrenal cortical neoplasms {32871402}.

Pathogenesis

Genetic predisposition occurs in a small fraction of adrenal cortical neoplasms {33534120; 31672302}. The past decade has seen significant progress in the understanding of genomic correlates of aldosterone- and cortisol-producing adrenal cortical adenomas and the pathways responsible for the physiological regulation of these hormones, which has facilitated a better understanding of the tumorigenesis of functional adrenal cortical disorders {26125435; 33534120}.

Aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas

Mutations in various genes encoding ion channels are pathognomonic of this disease, because depolarization of the membrane in a zona glomerulosa cell will stimulate aldosterone production irrespective of whether the depolarization event is caused by angiotensin II stimulation or a dysfunctional ion channel {33534120}. The normal regulation of aldosterone production in the zona glomerulosa via the renin–angiotensin system is primarily governed by the membrane potential. In the resting state, K+ channels in the zona glomerulosa cells are open constitutively, thus maintaining a hyperpolarized state in which voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels remain closed. Upon renin–angiotensin activation, K+ channels will close, leading to depolarization and the opening of voltage-dependent Ca2+ channels. The influx of Ca2+ will stimulate the transcription of CYP11B2 (encoding aldosterone synthase) {33534120}. Intriguingly, mutations in genes encoding various ion channels expressed in the zona glomerulosa mimic this physiological response, thus causing cell-cycle activation and aldosterone excess. To date, mutually exclusive somatic mutations in KCNJ5 (encoding a K+ channel) {21311022; 24082052}, ATP1A1 (Na+/K+ channel) {23416519; 23913004; 24082052}, ATP2B3 (Ca2+ channel) {23416519; 24082052}, CACNA1D (Ca2+ channel) {23913004; 23913001; 32844168}, CACNA1H (Ca2+ channel) {25907736; 32844168; 31983310}, and CLCN2 (Cl− channel) {29403011; 29403012; 33033789} have been described for the vast majority of tumours, including reports of mutations in as many as 90% of patients in American series and 96% of patients in Japanese series {30739536; 30085035; 32844168}. Of note, KCNJ5 mutations are found in approximately 40% of aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas, and they seem to be overrepresented in younger female patients with pronounced hormonal activity {26066531}. Additional genotype–phenotype correlates have been proposed, including differences in steroidogenic enzyme expression, cytomorphology, and clinical aspects such as the adrenal vein sampling lateralization index {22442279; 31408156}. Of clinical interest, subsets of patients may also display pathogenic germline mutations in KCNJ5, CACNA1H, CACNA1D, or CLCN2, leading to familial forms of primary aldosteronism with early-onset hypertension {33534120}. In addition to ion-channel aberrancies, subsets of aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas display activating somatic mutations in CTNNB1 (encoding the WNT pathway effector β-catenin), thus reinforcing the importance of this signalling network also in benign adrenal cortical tumours {28102204; 26815163}. These patients are often female, with a delayed age of disease onset.

Cortisol-producing adrenal cortical adenomas

Mutations are congregated in genes regulating the PKA pathway {33534120}. In the normal physiological regulation of cortisol secretion from the zona fasciculata, ACTH binds MC2R, in turn activating adenylyl cyclase, which triggers the conversion of ATP to cAMP. When cAMP levels rise, the regulatory subunits of PKA are inhibited, thereby freeing catalytic subunits to enter the nucleus, in turn leading to the transcription of target genes stimulating cell growth and cortisol production. Therefore, cortisol-producing tumours often display mutations or deletions in genes regulating this process, including PRKACA (the most common alteration, identified in ~40% of cases), PRKAR1A, GNAS, or PRKACB {24855271; 25750087; 24571724; 24747643; 25057884; 14500362; 29669941}. The discovery of the rare AKAP13::PDE8A fusion also expands the spectrum of cortisol-producing adrenal cortical adenomas {32875319}. Moreover, activating CTNNB1 mutations can occur in a subset of cortisol-producing adenomas {25750087; 24747643}. Cortisol-producing adenomas with PRKACA and PRKAR1A aberrancies are often associated with smaller tumours and overt Cushing syndrome, whereas CTNNB1-mutated cortisol-producing adenomas are associated with mild autonomous cortisol secretion {29594118; 24855271; 33151900}.

The observation of somatic KCNJ5 mutations in cortisol- and aldosterone-producing adenomas has been recorded {22863749; 29770148}. Similarly, the identification of germline ARMC5 variants in KCNJ5-mutant sporadic aldosterone-producing adenomas {25822102; 26446392} and germline PDE2A and PDE3B variants in patients with primary aldosteronism {33112806} challenge the occurrence of a potential crosslink between functional pathways. Nevertheless, current observations are that PKA-driven (overt adrenal Cushing syndrome) and WNT pathway (non-functioning adenomas or adenomas causing mild autonomous cortisol secretion) dysregulated tumours are often genetically and clinically distinct. These observations have also been reproduced using a pangenomic classifying algorithm, in which PKA-aberrant tumours also clustered separately from CTNNB1-driven lesions in terms of global mRNA profiles {33151900}. In terms of molecular backgrounds, the bulk of evidence argues in favour of distinct chromosome, mutation, expression, and epigenetic patterns in adrenal cortical adenomas versus carcinomas {33534120}. Therefore, future multiomics-based algorithms might be of potential clinical utility in separating these entities.

Non-functioning adrenal cortical adenomas

Evidence suggests that most non-functioning adrenal cortical adenomas, especially larger ones, are enriched in CTNNB1 mutations {33151900; 29594118; 24855271}.

Sex steroid–producing adrenal cortical adenomas

The molecular pathogenesis of exceptional cases of sex steroid–producing adrenal cortical adenomas is unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

Adrenal cortical adenomas, including non-functioning adenomas, are more commonly unilateral and solitary; however, bilateral multiple tumours occur. Adrenal cortical adenomas are usually solitary, well-circumscribed tumours, measuring ≤ 50 mm and weighing less than 50–100 g {22840871; 17504308}. Aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas usually have a yellow-gold colour on gross examination due to the prominent lipid-rich zona fasciculata–type clear cells {31672302}. Aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas usually measure ≥ 10 mm, an arbitrary size cut-off point that has been used in the distinction of adenoma from sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease {31672302}. Functioning adenomas can be < 10 mm, especially in patients with primary aldosteronism {32717746}. Cortisol-producing adrenal cortical adenomas are usually solitary, unilateral, and tan in colour, with atrophy (≤ 2 mm cortical thickness) due to suppression by glucocorticoid excess {31672302} [[Pakbaz S, Mete O. Adrenal cortical neoplasia: from histology to molecular biology. Diagn Histopathol. 2019 May;25(5):178–89. doi:10.1016/j.mpdhp.2019.02.004.]]. Some adenomas are pigmented (black adenoma) {31672302}. Adrenal cortical adenomas with extensive oncocytic change appear orange-brown on cut section {31672302}.

Histopathology

Adrenal cortical adenomas are composed of lipid-rich cells resembling the zona fasciculata and compact lipid-poor cells resembling the zona reticularis. Adrenal cortical adenomas are circumscribed and cellular with abundant vasculature. The cells are organized in nests or cords. Reticulin histochemistry often shows a preserved framework {28877067}. The tumours can have variable amounts of large oncocytic cells with intensely eosinophilic cytoplasm, with some nearly completely oncocytic, which can be functional but are often non-functional {19462261}. Mitotic activity is absent or low, and atypical mitotic figures are generally not seen. Histological features characteristic of malignancy, such as necrosis, high nuclear grade, prominent mitotic activity, atypical mitoses, angioinvasion (venous invasion), lymphatic invasion, and invasive growth (see section Adrenal cortical carcinoma), are not seen. Myelolipomatous and lipomatous change can be seen in adrenal cortical adenomas and is rather frequent in cortisol-producing adenomas, thought to occur as a result of cell senescence by cortisol excess {29596893}, without clinical significance. In addition to prominent oncocytic change, myxoid change can be seen in adrenal cortical adenomas {10716153}. Myxoid change is a concerning feature {10716153}, and the behaviour of adrenal cortical neoplasms with myxoid change can be difficult to predict with Weiss classification systems {20534995}. Pigmented (black) adenomas are composed predominantly or entirely of cells with compact eosinophilic cytoplasm that are similar to, but larger than, cells of the zona reticularis. There is a variable amount of brown or golden-brown pigment (lipofuscin) in the cytoplasm {31672302}.

Aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas are composed of lipid-rich zona fasciculata–type clear cells or compact zona reticularis–like and/or zona glomerulosa–like cells {29594118; 33534120}. Oncocytic change can be seen in aldosterone-producing adenomas, although oncocytic adrenal cortical adenomas are usually non-functioning {19462261}. Spironolactone bodies with concentric laminated concretions are often seen in the zona glomerulosa of aldosterone-producing adenomas in individuals treated with spironolactone {5504616; 26125435; 31672302}. Confirming the source of aldosterone excess in adrenalectomy specimens can be complicated. Aldosterone-producing cortical lesions can be unifocal or multifocal and unilateral or bilateral, and they may include microscopic clonal nodular proliferations {33534120}. Not detected on imaging studies, aldosterone-producing microtumours may be difficult to identify. Therefore, CYP11B2 immunohistochemistry is helpful in the evaluation of the adrenal gland(s) in individuals with primary aldosteronism to identify functional sites of aldosterone production {32717746; 26125435; 33534120; 29594118; 29542002}. CYP11B2 encodes aldosterone synthase, and ion-channel mutations result in depolarization and subsequent CYP11B2 transcription {33534120; 29594118; 26125435; 29542002; 32717746}. The recent histopathology of primary aldosteronism (HISTALDO) consensus classification combines the histological evaluation of an adrenal gland with the CYP11B2 staining pattern to identify adrenal nodules and standardize nomenclature to provide a clinically relevant diagnostic approach to reporting unilateral primary aldosteronism specimens {32717746}. For instance, a solitary CYP11B2-expressing adenoma or nodule is referred to as classic histology, whereas CYP11B2-positive diffuse hyperplasia and multifocal CYP11B2-positive (micro)nodules are regarded as non-classic histology {32717746}. Lack of postoperative biochemical improvement was reported in 42% of patients with non-classic histology, compared with 4.5% of patients with classic histology {32717746}. The histological and clinical features of aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical adenomas may correlate with underlying somatic mutations {33534120; 29594118}. Adenomas with wildtype KCNJ5 are more frequent in male patients and are often small, multifocal, and composed of compact cells, whereas those with KCNJ5 somatic mutations are more frequent in female patients, usually larger and solitary, and are composed of lipid-rich zona fasciculata–type cells {33534120; 29594118}.

Cortisol-secreting adrenal cortical adenomas are usually solitary, unilateral neoplasms composed of lipid-rich zona fasciculata–like clear cells, but they can have variable amounts of compact cells, oncocytic change, or pigment {26125435; 29594118; 31672303}. It is important to recognize the adjacent adrenal cortical atrophy, indicative of autonomous cortisol secretion or exogenous glucocorticoid treatment {22320848}.

Differential diagnosis and immunohistochemistry

The main differential diagnosis of adrenal cortical adenomas is with adrenal cortical carcinoma (see section Adrenal cortical carcinoma) and phaeochromocytoma. In the presence of predominant compact or oncocytic cells, the differential diagnosis also includes perivascular epithelioid cell tumours (PEComas; angiomyolipomas) {21526965; 32256603}.

The standard diagnostic classification for distinguishing adrenal cortical adenoma from carcinoma is the Weiss system {6703192; 2919718}, with the Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia system {15306935} used for neoplasms with > 90% oncocytic cells, and a separate US Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) system {12826878} used for paediatric adrenal cortical neoplasms. Additional classification systems include the reticulin algorithm {23774167; 19912359} and the Helsinki score {25582500; 27916625} as discussed in section Adrenal cortical carcinoma . Myxoid adrenal cortical neoplasms may be very difficult to classify with the standard Weiss system, and so the possibility of malignant behaviour is difficult to exclude in these neoplasms {20534995}.

SF1 is the most reliable biomarker for confirming the cortical origin of an adrenal neoplasm {29542002}. Several less specific biomarkers (e.g. melan-A, synaptophysin, α-inhibin, calretinin) may be used in a panel approach, but staining for these markers is also variable {29542002}. Synaptophysin {29542002} and α-inhibin {33826547} can be expressed in phaeochromocytomas. Unlike cortical neoplasms, phaeochromocytomas are positive for INSM1 and/or chromogranin A.

When PEComas/angiomyolipomas are considered, melan-A positivity is often misleading and should be integrated with other PEComa-specific markers, such as HMB45 and SMA {21526965}. Clear cell tumours, notably renal cell carcinoma, may metastasize to the adrenal and simulate cytological and architectural features of adrenal cortical adenoma. Immunohistochemistry should be interpreted with caution, because the nonspecific renal cell carcinoma marker CD10 may be positive in adrenal cortical adenomas {20390424}, and Xp11-translocation renal cell carcinomas can also express melan-A {28288037}.

Functional steroidogenic enzyme immunohistochemistry is useful in the assessment of adrenal cortical neoplasms {29542002; 31672302}; among these, CYP11B2 immunostaining has shown a particular benefit in identifying functional sites in primary aldosteronism {32717746; 23436930; 24837548; 29542002; 23443813; 30654730}.

Cytology

Smears show small sheets and tissue fragments that may include thin fibrovascular strands, and predominantly single uniform polygonal cells with delicate, granular, or vacuolated cytoplasm; uniform, round nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli; and occasional variations in cell size and enlarged pleomorphic nuclei consistent with endocrine organ atypia. The cells are fragile, and naked nuclei and lipid vacuoles are prominent in the background. Adrenal gland hyperplasia / benign cortical nodule and adrenal adenoma cannot be distinguished on FNA cytopathology {15376200}. The role of FNA in adrenal incidentalomas is contentious {32856984}, but FNA (including EUS-FNAB) offers a minimally invasive biopsy of adrenal lesions found in the workup of patients with possible metastatic carcinomas, including lung and pancreas primaries, and can provide material for molecular studies {6587703; 15376200; 32988789; 27257146}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline testing for pathogenic variants is considered in the context of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an adrenal cortical neoplasm without morphological features of malignancy; non-tumorous cortical atrophy is diagnostic of autonomous cortisol secretion in the absence of exogenous cortisol intake; immunohistochemical confirmation of adrenal cortical origin in all non-functioning adenomas with oncocytic or compact cell changes.

Desirable: CYP11B2 immunohistochemistry in patients with primary aldosteronism.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Adrenal cortical adenomas are benign and associated with an excellent prognosis. The evaluation of aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical neoplasms can be more complicated, and CYP11B2 immunostaining is helpful in identifying functional sites in primary aldosteronism and predicting possible biochemical recurrence. Uncontrolled Cushing syndrome is associated with significant mortality and morbidity.
