3.2.1.0 Thyroid follicular nodular disease

Definition

Thyroid follicular nodular disease is a multifocal non-inflammatory benign proliferation of thyroid follicular cells that results in multiple clonal and non-clonal nodules with highly variable architecture. Multinodular goitre (MNG) is a clinical term referring to an enlarged thyroid gland with multiple nodules caused by follicular nodular disease (FND).

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

5A01.2 Non-toxic multinodular goitre

Related terminology

Acceptable: nodular follicular disease; follicular nodular disease; adenomatous nodules; nodular hyperplasia; adenomatous hyperplasia; multinodular thyroid hyperplasia; multinodular goitre (clinical).

Not recommended: goitre; adenomatoid hyperplasia; diffuse goitre; colloid nodule/goitre.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

MNG can cause bilateral or unilateral enlargement of the thyroid gland. In some cases, enlarging thyroid nodules may extend into the superior mediastinum, known as retrosternal goitre {7475073}. Nodular hyperplasia has also been reported in thyroid tissue in ectopic locations, including in association with thyroglossal duct cysts, lingual thyroid tissue, and struma ovarii {16613822; 12588812; 26392367; 20172470; 10339866}.

Clinical features

MNG has variable signs and symptoms, ranging from being asymptomatic to producing compressive symptoms (upper airway compression, respiratory insufficiency, dysphagia, odynophagia, hoarseness). Retrosternal goitre can lead to stridor and superior vena cava syndrome. Rapid growth in a thyroid nodule can be due to the following: haemorrhage, infection, and malignancy (anaplastic carcinoma, lymphoma) {26392367; 21543434; 29996756; 30996065}.

The evaluation of thyroid nodules involves clinical history, physical examination, associated malignancy risk assessment, thyroid function tests, ultrasound evaluation, and (if needed) FNAB {27840362; 29996756; 30561068; 33040844}. Ultrasound evaluation of MNG has proved helpful in selecting thyroid nodules for FNA on the basis of specific features such as echogenicity, shape, microcalcifications (echogenic foci), and extrathyroidal extension {28114862; 30070413}. Various radiological societies have devised ultrasound risk-assessment schemes to help triage thyroid nodules for FNAB {33112199; 32396776; 33330093}. Most hyperplastic nodules on ultrasound will show mixed cystic and solid features or a spongiform appearance {28114862}.

Most patients with MNG are euthyroid; TSH screening is essential to exclude the possibility of toxic MNG. Radionuclide scanning is usually not performed in patients with MNG; however, it can be helpful in identifying hypofunctioning nodules for FNA in patients with subclinical hyperthyroidism or toxic MNG {33040844}.

The majority of asymptomatic cases of MNG classified as benign by ultrasound and FNA can be managed clinically; however, surgery may be indicated for cases causing symptoms {28114862; 30070413}.

Epidemiology

MNG has a female predilection (M:F ratio: 1:6–15). The incidence increases with age and is highest in individuals aged > 65 years {12588812; 26392367; 20172470; 10339866}. MNG is commonly encountered throughout the world; however, it is more prevalent in iodine-deficient regions (endemic MNG) than in non–iodine-deficient regions (sporadic MNG).

Etiology

Iodine deficiency is a well-known etiological factor for MNG, especially the endemic type. However, it is understood that iodine deficiency is not the sole etiological factor for the development of MNG {11507648; 11289736; 22473332}. It is probably a multifactorial process that involves an inherent predisposition (susceptibility to developing thyroid nodules, familial euthyroid goitre) and environmental factors {11024220}. Thus, risk factors for MNG include age; sex; smoking; iatrogenic and dietary goitrogens; dietary deficiency in selenium, iron, and vitamin A; iodine excess; estrogen; and genetic predisposition {23737781; 24144365; 29633305; 31542786; 32001830; 32633237; 33080613}. Inborn errors of thyroid metabolism consist of a spectrum of inherited biochemical abnormalities that can affect any step in the pathway of thyroid hormone synthesis, secretion, or action. These can lead to hypothyroidism and MNG, known as dyshormonogenetic goitre {12114713; 12114789; 15929313}.

Pathogenesis

Follicular nodules in MNG are caused by the proliferation of multiple cells in response to growth factors and cytokines. However, clonal nodules do occur in the background of MNG and represent true neoplasms {11750859; 9921946; 12843177; 15615818; 7551291}. It has been shown that the genes that play an important role in the thyroid hormone pathway (TG, TPO, SLC5A5 [NIS], DUOX2, AFAP1L2 [XB130], and TSHR) are the most likely candidates in the pathogenesis of MNG {30319546; 29112772; 23214057; 15611822; 31301652; 34915750}. Other loci involved in the pathogenesis of MNG are the multinodular goitre 1 (MNG) locus on chromosome 14 and loci on chromosomes Xp22 (MNG2), 2q, 3p, 3q (MNG3), 7q, and 8p. Molecular analyses of multigenerational families with MNG inherited in an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern have identified genetic variants (RGS12, GRPEL1, CLIC6, and WFS1) in familial goitre {31301652; 16054835; 12135533; 14564411; 15292347}. Additional studies have also identified somatic alterations in SPOP, ZNF148, and EZH1 in about 25% of nodules of MNG {28580939}.

Functioning or toxic MNG often consists of multiple clonal proliferations due to TSHR and GNAS {20336923; 21773967}, PRKAR1A (typically in the setting of Carney complex), and EZH1 mutations {27500488} (see section Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture). Familial and early-onset FND can also be associated with DICER1 syndrome {28323992}.

Macroscopic appearance

MNG glands can be slightly to massively enlarged, with reported weights ranging from 50 g to > 2000 g. The external surface can be vaguely to prominently nodular. Some nodules may be barely attached to the gland or totally separate from the gland in the surrounding soft tissue (known as parasitic nodules). The cut surface displays nodules with a range of sizes and shapes that show variable degrees of encapsulation, cystic change, fibrosis, calcification, and haemorrhage.

Histopathology

The characteristic histopathological finding in MNG is thyroid FND. The nodules usually show an admixture of large and small follicles, often with papillary formations. A dominant follicular or papillary growth pattern can also be present (see also the sections on follicular adenoma). The epithelium lining the follicles and papillae can range from tall columnar to low cuboidal. Nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma are absent. Large follicles lined by flattened epithelium and distended with colloid often merge to form large cystic spaces filled with thin colloid, known as colloid nodules. Encapsulated cellular nodules with either a follicular or papillary growth pattern morphologically appear similar to follicular adenoma and have therefore been termed adenomatous nodules or adenomatoid nodules. It is common to encounter foci of nodular hyperplasia intermixed with foci of haemorrhage (either due to degenerative changes or related to preoperative FNA), haemosiderin-laden macrophages, fibrosis, calcifications, and even ossification.

Cytology

Smears show usually abundant thin colloid and a variable cellularity consisting of follicular monolayer sheets and larger tissue fragments consisting of macrofollicles, sometimes containing colloid, separated by thin fibrovascular strands. Intact follicles and a variable number of stripped follicular nuclei can be seen. Cystic degeneration adds macrophages, haemosiderophages, and other inflammatory cells, and these may be the prominent feature with scant follicular epithelium in cyst aspirates. Follicular nuclei in the sheets are evenly spaced and round, with fine, even chromatin and small nucleoli in some cells, although enlarged round nuclei can be scattered through the sheets. Colloid follicular nodules show abundant thin colloid with scant follicular epithelium. Hyperplastic follicular nodules are usually highly cellular with follicular and sometimes papillary tissue fragments, and exuded colloid fire flares ring the epithelium. However, the cells exhibit bland nuclei. FNAB is an accurate diagnostic test for distinguishing benign follicular and colloid nodules from thyroid carcinomas {27840362; 29996756; 30561068; 33040844}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis of FNA specimens of thyroid nodules has proved to be useful in differentiating between hyperplastic nodules and neoplasms {28534687; 29336606}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: follicular cell proliferations lacking invasive growth and nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Desirable: an admixture of follicular and papillary growth patterns along with varying degrees of degenerative and reparative changes.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Benign follicular nodules do not metastasize and do not recur when completely excised. A recurrence rate of 30% has been reported in patients undergoing subtotal thyroidectomy {10828797; 18958517; 25594634}.
