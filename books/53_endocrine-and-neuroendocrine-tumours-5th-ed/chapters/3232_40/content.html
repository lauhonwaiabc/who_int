3.2.3.2 Invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma

Definition

Invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma (IEFVPTC) is a malignant well-differentiated follicular cell–derived neoplasm that is encapsulated and has an exclusive or almost exclusive follicular architecture, nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), and invasive growth.

ICD-O coding

8340/3 Invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma
8340/3 Minimally invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary carcinoma (capsular invasion only)
8340/3 Encapsulated angioinvasive follicular variant of papillary carcinoma
8340/3 Widely invasive follicular variant of papillary carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2D10.1 & XH0Q59 Papillary carcinoma of thyroid gland & Papillary carcinoma, encapsulated, of thyroid

Related terminology

Acceptable: invasive encapsulated follicular subtype of papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Minimally invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma (capsular invasion only); encapsulated angioinvasive follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma; widely invasive follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma

Localization

IEFVPTC can occur anywhere in the thyroid and in ectopic locations, including in association with thyroglossal duct cysts and struma ovarii {17721188; 19696610; 25577275; 26374222}.

Clinical features

IEFVPTC occurs predominantly in adults, with a median age of 40–50 years at diagnosis and an M:F ratio of < 1:3 {16900519; 23025507; 29343212}. Approximately 15% of PTCs in children represent IEFVPTC {20373982; 26131690; 26854950; 29438531}. Most patients present with a painless thyroid mass. Regional lymph node enlargement at presentation, a finding often observed with classic PTC or infiltrative follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma (FVPTC), is not seen with IEFVPTC {16900519; 23025507}. Distant metastases are present at diagnosis in as many as approximately 5% of patients {19191744}. The ultrasound characteristics of IEFVPTC overlap with those of non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (NIFTP): both tumours are typically solitary and round to ovoid, with smooth margins, variable echogenicity, and no calcifications {28699414; 32111537}. The invasive nature of a subset of IEFVPTC may be apparent on ultrasound {28332339}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of IEFVPTC is difficult to establish, because most population-based studies rely on data that predate the introduction of the name NIFTP or do not differentiate IEFVPTC from infiltrative FVPTC. In the USA, FVPTC overall has been reported to account for approximately a quarter of PTCs according to SEER data, with the incidence dropping after the introduction of the NIFTP terminology {32098591}. In a study evaluating tumours diagnosed in Japan, the Republic of Korea, China (including Taiwan, China), Thailand, and Viet Nam, IEFVPTC was reported to account for 1% of PTCs {29476382}.

Etiology

Although the vast majority of cases of IEFVPTC are sporadic, FVPTC has been reported in association with tumour syndromes including PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome, DICER1 syndrome, and Carney complex {3371825; 21190448; 21956414; 24617712; 29635258}. The finding of IEFVPTC in a background of multiple adenomatous nodules, adenomas, and nodular hyperplasia raises the possibility of an underlying hereditary syndrome, especially if the tumour occurs in childhood.

Pathogenesis

IEFVPTC has a molecular profile similar to that of follicular adenoma and follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC). RAS point mutations, the most frequent somatic alteration detected in encapsulated FVPTC (EFVPTC), are present in as many as two thirds of cases {20526288; 28940583}. Additionally, PAX8::PPARG rearrangements, although less frequent than RAS mutations, are also characteristic of EFVPTC {16219715; 24798894}. In contrast to classic PTC and infiltrative FVPTC, BRAF p.V600E mutations are infrequent in strictly defined IEFVPTC {20526288; 28940583; 31452453}. A small subset of IEFVPTC cases have a BRAF p.K601E mutation {28940583}, an EIF1AX mutation {26911375}, a TSHR mutation {23979959}, or a THADA::IGF2BP3 fusion {33487086}. TERT promoter mutations are uncommon in IEFVPTC, occurring in < 5% of tumours {28975450}.

Macroscopic appearance

Although IEFVPTC can be large (> 40 mm), the mean size of EFVPTC is 20–30 mm and most are < 40 mm {16900519; 23025507; 28332339; 29343212}. IEFVPTC tends to be larger than infiltrative FVPTC {28332339; 28699414; 29343212}. The capsule of IEFVPTC can vary in thickness, although it has been reported to be thicker than that of NIFTP {1373582; 21455197; 23025507; 31256687}. Gross evidence of invasion in IEFVPTC is rare. A diagnosis of widely invasive FVPTC is based on tumour grossly invading through the thyroid parenchyma.

Histopathology

A diagnosis of IEFVPTC requires capsular and/or vascular invasion. IEFVPTC is subclassified into three groups: (1) minimally invasive (capsular invasion only), (2) encapsulated angioinvasive (venous invasion present with or without capsular penetration), and (3) widely invasive. It is recognized that there are issues with interobserver reproducibility for IEFVPTC that reflect different definitions of capsular and vascular invasion, as well as different thresholds for assessment of nuclear features of PTC {11169933; 16798933; 18854266; 21804527; 22360502; 32236857; 33261309}.

To render a diagnosis of minimally invasive IEFVPTC, most authors require full-thickness capsular penetration. For cases with foci of invasion into but not through the capsule, serial/deeper sections should be considered to evaluate for complete capsular transgression in deeper levels. In a subset of cases, tumour is present discontinuously on the outside of the tumour capsule. For such foci to be considered evidence of invasion, they must have the same cytomorphology as that seen in the main mass.

Most authors define vascular invasion as the invasion of vessels within the tumour capsule or beyond, with intravascular tumour attached to the vessel wall, or admixed with fibrin or covered by endothelium. Foci of tumour that only push up against a vessel should not be considered vascular invasion. Additionally, foci resembling vascular invasion that are located within the tumour or tumour floating within a vessel lumen (without associated endothelium) are not diagnostic of vascular invasion. Because the extent of vascular (i.e. venous) invasion is likely to be prognostically significant, as has been demonstrated for FTC {15022277; 23327839; 26482605}, foci of vascular invasion should be counted (with foci in adjacent vessels counted separately). ERG or CD31 immunohistochemistry may be considered, to highlight vascular endothelial cells to aid in the evaluation of vascular invasion {20205756; 29744727}.

IEFVPTC and FTC are both encapsulated tumours with a follicular architecture and invasive growth; therefore, the distinction between entities is based on the presence of nuclear features of PTC in IEFVPTC. Compared with the adjacent non-neoplastic follicular cells, the nuclei of FVPTC are enlarged, elongated, crowded, and overlapping. Additionally, IEFVPTC has nuclear contour irregularities and longitudinal grooves; variable degrees of nuclear clearing with margination of chromatin to the nuclear membrane; and small, peripherally located nucleoli. The nuclear features of IEFVPTC tend to be more subtle than those seen with infiltrative FVPTC {16900519}. Nuclear pseudoinclusions are uncommon in IEFVPTC. PTC nuclear features are often variable throughout the tumour. In general, nuclear features tend to be more pronounced at the periphery of the nodule or in the areas of invasive growth. In some cases, IEFVPTC has scattered foci of follicular cells with well-developed nuclear features of PTC distributed throughout a tumour with subtle to essentially absent nuclear atypia – a phenomenon described as the sprinkling sign of FVPTC {24283853}. Bone metastases of IEFVPTC may lack nuclear features of PTC {10955452}.

IEFVPTC has microfollicular, normofollicular, or macrofollicular growth. Solid or trabecular growth can occur (alone or in combination with follicular-patterned growth); however, it is important that in tumours showing these growth patterns, a diagnosis of poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma is excluded. In areas of solid or trabecular growth, nuclear features of PTC should be maintained and the mitotic count should be < 3 mitoses/2 mm2. Tumour necrosis should not be seen in IEFVPTC. Some cases of IEFVPTC have deeply eosinophilic colloid. True papillae (i.e. with fibrovascular cores and lined by PTC nuclei) can be found occasionally but should be limited to < 1% of the tumour. Extensive intratumoural fibrosis, a finding that has been associated with distant metastases at diagnosis, can variably obscure evidence of invasion {26482605}.

Immunohistochemistry

IEFVPTC is positive for CAM5.2, EMA, CK7, PAX8, thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), and thyroglobulin. Ki-67 immunohistochemistry may be helpful when evaluating for progression to poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, because the Ki-67 index is < 5% in the majority of IEFVPTC cases but > 5% in most poorly differentiated thyroid carcinomas {20562850; 17188136}.

Differential diagnosis

NIFTP is differentiated from IEFVPTC by the absence of capsular and vascular invasion. Therefore, tumours fulfilling all the criteria for NIFTP except capsular/vascular invasion are labelled as IEFVPTC. The latter is separated from FTC by the presence of PTC nuclear features.

Cytology

On FNA, IEFVPTC is characterized by a microfollicular architecture along with variably prominent nuclear features of PTC that include nuclear enlargement, crowding, contour irregularities, longitudinal grooves, and chromatin clearing {28192645; 29343212}. Because nuclear features of IEFVPTC are variably present throughout the tumour, the nuclear atypia of IEFVPTC may not always be apparent on FNA. Thus, there is overlap in the cytology of IEFVPTC and tumours such as follicular adenoma and FTC. In contrast to classic PTC, IEFVPTC lacks papillae and psammomatous calcifications. Additionally, the nuclear features of IEFVPTC are generally more subtle than those of classic PTC (and infiltrative FVPTC), with pseudoinclusions rare to absent. IEFVPTC cannot be distinguished from NIFTP on cytology because the distinction is based on the demonstration of invasion {27564464; 28192645; 29091573}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In preoperative fine-needle biopsies, the presence of RAS and BRAF p.K601E mutations as well as PAX8::PPARG rearrangements raises the possibility of IEFVPTC {24798894; 16219715; 28940583}. However, these genetic alterations are not specific, being encountered in follicular adenoma, FTC, and NIFTP {27078145}. There is currently no role for molecular pathology in the diagnosis of IEFVPTC in the postoperative setting.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Minimally invasive and angioinvasive EFVPTC

Essential: encapsulated tumours with an exclusive or almost exclusive follicular architecture, nuclear features of PTC, and capsular and/or vascular invasion.

Widely invasive FVPTC

Essential: grossly apparent invasive growth; histologically characterized by an exclusive or almost exclusive follicular architecture, nuclear features of PTC, and (typically) extensive vascular invasion.

Staging

IEFVPTCs are staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems, incorporating age at diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

Like FTC, IEFVPTC spreads to distant sites, often bypassing lymph nodes {25721865}. Most patients with IEFVPTC that pursues an adverse clinical course present with distant metastases {25721865}. Minimally invasive EFVPTC is an extremely indolent tumour if distant metastasis is absent at presentation {26482605}. Widely invasive FVPTC and EFVPTC with extensive vascular invasion are at higher risk of distant metastasis and pursuing an aggressive clinical course than are minimally invasive EFVPTCs or encapsulated tumours with only focal vascular invasion (< 4 foci).
