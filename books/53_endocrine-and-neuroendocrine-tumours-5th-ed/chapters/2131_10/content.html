2.1.3.1 Gonadotroph PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Gonadotroph pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is a well-differentiated PitNET composed of SF1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with gonadotroph differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8272/3 Gonadotroph PitNET/adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: gonadotroph tumour.

Not recommended: gonadotropin-positive PitNET/adenoma; gonadotropin-producing PitNET/adenoma; gonadotroph cell PitNET/adenoma; gonadotrope PitNET/adenoma; gonadotropinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The majority of gonadotroph tumours are intrasellar masses with variable suprasellar and parasellar extension, including the cavernous sinus. Ectopic gonadotroph tumours are extremely uncommon, with only rare reports of cases occurring in the sphenoidal sinus, suprasellar region, third ventricle, and nasopharynx {961385; 12611618; 22430769; 2294174; 26037197}.

Clinical features

Unlike other PitNETs/adenomas of well-differentiated cell lineage, most gonadotroph tumours are clinically non-functioning (lacking hormone overproduction) and are therefore referred to by some as “silent gonadotroph tumours/adenomas”. Generally, these tumours come to medical attention with symptoms related to mass effects, such as visual disturbances, headaches, and hypopituitarism. Hypogonadism, including diminished libido and/or impotence, is present in most male patients {8656706}. Among premenopausal women, menstrual disturbances and/or galactorrhoea may be seen, whereas in postmenopausal women clinical manifestations are related to mass effects. Some cases may be diagnosed incidentally through imaging studies performed for other purposes {25800168; 30020466}. In rare cases, pituitary apoplexy can be the presenting symptom {16776337}.

Clinically functioning gonadotroph tumours are rare and diagnosed in patients with elevated levels of FSH/LH or non-suppressed FSH/LH in the presence of elevated gonadal steroids {26692108; 30825998}. Functioning tumours are more frequently identified in premenopausal women with a myriad of clinical symptoms, including menstrual irregularities, infertility, galactorrhoea, polycystic ovarian syndrome, and ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (e.g. abdominal and pelvic pain, bloating, and multiple ovarian cysts) {25166722; 26692108; 30825998}. Functioning tumours in postmenopausal women have been rarely reported, but the clinical presentation is similar to that of clinically non-functioning tumours and related to mass effect {25166722}. Likewise, tumours in men may have no symptoms specific to hormonal excess {26692108}; however, testicular hypertrophy and sexual dysfunction have been reported {2416559; 2627747; 21196695; 25166722}. Only rare cases have been reported in children with presentation of precocious puberty {25166722}.

Biochemical investigations

Preoperative hypersecretion of FSH and LH (defined as a > 2-fold increase above the upper limit of normal) may be observed in men but normally is not seen in premenopausal or postmenopausal women {8656706}. Mild hyperprolactinaemia (usually of < 100 ng/mL) is often found, due to pituitary stalk deviation {8656706; 9174695}.

Neuroimaging

Gonadotroph tumours are mostly large masses (mean size: 28 mm) {33128255}), often with suprasellar extension and compression of the optic pathways. Radiographic invasiveness (Knosp grade 3 or 4) has been noted in 13.2–56.4% of cases in recent series with updated pathological diagnosis {31401793; 32326042; 33128255}.

Epidemiology

Before the 2017 WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs, the incidence and prevalence rates of gonadotroph tumours were difficult to estimate, because of the imprecision in distinguishing these tumours from null cell adenomas. Recent pathological series classifying pituitary tumours according to the updated WHO classification have demonstrated that gonadotroph tumours represent about 31–49.5% of surgically removed tumours {28651368; 29434339; 33690870} and 70–75% of non-functioning pituitary tumours {26481628; 28911153; 30020466; 33690870}.

Gonadotroph tumours usually occur in the sixth to eighth decade of life {28911153; 29434339; 32326042; 33128255}, with a slight male preponderance {28911153; 32326042; 33128255}. These tumours are uncommon in paediatric patients (aged ≤ 20 years) {7731505; 15749410; 29404245; 29434339; 32333187}.

Etiology

The etiology for most cases is unknown, although rare case reports have suggested that some may be secondary to longstanding primary hypogonadism {7362277; 2447114}.

Pathogenesis

A small proportion of gonadotroph tumours arise in patients with syndromic diseases, including familial isolated pituitary adenoma (FIPA) {27742789; 33524974}. They account for about 20% of surgically resected tumours in the setting of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 {18300794}, and less commonly in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 {28824003}, SDH-related syndrome {30273935}, and AIP-associated FIPA {26186299}.

Sequencing studies of sporadic gonadotroph tumours showed a lower frequency of somatic mutation rates than in other pituitary tumour types. Somatic mutations involving genes associated with hereditary pituitary tumours (e.g. MEN1, CDKN1B, AIP, and PRKAR1A) are rarely observed in sporadic clinically non-functioning pituitary tumours, including gonadotroph tumours {32741481; 33524974}.

Genetic events other than recurring somatic mutations, including epigenetic changes, DNA methylation alterations, abnormal microRNAs, and copy-number variations, may play a role in the pathogenesis of most sporadic gonadotroph tumours {23450047; 26125663; 27707790; 29410024; 31731486; 31883967}. Still, sporadic non-functioning tumours including gonadotroph tumours are considered genomically quiet tumours, with rare copy-number gains or losses {27707790; 31883967}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gonadotroph tumours are usually large tumours (> 10 mm) with substantial suprasellar or infrasellar extension associated with sellar enlargement or destruction. They are usually soft, tan to dark-brown tumours. Tumours with apoplexy contain areas of necrosis and/or haemorrhage.

Histopathology

Gonadotroph tumours are mostly chromophobic or oncocytic and contain only scant, peripherally situated PAS-positive granules. Unlike normal gonadotrophs, tumour cells are polygonal to elongate with marked cell polarity. Although diffuse and sinusoidal patterns of the tumour cells may be seen, the distinctive trabecular pattern, perivascular pseudorosettes, and even papillary arrangement are consistent and prominent histological features {32370437}. Thus, histological neuroendocrine patterns are often seen in gonadotroph tumours.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical stains for gonadotropins show variable reactivity for FSH-β, LH-β, or α-subunit. FSH-β alone or combined with LH-β and/or α-subunit is the most common immunohistochemical type {29434339; 32326042}. Tumours positive for only LH-β are rare {29434339; 32326042}.

Immunohistochemistry for the transcription factors SF1, ERα, ERβ, and GATA3 is an additional tool for the characterization of these tumours, particularly in cases in which pituitary hormone immunostaining is faint/uncertain and especially for tumours that lack expression of pituitary hormones. PitNETs that lack expression of gonadotropins but express SF1 represent about 21% of all gonadotroph tumours {26481628}. In a large survey of gonadotroph tumours, SF1 and ERα expression was identified in 96% and 82% of these tumours, respectively {29434339}. SF1 nuclear labelling is largely strong and diffuse in tumour cells but may be slightly weak or even absent in oncocytic tumour cells {33128255}. GATA3 has been reported in the majority (89–98.5%) of gonadotroph tumours {30390035; 32193825; 33128255}.

Gonadotroph tumours express SSTR2 {22419713; 24182563; 32326042} and SSTR5 {22419713} in a variable percentage of the reported cases.

Mitotic count is relatively low in the majority of gonadotroph tumours {32326042}. Ki-67 labelling indexes vary in the literature, with 40.2–85.7% of cases reported as having labelling indexes of < 3% {29434339; 32326042}.

Differential diagnosis

Other clinically non-functioning tumours, in particular null cell and hormone-negative corticotroph tumours, should be distinguished from gonadotroph tumours, because they may behave differently. Metastatic carcinomas can mimic gonadotroph tumours, particularly those that lack gonadotropin immunostaining, because some metastatic carcinomas may express ER, GATA3, SF1 {30390035}, and INSM1. Because some of the gonadotroph tumours with pseudorosettes mimic gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (GEP-NETs), the possibility of metastatic GEP-NETs must be considered in the differential diagnosis for gonadotroph tumours {34342837}. Primary sellar paragangliomas {18320326; 18482839; 30217041} should be distinguished from hormone-negative gonadotroph tumours because of their common positivity for GATA3; expression of SF1 and cytokeratins is helpful in these situations {30217041}. However, negativity for keratins occurs in about 40% of gonadotroph tumours {29434339}. The latter finding along with GATA3 expression should not automatically be taken for a diagnosis of paraganglioma. Positivity for tyrosine hydroxylase and negativity for SF1 can help distinguish paraganglioma. Oncocytic pituicytoma should be ruled out in cases suspicious for a gonadotroph tumour with a more oncocytic appearance; they uniformly stain for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and S100 {19525896; 23887161}.

Cytology

Gonadotroph tumours are distinguished by their hypercellularity and homogeneous appearance in cytological preparations. Tumour cells may have enlarged, pleomorphic, or atypical nuclei with variable cytoplasm often embedded in a granular background. Papillary formations are easily recognized on cytological touch or smear preparations.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no specific molecular tests applied to gonadotroph tumours for routine diagnosis. Because a subset of tumours arise in the setting of hereditary syndromes, germline genetic screening has been recommended for all patients aged < 30 years with a macrotumour or any childhood-onset pituitary tumour, regardless of family history {21753072; 23321498; 25905184; 27742789; 31996917}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a PitNET/adenoma with variable immunoreactivity for gonadotropins (FSH-β, LH-β, α-subunit) and/or SF1; absence of PIT1 and TPIT expression.

Desirable: immunoreactivity for ER and GATA3.

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour extension based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}.

There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of gonadotroph tumours have an indolent clinical behaviour. In a recent European Society of Endocrinology survey on aggressive pituitary tumours and pituitary carcinomas, only 4% of aggressive pituitary tumours and 2.5% of pituitary carcinomas were of gonadotroph cell lineage {29330228}. No single biomarker has been found to independently predict aggressive behaviour of gonadotroph tumours {20228124; 22697380; 24431196}.

Surgery is the primary treatment for gonadotroph tumours. Like for other non-functioning tumours, the most significant predictive factor for recurrence is incomplete surgical resection (i.e. postoperative residual tumour), mostly due to cavernous sinus invasion. Tumour invasion is the major risk factor for incomplete removal {20460423; 26116412; 29259039}. However, the data associating invasiveness with tumour recurrence/progression are highly conflicting {15214978; 18312100; 18377253; 22569896; 29288467; 30138730; 26116412; 29259039}. Other factors that may be related to recurrence include a young age at diagnosis and large/giant tumours {17656591; 21900406; 21467969; 15214978; 18312100; 18377253; 22569896; 29288467; 30138730}.

The Ki-67 (MIB1) proliferation index has been one of the most studied biomarkers in non-functioning tumours, including the gonadotroph types, but its usefulness as a single predictive factor for progression has similarly yielded mixed results {17656591; 22419713; 19687686; 23400299; 29259039; 28651368}.

Postoperative radiation therapy may be indicated in cases with a large postoperative remnant or regrowth; pharmacotherapy seems to be of limited value in gonadotroph tumours, but it may occasionally be effective {21467969; 26070464}. Currently used drugs for the pharmacological treatment of PitNETs/adenomas are somatostatin analogues and DRD2 agonists. SSTR expression and ERα expression have been recently studied in gonadotroph tumours as potential predictors of tumour medical responsiveness and tumour clinical behaviour. However, these are exceedingly early data and the results are inconsistent {28911153; 32326042; 33241169}.
