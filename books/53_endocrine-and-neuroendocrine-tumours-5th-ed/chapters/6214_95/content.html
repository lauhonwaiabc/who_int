6.2.1.4 Adrenal cortical carcinoma

Definition

Adrenal cortical carcinoma is a malignant epithelial tumour originating from adrenal cortical cells.

ICD-O coding

8370/3 Adrenal cortical carcinoma

8370/3 Conventional adrenal cortical carcinoma

8370/3 Oncocytic adrenal cortical carcinoma

8370/3 Myxoid adrenal cortical carcinoma

8370/3 Sarcomatoid adrenal cortical carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

XH4KH2 Adrenal cortical carcinoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: adrenocortical carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Conventional adrenal cortical carcinoma; oncocytic adrenal cortical carcinoma; myxoid adrenal cortical carcinoma; sarcomatoid adrenal cortical carcinoma

Localization

Most adrenal cortical carcinomas manifest with unilateral adrenal gland involvement; however, exceptional examples can occur in heterotopic/ectopic locations such as the retroperitoneum, pelvic region (between the urinary bladder and prostate), and ovary {28940135; 21374072; 23603421; 28258518; 32822651; 33815299}. Adrenohepatic fusion/adhesion or heterotopia/ectopia may explain the occurrence of odd examples of hepatic adrenal cortical carcinomas {30333277}. For unknown reasons, left adrenal gland involvement has been recorded more frequently {21386792; 23774167}.

Clinical features

Approximately 10–15% of adrenal cortical carcinomas are incidentally detected during imaging studies for unrelated causes {30299884}; however, most patients become clinically apparent because of symptoms related to autonomous hormone secretion and/or an abdominal mass {30299884; 31672302}. Hormone secretion occurs in nearly half of the patients {16551738; 30299884}. In adults, adrenal virilization/feminization syndrome due to an adrenal mass is generally considered a sign of malignancy {31672302}. Autonomous glucocorticoid secretion (adrenal Cushing syndrome) or synchronous glucocorticoid and sex-steroid secretion (Cushing syndrome and virilization/feminization syndrome) accounts for the vast majority of functioning adrenal cortical carcinomas; however, isolated sex-steroid production (often androgen, less frequently estrogen) occurs less frequently than the former functional states {31672302; 30299884}. Primary aldosteronism due to aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical carcinomas is rare {24682757; 30475217; 9447288; 32717746}.

Patients with hypercortisolism often develop several signs of Cushing syndrome, including central obesity, muscle weakness, easy bruising, fatigue, and depression {33075802; 25425660}, but mild autonomous glucocorticoid secretion (subclinical Cushing syndrome) can also occur in adrenal cortical carcinomas {33652108}. Also, related clinical signs such as hypertension, osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes mellitus may exist. Many possible clinicopathological conditions must be taken into consideration in the clinical workup of patients with Cushing syndrome {25425660; 33534120}, but markedly rapid-onset symptoms with accentuated hypertension and poorly regulated blood glucose with increased precursor steroids (e.g. DHEAS) may be clues to an underlying cortisol-producing adrenal cortical carcinoma {24802156}. Female patients with androgen-secreting adrenal cortical carcinomas may exhibit clinical signs of virilization, whereas the hormonal excess might be masked in men (but not in children) because of the higher testosterone levels produced from the testes {29342266}. In rare instances of estrogen production, the inverse might be true, with male patients having gynaecomastia and adult female patients being relatively asymptomatic {30053001; 16596055}.

Apart from symptoms related to hormone excess, the second most common presentation is attributable to nonspecific signs of mass effect, when the tumour growth compresses nearby structures {19500769; 30299884}. Abdominal and/or flank pain are commonly encountered. Especially in non-functioning tumours, metastasis-related symptoms may be the initial finding at the time of diagnosis.

Most adrenal cortical carcinomas are distinguished from adenomas on CT because the former usually show a higher Hounsfield density (often > 10 Hounsfield units), heterogeneous CT contrast washout, a larger tumour size (often > 40 mm), and the presence of heterogenic appearances suggestive of necrosis and/or haemorrhage {33875173; 31672302}. In addition, a heterogeneous signal drop on chemical-shift MRI can be useful. Functional imaging studies with FDG PET can provide additional value for the prediction of malignancy risk in adrenal cortical neoplasms (e.g. tumour-to-liver maximum standardized uptake value ratio: > 1.5), because in general most carcinomas have a higher uptake of glucose {32978795}, but exceptions such as oncocytic adenomas exist.

Adrenal cortical carcinomas are usually larger than their benign counterparts, with median sizes ranging from 80 to 100 mm {15008991; 32438344; 30980285}. Because the clinical diagnosis of hormonally inactive carcinomas may be delayed {33813589}, they might occasionally reach impressive proportions (380 mm) {27916625}. Adrenal cortical carcinoma may infiltrate the periadrenal fat and adjacent organs (e.g. kidney, liver, spleen, large veins) {29868977}. Regional lymph node metastases are common, and distant metastatic sites include the lungs, bone, liver, and brain {31823340}.

Epidemiology

Adrenal cortical carcinoma accounts for the majority of deaths attributable to primary adrenal neoplasia. The annual incidence is 0.7–2 cases per 1 million USA inhabitants, affecting mostly female patients, with a broad age span (peaking in the fifth and sixth decades of life) {30221891; 8242539}. In the paediatric population, girls are also more frequently affected, and age at the time of diagnosis ranges from 1.5 to 16 years {33306282}. In adult patients, although the vast majority of cases are sporadic, with no known family history, subsets of affected patients are associated with one of several germline susceptibility syndromes (see Pathogenesis) {26660147}. For instance, the prevalence of germline TP53 p.R337H variants in 0.3% of the general population in southern Brazil may explain the higher frequency of adrenal cortical carcinoma in that geographical area with more frequent paediatric manifestations {24936644}.

Etiology

Besides the well-established genetic susceptibility syndromes (see Pathogenesis) {33534120}, cigarette smoking {27165744; 32871402; 31241762; 8621222} and oral contraceptive use (especially before the age of 25 years) {8621222} have been suggested as risk factors for these tumours. A recent review of SEER Program data from 2001 to 2016 underscored the association between tobacco use and adrenal cortical neoplasia {32871402}. Another study showed that a family history of cancer in general was associated with a risk of adrenal cortical carcinoma in both sexes, whereas alcohol consumption was associated with reduced risk in men {31241762}.

Pathogenesis

Adrenal cortical carcinoma mainly develops through the somatic acquisition of genetic alterations, leading to the activation of certain signalling pathways that promote proliferation {33534120; 26559569}. In adults, 90% of cases are sporadic, and the remainder are related to genetic susceptibility (see below).

Pathways and processes affected by common driver alterations in adrenal cortical carcinoma include (1) the WNT signalling pathway (e.g. CTNNB1 mutations and ZNRF3 deletions), (2) cell-cycle regulation (e.g. TP53, RB1, MDM2, CDK4, and CDKN2A mutations), (3) chromosome maintenance / chromatin remodelling (DAXX, ATRX, MEN1, and TERT mutations, as well as TERF2 amplifications), and (4) the PKA pathway (PRKAR1A mutation) {27165744; 26676358} [[Pakbaz S, Mete O. Adrenal cortical neoplasia: from histology to molecular biology. Diagn Histopathol. 2019 May;25(5):178–89. doi:10.1016/j.mpdhp.2019.02.004.]]. Aldosterone-producing adrenal cortical carcinomas lack the aldosterone-driver mutations identified in aldosterone-producing adenomas {30475217}.

Inactivating TP53 mutations are reported in approximately one fifth of all adrenal cortical carcinomas, thus constituting the most common mutational event {33534120}. TP53 encodes p53, an established tumour suppressor that induces apoptosis when triggered by environmental stress and DNA damage, and the importance of this signalling network is further accentuated by the finding of mutations in RB1 and CDKN2A in additional cases {24747642}. Somatic mutations in other genes traditionally associated with the development of hereditary syndromes, such as PRKAR1A, MSH2, APC, MEN1, and NF1, are also reported {24747642}. Apart from the regulation of apoptosis and cell-cycle progression described above, genetic alterations in genes regulating the WNT signalling pathway are commonly noted in adrenal cortical carcinomas, not least CTNNB1 mutations and ZNRF3 mutations and deletions {16140927; 24747642; 25490274}. Interestingly, TP53 or WNT pathway alterations are often mutually exclusive, but both are enriched in poor-prognostic tumours {24747642; 25490274; 27165744}. Additionally, mutations in the chromatin-remodelling genes ATRX and DAXX have also been established {24747642}.

Regarding chromosomal alterations, three types of profiles have been reported: (1) extended chromosome losses reflecting uniparental isodisomy (50% of tumours; variable outcome), with potential duplication of the remaining genome; (2) numerous non-recurrent alterations, with various levels of ploidy (50% of tumours; poorer outcome); (3) almost normal chromosomal structure, with a limited number of alterations (rare; better outcome). Beyond these general profiles, loss of the maternal 11p15 allele is one of the most common findings in these tumours {2921038; 7911125}. This genomic region contains the imprinted tumour suppressor gene H19 and oncogene IGF2, which are expressed from maternal and paternal alleles respectively.

Epigenetically, these tumours show various levels of DNA methylation in CpG islands of gene promoter regions. Hypermethylation in these regions is associated with a poorer outcome {27967600; 23093492; 30770352}.

The past few decades have seen extensive genomic studies including transcriptome and next-generation sequencing analyses on adrenal cortical carcinoma that yielded several molecular subclasses/clusters with prognostic relevance {27165744; 19147773; 12547710; 15613424; 19139432; 23093492; 24747642; 25490274}. The most validated algorithm to date divides tumours into two principal classes, based upon the mutation signatures, mRNA expression profiles, DNA methylation patterns, and copy-number landscape {24747642; 27165744; 31294750}. By these stratifications, a group of tumours are characterized by a low disease-progression rate. These cases seldom exhibit TP53 or WNT pathway mutations, adhere to an mRNA expression cluster traditionally associated with a more indolent disease that exhibits low levels of gene-specific methylation (low CpG island methylator phenotype), and do not display whole-genome doubling. On the other hand, the other subgroup of adrenal cortical carcinomas represent high-risk cases with frequent TP53 and WNT pathway gene mutations, an expression pattern often associated with a worse outcome, recurrent CpG island methylation (high CpG island methylator phenotype), and numerous non-recurrent alterations with (potentially) whole-genome doubling.

The identification of mutation signatures of smoking and mismatch repair deficiency in some cases further linked these tumours to tobacco use and Lynch syndrome {27165744}.

Genetic susceptibility

Li–Fraumeni syndrome is caused by germline inactivating TP53 gene mutations, and this alteration is noted in 50–80% of paediatric cases of adrenal cortical carcinoma, as well as in 3–5% of adult cases {26660147; 33534120; 7966399; 25584008; 21225464; 23752102}. Adrenal cortical carcinoma is also represented in patients with Lynch syndrome {21225464; 23752102; 33185660}, Carney complex {22312093; 23652670; 22112809}, familial adenomatous polyposis {20978149; 9293929; 11156460}, Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome {25365508; 2921038}, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 {8678766; 22084155; 12016472; 7491526; 17235589; 1352309}, neurofibromatosis type 1 {25520849; 16132517}, and possibly subsets of the familial paraganglioma phaeochromocytoma syndromes {28819017}. Isolated reports have expanded the association of adrenal cortical carcinoma with germline pathogenic variants of ATM {33836455}, CHEK2 {30623166}, MUTYH {28127763}, FH (hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma syndrome) {28737576}, FLCN {36701047}, and EGFR (especially in adolescents and young adults) {33326033}, as well as synchronous MSH2 and RET variants (without multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2) {33615670}.

Macroscopic appearance

Adrenal cortical carcinomas are often large, solitary, heterogeneous tumours that weigh > 100 g. However, smaller tumours (≤ 20 mm) can occur {23774167; 27916625}. The cut surface shows a yellowish-tan variegated appearance, with nodularity that may reflect intratumoural heterogeneity. Necrosis and haemorrhage are frequent in high-grade tumours. Gross extension into large veins, extra-adrenal adipose tissue, and adjacent organs is a feature of stage III and stage IV disease.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of adrenal cortical carcinoma is based on invasive growth (e.g. angioinvasion, capsular invasion, local gross invasion), increased proliferative activity, and a combination of cytological and architectural features. The histological features are usually assessed with one of several multifactorial algorithms / scoring systems {6703192; 12459628; 15306935; 23774167; 19912359; 25582500}. No single system has been shown to be completely sensitive or specific in all settings.

Unlike adenomas, adrenal cortical carcinomas often show solid, broad trabecular, or large nested growth. The latter is reflected in a loss of reticulin framework on Gordon–Sweet silver histochemistry {23774167; 28877067}, which is also a requirement of the reticulin algorithm {23774167; 28989884}. Variable qualitative reticulin alterations (e.g. pericellular mesh-like staining) can feature in benign and malignant adrenal cortical neoplasms; however, qualitative reticulin alterations in association with a loss of the reticulin framework is a feature of adrenal cortical carcinoma {28877067}. It is important to recognize sites of degeneration or haemorrhage that may show a disrupted reticulin framework {31672302}.

Tumour invasion (e.g. capsular invasion, lymphatic invasion, vascular invasion) is assessed at the tumour periphery, in and around the tumour capsule {33058949; 28877067; 31672302}. The demonstration of bona fide vascular invasion is a highly reliable criterion of malignancy and stands out as one of the diagnostic criteria that is of prognostic significance {28877067}. Vascular invasion often involves veins and is characterized by tumour cells invading through a vessel wall and/or intravascular tumour cells admixed with thrombus/fibrin {28877067; 33058949}; however, gross invasion into a large vessel can occur in advanced-stage disease.

Adrenal cortical carcinoma is a proliferation-driven malignancy, a finding that has been supported on numerous genomic studies. Therefore, the assessment of proliferative activity using both mitotic count {2919718; 28877067; 6703192; 12459628; 15306935; 23774167; 25582500; 24382573; 3967172} and Ki-67 immunohistochemistry {33443677; 30401946; 28877067; 12920217; 11196463; 19218281; 11517015} is important for the diagnostic workup and prognostication of these tumours {33058949; 31672302} [[Mete O, Davis JL, Rudzinski ER, et al. Protocol for the examination of specimens from patients with carcinoma of the adrenal gland. Version: Adrenal Gland 4.1.0.0. Northfield (IL): College of American Pathologists; 2020 Feb. Available from: https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cancer-reporting-tools/cancer-protocol-templates.]].

An increased mitotic activity, defined as > 5 mitoses/10 mm2 (50 HPF of 0.2 mm2), has been incorporated in all diagnostic algorithms {474519; 24382573; 25582500; 3967172; 6703192}. Proliferative heterogeneity can occur in these tumours; therefore, the mitotic count is assessed in 10 mm2 from areas with highest mitotic density. Phosphohistone H3 immunohistochemistry may help highlight mitoses in carcinomas with a low mitotic count {24434900}. Atypical mitotic figures are also important findings suggestive of abnormal chromosome content (aneuploidy) {33409811}.

Traditionally, adrenal cortical carcinomas are subdivided on the basis of mitotic activity into low-grade (≤ 20 mitoses/10 mm2) and high-grade (> 20 mitoses/10 mm2) {21383612; 2919718}, and this approach has shown prognostic value {2919718; 28877067}. The optimal cut-off point for paediatric adrenal cortical carcinomas remains to be validated [[Mete O, Davis JL, Rudzinski ER, et al. Protocol for the examination of specimens from patients with carcinoma of the adrenal gland. Version: Adrenal Gland 4.1.0.0. Northfield (IL): College of American Pathologists; 2020 Feb. Available from: https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cancer-reporting-tools/cancer-protocol-templates.]].

Studies assessing Ki-67 immunohistochemistry in adrenal cortical carcinomas have shown that most carcinomas have a Ki-67 labelling index > 5% {30401946; 33443677; 28877067; 12920217; 11196463; 19218281; 11517015}. In adrenal cortical carcinomas, the Ki-67 index has been regarded as a prognostic biomarker {33443677; 28877067; 30401946; 24434900; 25559399}, and it can also help rationalize the need for adjuvant mitotane therapy {30299884; 29542002}. Given the critical value of this biomarker, manual counting or automated image analysis algorithms should be applied in the assessment of the Ki-67 proliferation index {29542002; 33058949; 25421287; 26685085; 26980031}.

Punctate/comedo-type or geographical necrosis is frequently seen in high-grade carcinomas. In fact, the most recently proposed Helsinki scoring system weighted the importance of this finding by combining with increased mitosis and Ki-67 labelling index {25582500}.

The well-recognized histological subtypes, in decreasing order of frequency, are oncocytic, myxoid, and sarcomatoid carcinomas {22086150}. Oncocytic carcinomas require > 90% of the tumour to be composed of oncocytic cells {15306935; 33058949} and should not be assessed with the Weiss system because of their eosinophilic cytoplasm, diffuse growth pattern, and high-grade nuclei (see #26647Table 5.02, and #26648Table 5.03). The Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia system has been applied to these tumours {15306935} (see #26651Box 5.02), but the Helsinki score and reticulin algorithm can also be used {25582500; 27916625; 23774167; 33058949} (see #26649Box 5.03, and #26650Table 5.04).

Myxoid carcinomas have abundant extracellular connective tissue mucin {20444728; 20534995; 33549334}. In myxoid carcinomas, some Weiss parameters (e.g. diffuse growth pattern, lymphatic invasion, and nuclear atypia) may be absent or difficult to assess, thus increasing the risk of an underdiagnosis of malignancy.

Sarcomatoid carcinomas show loss of cortical differentiation {27589897; 27165744} and can be biphasic with typical differentiated areas; when monophasic, these tumours can be indistinguishable from true sarcomas of the adrenal {25246132} and retroperitoneal sarcomas involving the adrenal gland {27165744}.

The classification of paediatric tumours is a common challenge {19326954} and requires the application of the US Armed Forces Institute of Pathology (AFIP) criteria {12826878} (see #26652Table 5.05). The application of Ki-67 with a cut-off point of 15% has been suggested in the workup of paediatric adrenal cortical tumours {33443677; 30401946}.

Diagnostic biomarker studies

The differential diagnosis of adrenal cortical carcinoma includes adrenal cortical adenoma (including oncocytic adenomas), phaeochromocytoma, renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, sarcoma, metastatic melanoma, and other metastases (lung, colorectal, and bladder). The confirmation of the cortical origin of a non-functioning adrenal neoplasm is usually considered {29542002}. SF1 is the most reliable biomarker for confirming the cortical origin {29542002}. Several less specific biomarkers (e.g. melan-A, synaptophysin, α-inhibin, and calretinin) may be used in a panel approach, but staining for these markers is also variable {29542002}. Synaptophysin {29542002} and α-inhibin {33826547} can be expressed in phaeochromocytomas. EMA, CEA, and other common transcription factors are generally negative, and variable keratin expression can occur.

Adrenal cortical carcinomas with invasive growth or high-grade features often do not pose a diagnostic challenge; however, the distinction of low-grade adrenal cortical carcinomas confined to the adrenal gland and carcinomas with adenoma-like regions may require the use of ancillary biomarkers in addition to the accepted multifactorial systems {28877067; 29542002; 33534120}. Rare cases with equivocal features may sometimes be diagnosed as adrenal cortical neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential. Although an increased Ki-67 index (often > 5%) and overexpression of p53 can be helpful in some cases {29542002; 28877067}, IGF2 paranuclear immunoreactivity has been shown to be the most reliable translational biomarker in the distinction of adrenal cortical carcinomas from adenomas {28877067; 16918977; 19218281}. Molecular studies can also help in the distinction of carcinoma {19147773; 12547710; 19139432; 27165744; 31294750}.

Cytology

Cytological assessment may be considered in the distinction of metastatic disease from an adrenal cortical neoplasm. Smears are usually cellular, with well-differentiated carcinomas indistinguishable from adenomas, whereas high-grade carcinomas show necrosis, marked nuclear pleomorphism and hyperchromasia, macronucleoli, and mitoses {16880150}. FNAB including EUS-FNAB offers a minimally invasive biopsy of adrenal lesions found in the workup of patients with possible metastatic carcinomas, including lung and pancreas primaries, and can provide material for molecular studies {6587703; 15376200; 32988789; 27257146}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of malignancy can be rendered using molecular testing of an adrenal cortical neoplasm {19147773; 12547710; 19139432; 27165744; 31294750}; however, molecular tests are not used in standard clinical practice. Molecular characterization of adrenal cortical carcinomas has shown promise in the prognostication of patients with adrenal cortical carcinomas (see Pathogenesis and Prognosis and prediction) {31294750; 33534120; 27165744; 30770352; 27967600}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphological and/or immunohistochemical evidence of adrenal cortical differentiation, and evidence of malignancy usually based on a multiparameter scoring system (see note below); mitotic tumour grading and Ki-67 immunohistochemistry.

Desirable: confirmation of adrenal cortical differentiation using SF1; application of prognostic biomarkers.

Note: fulfils one of the established multiparameter scoring systems for malignancy, including:

Conventional adrenal cortical neoplasms in adults (one of the following): Weiss score ≥ 3, modified Weiss score ≥ 3, Helsinki score > 8.5, reticulin algorithm Oncocytic adrenal cortical neoplasms (one of the following): Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia (any major criterion), Helsinki score > 8.5, reticulin algorithm Paediatric adrenal cortical neoplasms: AFIP criteria score ≥ 4

Staging

Tumour staging is based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system or the European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumours (ENSAT) staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Advanced tumour stage, lack of completeness of tumour excision, tumour functionality, angioinvasion, high mitotic tumour grade, high Ki-67 proliferation index (see below), and molecular characteristics (e.g. methylome, microRNAome, transcriptome profiling) of an adrenal cortical carcinoma are considered important dynamic risk factors {30980285; 33875173; 30299884; 28877067; 2919718; 29542002; 25582500; 33443677; 30401946; 24434900; 25559399; 19139432; 19147773; 31294750}.

Angioinvasion is a well-recognized adverse feature, and it also stands as the best predictor of disease-free survival in patients with low-grade adrenal cortical carcinomas {28877067}. In terms of the Ki-67 labelling index, some clinical series have considered risk categorization based on 3-tiered Ki-67 cut-off points such as < 10%, 10–19%, and ≥ 20%; or < 20%, 20–50%, and > 50% {24434900; 25559399}; however, recent evidence suggests that a Ki-67 index of > 15% is a reliable biomarker for predicting a poor outcome in adult and paediatric tumours {33443677; 30401946}. A Helsinki score of > 17 also correlates with a poor prognosis {25582500}.

Molecular studies have underscored the role of p53 and β-catenin as prognostic biomarkers, because most carcinomas with adverse molecular clusters tend to show nuclear β-catenin expression and/or aberrant p53 staining (overexpression or global loss) {33534120; 29542002}. For this reason, the application of both p53 and β-catenin immunohistochemistry may help risk stratification, in addition to Ki-67 and mitotic count. Besides the mitotic count and Ki-67 index, other cell cycle–related biomarkers (e.g. BUB1B, HURP, and NEK2) showed correlation with adrenal cortical carcinomas {28877067}. Other correlates of a poorer clinical outcome include a high expression of SF1 {23158211; 20660055} and loss of ATRX and ZNRF3 expression {33513905}.

Mismatch repair immunohistochemistry is considered to screen for the possibility of Lynch syndrome–related adrenal cortical carcinomas {23752102; 33185660; 29542002; 33534120}; however, the combination of mismatch repair and PDL1 immunohistochemistry also helps determine eligibility for immunotherapy in select patients {33809752; 33918733}.

Several candidate biomarkers (e.g. RRM1 and CYP2W1) for mitotane response have been identified {22547773; 25144458}, but none of these have been validated for clinical practice at this time.

Transcriptome {19139432; 19147773} and pangenomic {24747642} studies clearly underscored the role of molecular risk stratification in adrenal cortical carcinomas {31294750}. The expression of two genes, BUB1B and PINK1, predicted prognosis {19139432}. A specific methylation signature has been established in adrenal cortical carcinoma with a particularly poor prognosis, suggesting that methylation patterns may predict clinical outcomes {23093492}. There is also evidence suggesting a role for the dysregulation of microRNA in adrenal cortical carcinoma, for example the downregulation of miR-195 and overexpression of miR-483-5p, which were identified in cases with increased mortality {19996210}. Moreover, low and high circulating levels of miR-195 and miR-483-5p, respectively, have been detected in adrenal cortical carcinoma patients with shorter survival times {23756429}. Although not yet easily translated into the clinical routine workup of adrenal cortical carcinomas, hypermethylation of G0S2 is strongly associated with the high CpG island methylator phenotype group of poor-prognostic and fatal carcinomas that are designated as cluster-of-clusters (CoC) III tumours {30770352}. Moreover, although TERT promoter mutations are rare in adrenal cortical carcinomas, aberrant TERT promoter methylation could constitute a helpful marker to pinpoint subsets of cases at risk of disease dissemination {24803525; 24951106; 29956721}.

The 5-year overall survival rate is 37–47% in adrenal cortical carcinoma {24423978; 21386792}. Although completely excised localized disease with no angioinvasion is potentially curable, the numbers for patients with disseminated (stage IV) adrenal cortical carcinoma are dismal, with few patients surviving the 5-year mark {30221891; 30980285}. Current recommendations are that all patients with adrenal cortical carcinoma are followed by tertiary centre experts {33875173}.
