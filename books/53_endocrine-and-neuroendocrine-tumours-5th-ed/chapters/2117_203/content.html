2.1.1.7 Acidophil stem cell PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Acidophil stem cell pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is an immature PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with partial lactotroph and limited somatotroph differentiation, oncocytic features, and giant mitochondria.

ICD-O coding

8280/3 Acidophil stem cell PitNET/adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: acidophil stem cell tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Acidophil stem cell tumours (ASCTs) arise within the sella but frequently extend into parasellar structures.

Clinical features

ASCTs are often rapidly growing neoplasms with hyperprolactinaemia that is lower than expected for the size of the tumour, and with some phenotypic features of acromegaly despite modest biochemical evidence of it (i.e. fugitive acromegaly) {6261917; 31766255; 34067494}. The hyperprolactinaemia is most often associated with amenorrhoea and galactorrhoea in women and loss of libido or impotence in men. Serum growth hormone (GH) and/or IGF1 are inappropriately normal or modestly elevated. Signs and symptoms of mass effect, such as headaches, visual deficits, and cranial neuropathies, are also reported.

Neuroimaging

The vast majority of ASCTs are large (> 10 mm), invasive tumours. On neuroimaging, an atypical, mostly inferior growth pattern is seen in roughly half of all cases, with invasion through the sellar floor into the sphenoidal sinus and nasopharynx {6261917}. Suprasellar extension is often less prominent than in other large, invasive macrotumours.

Epidemiology

ASCTs accounted for about 2% of all PitNETs and 7% of all PIT1-lineage tumours in a series of 1055 surgically resected PitNETs {29434339}. In a series of 15 patients, 6 were men and 9 were women, ranging in age from 17 to 60 years (median: 42 years) {6261917}.

Etiology

Not known

Pathogenesis

ASCT may arise from a mammosomatotroph precursor cell {6261917; 31766255}. Molecular studies have not been performed.

Macroscopic appearance

Like other PitNETs, ASCTs are white to grey and distinct from the normal pituitary gland; they are generally soft, explaining the relative preservation of normal pituitary function. Large tumours show variable degrees of invasion into adjacent anatomical structures.

Histopathology

ASCT usually grows in sheets and appears cytologically immature, with chromophobic to slightly acidophilic cytoplasm and variable oncocytic features due to the accumulation of mitochondria. The highly distinctive giant mitochondria that were originally described ultrastructurally manifest as variably sized clear vacuoles on routine histopathology, a useful clue to the diagnosis, particularly when prominent. On electron microscopy, the giant mitochondria retain a continuous double membrane but do not show cristae {6261917}. Ultrastructural features suggest that ASCT is composed of a single immature cell type with evidence of simultaneous lactotroph and somatotroph differentiation.

Immunohistochemistry

They are diffusely PIT1-positive and show variable prolactin and ER staining, with minimal to absent GH expression; stains for TPIT, SF1, GATA3, and other pituitary hormones are negative. CAM5.2 highlights scattered paranuclear fibrous bodies in one third of these tumours {29434339}. A potential immunohistochemical surrogate of ultrastructural findings is the antimitochondrial antigen stain, which variably highlights the increased number of mitochondria, as well as rimmed vacuoles suggestive of giant mitochondria.

Cytology

Not reported

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no molecular tests required to diagnose these tumours. Germline screening for inherited tumours should be considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a PitNET/adenoma with diffuse PIT1 staining, variable prolactin expression, focal to absent GH, and oncocytic features.

Desirable: ER positivity; giant mitochondria; scattered GH positivity and/or cytokeratin-positive fibrous bodies; hyperprolactinaemia that is less than expected for the size of the tumour; fugitive acromegaly; downward invasion pattern.

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour extension based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}.

There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

Large studies have not been published, but the data thus far suggest that ASCT is an aggressive PitNET/adenoma. In contrast to lactotroph tumours, ASCTs are typically refractory to dopamine agonist therapy {16995577}. Similarly, they can be somatostatin analogue–resistant, in contrast to the their more differentiated somatotroph counterparts; such therapeutic insensitivity can be the clue to the diagnosis. Therefore, other management options include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy {31721135}.
