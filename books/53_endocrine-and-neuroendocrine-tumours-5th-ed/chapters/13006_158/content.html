13.0.0.6 Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome

Definition

Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome (VHL) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by inactivating mutations in the VHL gene. Patients with this disorder develop angiomas and haemangioblastomas; paragangliomas including phaeochromocytomas; renal cell carcinomas (RCCs); pancreatic serous cystadenomas and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs); endolymphatic sac tumours (ELSTs); and cystadenomas of the epididymis, broad ligament, and mesosalpinx.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

193300 Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome; VHLS

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Acceptable: von Hippel–Lindau disease.

Subtype(s)

VHL types 1, 2A, 2B, and 2C have different manifestations and are associated with different genetic alterations in VHL.

Localization

The various lesions have distinct localizations.

Ears: About 10–15% of patients with VHL develop ELSTs {9145719}; they are the initial manifestation of VHL in 32% of individuals with these lesions {25867206}. These benign cystadenomas arise in the vestibular aqueduct and may extend into the extraosseous portion of the endolymphatic sac, the petrous bone, and the inner ear {16322231}. Large tumours may extend into the brain. They are bilateral in 30% of cases.

CNS: CNS haemangioblastomas occur in 80% of patients {23400300} and are most commonly localized in the cerebellum (44–72%), followed by the spinal cord (most frequently in the posterior half of the spinal cord) and brainstem (usually in the posterior medulla oblongata). Supratentorial lesions are rare, accounting for about 2.9% of cases {12546356}.

Retina: As many as 85% of individuals with VHL develop retinal haemangioblastomas (also known as retinal angioma or angiomatosis retinae) {17057815}, with an average age of 22.5 years at diagnosis {28972023}. Tumours are often multiple and are bilateral in about 50% of cases {33720516}.

Kidney: Renal cysts and RCCs are usually multiple and bilateral in patients with VHL. As many as 40% of patients with VHL develop multicentric and bilateral clear cell RCC, and > 70% develop renal cysts {8929948; 2274658}.

Adrenal gland and paraganglia: Approximately 30% of patients with VHL develop adrenal (phaeochromocytomas) and/or extra-adrenal paragangliomas, at a mean age of 30 years {10458336}. Phaeochromocytomas are bilateral in about 40% of cases. Paragangliomas arise at various sites of the sympathetic or parasympathetic nervous systems, from the base of the skull to the urinary bladder. Head and neck paragangliomas arise in a number of locations along the carotid sheath and middle ear, including the carotid bifurcation (carotid body), vagal ganglia, jugular bulb, and tympanic plexus. Sympathetic paragangliomas may be located in the thorax, abdomen (organ of Zuckerkandl), and pelvis (bladder base).

Pancreas: Pancreatic manifestations of VHL include PanNETs, cysts, and serous cystadenomas. In patients with VHL, PanNETs are multiple in 50–56% of cases {9665483; 19238077}. Pancreatic cystic lesions are frequent, occurring in as many as 91% of patients with VHL {11040195; 19238077; 22659535}.

Duodenum, ampulla, and gallbladder / cystic duct: Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) reported in patients with VHL include clear cell NETs of the ampulla and duodenum {23913169; 11688471} and of the gallbladder and common bile duct {2379881; 23478233}.

Reproductive organs: Epididymal papillary cystadenomas occur in as many as 60% of men with VHL. They arise from the efferent ductules of the epididymis and are often bilateral {20367315}. Very rarely, papillary cystadenomas may manifest in the broad ligament and mesosalpinx in women {22317868; 20157715}.

Peripheral locations: Occasionally, haemangioblastomas identical to those seen in the CNS are found in peripheral nerves (e.g. the spinal nerve roots, cauda equina, and sciatic nerve) {15350042; 22133049} or in extraneural tissues (e.g. adrenal glands, liver, lungs, and kidneys) {22544391; 24145646; 17895756; 1898683}.

Clinical features

Clinical presentations of VHL can be broadly divided into three categories: neurological, obstructive, and hormone excess conditions. The neurological manifestations often begin with retinal disease; haemorrhages result in compromised visual acuity {31095066}. Extraocular problems include cerebellar ataxia and spinal cord compression from haemangioblastomas. Hearing loss can occur due to ELSTs. Obstructive features include those from outflow interruptions along the pancreaticobiliary or (more commonly) the genitourinary tract from expanding cysts or RCCs. Hormone excess symptoms are related directly to the cell lineage giving rise to the causative phaeochromocytomas or paragangliomas or PanNETs {32606780; 31368132; 17122523}. Of note, VHL-related NETs can also produce ectopic hormones, leading to further diagnostic challenges.

Epidemiology

VHL has an incidence of 1 in 36 000 births. Age at diagnosis varies from infancy to 60–70 years. There is no sex predilection.

Etiology

VHL is due to germline mutations in VHL that inactivate VHL protein. Type 1 VHL is associated with large deletion or truncation mutations of VHL; these generally yield a protein with very little or no biological activity. Type 2 VHL is usually due to missense mutations that result in a protein with limited activity.

Missense variants occur with the greatest frequency, with all variants localizing to the α- or β-domains of VHL {31368132}. The most prevalent manifestations, including CNS, renal, and retinal neoplasms, are associated with frameshift variants and large deletions. Age-related penetrance is greater for CNS haemangioblastomas with truncating variants than for those with missense variants, whereas the reverse is true for phaeochromocytomas {31368132}.

Somatic second hits of the wildtype allele inactivate the VHL protein in various tissues.

Pathogenesis

VHL protein is an E3 ligase that ubiquitinates for degradation HIF1α and HIF2α, subunits of a heterodimeric protein that regulates the transcription of the genes encoding VEGF, PDGFB, TGF-α, erythropoietin, and cyclin D1, as well as other genes that regulate cell metabolism and proliferation. VHL protein normally plays a role in the response to hypoxia, but loss of normal function in patients with VHL results in an unrestrained hypoxic response with upregulation of VEGF, PDGFB, TGF-α, erythropoietin, cyclin D1, and other substances that are pro-oncogenic.

Macroscopic appearance

ELSTs are red/brown, fibrous, highly vascularized nodules {29923043; 9726042}.

Haemangioblastomas are usually well-circumscribed lesions but without true capsules. They are usually solid and cystic and highly vascular. The solid components are frequently in the wall of a cyst, which may be contiguous with the meninges. Cyst fluid may be either yellow (xanthochromic) or grey (proteinaceous). The cut surface of haemangioblastoma is usually beefy-red due to high vascularity and spongy with multiple cysts and cavernous spaces, but it may have bright yellow areas corresponding to lipid deposition {17877533}. Retinal haemangioblastomas vary in size and shape {24138046}.

RCC is usually encapsulated with well-defined borders. The cut surfaces are heterogeneous with variegated nodular growth and areas of necrosis and haemorrhage. A distinctive feature is the golden-yellow colour due to the abundant lipid content of tumour cells {27247714}.

Paragangliomas (including intra-adrenal phaeochromocytomas) in patients with VHL are unusual macroscopically in that they have a thick vascular tumour capsule and they frequently have a variegated cut surface showing areas of haemorrhage and degenerative change {29601266}, but they often also have areas of yellow parenchyma due to lipid accumulation; in the adrenal this can be mistaken for a cortical neoplasm {3592062}.

Pancreaticoduodenal/ampullary and gallbladder NETs are usually solid, well-circumscribed nodules that may be red-brown, grey, or yellow in colour due to lipid storage. They may contain fibrotic foci, probably reflecting the profibrogenic role of HIF {18580449; 18037992}.

Pancreatic cysts are usually multiple and multicystic, and filled with serous fluid. They are usually small (< 5 mm) and may include papillary areas {11073821; 19238077}.

In the reproductive organs, epididymal papillary cystadenomas are small tumours ranging from 10 to 40 mm. These are usually solid tumours with cystic spaces filled with colloid material, but they may be predominantly cystic {20367315}. The reported size of cystadenoma of the broad ligament and mesosalpinx is 30–60 mm {22296276}. Macroscopically, these tumours have a fibrous capsule and are partly cystic and lobulated, with haemorrhagic foci and stellate fibrous scars {22296276}.

Histopathology

ELSTs have two growth patterns {9726042}. The tumour may have a predominance of follicular-like architecture in which colloid-filled cysts are lined by a single layer of epithelium, mimicking thyroid follicular epithelium {9726042}. The second pattern has more papillary architecture and a more glandular and solid pattern with only occasional cystic structures, and with a markedly vascular stroma {9726042}. Small nests of plant cell–like cells with clear cytoplasm can be seen in the follicular pattern but are more prominent in the papillary pattern and can be mistaken for metastatic RCC {9726042}. ELSTs are positive for keratin AE1/AE3, EMA, CK7, GLUT1, VEGF, CAIX, and PAX8 in the majority of cases and are negative for CD10 and RCCm {30291511}.

Haemangioblastomas are circumscribed, often associated with a pseudocyst, and composed of variable proliferations of abundant mature vasculature thought to be reactive and the neoplastic so-called stromal cell component {9158701; 23400300}. The so-called stromal cells may be haemangioblast progenitor cells, and these cells have deletion of VHL {16618738; 9158701; 23400300}. There are two morphological patterns. Cellular (epithelioid) haemangioblastomas have prominent, larger, epithelioid so-called stromal cells with clear cytoplasm, that may be clustered in groups. The more common, and usually smaller, vascular-rich reticular (mesenchymal) haemangioblastomas have abundant vascularity and few scattered so-called stromal cells {16281910; 16949923}. Cellular haemangioblastomas are more often positive for GFAP (80%) than are reticular haemangioblastomas (7%) {16281910}. Ki-67 labelling indexes are higher in cellular haemangioblastomas than in reticular haemangioblastomas {16281910}. Cellular and reticular haemangioblastomas also have different cytogenetic profiles, with loss of chromosomes 6, 22q, and 19 in the cellular pattern and loss of chromosomes 19, 22q, and 3 in the reticular pattern {16949923}. Cellular haemangioblastomas recur more commonly than reticular haemangioblastomas (25% vs 8%) {16281910}. Haemangioblastomas are benign but can cause neurological deficits similarly to other lesions in the brain. As many as 8% of CNS haemangioblastomas resected from individuals with VHL have been reported as being metastases, the most common primary site being the kidney (RCC) {17219831}; haemangioblastomas are negative for PAX8, allowing the distinction from metastatic RCC {29629948}. The histological features of retinal haemangioblastomas are identical to those of CNS haemangioblastomas.

Paragangliomas in patients with VHL may have a thick fibrous or vascular capsule and a myxoid, oedematous, or hyalinized stroma. The tumour cells lack nuclear atypia and mitoses; they often have clear vacuolated cytoplasm that can mimic adrenal cortex {3592062; 12114747}, and they lack hyaline globules {12114747}. VHL-related phaeochromocytomas are not associated with the medullary adrenal hyperplasia often seen in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2–associated phaeochromocytomas {12114747; 25611110}. Tumour cells are positive for the general neuroendocrine markers INSM1, chromogranin A, and synaptophysin {3592062; 26386079}, as well as for GATA3, tyrosine hydroxylase, and DBH, and they are generally negative for keratins (with rare exceptions {31442521}). Intratumoural sustentacular cells express S100 and SOX10. CAIX is usually positive with a membranous staining pattern in the tumour cells of VHL-related phaeochromocytomas. Membranous CAIX expression in tumour cells occurs almost exclusively in VHL-related cases {23257898; 31383958}.

Renal pathology in VHL is characterized by a spectrum of proliferative and cystic lesions. VHL-related RCCs are often multifocal, bilateral, and clear cell type {31332543}. Other than being multifocal, they are indistinguishable from sporadic tumours, although as many as 95% are cystic in this setting {31332543}. Although clear cell RCC is the most common type of RCC in VHL, clear cell papillary-like RCC has also been reported {31332543; 26559379}. RCCs in VHL are often low stage at diagnosis {31332543}. The RCCs are often surrounded by a thick fibrous capsule and often have a microcystic growth pattern. Like sporadic clear cell RCCs, those associated with VHL have an alveolar arrangement of nests of epithelial clear cells with a delicate vascular network {26559379}. The rare cases that have clear cell papillary-like RCC features have branching papillae, papillary tufts, and apical nuclei, but immunohistochemically do not show the characteristic CK7 and CAIX immunopositivity (basolateral) of clear cell papillary RCC {26559379}. Individuals with a known history of VHL may undergo surveillance imaging for RCC. Biopsies may be performed on masses identified through surveillance, many of which may be ablated after the diagnostic biopsy or subsequently removed {31332543}. Early lesions show intratubular proliferations of clear cells. The kidneys may also show multiple small clear cell microtumours in the renal parenchyma, a feature seen in 44% of individuals with VHL-associated RCC {31332543}. VHL-related RCC is often associated with numerous renal cysts {26559379}, including benign cysts lined by one or two layers of clear cells without atypia, atypical cysts with epithelial hyperplasia with or without cytological atypia, and cysts with a component of RCC {31332543; 26559379; 3417291}. A cyst-dependent progression for clear cell RCC has been suggested in VHL {20431476}.

The pancreas in VHL exhibits a vast array of changes, including pancreatic cysts, pancreatic cystadenomas, NETs, and islet abnormalities {15299200; 28697137}. PanNETs are often multiple. Histologically, VHL-related PanNETs may appear identical to sporadic cases; however, they may have some distinctive features that have been attributed to a pseudohypoxic state due to the activation of HIF, such as nuclear atypia, clear cell change, oncocytic cytology, and hypervascularity {15299200}, and they may be cystic {28697137}. They are also associated with precursor lesions that are seen in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (islet dysplasia, microtumours, and peliosis {15299200}), emphasizing the importance of examining the non-tumorous parenchyma. Clear cell pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNENs) have been confused with metastatic RCC in the setting of VHL {11342771}. PanNETs show similar immunohistochemical features to those of sporadic PanNETs, but they also may express inhibin {23913169}.

Papillary cystadenoma of the epididymis is asymptomatic and may not be diagnosed, therefore the percentage of male patients with VHL who are reported to develop these lesions is variable {20367315}; however, a detailed histological autopsy study of 5 male patients with VHL identified that half of the epididymes had papillary cystadenomas, and the other half without these lesions had multifocal epithelial cell proliferations (epithelial microtumours), with four architectural arrangements: complex epithelial change, papillary change, clear cell change, or cystic change {16841375}. Like other lesions associated with VHL, papillary cystadenoma can be bilateral {24441657}. Histologically, this lesion features cystic spaces whose lining has intracystic papillary projections with fibrovascular cores and bland epithelial cells with glycogen-rich clear cytoplasm showing reverse polarity (i.e. the nuclei are oriented away from the basement membrane) {33673939; 20367315; 24441657}. The tumour cells are positive for CK7, usually positive for PAX8, and negative for RCCm {24441657}. The tumour cells are positive for CAIX {33673939; 24441657}.

Papillary cystadenoma of the mesosalpinx and broad ligament in female patients resembles papillary cystadenoma of the epididymis in male patients. Like papillary cystadenoma of the epididymis, papillary cystadenoma of the broad ligament in female patients appears to be a benign tumour in the setting of VHL {22296276}, unlike sporadic cases that may be associated with more aggressive behaviour {22296276}.

Cytology

The cytopathological features of the various neoplasms related to VHL are indistinguishable from those of their sporadic counterparts, which are described elsewhere in this volume and in the other fifth-edition volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of VHL is confirmed by the identification of a germline mutation in VHL that is known to be pathogenic.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: documented pathogenic sequence variant of VHL and manifestation of any of the associated neoplasms.

Desirable: manifestation of haemangioblastomas of the retina and/or CNS and any other associated neoplasm in the absence of a pathogenic sequence variant in VHL.

Staging

There is no uniformly accepted syndrome-specific staging system for VHL. Malignant neoplasms associated with this syndrome generally follow cancer staging suggested by Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria. It is recognized, however, that neoplastic components of this syndrome may not be sufficiently separated from a prognostic standpoint. Such limitations have prompted some groups to offer modifications that may address this inherent weakness {30306741}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for patients with VHL depends on the lesion(s) of each individual patient and kindred. Outcome is improved by screening that allows earlier detection of malignancies.

CNS haemangioblastoma as the initial presentation is associated with a higher risk of death from these lesions {29330336}. Mutations in exon 2 of VHL confer a higher risk and younger age at diagnosis of CNS haemangioblastoma than mutations in other exons {28388566}. Multicentric tumours, and particularly brainstem and spinal tumours, are difficult to treat and cause substantial morbidity. Surgical excision is the treatment of choice, and repeated surgical treatments are more frequently required in patients with VHL than in patients with sporadic haemangioblastomas {31089924}; repeated surgery is associated with a younger age at presentation {33110457}.

Retinal haemangioblastomas are rarely lethal but can cause blindness. Treatments include photocoagulation, cryotherapy, radiation, photodynamic therapy, and combinations of these modalities {17218907; 24138046}. Rarely, surgical excision is necessary.

RCC is the most frequent malignant neoplasm of patients with VHL and is therefore an important determinant of mortality. A larger tumour (> 30 mm) and/or bilateral tumours portend a worse prognosis {24671227; 30745424}. VHL-associated RCCs seem to grow more slowly than sporadic clear cell RCCs {33850741}. The mortality rates of VHL-associated RCC at 5 and 10 years are 4% and 7.5%, respectively {33416974}.

Phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma are not usually a cause of mortality in patients with VHL unless there are cardiovascular complications of hypertension. Metastatic disease is not common in VHL-associated tumours, but multifocal disease can create problems. Bilateral adrenal lesions can result in adrenal insufficiency and the need for complex lifelong medical therapy, so cortical-sparing adrenalectomy should be considered {31397861}. Some extra-adrenal paragangliomas may be surgically unresectable {33939658}, creating a need for medical therapies.

The all-cause mortality risk of VHL-related PanNET is not higher than that of sporadic PanNET; however, the mortality rate is higher in VHL patients with PanNETs than in patients who have no pancreatic neoplasia {33737209}. Advanced tumour stage is associated with a higher mortality risk, emphasizing the importance of surveillance for these tumours in patients with VHL {33737209}. Mutations in exon 3, especially of codons 161/167, increase the risk of metastatic PanNETs {29748190}. Surgical resection is advised for lesions larger than 25–28 mm {29748190; 33512331}, whereas a watch-and-wait approach is recommended for smaller tumours (< 20 mm) {33512331}.

Guidelines for clinical surveillance have been published {24355456; 28620007}.
