8.0.1.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasms in non-endocrine organs: Introduction

Neuroendocrine cells exist in almost every organ and tissue. Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) encompass all tumour types expressing markers of neuroendocrine differentiation. Neuroendocrine biomarkers comprise antigens specific to neuroendocrine cells and neurons. In contrast to neurons usually secreting neurotransmitters to affect an adjacent cell at a synapse, neuroendocrine cells produce peptides for distant (endocrine) and/or local (paracrine and autocrine) function(s). Neuroendocrine markers are constituents of peptide hormone or bioamine synthesis and secretion machinery {29132721; 32839579}. They include (1) transcription factors driving neuroendocrine differentiation, like INSM1 and insulin gene enhancer protein ISL1; (2) components of dense-core vesicles that store peptide hormones like chromogranins/secretogranins; (3) components of small synaptic-like vesicles that store small mediators (taurine, choline, GABA, etc.) like synaptophysin; and (4) receptors involved in neuroendocrine cell control, like SSTR1–5. In addition, neuroendocrine cells express transcription factors specific to their location (e.g. thyroid transcription factor 1 [TTF1], CDX2, SATB2), hormones, and enzymes associated with hormone processing {21808296}. Two main groups of neuroendocrine cells exist: epithelial and neuronal or paraneuronal, distinguished by the expression of keratins in the former.

Neuroendocrine biomarkers display different degrees of specificity and sensitivity when used for diagnostic purposes (see #26020Table 8.01). INSM1 and synaptophysin are expressed in almost all neoplastic cells with neuroendocrine differentiation {31857137}. Both, however, have limitations: INSM1 is expressed in a fraction of non-neuroendocrine thoracic carcinomas and in sarcomas {32203089}, and synaptophysin is expressed in adrenal cortex and pancreatic exocrine neoplasms like solid pseudopapillary neoplasms {27784972; 30037428}. In contrast, expression of chromogranin A and SSTRs is usually strong in well-differentiated epithelial NENs and weak to absent in many/most poorly differentiated epithelial NENs {30140036; 32839579}.

Epithelial neuroendocrine cells are encountered in the anterior pituitary, parathyroids, and pancreatic islets. Widespread neuroendocrine cells constitute the diffuse neuroendocrine system of several body sites, including the lungs, gut, thyroid, thymus, kidneys, urinary tract, liver, reproductive organs, breast, and skin {30140036}. Non-epithelial neuroendocrine tissues include the sympathetic and parasympathetic paraganglia found in every organ and in the soft tissue of the torso {30217041}, and the hypothalamus and posterior pituitary neurons {33641055}.

A rational and coherent classification system has been proposed for NENs for a unified approach to these lesions {30140036}, while recognizing that important organ-specific differences exist, as follows.

Epithelial well-differentiated NEN: Neuroendocrine tumour

Well-differentiated epithelial NENs (neuroendocrine tumours, NETs) are malignant tumours whose cells retain the structural features of hormone synthesis and secretion of normal neuroendocrine cells. The terminology for well-differentiated NENs remains somewhat organ-specific. Based on recommendations from an expert consensus group assembled by WHO in 2017, “neuroendocrine tumour” is the preferred term in most organs, and “carcinoid tumour” is not recommended {30140036}. However, in the lung, “carcinoid tumour” and “atypical carcinoid tumour” remain in widespread use and are considered to be acceptable [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Thoracic tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 5). https://publications.iarc.who.int/595.]].

In sites where specific grading systems have been established, three grades are usually defined: grade 1 (G1), grade 2 (G2), and grade 3 (G3). Most NETs are G1 or G2 and relatively indolent, but high-grade malignancy can also occur, with proliferation in the G3 range. NETs have unique molecular alterations that are distinct from the usual cancer drivers, with prominent epigenetic changes at all sites and different expression profiles {30315258; 33269427; 33433883; 33459926; 33641055; 33786701; 33922803; 33849943}. Differences occur, based on anatomical site. In about one third of NETs there is a familial predisposition (see section Chapter 13: Genetic tumour syndromes associated with endocrine lesions and tumours).

Epithelial poorly differentiated NEN: Neuroendocrine carcinoma

Poorly differentiated epithelial NENs (neuroendocrine carcinomas [NECs]) are high-grade malignant neoplasms whose cells often express little or no chromogranin A, SSTRs, or hormones, but retain a high expression of INSM1 and synaptophysin (as well as the less specific CD56, NSE, and PGP9.5, which are no longer recommended diagnostically). NECs are high-grade by definition, with high proliferative activity and small cell or large cell morphology; they have limited or no site-specific features {30140036}. The molecular profile of NEC is strikingly different from that of NETs and is characterized by TP53 and RB1 gene alterations at most anatomical sites {30140036; 33433884}, with additional site-specific genomic alterations. Poorly differentiated NECs may also contain a minor component of non-neuroendocrine differentiation; especially uncommon is a component of well-differentiated NET.

Epithelial mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm

Mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs; defined as “combined” in the lung and other sites) are epithelial cancers whose cells express neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine features in separate morphologically and immunohistochemically recognizable populations {32839579; 33686305; 33686306; 33686307; 33786701}. Equally significant components express neuroendocrine differentiation and a squamous, glandular, or other non-neuroendocrine phenotype. Although the two cell subsets of MiNEN are usually morphologically distinct, transitional areas may be seen. The presence of only minor cancer subpopulations of either phenotype do not qualify for a MiNEN diagnosis. In the digestive system, cut-off points are defined [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]]. Both MiNEN cancer components are graded according to current grading systems. The neuroendocrine component is usually a NEC, but NETs can be rarely seen.

Non-epithelial NEN: Paraganglioma

Non-epithelial NENs are represented by paragangliomas, both intra-adrenal (known as phaeochromocytoma) and extra-adrenal {30217041}. Although they also show a spectrum of differentiation, they express INSM1, GATA3, synaptophysin, and chromogranin A. Unlike epithelial NENs, paragangliomas express vimentin and are negative for keratins. Most paragangliomas also express tyrosine hydroxylase, a key enzyme in the catecholamine biosynthetic pathway. They all have malignant potential, but metastasis is uncommon. Paragangliomas very commonly have a genetic predisposition, and some of the familial syndromes overlap with those associated with NETs {33433885}.

Other NEN types

NEN types that bridge epithelial and neuronal NENs may be observed but are extremely rare. These epithelial-neuronal NENs are characterized by two neuroendocrine cell components, one expressing markers of neural differentiation (usually ganglion cells, with or without Schwannian stroma, but occasionally neurocytic) and one epithelial. Both epithelial and neuronal components are well differentiated and produce peptide hormones. Epithelial-neuronal NENs, termed composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumours (CoGNETs), occur in the pituitary, gut, and cauda equina region of the spine.

 

NENs belonging to endocrine organs, including the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, and endocrine pancreas, as well as paragangliomas, are discussed in dedicated chapters elsewhere in this volume. This chapter provides an overview of epithelial NENs in non-endocrine organs.
