2.1.2.1 Corticotroph PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Corticotroph pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is a well-differentiated PitNET composed of TPIT-lineage adenohypophysial cells with corticotroph differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8272/3 Corticotroph PitNET/adenoma

8272/3 Densely granulated corticotroph tumour

8272/3 Sparsely granulated corticotroph tumour

8272/3 Crooke cell tumour

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: corticotroph tumour.

Not recommended: ACTH-positive PitNET/adenoma; ACTH-producing PitNET/adenoma; ACTH-producing tumour/adenoma; ACTH tumour/adenoma; ACTHoma; corticotropinoma.

Subtype(s)

Densely granulated corticotroph tumour; sparsely granulated corticotroph tumour; Crooke cell tumour

Localization

Most corticotroph tumours are located in the pituitary gland. Ectopic manifestations may rarely occur in the nasal cavity, sphenoidal sinus, cavernous sinus, orbit, clivus, and suprasellar region {21803709; 24065616; 24927792; 25639312}. Corticotroph tumours can extremely rarely occur in teratomas {16137552; 33364180}.

Clinical features

Cushing syndrome is the constellation of symptoms and signs related to cortisol excess {26156970}. If the syndrome is caused by a corticotroph tumour (or very rarely hyperplasia), it is classified as Cushing disease {25425660; 26156970}. Most corticotroph tumours are biochemically functioning; however, some corticotroph tumours lack clinical and biochemical evidence of ACTH and cortisol excess, and are called silent corticotroph tumours {6244736; 10981760; 22619024; 25534889; 26177314; 34017065}. These silent corticotroph tumours account for 3–6% of all pituitary tumours, 10–20% of silent pituitary tumours, and about 40% of all corticotroph tumours {30020466}. Silent corticotroph tumours may more frequently present with neurological or ophthalmic symptoms, including pituitary apoplexy {15343517; 22619024; 22697380; 26177314}.

The diagnosis of Cushing disease is challenging. Many symptoms and signs, including insulin resistance and diabetes mellitus, depression, obesity, and hypertension, are common in the general population, whereas Cushing disease is relatively rare. In children, weight gain with growth failure, due in part to inhibition of growth hormone release, is characteristic {25425660}. Cyclic Cushing syndrome may be a feature of any form of Cushing disease {22697380}. Complicating the diagnosis further, there are pseudo-Cushing states (e.g. morbid obesity, depression, anxiety, alcoholism, pregnancy, and certain medication effects) that are associated with variable degrees of hypercortisolism, simulating Cushing syndrome {22697380; 25425660; 26156970}. Severe Cushing disease and Nelson syndrome (rapid enlargement of a corticotroph tumour after bilateral adrenalectomy) may cause hyperpigmentation due to an overproduction of POMC-derived peptides.

Biochemical investigations

The principal diagnostic tests rely on the autonomous nature of cortisol release in Cushing syndrome, assessment of normal feedback, and loss of circadian rhythmicity. The most reliable tests evaluate resistance to dexamethasone feedback, including the overnight test (1 mg dexamethasone given at midnight, serum cortisol measured at 09:00 the following morning) and the 2-day Liddle test (0.5 mg given every 6 h for 48 h, serum cortisol measured at 09:00 at the 48 h mark). The loss of the circadian rhythm may be demonstrated by elevated late-night serum or salivary cortisol. If the disorder is ACTH-dependent, it is usually due to pituitary-dependent Cushing syndrome (Cushing disease). The diagnosis requires confirmation using imaging studies.

Neuroimaging

Most patients with Cushing disease have a solitary microtumour, and about 5–10% have a macrotumour {15886242}. About 10% of patients with Cushing disease have no visible pituitary mass on MRI, even using high-definition 3 Tesla MRI and dynamic imaging software {26477323}. In this situation, bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling can verify a central source of ACTH. Recent studies have suggested a possible role for 11C-methionine PET-CT or 68Ga CRH PET-CT imaging in the localization of pituitary microtumours {26245763; 33079979}.

Densely granulated corticotroph tumours are mainly microtumours, whereas silent corticotroph tumours, sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours, and Crooke cell tumours often manifest as macrotumours {14508394; 30035294} [[Asa SL, Perry A. Tumors of the pituitary gland. Rockville (MD): American Registry of Pathology; 2020. (AFIP atlas of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 1).]]. Sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours are frequently hyperintense on T2-weighted MRI {29460202}. Silent corticotroph tumours tend to present as large tumours invading the cavernous sinuses {17881972; 22619024; 23685641; 25534889}.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence of pituitary Cushing disease is 3–10 cases per 1 million population {25609777}. This may well be an underestimate, because patients with mild, subclinical Cushing disease may go undiagnosed, and these cases have often been misclassified as silent corticotroph tumours {15343517}. Corticotroph tumours account for 15–17% of all PitNETs in surgical series {29434339; 32154573; 32306401}. The peak incidence is at age 30–50 years. There is a male predominance among prepubertal patients, whereas a female preponderance is seen in adults {8052272; 15292311; 26036813; 29434339; 32306401}. Corticotroph tumours account for approximately 55% of PitNETs in children aged < 11 years {10599692} and 75% of cases of Cushing syndrome in children aged > 5 years {26036813; 33515368}. The most common histological subtype is densely granulated corticotroph tumour, followed by sparsely granulated tumours; Crooke cell tumours are rare {29434339}.

Etiology

Genetic and epigenetic factors, abnormal hormonal stimulation, and dysregulation of growth factors and their receptors contribute to the tumorigenesis of corticotroph tumours.

Pathogenesis

Most somatic gene alterations described in corticotroph tumours are not specific to that tumour type {22098190; 25639319; 33433883}. However, activating somatic USP8 mutations are found in about 25–60% of corticotroph tumours {25485838; 25675982; 25942478; 26578638; 28505279; 30315484; 31273566; 31581124; 32053843}; they are associated with increased POMC mRNA levels and increased EGFR (HER1) expression {25485838; 25675982; 25942478; 26012588}. USP8 mutations are most often found in functioning densely granulated corticotroph tumours and are usually absent in silent corticotroph tumours {25675982; 25942478; 32185515}. USP8 mutation–driven corticotroph tumours have higher expression levels of SSTR5 and MGMT than USP8-wildtype tumours {26578638}, suggesting possible responsiveness to pasireotide {26578638}. USP48 {30093687; 31717455} and BRAF mutations have also been reported in corticotroph tumours {30093687}. TP53 alterations have been identified in a subset of USP8-wildtype corticotroph tumours in one series {31222332; 33221858}, and in association with ATRX mutations in aggressive and metastatic corticotroph tumours {33106857}.

DAXX-related and/or ATRX-related alternative lengthening of telomeres is a feature of recurrent/aggressive mostly functioning corticotroph tumours {28719461; 33106857; 32203094}. Increased LGALS3/galectin-3 expression was reported in aggressive corticotroph tumours {24007691}. Invasive corticotroph tumours and Crooke cell tumours are associated with increased expression of miR-106b~25 cluster and its host gene MCM7 on 7q22 {28432562}. MCM7 protein expression is also higher in invasive corticotroph tumours {26620390}.

Corticotroph tumours often show normal expression of stimulatory CRH and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) receptors, normal glucocorticoid receptor expression, and slightly diminished ACTH feedback receptor expression, with no frequent mutations commonly found; the ratio of 11β-dehydrogenase isozymes 1 and 2 is altered, resulting in disrupted cortisol feedback, but this is not specific to corticotroph tumours. The expression of cyclins, CDKs, and their regulators is altered; the specific alterations in corticotroph tumours are the greatly reduced expression of p27 and the increased expression of cyclin E, and there are also changes in the feedback transcription factors SMARCA4 and HDAC2 {17043312}. Like other pituitary tumours, corticotroph tumours show increased activity of the pro-proliferative signalling pathways based on PI3K/mTOR and MAPK {22098190; 21264206}. A recent study suggested miR-182-5p as a candidate circulating biomarker of Cushing disease {33692756}.

Transcriptome analyses of a small series identified overrepresentation of genes related to endoplasmic reticulum processing in functioning corticotroph tumours {33066652}. Silent corticotroph tumours have a specific pattern of expression of adhesion molecules, which differs from that in functioning tumours and is suggested to play a role in their aggressiveness {33066652}. The latter would be in line with the differential expression of biomarkers related to cell invasion, motility, and migration in silent corticotroph tumours as compared with gonadotroph tumours {24091601}. In addition, loss of membranous E-cadherin expression, a hallmark of epithelial–mesenchymal transition, has been correlated with larger and more invasive corticotroph tumours {21595730}. Methylome studies also showed distinct signatures between functioning and silent corticotroph tumours {32185515}, and exclusive GATA3 expression in silent corticotroph tumours {32185515}. Epigenetic silencing of p27 has been reported to be mediated by Skp2-regulated degradation in corticotroph tumours {12408227}, but this is disputed {14558671}.

FGFR4 polymorphisms have been shown to play a role in tumour growth and sensitivity to glucocorticoid hormone negative feedback through STAT3 signalling; patients homozygous for the FGFR4-R388 variant have a higher frequency of silent corticotroph macrotumours than those with FGFR4-G388, who were more likely to have small but hormonally active microtumours {24625004}.

Genetic susceptibility

Only very few cases of Cushing disease have been reported in familial isolated pituitary adenoma (FIPA) {20507346}, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) {21916912}, or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 (CDKN1B) {21264206}. Pathogenic germline USP8 variants can cause paediatric Cushing disease {31162547}. There have been rare case reports of corticotroph tumours in patients with tuberous sclerosis (TSC1/TSC2) {16343106; 17596199}, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 {23072303}, and Lynch syndrome {28701629; 28938458}, and glucocorticoid resistance syndrome due to germline NR3C1 mutations {8863343}. Germline pathogenic CABLES1 variants were reported in corticotroph tumours {28533356}. Although pathogenic germline DICER1 mutations are characteristics of pituitary blastomas (see section Pituitary blastoma), rare examples of missense germline DICER1 variants have been reported in USP8-wildtype paediatric corticotroph tumours; however, at the somatic level, none of these had loss of heterozygosity for the DICER1 variants {32714280}.

Macroscopic appearance

Corticotroph tumours are often soft and white to greyish-red.

Histopathology

Corticotroph tumours are composed of neuroendocrine cells with variable amounts of basophilic secretory granules that are highlighted using PAS staining and ACTH immunohistochemistry. Corticotroph tumours typically have a sheet-like architecture, but they may also show papillary-like patterns such as perivascular pseudorosettes. The interface with normal gland may be indistinct, with infiltration of adjacent intact normal parenchymal elements by the tumour cells in small groups or singly. Morphologically, corticotroph tumours have been divided into three histological subtypes on the basis of their density of secretory granules and organization of their cytoplasmic keratin filaments using low-molecular-weight cytokeratin reactivity patterns. These include (1) densely granulated corticotroph tumours, (2) sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours, and (3) Crooke cell tumours. Most corticotroph tumours fall into one of these histological subtypes; however, occasional tumours have both sparsely and densely granulated components, and some have focal Crooke cell change {29434339}. The term “silent corticotroph tumour” is not a histological subtype; these tumours can feature all morphological subtypes of corticotroph tumours {10981760; 12446925; 22619024; 22697380; 28458905}. Most corticotroph tumours are solitary neoplasms; rarely, however, they may represent a component of a double PitNET {26067545; 11383480; 25402386}, or they may occur in combination with a gangliocytoma {8264895; 7921420} or (extremely rarely) an adrenocortical choristoma {9527001; 11081209; 23872913; 34296770; 33586921}.

Immunohistochemistry

Corticotroph tumours are positive for ACTH, TPIT, and NeuroD1, and they are negative for PIT1, SF1, and hormones of PIT1 lineage (growth hormone, prolactin, and TSH) and of SF1 lineage (gonadotropins) {22697380; 24144285; 26177314; 29434339}. Rarely, corticotroph tumours can express GATA2/GATA3 and α-subunit {18228160; 30390035; 32193825}. ACTH staining may be weak or absent in some cases, emphasizing the importance of pituitary transcription factors for correct classification {28823042; 32813227}. Corticotroph tumours usually have diffuse strong cytoplasmic keratin expression using CAM5.2 {22697380; 26177314; 29434339; 33098891}. The concentric pattern of keratin staining in Crooke cells is distinctive {33098891}. The proliferative activity of corticotroph tumours is variable {29434339; 32306401}. Loss of p27 (encoded by CDKN1B) is a frequent finding in corticotroph tumours {9467944; 11579684; 12408227; 25639319; 29434339}, and a more common in functioning corticotroph tumours than in silent corticotroph tumours {29434339}.

Densely granulated corticotroph tumours have a basophilic appearance on H&E-stained sections. The tumour cells have abundant secretory granules, which correlate with their diffuse and strong staining for PAS and ACTH {22697380; 24144285; 26177314}.

Sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours are composed of faintly basophilic or chromophobic PAS-positive tumour cells with weak or patchy positivity for ACTH, consistent with the scant, small secretory granules seen ultrastructurally {22697380; 24144285; 26177314; 26481628}. Rarely, ACTH can be negative, and diffuse TPIT positivity confirms corticotroph differentiation {26481628}.

Crooke cell tumours are composed of neuroendocrine cells with Crooke hyaline change. Ring-like cytokeratin expression is typical of these neoplasms {22697380; 24144285; 26177314; 29453601}. Positivity for PAS and ACTH is dislocated to the cell periphery and juxtanuclear region. Earlier studies suggested CK20 as a marker of corticotroph and Crooke cells {16299403; 17525485}; however, a recent study failed to reproduce this finding {33098891}, instead confirming CAM5.2, CK18, and AE1/AE3 as appropriate keratins to highlight Crooke hyaline change {33098891}.

Differential diagnosis

Corticotroph hyperplasia is an extremely rare cause of Cushing disease {26063496}, but it is often not identified in surgical pathology practice; however, it has been reported in the setting of ectopic CRH-secreting tumours {1283118; 17039427; 23233312; 25183169; 26202047; 23478233} or untreated Addison disease {24216004; 23478233; 25004119}.

The assessment of the surrounding anterior pituitary is critical for identifying Crooke hyaline change in non-tumorous corticotrophs. This morphological biomarker of glucocorticoid excess occurs in about 80% of patients with functioning corticotroph tumours {26147609; 33815279} and is not seen in patients with pseudo-Cushing states or silent corticotroph tumours {22697380; 24144285; 26177314}.

Cytology

Although cytology is not used in the routine diagnostic setting, a homogeneous cellular smear consisting of cells with basophilic granular cytoplasm suggests a corticotroph tumour.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no molecular tests required to diagnose corticotroph tumours. A lack of ATRX immunoreactivity is a marker of aggressive and/or metastatic tumours and should be confirmed by molecular genetic analysis. Germline screening for inherited tumours may be considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Densely granulated corticotroph tumours

Essential: TPIT positivity; diffuse ACTH or PAS reactivity; negativity for PIT1 and SF1; often diffuse keratin; basophilic cytoplasm.

Sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours

Essential: TPIT positivity; variable to absent/scarce ACTH or PAS expression; negativity for PIT1 and SF1; often diffuse keratin; faintly basophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm.

Crooke cell tumours

Essential: TPIT positivity; relocation of ACTH expression and PAS-positive granules to the cell periphery; perinuclear, ring-like keratin.

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour extension based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}.

There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. An expert pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

Persistent untreated Cushing disease is lethal {26067718}. Surgery is the treatment of choice and is often curative for microtumours. The surgical cure rate is lower in macrotumours with cavernous sinus invasion. Silent corticotroph tumours may tend to be more aggressive in terms of invasion and recurrence rate {10981760; 25216855}. However, a recent meta-analysis could not confirm a higher recurrence risk of silent corticotroph tumours compared with other non-functioning PitNETs {30590584}.

Crooke cell tumours are often associated with clinically aggressive disease {14508394; 23134557} (a case of invasive and metastatic Crooke cell tumour is demonstrated in section Metastatic PitNET); however, sparsely granulated corticotroph tumours are also associated with some degree of biological aggressivity {30035294; 34135861} that requires further validation.

Like other types of PitNETs, invasive and proliferative corticotroph tumours show an increased risk of disease recurrence/progression {23400299; 32306401} and metastatic spread {28420444}. ATRX-mutated corticotroph tumours exhibit aggressive behaviour {33106857}. An absence of Crooke hyaline change of non-tumorous corticotrophs may predict recurrence in patients with functioning corticotroph tumours {33815279}. SSTR5 immunohistochemistry may help predict responsiveness to pasireotide. Clinically aggressive corticotroph tumours with low or absent MGMT expression have been reported to respond to temozolomide treatment {21061089; 21822153; 22076588; 25646794; 33480240}.
