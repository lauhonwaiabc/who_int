3.3.1.1A Medullary thyroid carcinoma

Definition

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a malignant neuroendocrine tumour derived from the calcitonin-producing parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland.

ICD-O coding

8345/3 Medullary thyroid carcinoma

8345/3 Medullary microcarcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2D10.4 Medullary carcinoma of thyroid gland

Related terminology

Acceptable: C-cell carcinoma.

Not recommended: parafollicular cell carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Medullary microcarcinoma

Localization

Although MTCs may arise throughout the thyroid, they follow the distribution of normal C cells and so are most likely to arise in the region of highest C-cell density at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the thyroid lobes.

Clinical features

MTC typically presents as a solitary palpable thyroid nodule in the upper to middle thirds of the lateral lobes {24343523}. Invasion of surrounding structures may occur, resulting in dysphagia or respiratory difficulty. At diagnosis, lymph node metastases are present in 50–75% of patients {25351655; 25810047} and distant metastasis in 10–15% {27313709}. The most common sites for metastatic disease are the mediastinum, liver, lungs, and bone {24037980}. The high levels of serum calcitonin secreted from these tumours can lead to symptoms such as flushing, diarrhoea, and/or weight loss {24037980}. Rarely (< 1% of cases), these tumours can be non-secretory {31142313}. CEA is another useful serum tumour marker, found in > 50% of patients with MTC {24037980}. An increasing CEA level in the presence of a stable calcitonin level can be a sign of dedifferentiation of the tumour and is associated with a worse prognosis. Conversely, a rapid decrease in serum CEA predicts cure by surgery {20664475; 18629788}. Some tumours secrete various other hormones such as serotonin, substance P, VIP, ACTH, CRH, and catecholamine metabolites {24037980}. This may give rise to paraneoplastic Cushing syndrome {31885947}.

Patients with hereditary MTC commonly present at a younger age and with multifocal and bilateral disease. They can present with diarrhoea, flushing, or weight loss caused by excessive calcitonin secretion by the tumour, or they can be diagnosed after presenting with an associated disease such as phaeochromocytoma or hyperparathyroidism {24037980}. Currently, most patients with hereditary MTC are identified by genetic testing of at-risk family members, resulting in younger patient ages at diagnosis and earlier identification of tumours in appropriately screened individuals {23744408}.

Imaging

Preoperative ultrasonography typically demonstrates nonspecific features of malignancy, including hypoechoic solid nodules with irregular borders and intranodular vascularization {25344474}. Functional imaging modalities, primarily FDOPA and FDG PET-CT, play a crucial role in the evaluation of disease extent during the initial staging and postoperative follow-up of patients with MTC {31360432}. FDOPA PET-CT is particularly sensitive for detecting occult metastatic disease and is typically followed by targeted imaging using alternative techniques depending on specific organ involvement {22223169; 31360432}.

Epidemiology

MTC accounts for approximately 2% of thyroid malignancies and 8% of thyroid cancer–related deaths in the USA {33492588}. The average age at diagnosis of sporadic MTC is 45–55 years, and there is a slight female predominance {33492588}. As many as 25% of MTCs are hereditary, resulting from autosomal dominant germline gain-of-function mutations in the RET proto-oncogene, indicating multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A) or type 2B (MEN2B) {24699901; 31896937}. These patients typically present in the second and third decades of life {33114488}. Hereditary isolated MTC, formerly known as familial MTC, is recognized as a subtype of MEN2A by the American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines on MTC published in 2015 {25810047}.

Etiology

About 25% of cases of MTC are associated with germline RET mutation (i.e. MEN2 syndrome). The etiology of sporadic MTC is unknown. There is no evidence of an association with external ionizing radiation, occupational exposure, or cigarette smoking {22960778}. A number of studies have demonstrated an association between sporadic MTC and goitre or thyroid nodules {12074506; 22960778}. Some authors have also reported an association between MTC and Hashimoto thyroiditis in female patients, although this finding requires confirmation in larger prospective studies {25056007}. Germline MET mutation is associated with familial MTC {29219214}.

Pathogenesis

Activating RET mutations drive tumorigenesis via the activation of multiple pathways including the MAPK and PI3K pathways, which promotes cell growth, proliferation, survival, and differentiation {26868437}. Alternate mechanisms of activating the MAPK pathway, including RAS mutations, drive many RET-wildtype MTCs {22865907; 31605946; 21325462; 23264394; 24828033; 27635947}. Epigenetic and other alterations, including somatic copy-number loss of CDKN2C {27610696}, mTOR pathway activation {25163725; 22753663}, increased expression of the histone methyltransferases EZH2 and SMYD3 {24813658}, and TERT promoter methylation and copy-number gain {26870890}, have also been reported to contribute to neoplastic progression.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours range in size from < 1 mm in diameter to those that replace the entire thyroid lobe. Sporadic MTC is typically unilateral, whereas heritable MTC is often bilateral and multicentric {24343523}. The tumours are typically well circumscribed but unencapsulated with a solid tan-yellow cut surface. Occasionally, they may appear infiltrative {15805688}. Prophylactic thyroidectomies in patients with familial syndromes require complete examination of the thyroid, because gross tumours may not be seen.

Tumours measuring < 10 mm in diameter have been termed medullary microcarcinoma {24343523}. Most of these tumours are found incidentally in thyroids removed for other reasons or have been detected on the basis of abnormal calcitonin levels in patients with nodular thyroid disease {24343523}. Some authors have suggested that the term “microcarcinoma” should be reserved for tumours measuring < 5 mm, because tumours of this size have not been associated with any reports of nodal metastasis or postoperative hypercalcitoninaemia {19568813; 29336779; 30366876; 31343983}.

Histopathology

The morphological spectrum of MTC is broad and includes features that may mimic the entire spectrum of thyroid malignancies. Typically, tumours show solid, nested, insular, or trabecular growth patterns separated by varying amounts of fibrovascular stroma that give rise to an organoid architecture {15805688; 24343523}. The cells are typically polygonal with granular amphophilic cytoplasm and fine nuclear chromatin {15805688}. Nucleoli are usually inconspicuous. Various other cell types can also be seen, including plasmacytoid and spindle-shaped cells, with frequent admixtures of more than one cell type in a single tumour. There is typically a low to moderate degree of nuclear atypia, although some cases show scattered markedly pleomorphic cells with bizarre nuclei and sometimes multinucleated forms. Occasional nuclear pseudoinclusions may be present. Mitotic activity is usually low and tumour necrosis is uncommon {32313184; 32452872}. Calcification, including psammoma-like bodies and ossification, may be seen.

Stromal amyloid deposition is seen in 50–90% of cases and may be highlighted with Congo red histochemistry {15459123; 33575974}. Spectrometric analyses have shown that calcitonin is the primary constituent of the amyloid in MTC, although a number of amyloid-associated proteins, such as katacalcin, apolipoprotein E, and vitronectin, have also been shown to colocalize with the deposits {15459123; 26304852; 29581064}.

Electron microscopy reveals small (< 200 nm in diameter) and medium-sized (200–300 nm in diameter) secretory granules with round, uniform cores surrounded by a tight or loosely fitted membrane {2499093}. Both granule types contain calcitonin {633851; 839736}.

Tumours from patients with heritable forms of MTC are histologically indistinguishable from those occurring sporadically. An association with C-cell hyperplasia has been reported in inherited forms of MTC; however, this finding is not specific and is often reported in association with sporadic tumours and in thyroids removed for reasons other than MTC {8989230; 2900208; 30694510}.

Immunohistochemistry

MTC is positive for calcitonin in 95% of cases, although the staining may be focal {24343523}. Neuroendocrine markers, such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, NSE, and INSM1, are positive. CGRP is also positive in a high proportion of cases, and occasional calcitonin-negative cases may express CGRP {24599901; 31142313}. CEA is expressed in most cases and may be expressed in the rare calcitonin-negative cases {24343523}. Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) is expressed in most cases, although the intensity of staining is typically less than in normal follicular cells and their tumours {10792482; 10981870}. MTC is negative for thyroglobulin. However thyroglobulin from adjacent non-neoplastic follicles may leak over the neoplastic cells and cause difficulty in interpretation in small biopsies. Staining for polyclonal PAX8 is variable and weak {16669872; 18084247}. However, MTC is negative for monoclonal PAX8 {31912298}. SSTR2A and SSTR5 may be expressed {23150029}. It is noteworthy that calcitonin can be expressed in a variety of extrathyroid neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) especially in intermediate-grade laryngeal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) {15098009}. The lack of diffuse and strong TTF1 in the latter can help separate it from MTC {15098009}.

Histological patterns

Numerous histological patterns of MTC have been described. Although most of these patterns have no prognostic significance, their recognition is important to avoid confusion with other tumour types {24343523}. In most instances, tumours show admixtures of conventional and variant cytoarchitectural morphologies, and the quantitative criteria used to define these patterns in heterogeneous tumours have yet to be established. The papillary and pseudopapillary patterns may be particularly challenging to distinguish from papillary thyroid carcinoma. In the papillary pattern, the tumour cells are aligned along true fibrovascular cores that are often hyalinized and contain amyloid deposits {12114676; 23894022}. The tumour cells may also contain intranuclear pseudoinclusions, making the distinction from papillary thyroid carcinoma even more challenging {25246133}. More commonly, there is a pseudopapillary pattern, in which groups of tumour cells are artifactually separated from the adjacent stroma owing to tissue fragmentation {23894022; 25246133}.

In the follicular (tubular/glandular) pattern, the tumour cells are arranged in follicular structures that may contain luminal eosinophilic material that may represent secreted calcitonin and other proteins {12114753}. Unlike in follicular carcinoma, the tumour cells and these secretions are calcitonin-positive and thyroglobulin-negative {12114753}.

The spindle cell pattern is composed of elongated fusiform cells arranged in loose clusters or as single units {24316908}. Some tumours may show a striking angiosarcoma-like appearance {17580345}. Tumours may also contain a predominant population of plasmacytoid cells, squamous cells, giant cells, clear cells, or oncocytic cells {24343523}. The oncocytic pattern of MTC often mimics oncocytic follicular cell neoplasms, featuring sheets of polygonal cells with abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm {31727331}. The melanotic pattern is extremely rare and is characterized by variable amounts of melanin pigment in the cytoplasm of tumour and/or stromal cells {30424779}. The amphicrine pattern is composed of cells containing both mucin and calcitonin {4060258; 9355239}.

The paraganglioma-like pattern resembles the classic growth pattern of paraganglioma/phaeochromocytoma with a Zellballen architecture and possible S100-positive sustentacular-like cells {10674273; 16405409; 18323289; 18631354; 22209341; 31778356; 32125784}. The presence of cytokeratin and TTF1 distinguishes this MTC pattern from intrathyroidal paraganglioma, which lacks these markers.

The encapsulated pattern is characterized by a complete fibrous capsule, and these tumours may appear entirely cystic macroscopically {12165666; 31190466}.

The small cell pattern, reminiscent of small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC), is very rare. Diagnosis of this pattern should be made with caution, particularly when the tumour cells do not express calcitonin {29323074}. Calcitonin can be normal or only slightly elevated in serum in this pattern {21053568}. Some tumours initially interpreted as small cell MTC have since been shown to represent metastatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) originating in other locations {9236831; 18446450}. It has also been suggested that some of these tumours may be related to the Ewing tumour family on the basis of EWSR1::FLI1 rearrangements {23637256}.

Cytology

Smears are usually highly cellular with single dispersed plasmacytoid, polygonal, round, or spindle cells usually predominating, with a variable number of loosely cohesive or syncytial tissue fragments and in some cases binucleated or giant cells {21157553; 28643384}. Nuclei are round to oval with mixed fine and coarse, stippled chromatin, resembling neuroendocrine nuclei, with occasional nuclear pseudoinclusions and inconspicuous nucleoli. The eccentric cytoplasm varies in amount and may contain azurophilic/red (Giemsa) granules. Amyloid may be seen in variable amounts in the background as relatively dense and hard-edged, amorphous mauve (Giemsa) or pale-green to eosinophilic (Pap) material {21157553}. When tumour cells are round or polyhedral with finely granular cytoplasm, the differential diagnosis is follicular cell–derived neoplasms {18684023; 28802094; 31190466; 32827355}, but cellular dyscohesion is a hallmark of MTC {32827355}. Metastatic NEC and melanoma can also mimic MTC. The FNAB diagnosis of MTC can be challenging, owing to its diverse appearances and morphological overlap with other thyroid neoplasms, and the diagnostic accuracy is less consistent than for follicular cell–derived neoplasms {25047365}. In a meta-analysis of 641 MTCs, the overall sensitivity was found to be only 56.4% {25047365}. Immunocytochemistry for calcitonin and neuroendocrine markers is helpful in making the correct diagnosis. Elevated serum calcitonin may aid in the identification of MTC in nodules that have a benign ultrasonographic appearance {25041034; 31708652}. In a retrospective analysis of 140 patients with MTC, the sensitivities of FNAB and serum calcitonin for the diagnosis were 51.3% and 98.7%, respectively {27313709}. Measurement of calcitonin levels in the washout fluid of thyroid FNAB in patients with borderline or elevated serum calcitonin values has been shown to have a high sensitivity {19636196; 23627255}. Molecular testing provides another strategy for improving the diagnostic accuracy of FNAB {33269526}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Approximately 25% of patients with MTC will have MEN2, and germline testing for RET mutations is recommended for all patients, regardless of family history {25810047}. Depending on available resources, one approach is to first sequence mutation hotspots – the cysteine codons in exons 10 and 11 (codons 609, 611, 618, 620, 630, and 634), and additional RET codon mutations in exons 13, 14, 15, and 16 {31510104}. Other institutions sequence the entire RET coding region or use a 2-tiered approach, beginning with hotspot exons.

Somatic-only RET mutations have been reported in approximately half of all sporadic MTCs {18073307; 19401695; 21422198; 22404432; 22865907; 31605946} and are important to identify, because MTCs associated with either somatic or germline RET mutations are likely to respond to treatment with targeted RET inhibitors {32846061}.

The most common somatic RET mutation in sporadic MTC is the RET p.M918T mutation, which accounts for as many as 80% of RET mutations in sporadic MTC {18073307; 21325462; 21422198; 22404432; 22865907; 31605946; 37842841}. Mutations at codon 634 account for approximately 15% of RET mutations {18073307}. Interestingly, the prevalence of the p.M918T mutation is substantially lower in medullary thyroid microcarcinomas {22404432}, and substantially higher in the setting of advanced disease {27468888}, suggesting that RET mutations might be secondary events driving tumorigenesis rather than initiators of tumour development {22545752}. In addition, aneuploidy (trisomy and tetrasomy) of chromosome 10 is seen in approximately one quarter of sporadic MTCs in a variable proportion of tumour cells, resulting in RET copy-number alterations, with a higher prevalence detected in RET-mutant MTC {21867742}.

A few non-RET molecular alterations have been reported in MTC. Approximately 70% of tumours that are wildtype for RET harbour RAS gene mutations, with HRAS mutations being the most common, followed by KRAS mutations, and rarely NRAS mutations {22865907; 31605946; 21325462; 23264394; 24828033; 27635947}. In a recent study, RAS alterations were found in 24% of sporadic MTCs, with 17% harbouring HRAS mutations, 7% having KRAS mutations, and < 1% having NRAS mutations {31605946}. RET and RAS mutations are virtually always mutually exclusive; however, rare tumours harbouring both RET and RAS mutations have been reported {31605946; 21325462}. Fewer than 20% of sporadic MTCs are wildtype for both RET and RAS {31605946; 23264394}, and sequencing studies have found that these tumours also lack other recurrent mutations {23264394; 24828033; 27207748; 30120715; 31605946}. There have been isolated reports of RET and ALK gene rearrangements {25546157; 26295973}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a primary non-follicular cell–derived thyroid tumour with morphological and immunohistochemical features of neuroendocrine derivation including expression of calcitonin and/or CEA.

Desirable: lack of expression of thyroglobulin.

Staging

MTC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Among patients presenting with a palpable thyroid nodule, the rate of clinical cervical lymph node involvement at the time of diagnosis is as high as 75% in some series {25351655}, and the rate of distant metastasis is as high as 15% {20495084; 31896937; 27313709}. Nodal metastases most commonly involve central compartment nodes (affected in 50–75% of cases), the ipsilateral jugulocarotid chain (in 50–60%), and the contralateral jugulocarotid chain (in 25–50%) {19836492; 12727956; 26081082}. Distant metastases occur most commonly in the liver, lungs, and bones, and less frequently in other sites such as the brain, skin, adrenal glands, and breast {20627492; 25810047; 30461619}.

Reported 5-year and 10-year overall survival rates are 75–96% and 64–91%, respectively, depending primarily on disease stage {17019736; 18331612; 20878247; 30618340}. In cases of hereditary MTC, early detection of thyroid-confined disease through biochemical or genetic screening has resulted in significant improvements in prognosis {25810047}, with some studies reporting overall survival rates comparable to those of the general population {30618340}. It has also recently been reported that patients with early-stage disease and without regional lymph node metastasis have similar survival rates to the general population {30618340}. Other factors known to influence survival include larger tumour size, older age, male sex, extrathyroidal extension, and serum calcitonin and CEA levels {10699905; 18073307; 25366585; 25810047; 30051240; 30223887; 30800639}.

RET mutations have also been shown to be prognostically significant in MTC. In sporadic tumours, the presence of a RET mutation has been shown to correlate with higher disease stage at diagnosis, disease recurrence, and shorter survival {11241313; 18073307; 27468888; 29615431; 37842841}. Specifically, tumours harbouring RET mutations in exons 15 and 16 (which includes the RET p.M918T mutation) are associated with the worst outcome, whereas tumours with other RET mutations have the most indolent behaviour, and those with no RET mutations tend to have an intermediate prognosis {19401695}. Thus, the poor prognosis associated with RET-mutant sporadic MTC is largely attributable to the high rate of the RET p.M918T mutation {33492588}. In patients with MEN2, the specific location of the inherited RET mutation also determines the timeline of disease manifestation and prognosis {33492588}. The vast majority of patients with MEN2B harbour the aggressive RET p.M918T mutation and, if this is unrecognized, they typically die by the age of 21 years {33492588}. Patients with MEN2A present with MTC between 25 and 35 years of age, with lymph node metastases present in > 70% {33492588}. The age of disease manifestation in MEN2A is determined by the specific mutation present, with tumours harbouring the exon 11 RET p.C634 mutation demonstrating an earlier onset than tumours with all other mutations (largely in exon 10) {33492588}. MTCs associated with both sporadic and germline RET mutations have demonstrated a good response with rationally designed RET inhibitors {32846061}.

Until recently there has been no widely accepted histopathological grading scheme for MTC. Recently, histological grading schemes based on proliferative activity (mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index) and tumour necrosis {32452872; 32313184} have been validated. In the original studies {32313184; 32452872} and in a subsequent validation cohort {33763905}, the grading systems had significant impacts on overall survival {32452872}, disease-specific survival {32313184; 33763905}, local recurrence–free survival {32313184; 32452872}, and distant metastasis–free survival {32313184}. Subsequently, a 2-tiered consensus grading scheme has been developed {32313184; 32452872}. Using this grading system, high-grade MTCs are defined as tumours with at least one of the following three features: mitotic count ≥ 5 mitoses/2 mm2, Ki-67 proliferation index ≥ 5%, and/or tumour necrosis {34731032} (see #31219Table 3.07). It is emphasized that the Ki-67 index and mitotic counts are continuous variables, so in addition to histological grade, the precise mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index and the presence or absence of tumour necrosis should be reported {34731032}.
