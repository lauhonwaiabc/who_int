4.0.0.2 Parathyroid adenoma

Definition

Parathyroid adenoma is a benign parathyroid neoplasm composed of chief, transitional oncocytic, oncocytic, or clear cells, or a mixture of cell types.

ICD-O coding

8140/0 Parathyroid adenoma

8290/0 Oncocytic (oxyphil cell) parathyroid adenoma

8322/0 Water-clear cell parathyroid adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2F37.Y & XH3DV3 Other specified benign neoplasm of endocrine glands & Adenoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Oncocytic (oxyphil cell) parathyroid adenoma; water-clear cell parathyroid adenoma

Localization

Parathyroid adenomas arise in parathyroid glands, with the inferior glands affected slightly more frequently than the superior glands. Parathyroid adenomas can also occur in supernumerary parathyroid tissue or in ectopic regions related to embryonic parathyroid development (thyroid, retro-oesophageal space, hypoglossal nerve, thymus, mediastinum, carotid sheaths, and pericardium) {7135202; 19691113}. Atypical and ectopic localization may be a source of disease recurrence, especially in multiglandular disease including multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) type 1 (MEN1)-related multiglandular multiple adenomas. As many as 15% of patients have double adenomas, which show a predilection for the superior parathyroid glands (fourth pouch disease) {14668733}. Inferior parathyroid glands/adenomas may be intrathyroidal.

Clinical features

Parathyroid adenomas account for > 85% of primary hyperparathyroidism. Historically, relapsing renal stones and bone pain due to bone loss and osteitis fibrosa cystica were more common before the implementation of serum calcium screening {19595349}. Hypercalcaemic crisis with nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and dehydration and acute pancreatitis occurred when calcium levels were > 3 mmol/L. Since the introduction of automated calcium measurements, the detection of primary hyperparathyroidism has risen by a factor of 4–5 {19595349}, and patients are relatively asymptomatic or have weakness, fatigue, or anxiety {19595349}. The relative proportion of patients presenting with symptomatic versus asymptomatic disease is dependent on the level of population screening for serum calcium {33716964}. The deranged trabecular bone mineralization/organization is linked to an increase in sometimes asymptomatic vertebral fractures {25646791}. Between 12% and 50% of patients have nephrolithiasis on imaging studies {25646791; 29955845}.

In clinical settings that predispose to secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism (e.g. chronic renal failure), the finding of multiple abnormal parathyroid glands generally results in the diagnosis of parathyroid hyperplasia rather than adenoma. However in the absence of predisposing conditions, the overwhelming majority of cases of primary hyperparathyroidism associated with multiglandular parathyroid disease (previously classified as primary hyperplasia) are now recognized as multiglandular adenomas. For instance, MEN1-related multiglandular parathyroid lesions are composed of multiple clonal proliferations, constituting multiple multiglandular adenomas {2568586; 11739416; 26163537}. In MEN1 syndrome, hyperparathyroidism is the first manifestation in approximately two thirds of patients {17379178} and affects > 93% of patients {33564173}. About 20–30% of patients with MEN type 2A will develop hyperparathyroidism. Hyperparathyroidism is not found in patients with MEN type 2B or isolated familial medullary thyroid carcinoma (a clinical subtype of MEN type 2A) {29465928}. Hyperparathyroidism is a leading symptom of MEN type 4 {33760487}.

There is a correlation between adenoma size and calcium and PTH levels. Parathyroid adenomas measuring > 10 mm are more frequently associated with bone disease and hypercalcaemic crisis {30766921}. Oncocytic parathyroid adenomas are more frequently symptomatic and are associated with slightly higher preoperative serum calcium (10.8 vs 10.4 mg/dL) than are age- and sex-matched non-oncocytic adenomas {26091632}. Another study found oncocytic adenomas to be larger than non-oncocytic adenomas (1011 vs 557 mg) {11859206}. As PTH is now more frequently measured in the absence of elevated calcium levels, normocalcaemic primary hyperparathyroidism is increasingly detected. Although these patients seem to benefit from surgery, with a reported postoperative reduction of renal and bone disease, this is still debated and an individualized approach is suggested {32803770}.

Preoperative localization studies include ultrasonography, 99mTc sestamibi (MIBI) scintigraphy/SPECT, four-dimensional parathyroid CT (4DPCT), MRI, and (most recently) 18F-fluorocholine PET-CT. Although the combinations of ultrasound and scintigraphy or ultrasound and 4DPCT are still standard, choline PET-CT shows the highest sensitivity and specificity and may become the method of choice, especially in persistent/recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism {32892309}.

A significant drop in intraoperative PTH (often > 50% within 10–15 min) after the removal of an enlarged cellular gland with no features of malignancy can be used to confirm the removal of the adenoma responsible for causing clinical hyperparathyroidism {26163537}.

Epidemiology

Although primary hyperparathyroidism was historically considered an uncommon disease, the implementation of serum calcium screening has led to an increase in the reported incidence. In 90% of all patients, chronic hypercalcaemia is attributable to primary hyperparathyroidism {23418315}. Incidence rates varying from 16 cases per 100 000 person-years in Denmark {23461985} to 50 cases per 100 000 person-years in the USA {23418315} have been reported. The M:F ratio is 1:2–3 for sporadic cases. In a population-based study from California (1995–2019), the prevalence of primary hyperparathyroidism increased from 76 cases to 233 cases per 100 000 person-years in the female population and from 30 cases to 85 cases per 100 000 person-years in the male population. The incidence was highest among Black people, followed by White people, Asian people, Hispanic people, and people from other ethnic groups {23418315}. The incidence increases with age in all ethnic groups, and is as high as > 300 cases per 100 000 person-years in Black women aged > 80 years {23418315}. About 1.5 of 1000 parathyroid adenomas are composed of almost entirely clear (water-clear) cells (clear cell parathyroid adenomas) {33994174}. The epidemiology of normocalcaemic primary hyperparathyroidism is still unclear. A Swedish study reported 230 cases in 5771 postmenopausal women (3.4%) {12045863}.

Etiology

The etiology of most sporadic parathyroid adenomas is unknown. As many as 10% of parathyroid adenomas are hereditary, either as part of a syndrome or non-syndromic {27306766}. MEN1 syndrome–related parathyroids show multiglandular parathyroid disease that is characterized by multiple multiclonal adenomas (although this is often mislabelled clinically as hyperplasia in MEN1 syndrome) {11739416; 32468209}, and this disorder and other related genetic susceptibility syndromes are more accurately classified as multiglandular adenomas. In tertiary hyperparathyroidism, autonomous hyperparathyroidism occurs as a result of the hyperplasia-to-neoplasia progression sequence in the background of longstanding chronic renal failure {23478233}.

Genetic susceptibility

The syndromes in which parathyroid adenomas occur are MEN types 1, 2, and 4, as well as CDC73-related disorders including hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome. Pathogenic MAX mutations (MEN type 5) have also expanded the spectrum of multiglandular parathyroid adenomas {33367756}. Non-syndromic inherited forms are summarized as familial isolated hyperparathyroidism (see respective syndromes). Age < 45 years, male sex, and multiglandular disease are risk factors for an underlying germline mutation {29408534}.

Environmental carcinogens

Neck irradiation is a recognized risk factor for parathyroid adenomas, especially in children, occurring after a longer latency than thyroid disease {15657569}. External ionizing radiation exposure is also a risk factor, as observed in external therapeutic ionizing radiation to the head and neck and in survivors of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and the Chernobyl nuclear power plant accident {8265793; 31841481}. An increased rate of parathyroid hyperplasia (with a strong background of vitamin D deficiency) was described 27–32 years after exposure to radiation in the Chernobyl accident {31841481}. Long-term lithium therapy (> 10 years) can lead to parathyroid adenomas and (rarely) to four-gland hyperplasia {12658498; 19001061}.

Pathogenesis

The mode of inheritance and the genes involved in familial hyperparathyroidism are reported in #24099Table 4.01 . In sporadic parathyroid adenomas, the most frequent somatic alterations are loss of heterozygosity of 11q13 (35%) and biallelic MEN1 inactivation (20%) {30475215}. Although CCND1 (encoding cyclin D1) only rarely undergoes rearrangements with PTH (detected in up to 8% of parathyroid adenomas), cyclin D1 overexpression is detected in 18–40% of cases, which in turn might be influenced by aberrant promoter hypermethylation of various CDK inhibitors {28600574}. The causal link between tumour development and MEN1/CCND1 dysregulation is strengthened by the observation of hypercellular parathyroids with hyperparathyroidism in aberrant MEN1 and CCND1 mouse models.

Other somatic mutations in parathyroid adenomas are rare and have mostly been described in CDKN1B (encoding p27) {21289244}, the epigenetic modifier EZH2 {22740705; 34215996}, ZFX (encoding a transcription factor with a role in self-renewal) {25594030}, and the transcriptional regulator ASXL3 {29982334; 34215996} and MTOR {34215996}. In chronic renal failure–related secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism, the nodular parathyroid tissue demonstrates different somatic alterations than adenomas identified in primary hyperparathyroidism {34215996}. Somatic mutations of other familial drivers (CDKN1A, CDKN1B, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, CDKN2C, and GCM2) are very rare in parathyroid adenomas. CDC73 mutations are mostly restricted to atypical parathyroid tumours and parathyroid carcinomas, as well as adenomas occurring in hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome, although small subsets of clinically recurrence-free adenomas with cystic features have been found to carry this aberration {16728578}. CDC73-mutated parathyroid adenomas also display a unique cytogenetic profile compared with CDC73-wildtype cases {23029479}. Although no somatic mutations in RET, CASR, or VDR are found in sporadic parathyroid adenomas, expressional dysregulation of the calcium sensor and vitamin D receptors may play a role in the hyperparathyroidism phenotype {30475215}. On the epigenetic level, aberrant methylation of tumour-related genes, including WT1, PRDM2 (RIZ1), and RASSF1 (RASSF1A), has also been reported in parathyroid adenomas {21638518; 14668725; 20208994}.

Macroscopic appearance

Parathyroid adenomas are ovoid, pink or tan tumours surrounded by a delicate pseudocapsule. A rim of atrophic parathyroid tissue is often identifiable at the vascular pole of a gland {26163537}. Sectioning the gland horizontally through the vascular hilum often demonstrates this rim of suppressed non-neoplastic tissue {26163537}. A normal gland measures up to 6–8 mm and weighs up to 40–60 mg {26163537}. Tumours weighing < 100 mg and with a diameter < 6 mm {20347738} may correspond to microadenomas and may be incidental findings rather than the cause of clinical hyperparathyroidism. One definition of a giant parathyroid adenoma is a tumour weighing > 2 g {17636522}, but adenomas may measure several centimetres and weigh tens if not hundreds of grams {30405093}. Some parathyroid adenomas can be bilobated or multilobulated. Parathyroid adenomas may contain fluid-filled cysts and can be termed cystic parathyroid adenomas if cysts make up > 50% of the tumour volume. Cystic adenomas tend to be larger, with higher calcium and PTH levels, and they may be confused with thyroid cysts because subsets are negative on 99mTc sestamibi (MIBI) scintigraphy {30123260}. Cystic changes may be accompanied by prominent areas of fibrosis or foci of calcification {24293304}. Spontaneous infarction or haemorrhage in parathyroid adenomas may occur, sometimes leading to adherence to surrounding structures and rarely leading to spontaneous remission of hyperparathyroidism {18470672}.

Histopathology

Parathyroid adenomas are histologically well circumscribed or encapsulated by a thin fibrous pseudocapsule. Stromal fat is usually absent or sparse. A rim of atrophic parathyroid tissue is observed in more than half of the adenomas, but it is detected less frequently in larger tumours {6691181}. Rim-like areas can also occasionally be identified in parathyroid hyperplasia. It is therefore necessary to correlate the morphological features with clinical findings (e.g. absence of secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism), radiographic findings, and laboratory findings (e.g. significant drop of intraoperative PTH or postoperative biochemical cure) {26163537}. A slightly cellular rim may be seen in the setting of underlying vitamin D deficiency.

Parathyroid adenomas are usually composed of chief cells with a round nucleus and scant cytoplasm, but they can be composed of a mixture of parenchymal cell types including chief, transitional, oncocytic, and clear cells. There may be lobules or nodules within the adenoma. Oncocytic change may occur focally. When oncocytic cells constitute > 75% of the adenoma, it is termed oncocytic/oxyphilic parathyroid adenoma.

The tumour cells in parathyroid adenomas are arranged in sheets or nests in a delicate capillary network. The tumour cells may have a palisaded appearance around blood vessels. The tumour cell nuclei are slightly larger than those of the adjacent parathyroid tissue. Formation of follicular structures is not uncommon, and a few adenomas demonstrate a predominantly follicular growth. In such cases, the absence of birefringent calcium oxalate crystals and the presence of well-defined cell membranes can help in the differentiation from thyroid tissue. Papillary growth is rare {3337625}. Chief cells are polygonal with clear or slightly eosinophilic cytoplasm. The nuclei are lymphocyte-like and centrally located and have well-defined cell membranes. Multinucleated tumour cells and patchy endocrine nuclear atypia may occur. Occasional mitotic figures (< 5 mitoses/10 mm2) can be identified.

Most parathyroid adenomas are composed predominantly of chief cells, often with a mixture of oncocytic (oxyphil) and clear cells. Oncocytic (oxyphil cell) parathyroid adenomas account for about 3–6% of parathyroid adenomas {26091632}. Water-clear cell parathyroid adenomas are uncommon {22638654; 33994174}.

In large and giant adenomas, fibrosis, cholesterol clefts, contour irregularity, and/or haemorrhage with haemosiderin deposition and cyst formation are more frequent. In cases with broad fibrous bands, necrosis, or elevated mitotic counts in the absence of invasive growth, the diagnosis of atypical parathyroid tumour (see section Atypical parathyroid tumour) should be considered. Adenomas and atypical parathyroid tumours are distinguished from parathyroid carcinomas by the absence of unequivocal invasive growth. The rim of non-neoplastic parathyroid tissue adjacent to parathyroid adenoma is often indistinguishable from normal parathyroid tissue, although this tissue may be considered suppressed because the proliferation rate and secretory set point are often decreased {9506741}. Confident separation of adenoma from nodular chief-cell hyperplasia, including in asymmetrical and asynchronous cases, is not possible by morphological criteria alone in a single gland. However, pathologists and clinicians encounter this issue less often with more extensive radiographic assessments and with the utilization of rapid intraoperative PTH testing {26163537}.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemically, parathyroid adenomas are positive for PTH and GATA3, and for the neuroendocrine markers chromogranin A and synaptophysin {29626276}. Thyroglobulin, thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), and monoclonal PAX8 stains are negative. The vast majority of sporadic parathyroid adenomas show nuclear parafibromin expression {16728578; 16931959; 30923346}. Morphological clues to tumours associated with CDC73 mutations, termed parafibromin-deficient parathyroid tumours, include eosinophilic cytoplasm with perinuclear clearing, nuclear enlargement, microcystic change, and a sheet-like growth pattern {29324469}.

Differential diagnosis

On the pathological examination of only one parathyroid gland in the absence of clinical information, the distinction between adenoma and different types of hyperplasia is not possible with certainty and a descriptive diagnosis such as “pathologically enlarged fat-depleted parathyroid gland consistent with…” may be preferable.

Cytology

Smears show low to moderate cellularity with variable patterns including cohesive sheets and more complex papilliform (with thin fibrovascular cores) and microfollicular tissue fragments {11891946}. The nuclei are regular in arrangement, small, central, and round to oval, with a regular membrane and even fine chromatin {28567111; 33114338}. The scant cytoplasm is pale, finely granular, and eosinophilic (Pap), and it can be oxyphilic or vacuolated {28567111}. There is no thin colloid in the clean background where stripped nuclei may be present. Without clinical information, parathyroid adenoma may be misdiagnosed as a thyroid lesion, when the FNAB target is a thyroid nodule or a lesion that may be in ectopic parathyroid tissue; but with clinical and ultrasound correlation the diagnosis can be suggested, and immunocytochemical studies for PTH on cell blocks and PTH measurements using needle washouts can confirm the diagnosis {28217943; 32833315}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No diagnostic molecular tests are indicated in sporadic parathyroid adenomas. Screening for an underlying germline abnormality is recommended for individuals with primary hyperparathyroidism if any of the following features are present: (1) age at disease onset < 45 years, (2) a family history of primary hyperparathyroidism or other tumours that can occur in inherited syndromes, (3) a personal history of a neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN; e.g. pituitary, pancreas) or other tumours that can feature primary hyperparathyroidism, (4) primary hyperparathyroidism due to multiglandular parathyroid disease (e.g. MEN1-related multiple multiglandular adenomas), and (5) histological findings of a possible CDC73-associated tumour or mutation-associated immunohistochemistry findings (e.g. loss of menin, parafibromin, and p27) suggestive of inherited disease {28881068; 33269427}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an enlarged cellular gland (> 6–8 mm and > 40–60 mg) consisting of a benign parathyroid cell proliferation, often surrounded by a rim of atrophic parathyroid parenchyma.

Desirable: a significant drop in intraoperative PTH; postoperative biochemical cure; additional parathyroid gland(s) with no pathological diagnosis.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Parathyroid adenomas are benign. Recurrent hyperparathyroidism develops in < 2% of patients {30511037}. In the setting of multiglandular disease the prognosis is dependent on the underlying clinical or genetic syndrome {32468209}.
