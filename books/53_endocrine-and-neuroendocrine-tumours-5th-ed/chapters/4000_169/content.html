4.0.0.0 Parathyroid hyperplasia

Definition

Parathyroid hyperplasia is defined as an absolute increase in non-neoplastic parathyroid parenchymal cells in multiple parathyroid glands.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

5A51.0 Primary hyperparathyroidism

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Primary hyperplasia; secondary hyperplasia; tertiary hyperplasia

Localization

Hyperplasia may occur in both eutopic and ectopic parathyroid glands.

Clinical features

Primary hyperplasia is where hyperplasia occurs in the absence of a physiological stimulus. Because multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) and many other forms of hereditary hyperparathyroidism are now recognized as usually being due to multiple adenomas, this is extremely rare.

Secondary hyperplasia is where physiological stimuli (e.g. high serum phosphate, low serum calcium, and/or vitamin D deficiency) lead to a compensatory hyperplasia. Parathyroid hyperplasia in secondary hyperparathyroidism is characterized by the underlying disease leading to decreased calcium or 1,25D3. Chronic renal disease is the most common cause, followed by chronic malabsorption or vitamin D deficiency {21454719}. Hyperparathyroidism is associated with poor outcomes in chronic renal disease {15284307}. Cardiovascular disease and bone disorders with an increased risk of fractures are the most common clinical complications {15284307}.

Tertiary hyperparathyroidism is defined as a progression of secondary hyperparathyroidism, where the glands acquire the ability to autonomously secrete PTH despite elevated serum PTH and calcium levels. Untreated, this is associated with increased renal transplant rejection and decreased survival {28518414}.

By definition, hyperplasia is a potentially reversible condition that is caused by a known stimulus. Most instances of multiglandular parathyroid disease associated with a genetic predisposition (e.g. the MEN syndromes) that were previously classified as primary hyperplasia are now recognized as multiple, sometimes coalescing, parathyroid adenomas and should be classified as such. These are therefore now discussed in the subsequent section on parathyroid adenoma.

Epidemiology

Historically, about 5–10% of cases of primary hyperparathyroidism were reported to be due to hyperplasia {22187986}. However, this figure dates from a time when MEN and other causes of hereditary hyperparathyroidism were considered hyperplasias rather than being recognized as multiglandular adenomas. Now, particularly with the increased availability of germline testing, true primary hyperplasia is very rare. Water-clear cell hyperplasia is exceedingly rare and to date has not been associated with a specific genetic predisposition. Chronic renal disease is a worldwide public health problem, and secondary hyperparathyroidism is a very frequent complication {28853301}.

Etiology

True primary hyperplasia not associated with a genetic predisposition to multifocal parathyroid neoplasia remains to be fully characterized.

In secondary hyperparathyroidism due to renal insufficiency, the three factors of hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia, and low 1,25D3 levels lead to an increased secretion of PTH. In the early phases of renal insufficiency, phosphate is retained by the kidney. The increase in PTH and FGF23 counteracts the phosphate accumulation but reduces the serum concentration of 1,25D3. The binding of calcium to phosphate leads to hypocalcaemia, stimulating PTH secretion indirectly. Hypocalcaemia increases parathyroid chief-cell proliferation, supported by the absence of the antiproliferative 1,25D3 {32913635}. External radiation and long-term lithium therapy (> 10 years) can lead to parathyroid adenomas and (rarely) to four-gland hyperplasia {12658498; 19001061; 31841481}.

Over time, the accumulation of additional genetic alterations {7738171; 12039978; 34215996} in combination with a progressive reduction in the expression of PTH receptors {32913635} occurs in parathyroids showing secondary hyperplasia. This leads to the development of tertiary hyperparathyroidism, where the gland is refractory to the action of its natural inhibitors.

Pathogenesis

In patients with renal failure on dialysis, hyperphosphataemia directly stimulates PTH secretion and maintains a persistent stimulus for parathyroid hyperplasia. High phosphate levels prevent activation of CaSR and thereby stimulate PTH secretion despite normal serum calcium levels {31619668}. Phosphate also generates skeletal resistance to the calcaemic effect of PTH {1762306}, further contributing to hypocalcaemia and stimulating parathyroid hyperplasia. In renal insufficiency, the production of 1,25D3 is reduced in the kidney, additionally contributing to hypocalcaemia and parathyroid hyperplasia. FGF23 is able to reduce PTH secretion by binding to the FGFR1–Klotho receptor complex {17086194}. In patients with secondary hyperparathyroidism, the downregulation of Klotho and FGFR1 is often observed, and FGF23 thereby loses the ability to suppress PTH secretion {28094278}. A progressive reduction of parathyroid receptors contributes to the transformation to nodular hyperplasia and tertiary hyperparathyroidism.

Clonality studies have demonstrated that 38% of cases of secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism are monoclonal {7738171} and 24% of cases of tertiary hyperparathyroidism demonstrate clonal chromosomal aberrations using comparative genomic hybridization analysis {12039978}. Therefore, although most of the somatic mutations identified in chronic renal failure–related secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism are different to those identified in usual adenomas {34215996}, many cases of secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism actually represent true neoplasms progressing via a hyperplasia-to-neoplasia sequence.

Macroscopic appearance

Primary chief-cell hyperplasia or multiglandular disease in the absence of a genetic predisposition is so rare it remains to be fully characterized. In primary water-clear cell hyperplasia, the hyperplastic parathyroids weigh 1000–6000 mg; the upper glands tend to be larger than the lower glands {21455204}. In secondary hyperplasia, the weight varies with the stage of the disease, with weights of 120–6000 mg reported {5823010}. In tertiary hyperplasia, nodularity is seen in > 50% of cases {4018775}.

Histopathology

For all parathyroids with hyperplastic changes, close clinicopathological correlation is important. Histologically, primary chief-cell hyperplasia is characterized by an increased number of chief cells as compared with stromal fat cells {26163537} in multiple parathyroids. Hyperplasia may be diffuse, nodular, or mixed {26163537}. Primary nodular disease is almost always associated with germline predisposition syndromes and represents multifocal adenomas; therefore, this is discussed in section Parathyroid adenoma . Immunohistochemistry may be useful in the differential diagnosis. For example, loss of menin immunostaining is found more frequently in patients with MEN type 1 (MEN1), but it can also occur in sporadic disease {20175455; 29626276}. Water-clear cell hyperplasia is a diffuse proliferation composed entirely of clear cells with distinct cell borders {11175646}.

Hyperplasia in secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism involves multiple glands asymmetrically and is characterized by an increase in parenchymal cells and a decrease in stromal and intracellular fat {6719054}. The growth pattern can be diffuse, trabecular, acinar, pseudofollicular, or cord-like. Increasing severity of hyperparathyroidism correlates with a more nodular growth pattern, and advanced cases may show areas of haemorrhage, cyst formation, fibrosis, and chronic inflammation {26163537}. Particularly in the setting of tertiary hyperparathyroidism, the individual glands may show highly atypical architectural features that can mimic carcinoma, including the presence of pseudoinvasion and a high proliferation index {32687943}. Although these patients may be at high risk of local recurrence or diffuse recurrence in the neck (parathyromatosis), distant metastasis is exceptional in renal failure–induced hyperparathyroidism {19836494; 26163537}. Therefore, in the setting of established chronic renal failure, the diagnosis of parathyroid carcinoma should not be made in disease confined to the neck unless there is unequivocal vascular space invasion or very marked invasive growth – for example, unequivocal extension into adjacent organs such as the thyroid, oesophagus, or skeletal muscle {31708875; 32687943}. Parathyromatosis (defined as local recurrence throughout the neck and/or mediastinum due to mechanical displacement or hyperplasia of rests of parathyroid tissue) may show fibrosis and pseudoinvasion of fat in a buckshot pattern that can be impossible to distinguish from parathyroid carcinoma without the history of hyperplasia and close clinicopathological correlation. Although biomarker studies are useful in the distinction of parathyroid carcinoma from parathyromatosis {26177319}, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, and distant spread are absent in parathyromatosis and always warrant the diagnosis of carcinoma {32687943; 33394375}.

Differential diagnosis

Although multinodularity and the absence of a rim of non-tumorous parathyroid tissue are clues to hyperplasia, in the absence of clinical correlation (e.g. knowledge that the other glands are normal or that PTH has normalized after removal), the distinction between adenoma and hyperplasia is not possible with certainty and a descriptive diagnosis such as “pathologically enlarged cellular (fat-depleted) parathyroid gland consistent with…” may be appropriate.

Cytology

There are no cytological criteria described for the diagnosis of parathyroid hyperplasia.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No diagnostic molecular tests are indicated in parathyroid hyperplasias caused by secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism. In the setting of apparently primary hyperplasia or multiglandular disease, screening for an underlying germline abnormality is recommended. If an underlying genetic predisposition (e.g. MEN1) is identified, reclassification as multiglandular/multinodular parathyroid adenomas is usually appropriate.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical or pathological evidence of involvement of more than one parathyroid gland.

Desirable: the presence of a clinical disorder known to cause secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, or the exclusion of a genetic syndrome known to cause multiglandular adenomatous disease.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis depends on the underlying disease. Secondary hyperparathyroidism and (especially) tertiary hyperparathyroidism are treated by surgery when they are refractory to conservative medical therapy {32913635}. Local recurrence may occur after surgery, a condition termed parathyromatosis if it occurs diffusely in several foci. Patients with parathyroid hyperplasia that demonstrates marked nodularity, an elevated proliferation index, or redevelopment of chronic renal failure are at higher risk of recurrence {19836494}.
