6.0.0.1 Adrenal gland tumours: Introduction

Although some entities in the classification of adrenal cortical diseases are quite classic, the classification of some others has changed greatly in recent years. The importance of a classic entity, such as adrenal rests, centres on the adrenal cortical tumours that can arise in them as well as the hyperplasia that can occur, such as in Nelson syndrome. Adrenal rests may also stimulate other steroid cell, Leydig cell, and renal cell tumours. Adrenal pseudocysts are encountered by most pathologists, whereas true adrenal cysts (endothelial or epithelial) are rare {15378490}. Endothelialization of pseudocysts can be mistaken for an endothelial cyst or a vascular neoplasm. Most grossly cystic adrenal glands are adrenal cortical neoplasms, usually adenomas (rarely carcinomas) or phaeochromocytomas with prominent cystic degeneration or pseudocysts {15378490}. Pseudocysts can be mistaken for necrosis, which would be an ominous finding for an adrenal cortical neoplasm.

Adrenal myelolipomas are non-functioning, usually asymptomatic, incidental findings; they are usually unilateral, although they are more frequently bilateral in congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), where they occur more often than in the general population {33471666}. CAH, an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in genes involved in the adrenal cortical enzymatic steps of steroid production resulting in the proliferation of adrenal cortical cells, remains a significant issue clinically, with the classic type being most severe and usually identified by neonatal screening. Non-classic CAH is more common and milder than classic salt-wasting or classic simple virilizing CAH. If untreated, the adrenal glands show marked bilateral cortical hyperplasia, with lipid depletion imparting a dark colour to the adrenal glands. Testicular adrenal rest tumours occur in 14–86% of patients with CAH and can be mistaken for Leydig cell tumours if the clinical situation is not known {30882882}.

Sporadic subcentimetre adrenal cortical nodules may be clonal {17890895; 33534120}, and so the term “sporadic nodular adrenal cortical disease” has replaced “cortical nodular hyperplasia” for incidental non-functioning cortical nodules [[Pakbaz S, Mete O. Adrenal cortical neoplasia: from histology to molecular biology. Diagn Histopathol. 2019 May;25(5):178–89. doi:10.1016/j.mpdhp.2019.02.004.]] {33534120}. Primary bilateral nodular adrenal cortical disease consists of multiple independent clonal proliferations, and therefore older terms such as “primary bilateral micronodular” or “macronodular adrenal cortical ‘hyperplasia’” are no longer appropriate {33534120}. However, the term “diffuse hyperplasia” is appropriate for adrenal glands affected by excess pituitary ACTH production. Virtually all cases of bilateral micronodular adrenal cortical disease and a significant fraction of cases of bilateral macronodular adrenal cortical disease are caused by genetic susceptibility {33534120; 29594118; 33776926}. In bilateral nodular adrenocortical disease, germline susceptibility is followed by a somatic second hit involving the other allele. Bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease is linked to pathogenic germline variants leading to the activation of the PKA pathway, including PRKAR1A, PRKACA, PDE11A, PDE8B, and 2p16 CNC2 locus alterations {33534120; 33776926; 29594118}. Bilateral macronodular adrenal cortical disease has been linked to several germline alterations, including ARMC5, MEN1, FH, and APC alterations; somatic GNAS mosaicism; and rare PDE11A, PDE8B, and EDNRA variants {24283224; 25853793; 31482957; 19509103; 20978149; 32783015; 29594118; 23754170}.

The understanding and classification of primary aldosteronism has advanced greatly in recent years. Bilateral adrenal gland involvement occurs in 60–70% of cases of primary aldosteronism with bilateral diffuse zona glomerulosa hyperplasia and bilateral aldosterone-producing microadenomas (< 10 mm) {26125435; 31672302; 33534120; 26610199}. The distinction of bilateral aldosterone excess from unilateral disease (often due to adenoma or microadenoma) is important in deciding surgical intervention {28420172; 31672302}. Aldosterone-producing cortical lesions can be unifocal or multifocal and unilateral or bilateral, and they may include microscopic clonal nodular proliferations. Aldosterone-producing microtumours can be difficult to identify. Therefore, CYP11B2 immunohistochemistry is helpful in the evaluation of adrenal gland(s) in individuals with primary aldosteronism, to identify functional sites of aldosterone {29542002; 32717746; 33534120}. The recent histopathology of primary aldosteronism (HISTALDO) consensus classification combines the histological evaluation of an adrenal gland with the CYP11B2 staining pattern to identify adrenal nodules and standardize nomenclature to provide a clinically relevant diagnostic approach to reporting unilateral primary aldosteronism specimens {32717746}.

The confirmation of the adrenal cortical origin of any non-functioning adrenal neoplasm should be considered routinely. SF1 is the most specific biomarker for confirming adrenal cortical origin {29542002}.

The distinction of adrenal cortical carcinoma from adenoma in organ-confined tumours may be difficult and a number of different approaches are used, with some being more appropriate in specific settings {33058949}. The classic pathological criteria proposed for adrenal cortical carcinoma described by Dr Weiss in 1984 and 1989 continue to be widely used. Because the Weiss system may lead to the overdiagnosis of some oncocytic adrenal tumours as malignant, the Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia system is used for pure (> 90%) oncocytic adrenal cortical tumours. In myxoid carcinomas, some Weiss parameters may be absent or difficult to assess, thus increasing the risk of an underdiagnosis of malignancy. More recent classification systems, including the reticulin algorithm and the Helsinki score, have been helpful in conventional adrenal cortical neoplasms as well as in myxoid and oncocytic cortical neoplasms {25582500; 27916625; 23774167; 33058949}. Paediatric adrenal cortical tumours continue to be primarily classified with the system described by Dr Wieneke in 2003 {12826878}.

Adrenal cortical carcinomas with invasive growth or high-grade features do not usually pose a challenge for diagnosticians; however, biomarkers may help in the distinction of low-grade adrenal cortical carcinomas confined to the adrenal gland and carcinomas with adenoma-like regions {28877067; 29542002; 33534120; 33058949}. Rare cases with equivocal features may sometimes be diagnosed as adrenal cortical neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential. Although an increased Ki-67 proliferation index (often > 5%, and > 15% usually malignant) and p53 overexpression can be helpful in some cases {29542002; 28877067}, IGF2 paranuclear immunoreactivity has been shown to be the most reliable translational biomarker in the distinction of adrenal cortical carcinoma from adenoma {28877067; 16918977; 19218281}. Molecular studies can also help in the distinction of carcinoma {19147773; 12547710; 19139432; 27165744; 31294750}. There is now clear evidence for grading adrenal cortical carcinoma on the basis of the mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index, and although precise cut-off points are still debated, the provision of a formal mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index in all cases is now recommended {25559399; 24434900; 18187873; 29921900; 33058949}.
