4.0.0.4 Atypical parathyroid tumour

Definition

Atypical parathyroid tumour (APT) is a parathyroid neoplasm that demonstrates atypical cytological and architectural features but lacks unequivocal capsular, vascular, or perineural invasion or invasion into adjacent structures or metastases.

ICD-O coding

8140/1 Atypical parathyroid tumour

ICD-11 coding

2F9A Neoplasms of unknown behaviour of endocrine glands

Related terminology

Acceptable: atypical parathyroid neoplasm; parathyroid tumour of low/uncertain malignant potential.

Not recommended: atypical parathyroid adenoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

APT can involve any parathyroid gland.

Clinical features

APTs usually occur sporadically, but they can occur in familial isolated hyperparathyroidism, hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome, and in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 {31085770}. In a review of 672 APTs, all but 24 occurred sporadically {31085770}. Patients may be asymptomatic or have fatigue, weakness, weight loss, nausea, polyuria, or polydipsia. Clinically, APTs may be indistinguishable from parathyroid carcinomas, but a palpable neck mass is much less common in APT (3%) than in carcinoma (13%) {26154578}. Serum calcium is elevated (< 13.5 mg/dL) but is generally lower than in carcinoma and often intermediate between that of adenoma and carcinoma.

Preoperative localization may include ultrasound, scintigraphic studies, dual-phase SPECT, MRI, 18F-fluorocholine PET-CT. Choline PET-CT shows the highest sensitivity and specificity and may become the method of choice in persistent/recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism {32892309}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of APT may be dependent on the pathological criteria used and ranges from 0.5% to 4.4% in individuals undergoing surgery for primary hyperparathyroidism {31085770}. Most cases occur sporadically, but APT can occur in familial isolated hyperparathyroidism, hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome, and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1. Sporadic cases are more common in women, with an M:F ratio of approximately 1:1.5 and a mean age of 48.2 years (± 18.5 years, range: 16–80 years) {31085770}. Familial cases show an equal sex distribution and on average occur two decades earlier {31085770}.

Etiology

The etiology of APT is unknown. A minority of cases occur in familial disease.

Pathogenesis

It is unclear whether there is true progression from benign to malignant in parathyroid lesions, including in hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome, in which the prevalence of parathyroid carcinoma is high. APTs rarely have somatic MEN1 mutations {32411220}. In a study of 38 APTs, 25 (66%) showed loss of heterozygosity (at the CDKN1A [P21] locus in 9 cases, at CDC73 and PTEN in 6 cases, and at RB1 in 4 cases) {26210223}. Targeted sequence analysis of 50 APTs for 15 tumour-related genes showed that 16 (32%) had least one alteration (CDC73 in 12 cases [24%], EZH2 in 4 cases [8%], HIC1 in 1 case [2%], and CDKN2A in 1 case [2%]) and suggested that the genetic profile of APTs more closely resembles that of parathyroid carcinoma than adenoma {33778063}.

Macroscopic appearance

APTs are usually solid but can be cystic, with a median size of 30 mm (10–72 mm), and are adherent to adjacent structures in half of the cases {31085770}.

Histopathology

APTs are non-invasive parathyroid neoplasms composed of chief cells with variable amounts of oncocytic and transitional cells. They show some morphological features commonly seen in parathyroid carcinoma, such as adherence to adjacent structures, mitotic activity, cytological atypia, monotonous sheet-like or trabecular growth, fibrosis (sometimes band-like), and tumour cells extending into (but not through) the capsule. They are distinguished from parathyroid carcinoma by the lack of unequivocal full-thickness capsular invasion with clear-cut extension into soft tissue, vascular or perineural invasion, or invasion into adjacent structures.

Conventional parathyroid adenomas (particularly if large) commonly demonstrate focal fibrosis, an occasional mitotic figure, or focal capsular thickening. However, the diagnosis of APT is reserved for parathyroid neoplasms with significantly atypical features that are worrisome for a parathyroid carcinoma but for which definitive invasion cannot be documented. This diagnosis should be based on the constellation of features and the clinical context.

APTs may show monotonous, solid growth of sheets of cells or closely packed nests, similar to that seen in parathyroid carcinoma. Fibrosis can be identified in secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism and with prior manipulation (e.g. FNAB) and is insufficient to warrant the diagnosis of APT if it can be confidently attributed to posttraumatic regenerative change. The association of fat necrosis and haemosiderin deposition with fibrosis or contour irregularity is a useful clue to posttraumatic regenerative change {17021134; 26163537; 28061891; 33740321}. Mitotic activity is seen in benign and malignant disease. A mitotic rate of > 5 mitoses/10 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index of > 5%, necrosis, macronucleoli, or atypical mitoses are more common in malignancy {8338192; 12114810; 31085770}. However, a definitive diagnosis of carcinoma requires invasive growth or metastasis.

Immunohistochemistry

APTs show positivity for PTH, GATA3, and chromogranin A. Numerous biomarkers, including combinations, have been evaluated in parathyroid neoplasms, including Ki-67, cyclin D1, p27, RB1, BCL2, MDM2, p53, galectin-3, E-cadherin, and PGP9.5 {10078919; 11859206; 12114697; 12605367; 19107770; 23001705; 26154578; 27490759; 29626276; 31734935}. All show some utility, but they do not consistently differentiate benign from malignant parathyroid neoplasms.

Parafibromin is encoded by CDC73. Absent nuclear parafibromin immunoreactivity in the presence of an internal control (termed parafibromin deficiency) indicates biallelic CDC73 inactivation, which is commonly associated with germline mutation {33269427; 16931959; 29324469; 30923346}. Parafibromin has shown utility in predicting outcome in APTs {20473645; 24081804}. Given the association of somatic CDC73 mutations and sporadic parathyroid carcinoma, and the increased risk of carcinoma and metachronous tumours in individuals with germline CDC73 mutation, long-term follow-up is recommended with parafibromin-deficient tumours {16931959; 27490759; 29626276; 33269427}.

Cytology

APTs have not been extensively studied cytopathologically, but the distinction from parathyroid adenoma and carcinoma is reported as not possible {28217943}. Parathyroid must be differentiated from thyroid, and immunostains can be performed or PTH evaluated with needle washout fluid {32833315}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline CDC73 mutation testing is recommended in parafibromin-deficient APT {32687943; 29324469; 30923346}, but otherwise molecular testing is usually not performed unless there is a suspicion of hereditary disease.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: atypical cytological or architectural features; absence of invasive growth.

Desirable: large size; marked hypercalcaemia.

Staging

According to American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) eighth-edition staging, APT is staged as Tis.

Prognosis and prediction

Although APTs are considered an intermediate type of parathyroid neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential and metastases have been reported, the great majority do not recur after simple excision, and some suggest they may be followed similarly to adenomas in the absence of concerning clinical or immunohistochemical features (e.g. parafibromin deficiency) {31085770; 32687943}. APTs with parafibromin deficiency have an increased risk of recurrence, predominantly as metachronous disease in other glands but occasionally as true recurrence, and warrant long-term follow-up {20473645; 24081804; 29324469; 30923346}.
