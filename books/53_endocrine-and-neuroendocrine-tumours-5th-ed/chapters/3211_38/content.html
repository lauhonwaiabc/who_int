3.2.1.1 Follicular thyroid adenoma

Definition

Follicular adenoma (FA) is a benign, encapsulated, follicular cell–derived neoplasm without invasion or the nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC).

ICD-O coding

8330/0 Follicular thyroid adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2F37.Y & XH0LM0 Other specified benign neoplasm of endocrine glands & Follicular adenoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

FAs can occur anywhere in the thyroid gland. They have also been reported in ectopic locations, including in association with thyroglossal duct cysts, lingual thyroid tissue, and struma ovarii {8253550; 12553553; 19696610; 25103611}.

Clinical features

The mean age at diagnosis of FA is 45–55 years {20025540; 32280082; 32689909}. Although FAs commonly occur in adults, they can also occur in children {30768868}. FAs usually present as painless, palpable thyroid masses that are detected on routine physical examination or are incidentally discovered on imaging studies. A small subset of large FAs are associated with compressive symptoms such as dysphagia or dyspnoea {24857573}. On ultrasound, FAs are typically solitary, well-demarcated, solid, homogeneous, ovoid, isoechoic or hypoechoic nodules. Most cases also show a prominent halo. Although there are some sonographic features that are more common in follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) than FA (irregular margins, interrupted capsular calcification), FA and FTC are usually indistinguishable on ultrasound because of their overlapping features {19746451; 31182260; 32689909}. Most patients with FA are euthyroid {32689909}. On radionuclide scan, FAs generally show less radioiodine uptake than the adjacent thyroid parenchyma (i.e. they are hypofunctioning, cold nodules). Rare FAs are hyperfunctioning and hot on radionuclide scan, showing increased uptake compared with the adjacent gland (see section Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture).

Epidemiology

The incidence of FA is difficult to assess because there is histological overlap between FA and hyperplastic/adenomatous thyroid nodules, and because most population-based studies report the incidence of malignant tumours only {30360906; 12114776}. However, from data from autopsy series, the incidence of FA in adults is estimated to be approximately 5% {2676140; 9825004; 3378788; 5915940}. FA shows a female predominance {32689909}.

Etiology

Radiation exposure during childhood is a known risk factor for FA. A relative risk ratio as high as 15 has been reported, with increased risk associated with a younger age at the time of radiation exposure and with a similar risk generally reported for male and female patients {8488255; 19515442; 2594972; 25545696}. FAs typically develop 10–15 years after radiation exposure, but the risk persists for > 50 years {8488255; 25545696; 12733137}. Iodine deficiency has also been associated with the development of FA {26146517}.

Most FAs are sporadic; however, they can occur as part of a tumour syndrome, such as PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (which includes Cowden syndrome), DICER1 syndrome, and Carney complex {33495912; 29239041}. Thyroidectomy specimens from patients with Cowden syndrome are characterized by multiple adenomatous nodules, FA (often multiple), and C-cell hyperplasia, with or without follicular or papillary thyroid carcinoma {10594284; 21921783}. DICER1 syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by heterozygous inactivating germline DICER1 gene mutations {29343557}. It is associated with nodular hyperplasia and an increased risk (as high as 16-fold) of thyroid cancer; however, FA can also occur in patients with DICER1 syndrome {28323992; 32228164; 24617712; 33495912}. About a quarter of patients with Carney complex, an autosomal dominant disorder usually caused by germline mutations in PRKAR1A, have thyroid tumours, including FAs (which are often multiple) {19293268; 29635258}. The finding of a FA in a background of multiple adenomatous nodules and nodular hyperplasia with or without an associated thyroid carcinoma raises the possibility of an underlying hereditary syndrome, especially if the FAs are multiple or occur in childhood. PTEN immunohistochemistry can be used to evaluate for Cowden syndrome; loss of staining in all or a subset of adenomatous nodules is suggestive of Cowden syndrome {21921783}, although clinical evaluation is required to confirm the diagnosis.

Pathogenesis

FAs are clonal neoplasms {1975986; 1973172; 10102611}. The most common somatic mutations in FA are RAS point mutations. RAS mutations have been reported in approximately a quarter of FAs {10468914; 22650231; 12788883; 12727991}. NRAS mutations are most common, with less frequent HRAS mutations and KRAS mutations {10468914; 22650231}. Most NRAS and HRAS mutations involve codon 61, whereas most KRAS mutations involve codon 12/13 {10468914; 22650231}. PAX8::PPARG rearrangements occur in about 5–10% of FAs {12727991; 12161538; 12170088}. RAS mutations and PAX8::PPARG rearrangements are generally mutually exclusive. EIF1AX mutations are identified in about 5% of FAs {26911375}. The most common mutation hotspot is the A113_splice, followed by a cluster of mutations in exon 2. In FA, EIF1AX mutations generally do not co-occur with RAS mutations as has been reported for some thyroid malignancies {26911375}. DICER1 RNase IIIb–domain mutations resulting in impaired microRNA processing have been reported in FA, demonstrating that somatic DICER1 mutations are not confined to thyroid malignancies {30956758}. THADA::IGF2BP3 fusions, associated with aberrant insulin growth factor receptor signalling, can be encountered in a small subset of FAs {33487086}. Hyperfunctioning FAs are characterized by TSHR and GNAS mutations (for additional information see section Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture). Mutations in genes of the PI3K/PTEN/AKT pathway are absent or rare in FA {17426084}. FAs also lack TERT promoter mutations, although they have been reported (rarely) in FA with atypical features {32282345}.

Macroscopic appearance

FAs are usually solitary, round to oval encapsulated nodules, with a mean size of 20–30 mm {17680588; 32280082; 32689909}; however, tumours measuring > 40 mm occur {17703450}. It is important to adequately examine the tumour capsule in large FAs to exclude invasion. Of note, size cannot be used to differentiate between FA and FTC {20025540; 9623727; 17680588; 32280082}. The cut surface is usually homogeneous and can be greyish-white, tan, or brown. Tumours that are greyish-white are more cellular, whereas tan to brown tumours have more colloid. Haemorrhage secondary to FNA may be present.

Histopathology

FAs are encapsulated, follicular-patterned tumours that lack capsular or vascular invasion. The capsule of FA ranges from thin to thick, although it is generally thinner than that seen with FTC {1373582; 21455197}. FAs can have a microfollicular, normofollicular, or macrofollicular growth pattern. Solid or trabecular growth can also occur, usually in combination with follicular-patterned growth. The tumour cells are generally cuboidal or polygonal and they have a moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm, round nuclei with smooth nuclear contours, and variably coarse chromatin. Scattered cells with marked nuclear enlargement and hyperchromasia (endocrine atypia) may be present. However, nuclear features of PTC are absent. Mitoses are usually rare (< 3 mitoses/2 mm2), and coagulative tumour necrosis is absent. Although the stroma of FA is often scant, it can be oedematous, myxoid, or hyalinized. Calcification and osseous metaplasia may be present in tumours with extensive hyalinization. Infrequently, FAs have areas of lipomatous metaplasia. Fibrosis and/or haemorrhage secondary to prior FNA may be present.

The histological distinction between FA and hyperplastic nodule is straightforward when the FA is solitary and has a well-defined capsule and an architecture that differs from the surrounding parenchyma. However, FA occurring in a background of FND, clinically known as multinodular goitre, can be difficult to distinguish from hyperplastic nodules with adenomatous change, and it should be acknowledged that without mutation status the distinction may be arbitrary.

Because distinction between follicular thyroid carcinoma and FA rests on the histological evaluation of the capsule, the capsule should be sampled as extensively as resources permit. Moreover, serial/deeper sections should be considered to resolve foci of questionable capsular or vascular invasion. If deeper levels do not allow for the definitive classification of a tumour as FA or follicular thyroid carcinoma, a diagnosis of follicular tumour of uncertain malignant potential can be rendered (see section Thyroid tumours of uncertain malignant potential). However, this category should be used sparingly.

Immunophenotype

FAs are positive for CAM5.2, EMA, CK7, and the transcription factors PAX8 and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), whereas they are negative for CK20 and calcitonin {2428725; 10981870; 21552115}. The cytoplasm and associated colloid of FA are positive for thyroglobulin {10981870}. FAs are usually negative for galectin-3, HBME1, and CITED1 {17050067; 17308358}. Consistent with the low mitotic rate of FA, the Ki-67 proliferation index is low (usually < 5%) {9504687}. There are no immunohistochemical stains that reliably distinguish FTC from FA (although stains that highlight endothelial cells can be used to evaluate vascular invasion).

Histological patterns

Specific patterns of FA are rare. Hyperfunctioning (so-called toxic or hot) adenomas are discussed separately (see section Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture). Some FAs that are not hyperfunctioning demonstrate papillary hyperplasia (FA with papillary hyperplasia). FAs with papillary hyperplasia are often cystic and have papillae that are usually non-branching. Collections of follicles may be present within the papillary structures. The papillae are lined by cells that range from cuboidal to columnar. Unlike in hyperfunctioning FA, the cytoplasm is not vacuolated and the associated colloid is not bubbly and lacks peripheral scalloping. It is important that FAs with papillary hyperplasia are not mistaken for PTC. FAs with papillary hyperplasia have round nuclei without pallor, grooves, or pseudoinclusions, i.e. they lack the nuclear features of PTC. Additionally, psammomatous calcifications are absent.

Clear cell FAs have a predominance of clear cells. Clear cell change, which can occur in a range of benign and malignant thyroid tumours {2829386; 4061729; 3957248; 30370813; 30931661}, occurs secondary to an accumulation of glycogen, lipids, thyroglobulin, or vesicles of apparent mitochondrial derivation {3957248; 4061729}. Because clear cell FA is a rare tumour, other tumours with clear cytoplasm should be excluded, including metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, and parathyroid tumours. It should be noted that thyroglobulin staining can be focal or weak in thyroid tumours with clear cytoplasm {3061922}; hence, the combined use of TTF1 and PAX8 may be more helpful diagnostically.

The signet-ring cell pattern of FA has cells with cytoplasmic vacuoles with eccentrically displaced nuclei {4061729; 2996373}. The cytoplasmic vacuoles, which have been shown to be similar to follicular lumina, have accumulation of thyroglobulin {2996373}. The nuclei are generally uniform and bland. The signet-ring cell pattern of FA should be distinguished from a metastatic adenocarcinoma with signet-ring cell morphology, which can rarely present as a thyroid nodule {20014306}.

Mature adipose tissue can be seen in a range of neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions of the thyroid {2660611}. Lipoadenoma (also called adenolipoma or thyrolipoma) may have areas of yellow on cut surface. Histologically, the tumour has variable proportions of mature adipose cells dispersed throughout {6433552; 3957249}. Lipoadenomas have been reported in patients with Cowden syndrome {26185318; 10594284} and after exposure to radiation {2660611}.

FA with bizarre nuclei is characterized by markedly enlarged and hyperchromatic tumour cells arranged singly or in clusters throughout the tumour. These bizarre nuclei can be considered as endocrine atypia and are not a marker of malignancy. Mitotic activity and tumour necrosis are absent in these tumours.

In the rare spindle cell pattern of FA, the tumour demonstrates extensive spindle cell metaplasia {11863215; 15516778}. The spindle cells are bland and have nuclei that range from elongated to more plump with chromatin that may be finely granular or coarse, and with variably distinct nucleoli. Because of the rarity of this tumour, it is essential to rule out the spindle cell subtype of medullary thyroid carcinoma and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, which rarely may be encapsulated {24865498}. To render a diagnosis of the spindle cell pattern of FA, the tumour must have a low mitotic count (< 3 mitoses/2 mm2) and must lack tumour necrosis. The tumour should be positive for TTF1 and thyroglobulin, negative for calcitonin, and have a Ki-67 proliferation index < 5%.

Black FA can be seen in patients treated with minocycline, as similarly reported in patients with black thyroid {10937053}. On cut section the tumour is black. Histologically, brown to black pigment is seen within the colloid and cytoplasm of the follicular cells. Dense osmophilic material present within the lysosomes of the follicular cells can be seen by electron microscopy.

Cytology

Smears are usually highly cellular and show a monotonous population of follicular cells, arranged singly, in cohesive sheets, and as microfollicles, in a bloody background with limited thin colloid. Some cases may show prominent nuclear overlapping and crowding. Nuclei are round with even chromatin, although some larger nuclei, a degree of nuclear pleomorphism, and more prominent single nucleoli may be seen in some cases. FA and FTC cannot be distinguished on FNAB, because the diagnosis of carcinoma is based on the demonstration of capsular and/or vascular invasion. Because nuclear features of non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features and encapsulated follicular variant of PTC may be subtle, it can be difficult to distinguish these tumours from FA on FNAB, and many will be reported as follicular neoplasm / suspicious for follicular neoplasm.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a non-invasive, encapsulated, follicular-patterned tumour; absence of nuclear features of PTC; architecture and cytomorphology of FA should be distinct from the background thyroid parenchyma.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

FAs are benign tumours that do not metastasize and do not recur if completely excised.
