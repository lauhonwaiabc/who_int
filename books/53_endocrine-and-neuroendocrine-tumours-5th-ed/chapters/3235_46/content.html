3.2.3.5 Oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland

Definition

Oncocytic carcinoma (OCA) is an invasive malignant follicular cell neoplasm composed of oncocytic cells (≥ 75%) in which the nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and high-grade features are absent.

ICD-O coding

8290/3 Oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland

8290/3 Minimally invasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland (capsular invasion only)

8290/3 Encapsulated angioinvasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland

8290/3 Widely invasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland

ICD-11 coding

2D10.Y & XH09D6 Other specified malignant neoplasms of thyroid gland & Oxyphilic adenocarcinoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: Hürthle cell carcinoma; oxyphilic carcinoma; follicular carcinoma, oncocytic variant; follicular carcinoma, Hürthle cell type.

Subtype(s)

Minimally invasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland (capsular invasion only); encapsulated angioinvasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland; widely invasive oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland

Localization

OCA can arise anywhere in the thyroid, including in ectopic locations such as lingual thyroid and the mediastinum {657301; 20013317; 29898455; 32444244; 33760495}.

Clinical features

OCA usually presents as a slowly enlarging, painless solitary thyroid nodule. Patients with widely invasive tumours report a palpable neck mass with compressive symptoms, whereas minimally invasive tumours are usually either identified as a palpable thyroid nodule or found incidentally on radiological screening {27943373; 10572560; 33364419}. Distant metastasis at the time of presentation is seen in 15–27% of cases, and in as many as 40% of cases with extensive vascular invasion {33374707}. Thyroid function tests are usually within normal ranges in most patients with OCA {33374707; 26462967}.

Thyroid ultrasound cannot differentiate between oncocytic adenoma and OCA. Most OCAs show a variety of ultrasound appearances, ranging from hypoechoic to hyperechoic nodules with a peripheral halo due to the tumour capsule and compressed peripheral vessels {33374707}. Tumours with invasion beyond the tumour capsule will show irregular margins, and extrathyroidal extension can be seen on ultrasound. On radionuclide scan most OCAs are hypofunctioning; however, rare hyperfunctioning cases have been reported {31798874}. Most OCAs are FDG-avid tumours {23653645; 31466411}.

Epidemiology

OCA is an uncommon thyroid malignancy, accounting for 3.6% of thyroid carcinomas in the USA {10897019}. OCA is rare in children and occurs across a wide age range in adults (mean age: 58 years) {22893587; 33374707}. OCA is more common in women (M:F ratio: 1:2.2) {22893587; 38572629}.

Etiology

There are no known etiological factors for OCA. Some of these tumours occur in individuals with genetically defined tumour syndromes such as PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (e.g. Cowden syndrome) and Carney complex (PRKAR1A mutation) {22514109; 22136209; 29239041; 26837556; 33088792; 26530486}.

Pathogenesis

Accumulation of abnormal mitochondria in follicular cells is a defining feature of oncocytic neoplasia of the thyroid. Complete mitochondrial DNA sequencing of 45 benign and malignant oncocytic tumours identified mitochondrial DNA mutations clustering within complex I of the electron transport chain in a majority of cases {17517629}. These mutations were homoplasmic or highly heteroplasmic (> 70%), above the threshold for a biochemically damaging effect on mitochondria, and they are responsible for the oncocytic phenotype {16778181; 17517629}. A recent OCA study confirmed the findings and identified mitochondrial DNA mutations in 24 (60%) of 40 primary and metastatic tumours {30107175}. The high prevalence of deleterious complex I mitochondrial DNA mutations in OCA suggests that dysfunctional complex I may be advantageous for tumour growth {30107175}.

In OCA, chromosome losses are widespread, leading to near-haploidy, and they are observed more frequently than chromosomal gains {10923924; 22675538}. Specifically, monosomies of chromosomes 8, 22, and 2, as well as gains of chromosomes 7, 12, and 17, are most common {10923924}. Tumours with a greater number of chromosome losses exhibited poorer outcomes by retrospective analysis {11420462}. Minimally invasive OCAs and those with limited vascular invasion are frequently diploid, whereas widely invasive OCAs and those with extensive vascular invasion are polysomic and almost always exhibit amplification of chromosome 7 {30107176}. A subset of near-haploid OCAs undergo whole-genome duplication, resulting in uniparental disomy of much of the genome {30107176}. A comparison of related primary and metastatic tumours revealed that chromosomal content is stable, suggesting it does not play a role in metastatic progression {30107175}.

Macroscopic appearance

OCAs are usually solitary round to oval nodules, usually encapsulated, with an average size of 30–40 mm {20842220; 23952654}. The capsule of an OCA is thicker than that of an oncocytic adenoma and is often partly calcified. The tumour itself ranges from tan to mahogany in colour and is usually solid. The larger the neoplasm, the greater the risk of malignancy, with an estimated two thirds of lesions > 35 mm showing invasive growth. Some OCAs that have been biopsied (either FNAB or core biopsy) undergo partial or complete infarction that can be noted grossly.

OCAs can show gross capsular invasion and (rarely) vascular invasion in the extrathyroidal soft tissues.

Histopathology

OCAs are usually thickly encapsulated neoplasms that demonstrate capsular and/or vascular invasion. By convention, ≥ 75% of the tumour cells should display oncocytic cytology {1636432; 23543667}. Although some tumours show a follicular (often microfollicular) pattern, most have solid or solid/trabecular growth. The lesional capsule may have multiple foci of dystrophic calcifications. The intraluminal non-lamellated calcifications seen in oncocytic adenomas or oncocytic hyperplastic nodules are seen less often in OCA, because most of these lack a follicular growth pattern {30325491; 24508690; 20013317; 32444244; 24406625}.

Like follicular thyroid carcinoma, OCA is classified as minimally invasive, encapsulated with vascular invasion, and widely invasive. The term “minimally invasive” is restricted to tumours showing only tumour capsule invasion, whereas the encapsulated tumours showing vascular invasion are further subdivided into tumours with limited (< 4 vessels) or extensive (≥ 4 vessels) vascular invasion. The criteria for capsular and vascular invasion are similar to those used in follicular carcinoma {20013317; 24406625; 24508690; 30325491; 32444244}.

Infarcted areas encountered in OCAs that have been biopsied are often associated with haemosiderin and macrophages, distinguishing them from true tumour necrosis that portends a poorly differentiated carcinoma or high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma. Some of the post-FNA changes can mimic capsular invasion, but linear geography and associated inflammatory changes can assist in differentiating these phenomena from true capsular invasion {7936753; 10478135}.

Tumour cells have abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm with round nuclei and prominent central nucleoli. Areas of random nuclear atypia, including multinucleated giant cells, may be seen. Electron microscopy shows numerous abnormal mitochondria {32444244; 20013317; 33374707; 33108684; 29102432; 28554832; 2783381; 26003547}.

The stroma of OCA is often sparse, with rare (usually very large) tumours showing significant fibrous stroma. Because the neoplasms are usually stroma-poor, a pseudopapillary architecture as an artefact of tissue processing can be mistaken for PTC. Nuclear cytology is helpful in distinguishing these entities.

OCAs have a different biological potential to non-OCA follicular carcinomas. Although the latter spread exclusively through the venous system, OCAs can metastasize to lymph nodes as well as haematogenously. However, it is important to note that some examples of locoregional node metastasis represent tumour plugs in veins in the soft tissues of the neck, and not lymph nodes replaced by tumour {22524498}.

Distant metastases of OCA target lungs, bones, and liver. Some hepatic metastases are solitary and may be misdiagnosed as primary hepatocellular carcinoma {32398118; 28163939; 15053244}.

Small series of OCA with certain histological features have been reported to have a more guarded prognosis than usual OCA. The microscopic features of concern include tumour necrosis, numerous mitoses (≥ 1.5/mm2), abnormal mitoses, and foci of small cell change (cytoplasmic loss) {25898815}. Tumours with these features are likely to be radioiodine resistant {30206719} and in metastatic sites may fail to express thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and thyroglobulin, causing diagnostic confusion. OCAs with necrosis and mitotic activity are best classified as high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma, oncocytic type (see section High-grade follicular cell–derived non-anaplastic thyroid carcinoma)

No immunostains can distinguish OCA from oncocytic adenoma. All oncocytic follicular tumours show an immunoprofile similar to that of other follicular-derived neoplasms, with positivity for thyroglobulin, CK7, PAX8, and TTF1. Calcitonin staining is negative. In some cases, thyroglobulin will show punctate staining confined to the perinuclear area {29744727}.

Cytology

Smears are usually cellular and show > 75% oncocytic follicular cells, but the features of oncocytic adenomas and OCA are the same, and distinguishing between oncocytic follicular adenoma and carcinoma is not possible on FNAB alone {32013786; 32444244; 27385138; 21173136; 16604553; 22672530}. Smears show a monotonous population of oncocytic follicular cells with characteristic voluminous granular cytoplasm, sharp cytoplasmic borders, usually centrally located nuclei with an even chromatin pattern, and prominent round single nucleoli. Thin fibrovascular strands (transgressing vessels) can be seen in the tissue fragments, separating the oncocytic cells. Intracytoplasmic lumina can be seen {11473452; 16181309; 16453320}. Prominent or large irregular nucleoli and multinucleation are not diagnostic of carcinoma {18232004; 12378592}, and prominent nuclear grooves can be seen in oncocytic follicular cells leading to a mistaken diagnosis of subtypes of PTC that can have oncocytic cytoplasm. In some cases, the nucleus of the oncocytic follicular cells can be located eccentrically, mimicking medullary thyroid carcinoma {32444244; 16453320}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Mutations typical of thyroid cancer genes, including RAS genes, TSHR, EIF1AX, TP53, PTEN, BRAF, PAX8::PPARG, and MEN1 {33792557; 25880213; 30107176; 23543667; 30107175; 25625803; 26878173; 23979959; 31293091; 26530486; 33108684}, are generally uncommon in OCA, causing false negative molecular results in preoperative FNA samples. Alterations of chromatin regulatory genes are reported at low frequency {30107176; 30107175; 32674319; 33318036}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an invasive follicular cell–derived neoplasm consisting of oncocytic cells that account for ≥ 75% of the tumour; absence of PTC nuclei and high-grade features.

Staging

OCA is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems, incorporating age at diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis largely depends on the extent of vascular invasion: the more vessels invaded, the more guarded the prognosis (mortality rate: > 90% at 10 years) {33117686}. There appear to be molecular changes associated with those OCAs having extensive vascular invasion; patients affected by such OCAs have a more aggressive clinical course {33117686; 32683470; 32574405; 32398118; 30848171; 32444244; 31397224; 31408918}.

The overall survival rate for OCA ranges from almost 100% in minimally invasive cases (capsular invasion only) to 10% for widely invasive lesions. Tumours with high-grade features and oncocytic poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma have a worse prognosis {25898815}. A small percentage of OCAs undergo transformation to anaplastic carcinoma. This may occur after a history of OCA with recurrence, or it may be noted in the initial diagnostic biopsy or surgery with a tumour showing anaplastic carcinoma intermixed with OCA {32683537}.
