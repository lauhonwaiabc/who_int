7.0.2.1 Phaeochromocytoma

Definition

Phaeochromocytoma is a neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN) that originates from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla and is an intra-adrenal paraganglioma.

ICD-O coding

8700/3 Phaeochromocytoma

ICD-11 coding

2D11.1 & XH3854 Malignant phaeochromocytoma of adrenal gland & Phaeochromocytoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: intra-adrenal paraganglioma; adrenal sympathetic paraganglioma.

Not recommended: chromaffin cell tumour; chromaffinoma; benign phaeochromocytoma; malignant phaeochromocytoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Phaeochromocytoma is an adrenal medullary tumour. These tumours may rarely occur in ectopic adrenal tissue {31610522}. Neoplasms with similar cytomorphological, immunohistochemical, and functional features that arise from dispersed extra-adrenal paraganglia are termed paragangliomas.

Clinical features

Clinical and biochemical features

Most phaeochromocytomas can synthesize catecholamines, and patients with this tumour classically present with signs and symptoms related to catecholamine excess. However, a widening clinical spectrum is now recognized, reflecting differences in patients’ nationalities {32750708}, geographical locations, age groups, and genetic background {33479747}. In contrast to the classic presentation, the majority of phaeochromocytomas are now discovered during the workup of adrenal incidentalomas, in cancer imaging studies, or during the screening of patients with genetic susceptibility syndromes {33479747} (see section SDH-deficient tumour syndromes). Many patients with phaeochromocytomas discovered by these routes have few or no symptoms {33479747}, but the tumours are almost always biochemically functional and amenable to biochemical testing {29332973}. In patients with paraganglioma syndromes, associated neoplasms may present before phaeochromocytomas and can be the first clue to the presence of disease.

In symptomatic patients, the most frequent manifestation is hypertension, most often paroxysmal in adults and sustained in children {31246590}. Classic symptoms are headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis; however, < 25% of patients exhibit this classic triad, and some patients have no symptoms {33479747}. High levels of catecholamines may result in chronic and acute myocardial injury mimicking ischaemic injury, including dilated cardiomyopathy and takotsubo cardiomyopathy, which may present as life-threatening emergencies {33882857}. Other signs and symptoms may include abdominal pain and constipation {26060051}, weight loss, tremor, facial pallor, panic attacks, asthenia, hypertensive crises, and seizures {32491270; 32432211}. Some patients experience (groups of) symptoms as recurrent self-limited spells, which are diagnostically useful but nonspecific to phaeochromocytoma {7630214}. Factors contributing to an absence of symptoms in some patients include small tumour size, intratumoural O-methylation of catecholamines to produce metanephrines {14586065; 15317907}, or a low expression of catecholamine biosynthesizing enzymes associated with some hereditary genotypes {18840642}.

Occasionally, phaeochromocytomas cause endocrine paraneoplastic syndromes by producing ectopic regulatory peptides. The most common is Cushing syndrome, caused by ACTH {19818286} or (rarely) CRH {17039427} secretion. Others include WDHA syndrome (watery diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, and achlorhydria; also called Verner–Morrison syndrome) caused by VIP {175362} and most often associated with composite phaeochromocytomas {25081061; 3623551}. Secretion of various other hormones has been reported, with or without accompanying symptoms {31137729; 31490776}. Examples with accompanying symptoms include β-hCG (hyperandrogenism) {31490776}, vasopressin (haemodynamic instability) {9625677}, growth hormone / GHRH (acromegaly) {9625677}, and calcitonin (hypercalcaemia) {464464}. In addition, inflammatory paraneoplastic symptoms have been reported in association with IL-6 production {33715142}.

Biochemical testing generally precedes imaging studies during clinical workup. Measuring 24 h fractionated urine or plasma free catecholamine metabolites (metanephrine, normetanephrine, 3-methoxytyramine) is often preferable to measuring parent catecholamines, because the metabolites are generated intratumourally {15317907} from parent amines that leak into the tumour cell cytoplasm from secretory granules. These are therefore more sensitive biomarkers than catecholamines per se, which are also produced by sympathetic neurons {29332973}. The serum chromogranin A level is often elevated but is a less sensitive marker than metanephrines {31027285}. New testing approaches aim to identify succinate or other markers associated with specific gene mutations {29332973; 35993574}.

Phaeochromocytomas exhibit a strong correlation between genotype and biochemical phenotype, and accordingly sort into multiple genomic clusters {28162975; 25385035; 16103922; 29332973}. Cluster 1 (the pseudohypoxia cluster) consists of tumours with mutations of genes that affect hypoxic signalling, principally VHL and the SDH genes {29450727; 16103922}, and manifests with dopaminergic, noradrenergic, mixed dopaminergic and noradrenergic, or non-functioning tumours; cluster 2 {16103922} (the tyrosine kinase cluster) is characterized by mutations of RET, NF1, and other genes that affect kinase signalling; and the recently described cluster 3 (the WNT-altered pathway cluster) {28162975} tends to manifest with either adrenergic or mixed adrenergic and noradrenergic tumours {29332973}. In Europe and North America, metanephrine elevation points both to specific genes and to intra-adrenal tumour location, which has led to the belief that extra-adrenal paragangliomas generally cannot produce epinephrine {29332973}. Although this is consistent with the fact that the normal adrenal medulla is essentially the body’s only source of circulating epinephrine {29332973}, a large study from China shows a different genetic and phenotypic landscape from North America and Europe, including fewer germline mutations and multiple epinephrine-producing paragangliomas caused by somatic HRAS and FGFR1 mutations {32750708}. These findings suggest that underlying mutations are the main determinant of biochemical phenotype and that the rarity of epinephrine-producing paragangliomas in populations of European descent is caused by a predominance of cluster 1 mutations {32750708}.

Imaging

Of the structural/anatomical imaging modalities, CT and MRI are equally considered the first choice for defining tumour size and extent {30684056}. Because of CT-related radiation concerns, MRI is often employed in paediatric age groups {31246590; 30589099}. Ultrasound is considered for children aged < 10 years who cannot tolerate MRI {30589099; 27678251}. Most phaeochromocytomas demonstrate a characteristic delineated hyperintense appearance (the radiological lightbulb sign) on T2-weighted MRI {30684056}. However, heterogeneous or lower intensity may also occur {31538225}. Whole-body MRI provides additional value in the detection of bilateral and extra-adrenal tumours in patients harbouring germline mutations {31538225}.

Functional imaging studies are used for purposes including the workup of patients with an adrenal mass and inconclusive biochemical results, assessment of multifocal disease, and staging/screening for metastatic and locoregional tumour spread. In addition, theragnostic imaging, defined as the use of a tracer molecule labelled with one radioisotope for imaging followed by the same tracer with a different isotope for treatment, is increasingly employed. The sensitivity of a functional imaging modality is influenced by tumour location, tumour mutation status, and the transporters or receptors that the tumour may express {29450723; 25554089}. A long-established use of this approach is imaging with iobenguane (metaiodobenzylguanidine; 123I-MIBG), which binds to the norepinephrine transporter, followed by 131I-MIBG for radiotherapy of unresectable or metastatic disease if high signal intensity indicates that the transporter is highly expressed {29450723}. Similarly, SSTR imaging using 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT may determine eligibility for peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with 177Lu- or 90Y-DOTATATE {29450723; 25554089; 31569282}. 64Cu has recently been proposed as an effective substitute for 68Ga {33073559}.

Epidemiology

The estimated incidence of phaeochromocytomas ranges from 0.4 to 9.5 cases per 1 million person-years, with national and regional differences resulting in part from different founder mutations causing varied prevalence rates and types of hereditary tumours {33479747; 32750708}. Nationwide population studies show that the incidence of phaeochromocytoma has increased approximately 5-fold since the 1970s {33479747}, mostly due to an increase in small incidentalomas in older patients with few or no symptoms {33479747}. Most phaeochromocytomas are diagnosed in patients in their fifth or sixth decade of life, with a roughly equal sex distribution or a slight female predominance {33479747}. As many as 20% occur in children {28752085}, who may be as young as 2.7 years {20583150}.

As many as 40% of phaeochromocytomas overall and as many as 80% in children {27865588; 28324046} are associated with germline mutations in hereditary susceptibility genes. The risk of metastasis varies according to the genotype and guides recommendations for screening {31246590; 28752085}. Multifocal tumours, including phaeochromocytomas with synchronous or metachronous paragangliomas, are more frequent in children than in adults {28324046} and are suggestive of hereditary disease. However, hereditary disease can also manifest in elderly patients as an apparently sporadic tumour {14739494}.

Etiology

Genetic factors

Genetic and/or epigenetic alterations of ≥ 23 mutually exclusive genes are responsible for tumour development. At least 17 are hereditary susceptibility genes, including RET, NF1, VHL, TMEM127, SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SDHAF2, FH, MAX, EPAS1, DLST, MDH2, GOT2, SLC25A11, and DNMT3A. Other genes that are not implicated in heredity but are somatically mutated in sporadic tumours include HRAS, BRAF, SETD2, FGFR1, TP53, ATRX, ARNT, IDH1, H3-3A (H3F3A), MET, and CSDE1 {33059362}. Some mutated genes, especially NF1, RET, and VHL, are associated with both hereditary and sporadic tumours, whereas others, especially SDH genes, are exclusively hereditary {33059362}.

Risk factors

The environmental factors triggering tumour development are largely unknown. Chronic hypoxia due to cyanotic congenital heart disease has been proposed as a risk factor {31687640}. Some of the patients described have underlying genetic susceptibility, suggesting that hypoxia is a phenotype modifier affecting penetrance, and the majority of them harbour VHL or SDH gene mutations, which drive hypoxic signalling {27865588}. Other patients have no detected underlying genetic abnormality {30340856}.

Pathogenesis

According to transcriptional profiling, most alterations cluster to either hypoxia signalling (cluster 1) or kinase signalling (cluster 2) pathways {28162975; 25385035; 16103922}. In cluster 1 tumours, different cellular mechanisms cause stabilization of hypoxia-inducible transcription factors and consequent upregulation of hypoxia signalling target genes. Additional features of tumours caused by mutations in Krebs cycle genes (e.g. SDH genes, FH) are metabolic rewiring and inhibition of demethylases and deacetylases by oncometabolites, resulting in a hypermethylator phenotype and altered gene expression profiles {25385035; 29450727} including hypoxia-related microRNAs (e.g. miR-210) {24623741}. Mutations in cluster 2 tumours (e.g. RET, NF1, TMEM127) result in the activation of kinase signalling pathways via different mechanisms involving the MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways {22391976}. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project on the genomic characterization of phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas also revealed rare somatic MAML3 oncogene fusions and CSDE1 mutations that defined the WNT-altered pathway (cluster 3 disease) in sporadic adult phaeochromocytomas {28162975}.

Macroscopic appearance

Phaeochromocytomas originate from the adrenal medulla and may extend into adjacent structures. They are circumscribed but usually unencapsulated. As they expand, they compress the adrenal cortex, and the capsule of the adrenal becomes the apparent capsule of the tumour. A tumour pseudocapsule may be present. However, a thick vascularized capsule in association with degenerative vascular stroma raises the possibility of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {25611110}. The tumour diameter is usually about 30–50 mm but may exceed 100 mm. In small tumours, the cut surface is pink-grey to tan (in contrast to yellow adrenal cortical neoplasms), but it may become slightly yellow after exposure to air or formalin. As tumours enlarge, haemorrhage, cystic change, and fibrosis are variably present. Sampling of the non-tumorous adrenal tissue is required in order to search for additional adrenal medullary micronodules indicative of a hereditary disease, especially multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, or more rarely VHL-related, SDH-related, or MAX-related genetic disease {25379023; 21172883; 27838885}. The tumour periphery should also be searched for residual adrenal cortex to confirm an origin within rather than adjacent to the adrenal, which would have different genetic and clinical implications.

Molecular profiling has shown that distinct medullary nodules usually represent synchronous microphaeochromocytomas rather than adrenal medullary hyperplasia {23478233; 25379023}, making the latter terminology no longer tenable. In the absence of medullary nodules, diffuse hyperplasia can be recognized if medullary tissue, which is normally confined to the head and body of the adrenal, extends prominently into the alae and tail {1275056}. However, normal anatomical variation should be kept in mind, and mild diffuse hyperplasia is not reliably detectable without morphometric analysis {1275056}.

Histopathology

Phaeochromocytomas exhibit wide architectural and cytological variation, sometimes in a single tumour {11979086}. A Zellballen pattern is most common, consisting of nests of tumour cells separated by peripheral capillaries. Trabecular or diffuse growth with large confluent nests and/or pseudorosettes variably occurs. Conventional tumours consist of cells resembling normal chromaffin cells that display basophilic to amphophilic granular cytoplasm. In some tumours, the cells may be smaller or larger than normal, the latter often with vesicular nuclei and conspicuous nucleoli. PAS-positive hyaline globules and nuclear pseudoinclusions are often present. Nuclear atypia and cellular pleomorphism are sometimes conspicuous, but mitoses are rare {18549892}. Less common features are spindle cells, clear cells (lipid degeneration), focal or extensive oncocytic cells {25252620}, and lipofuscin or cutaneous-type melanin {26578456}. Scattered ganglion cells may be present but are not sufficient for a diagnosis of composite phaeochromocytoma, which requires complete architectural features of ganglioneuroma or ganglioneuroblastoma (see section Composite phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas).

Immunohistochemistry

Most phaeochromocytomas are positive for tyrosine hydroxylase, DBH {33405069}, GATA3, and general neuroendocrine biomarkers (chromogranin A, synaptophysin, INSM1 {32219703}), and are negative for keratins {32407815; 27529763}. S100 and SOX10 immunohistochemistry stains intratumoural sustentacular cells. Tyrosine hydroxylase expression can be focal or, rarely, negative in non-functioning tumours {18840642}. Immunohistochemistry is sometimes required, to distinguish between a phaeochromocytoma and an adrenocortical neoplasm, particularly in cases with predominant clear or oncocytic cell change. Positivity for melan-A and SF1 and negativity for chromogranin A, GATA3, and tyrosine hydroxylase are characteristic of adrenal cortical tumours {29542002}. Synaptophysin and inhibin {33826547} can be expressed in both phaeochromocytoma and adrenal cortical tumours and should not be used to distinguish the two.

Like in other NENs, the tumour proliferation rate should be routinely assessed {32407815} by performing a formal mitotic count from areas with a high mitotic density and determining the Ki-67 (MIB1) labelling index (by counting ≥ 1000 tumour cells from hotspots, either using an automated nuclear algorithm or by image-captured counting). Mitoses are often very rare or absent, and the Ki-67 index is usually less than about 10% {11979086}. A high proliferative fraction should raise the suspicion that a tumour is not a phaeochromocytoma.

Histological features are not a reliable indicator of tumour genotype but can be suggestive in some cases. Cytoplasmic eosinophilia and/or intracytoplasmic vacuoles and clearing, as well as tight, well-formed, rounded nests of neoplastic cells, may be seen more commonly in SDH-related tumours {27529763; 29239044; 31789631}. Clear cell change, especially in association with other morphological findings (e.g. a thick vascular tumour capsule, a myxoid and hyalinized stroma, a rich vascular network intermixed with tumour cells displaying variable clear and amphophilic cytoplasm, an absence of cytoplasmic hyaline globules) raises the suspicion of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {12114747; 25611110; 29239044}. However, molecular immunohistochemistry is a complement to genetic testing and can predict or validate test results {33433885}. Loss of SDHB expression serves as a surrogate biomarker for tumours caused by SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, or SDHAF2 alterations {25720320; 20236688; 19576851}. In addition to loss of SDHB, loss of SDHA expression is found in tumours driven by SDHA mutations {25720320; 21752896}. Loss of MAX expression may be a clue to MAX mutation (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5), but its sensitivity and specificity have been questioned {33367756; 29779206; 26670126}. Loss of fumarate hydratase together with the presence of 2-succinocysteine {29079178; 29239044; 30281371; 35993574} can facilitate screening for FH-related tumours. Membranous CAIX supports a diagnosis of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome but can also be found in some SDH-related tumours {23257898; 33826547}. Recently, α-inhibin was suggested as a novel immunohistochemical biomarker of pseudohypoxia-related tumours {33826547}.

Diagnosis of metastases

Multifocal primary paragangliomas in various sites should not be mistaken for the metastatic spread of phaeochromocytoma {30217041}. Metastasis is defined by a tumour identified at sites where normal paraganglia do not occur (i.e. histologically confirmed lymph node or bone). Familiarity with the normal anatomical distribution of autonomic nervous system elements is essential when diagnosing metastatic disease {30217041}. Involvement of the lungs or liver is not always a sign of metastatic disease, because primary paragangliomas can occur, rarely, in those locations {30217041}. The absence of sustentacular cells (positive for SOX10 and S100) may favour metastatic disease {1673596}.

Cytology

FNAB of phaeochromocytomas is not advised because of potential bleeding or catecholamine release with possible life-threatening hypertensive crisis {19958944}. When FNAB is used to assess an adrenal or other intra-abdominal mass where phaeochromocytoma is suspected, preprocedural biochemical testing for plasma fractionated metanephrines or 24 h urine fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines is required in order to exclude a phaeochromocytoma. When it is inadvertently undertaken, complications may occur, although FNAB and core needle biopsy may have different rates of individual complications {16900997}. The smears are often highly cellular and bloody, with discohesive syncytial tissue fragments and dispersed single cells that vary from small to large and have abundant round to spindle cytoplasm that may contain red granules (on Giemsa stain) or hyaline intracytoplasmic bodies (on Pap) and oval to pleomorphic nuclei with fine speckled chromatin. Scattered large, highly pleomorphic nuclei with variably prominent nucleoli, binucleation, and intranuclear pseudoinclusions are usually present {16900997}. Mitoses may be seen, along with necrosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology is not required to render the diagnosis of a phaeochromocytoma. However, all patients should be offered some degree of germline screening, taking into consideration the clinical and pathological findings as well as the local resources.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a NEN arising from the adrenal medulla with consistent morphology and immunohistochemical confirmation in relevant cases.

Desirable:

Succinate dehydrogenase immunohistochemistry to guide subsequent genetic testing Routine assessment of cell proliferation (mitotic count, Ki-67/MIB1 labelling index) Molecular immunohistochemistry biomarkers relevant to tumour pathogenesis Documentation of elevated levels of plasma free metanephrines or urine fractionated metanephrines (at least metanephrine and normetanephrine) Functional imaging studies and routine germline testing; measurement of all three metabolites of parent catecholamines (3-methoxytyramine, metanephrine, and normetanephrine)

Staging

The eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system is applied to these tumours. Multidisciplinary international expert pathology reporting templates with integrated clinicopathological data are available {24476517; 32407815}. A recent international pathology-standardized reporting guide was introduced by the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) {32407815}. Tumour size ≥ 50 mm and the presence of invasion into surrounding tissues including extra-adrenal adipose increase tumour stage in the AJCC system {28234804}.

Prognosis and prediction

All patients with phaeochromocytomas are currently considered to have a lifelong risk of metastases, and therefore, conceptually, all phaeochromocytomas are considered malignant. The risk of metastasis ranges from ~5 to 15%. There is no single histological finding or biomarker to reliably predict metastatic disease, and multiparameter scoring systems have been proposed. The most readily accepted adverse histological findings common to most systems are a high proliferative fraction, comedonecrosis, a diffuse growth pattern, and high cellularity {32407815}. Four systems currently exist: the phaeochromocytoma of the adrenal gland scaled score (PASS) {11979086}, the grading system for adrenal phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma (GAPP) {24521857}, the modified GAPP {29117221}, and the recently proposed composite phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma prognostic score (COPPS) {30868297}. Validation studies have confirmed that both PASS and GAPP have predictive value to varying degrees, especially as a rule-out rather than a rule-in approach for risk stratification {32877928; 30769931}. Both systems also may overestimate risk if the rule-in approach is used {29779047}. The use of scoring systems is neither endorsed nor discouraged, but it is generally agreed that histological parameters are best interpreted in conjunction with clinical and molecular data {32407815}. The prognosis also depends on complete tumour resection. One approach is to suggest, on the basis of all clinical, molecular, and pathogenic findings, that a tumour may be of “low to average” or of “higher than average” risk of metastasis.

From a molecular perspective, germline SDHB mutations confer the highest risk of metastasis {32062700}. Somatic mutations in ATRX or SETD2, a high total somatic mutation burden, MAML3 fusions, the WNT-altered pathway, and TERT activation have also been associated with increased risk {28162975; 30301828}.
