13.0.0.1 Genetic tumour syndromes associated with endocrine lesions and tumours: Introduction

Since the first description of a patient with a combination of pituitary and parathyroid endocrine tumours in 1903, many tumours that were initially considered sporadic occurrences are now considered parts of well-defined syndromes. With the rapidly increasing use of molecular genetic techniques, including next-generation sequencing, in both diagnostic pathology and clinical genetics, numerous new constitutional gene mutations have been identified in tumours. These genetic alterations have other phenotypic consequences as well, resulting in clinically recognizable tumour-predisposing syndromes. It is increasingly challenging for pathology to keep pace with scientific discoveries by assimilating novel concepts in tumour classification, molecular genetics, and prognostication. Knowledge of underlying genetic variants can often have an important role in determining optimal therapy and surveillance guidelines.

Histopathology plays a critical role in the identification of hereditary disease, because it offers tools to complement molecular genetics, including morphological findings and immunohistochemistry. The latter can be a reliable and time-saving technique in the evaluation of some somatic mutations in tumours and/or of germline mutations in patients with familial cancer syndromes {16103922; 21921783; 25720320; 30730313}.

The last edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs, published in 2017, contained a chapter on inherited tumour syndromes, containing dedicated sections on 12 syndromes associated with endocrine tumours. Within the past few years, additional syndromes involving the endocrine organs have been identified. This current edition of the WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours now describes 15 syndromes associated with endocrine lesions and tumours. These hereditary syndromes are associated with a range of benign and malignant neoplasms involving multiple organ systems and are not limited to the endocrine system.

For the first time, germline mutation in MAX is discussed and classified as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 – a hereditary syndrome characterized by multiple endocrinopathies, including paraganglioma and/or phaeochromocytoma (PPGL) and pituitary and parathyroid disease, as well as tumours of neural crest origin, such as ganglioneuroma and neuroblastoma.

Knowledge of the molecular associations of the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-deficient tumour syndromes has recently expanded, necessitating clarification of the terminology for the family of SDH-deficient tumour syndromes {29239034}. Although previously defined clinically, the term “Carney triad” is now defined by dual clinical and molecular criteria as a syndromic SDH-deficient tumour syndrome where no germline SDH mutation has been identified. Older terminology, such as “Carney–Stratakis dyad” (which was previously defined as the clinical association of familial SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour and paraganglioma without consideration of the actual gene mutated) is now discouraged in favour of terminology based on the identification of the mutated gene {30877234}. Under this approach, the clinical association of SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour, SDH-deficient paraganglioma, and pulmonary chondroma would now only be considered Carney triad if no pathogenic germline mutation is identified in any of the SDH genes. Similarly, the association of a personal and family history of SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour and SDH-deficient paraganglioma, which would previously warrant clinical classification as Carney–Stratakis dyad, would now be classified on the basis of the underlying genetic abnormality – for example, germline SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, or SDHAF2 mutation syndrome {21173220}.

Familial hyperinsulinism has also been added to the genetic tumour syndromes, with multifocal pancreatic B-cell proliferations and with the identification of MAFA mutation, which distinguishes this from sporadic insulinomatosis {29339498}.

Besides the addition of a few newly discovered familial diseases, as a result of molecular tests and clinical and pathological correlation, it is now well recognized that the familial follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma (non-medullary thyroid carcinoma) is classified as either syndromic or non-syndromic familial follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma. This topic is well covered in two sections that highlight the important role the pathologist plays in recognizing some of these diseases. The distinct thyroid pathology in some of the syndromes should alert the pathologist to a possibility of a familial cancer syndrome {21190448; 29785019; 34019236}. The genes involved in the non-syndromic subtypes have been better characterized more recently, and the thyroid tumours of the non-syndromic follicular cell–derived / non-medullary thyroid carcinoma subtypes are expanding rapidly {29239041}.

These syndromes have been covered in detail because their clinical, histopathological, and genetic characteristics are well characterized in the endocrine system, and some may have associated neoplasms with unique features allowing distinction from their sporadic counterparts.

Not all of the syndromes described in this chapter on genetic tumour syndromes are linked to cancer; some are associated with benign (and premalignant) proliferations, the study of which has contributed to our understanding of the function of their causative genes in important biological processes such as development and tissue homeostasis.
