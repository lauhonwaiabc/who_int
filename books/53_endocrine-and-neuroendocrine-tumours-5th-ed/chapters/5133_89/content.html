5.1.3.3 Mixed acinar-neuroendocrine carcinoma

Definition

Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma is a pancreatic mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN) composed of morphologically and immunohistochemically recognizable acinar cell carcinoma associated with a neuroendocrine component, usually neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), each of which constitutes ≥ 30% of the tumour volume.

ICD-O coding

8154/3 Mixed acinar-neuroendocrine carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y & XH8E54 Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures & Mixed neuroendocrine non-neuroendocrine neoplasm

Related terminology

Acceptable: mixed acinar–endocrine carcinoma.

Not recommended: acinar cell carcinoma–neuroendocrine carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These carcinomas are located throughout the pancreas [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]] {15517367; 23026929; 32111396}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with nonspecific symptoms like abdominal pain and weight loss {23026929}. However, mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas causing jaundice or lipase hypersecretion syndrome have been reported [[Basturk O, Adsay V, Hruban RH, et al. Pancreatic acinar cell carcinomas with prominent neuroendocrine differentiation: clinicopathologic analysis of a distinct and diagnostically challenging neoplasm. Mod Pathol. 2014 Feb 7;27(Suppl 2):447A. Abstract no. 1837. doi:10.1038/modpathol.2014.24.]].

Epidemiology

Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas account for 15–20% of all acinar cell carcinomas {8037290; 23026929; 27320062}. Most of the patients are in their sixties (age range: 37–88 years), and there is no sex predilection {8037290; 15517367; 23026929; 31869246}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

No association with genetic syndromes has been documented. Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas are reported to have the same genetic changes as those seen in pure acinar cell carcinomas, including APC/β-catenin pathway alterations, BRAF rearrangements, and DNA repair defects such as mismatch repair deficiency and BRCA pathway defects {24590585; 25266736; 25441307; 31869246; 32358589}. A recent molecular study suggested that MYC amplification may be a mechanism involved in the neuroendocrine differentiation of acinar cell carcinomas {29721608}.

Macroscopic appearance

These carcinomas typically present as large (range: 15–300 mm), poorly defined, multilobulated tumours with a fleshy cut surface. Haemorrhage and necrosis are common. Intraductal spread may be seen {26837489; 27320062; 32538468}.

Histopathology

Most acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas resemble acinar cell carcinomas and display neuroendocrine differentiation but have a homogeneous morphology. Such cases do not fit the definition of a MiNEN and are best regarded as acinar cell carcinomas with extensive neuroendocrine differentiation, which does not affect their clinical behaviour {25266736} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]]. Acinar cell carcinomas with scattered neuroendocrine cells (< 30% of the tumour cells) do not qualify as mixed carcinomas and should be diagnosed as acinar cell carcinoma [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]].

A few cases show morphologically and immunohistochemically discernible acinar and neuroendocrine components, which form closely connected sheets and nests {27320062}, and these cases fit the definition of a MiNEN.

Immunohistochemistry

In cases fitting the definition of a MiNEN, immunoexpression of both acinar (e.g. trypsin, chymotrypsin, and BCL10) and neuroendocrine (e.g. chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and INSM1) markers, each in ≥ 30% of the tumour cell population, can help recognize the mixed nature of these neoplasms {8037290; 19066953}.

Differential diagnosis

The distinction of mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma from pancreatic NEC (PanNEC) and even well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (PanNET) can be problematic. In fact, in one study, 15% of cases originally classified as PanNEC proved to be mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas on further analysis {24503751; 27320062; 33786701}.

Cytology

Smears from mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas are often cellular and reveal a monotonous population of predominantly single, atypical tumour cells; some loosely cohesive clusters; and rare pseudoacinar formations. The tumour cells are round to oval with small to moderate amounts of delicate to granular cytoplasm. The nuclei have mild to moderate anisonucleosis and granular chromatin with small nucleoli {23408736; 30284406; 32529486; 33128511}. However, these features significantly overlap with those of well-differentiated PanNET, PanNEC, and acinar cell carcinoma. Therefore, demonstration of both acinar and neuroendocrine differentiation by immunohistochemistry is required to suggest the possibility of mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not yet required for diagnosis. However, molecular investigation may be helpful in distinguishing the components of these tumours in small biopsies.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a mixed tumour composed of two components; ≥ 30% of the neoplasm should be composed of each type; immunohistochemical evidence of acinar and neuroendocrine differentiation.

Desirable: Ki-67 immunohistochemistry.

Staging

The pathological staging of mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas is based on the eighth edition (2017) of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]] for carcinomas of the exocrine pancreas.

Prognosis and prediction

Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas mainly behave like acinar cell carcinomas {8037290; 15517367; 23026929} and are more aggressive than PanNETs {32111396}. Most patients have nodal and/or distant metastases at presentation {32111396}. After resection, the reported 5-year survival rate for patients who undergo surgery is 30–50%, similar to the rate associated with acinar cell carcinomas {22042785; 23026929; 32111396; 33309225; 32538468}. There is no evidence that the presence of a neuroendocrine component should affect the oncological treatment of mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas.
