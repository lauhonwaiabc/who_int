13.0.0.2 Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1

Definition

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by pathogenic germline MEN1 variants.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

131100 Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type 1; MEN1

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.0 Multiple polyglandular tumours

Related terminology

Acceptable: MEN1 syndrome.

Not recommended: multiple endocrine adenomatosis type 1; familial Zollinger–Ellison syndrome; Wermer syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Patients with MEN1 present with multifocal endocrine neoplasms in the parathyroids (including ectopic or supernumerary parathyroid glands in the mediastinum), anterior pituitary, pancreas, and duodenum, and less commonly in the stomach, adrenal, thymus, and lungs. In addition, various non-endocrine lesions may occur in the skin, CNS, and soft tissues.

Clinical features

The cardinal components of MEN1 syndrome consist of primary hyperparathyroidism, pituitary adenomas / pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs), and duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (NETs). Other endocrine tumours that can occur in patients with MEN1 include bronchopulmonary NETs, thymic NETs (TNETs), adrenocortical tumours, and phaeochromocytomas. Facial angiofibromas, collagenomas, lipomas, leiomyomas, and CNS tumours (e.g. meningiomas, ependymomas), as well as hibernomas and breast cancers, have also been described {22723327; 25099597; 33564173}. Lipomas, angiofibromas, and collagenomas occur in 30%, 85%, and 70% of patients, respectively, by the age of 4 years {11739416}.

Primary hyperparathyroidism is the most common manifestation and occurs in virtually all patients with MEN1. Hypercalcaemia should lead to the measurement of intact PTH {25162665}. Because multiglandular parathyroid involvement can be asymmetrical and asynchronous, most patients with MEN1 syndrome are not subjected to the more common restricted surgical procedure {21713580; 22470073}. Prophylactic partial thymectomy is also considered by experienced surgeons, given that mediastinal recurrence due to ectopic or supernumerary parathyroid glands occurs in as many as 12% of cases {21713580; 22735224; 22470073}.

Duodenopancreatic NETs (functioning or non-functioning) are usually diagnosed from symptoms related to functioning NETs (e.g. gastrinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, VIPoma) or from imaging studies performed for digestive symptoms or during the screening of non-functioning NETs in MEN1 variant carriers. Serum tumour markers are not useful for diagnosing non-functioning NET in patients with MEN1 {30202829}. For diagnosing gastrinomas, which are often located in the duodenum, serum levels of gastrin (fasting) are assessed. For functioning pancreatic NETs (PanNETs), glucose (fasting), insulin, PP, glucagon, and VIP should be assessed. Conventional (e.g. CT, MRI, EUS) imaging studies are required for localizing small non-functioning NETs {30202829}. Functional imaging studies (e.g. 111In-pentetreotide [Octreoscan] SPECT-CT, 68Ga-DOTATATE/DOTATOC PET-CT, FDG PET) can help determine the extent of various NETs in patients with MEN1 {26242621; 28263205; 28790162; 30202829; 32971521}.

MEN1-associated PitNET/adenoma can be suspected when patients develop symptoms related to pituitary hormone excess (e.g. galactorrhoea and amenorrhoea, acromegaly, Cushing disease) or when a non-functioning macrotumour causes visual field defects or intracranial mass–related symptoms. Biochemical assessment (e.g. IGF1, prolactin, and dynamic endocrine tests) and MRI studies are typically performed {26126205}. Screening protocols identified an increased frequency of pituitary microtumours (< 10 mm) on MRI studies {26126205}; however, invasive pituitary macrotumours (> 10 mm) are overrepresented in some clinical series {18300794}. When an ectopic source of GHRH is suspected as the cause of acromegaly {3145190; 8126126; 22983831}, plasma GHRH over the threshold of 250–300 ng/L has been shown to have excellent specificity {22442262; 23122576}.

MEN1-associated adrenal lesions can be non-functioning {12016472; 17235589}, or they can be seen in association with Cushing syndrome or primary aldosteronism {1639957; 12016472; 21415556; 22084155}. In general, adrenal cortical carcinomas are rare, but they are more common than sporadic manifestations {22084155}.

Most paediatric-onset MEN1 manifestations are diagnosed after a positive predictive genetic test, and affected individuals are generally asymptomatic. In the absence of symptoms or hormone excess, biochemical screening is considered effective in patients aged 5–10 years, and imaging studies (e.g. brain, thoracic, and abdominal MRI) are performed in patients aged 10–15 years or older {22723327; 25594862; 28674121; 28530019; 30339208}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is estimated to be 3–20 cases per 100 000 population {33249439}. About 10% of patients have germline MEN1 sequence variants arising de novo, with no family history {9463336; 23933118}. The mean age at the time of diagnosis ranges between the fourth and fifth decade of life for various MEN1-associated manifestations {28132167; 21950691; 11836268; 26126205; 30637623}. Age-related penetrance and variable expression can occur {23933118; 33249439}.

There is variable female prevalence recorded in MEN1 registries from Italy {28132167}, Japan {21950691}, and the Groupe d’Étude des Tumeurs Endocrines {21551167}. No significant sex differences were identified in MEN1-related primary hyperparathyroidism {21551167}. In addition, pulmonary NETs occurred equally in both sexes in large series {24915123}. However, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) {21551167} and TNETs {21551167; 24915123; 21950691} were recorded more frequently in male patients. Unlike the exclusive male prevalence in some reported European databases {21551167; 24915123}, the frequency of MEN1-associated TNET in female patients was significantly higher in Japan {21950691}. Pituitary adenomas / PitNETs showed a female predilection {11836268; 21551167; 26126205; 30637623}.

The prevalence rates of primary hyperparathyroidism, gastroenteropancreatic NETs (GEP-NETs), and pituitary adenomas / PitNETs are 93–94%, 53–59%, and 38–50% in MEN1 registries, respectively {28132167; 21950691; 11836268; 26126205}. The prevalence of insulinoma was higher in MEN1 patients in Japan than in patients of European descent {21950691}. Gastric enterochromaffin-like cell (ECL-cell) NETs occur in 23–29% of such patients {11344233; 18270260}. Depending on the diagnostic criteria applied (either histologically confirmed or radiologically identified), bronchopulmonary NETs can occur in about 5–23% of patients with MEN1 {24389729; 24915123; 26070346; 26113081; 28785839; 33135721}. The estimated prevalence of TNETs is < 5% {24915123}. Adrenal lesions have been reported in 20–70% of patients with MEN1 {10216472; 16549608; 18426829; 22084155}.

Etiology

Pathogenic germline MEN1 sequence variants, which are inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, are responsible for the development of MEN1 syndrome {23933118; 33249439}.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenic germline MEN1 variants occur in the majority of patients with MEN1 and include a large variety of genetic abnormalities, including those leading to premature truncation, frameshift and nonsense mutations, or large deletions, including those in intronic areas leading to splice-site defects {17879353; 26767918; 33249439}. These are accompanied by loss of the wildtype allele in > 90% of MEN1-driven tumours. The 610-amino-acid protein product is called menin, for which the exact role in tumorigenesis remains to be established, although many roles have been proffered, including in the epigenetic regulation of gene transcription, cell division and proliferation, cell motility, cytoskeletal structure, DNA repair, genomic stability, cell signalling, and transcriptional regulation, through the potential interaction with about 50 proteins {9103196; 9215690; 17879353; 22829075; 23652667; 23850066; 26560942; 28899949; 33249439}. Menin loss has shown correlations with various epigenetic aberrancies in MEN1-related tumours, such as loss of H3 p.K4me3 through inactivation of the KMT2 (MLL2) complex and gain of H3 p.K27me3 at specific genetic sites {25537453}, DNA hypermethylation, and histone acetylation {33249439}. In the absence of loss of heterozygosity at the MEN1 locus, epigenetic silencing via microRNAs may also contribute to the inactivation of the second allele {22761894; 33249439}.

Macroscopic appearance

Patients with MEN1 often present with multiglandular parathyroid disease. The individual parathyroid glands are variably involved, but all are grossly enlarged (> 6–8 mm) and increased in weight (> 40–60 mg). The cut section of parathyroid glands may show nodularity. In patients with MEN1 syndrome, pituitary adenomas / PitNETs are grossly indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts and are white to yellow soft tumours. Multiple contiguous adenomas/PitNETs cannot be distinguished with the naked eye. MEN1-associated pancreatic disease typically shows multiple tan to yellow parenchymal tumour nodules with a homogeneous cut surface. Cystic changes can occur in PanNETs with glucagon expression {20922407}. Pancreatic neuroendocrine microtumours (0.5–5 mm) may be difficult to appreciate grossly. Duodenal NETs are often identified as multiple small nodules in the upper duodenum or at the margin of an ulcer. Duodenal NETs that measure less than 3–5 mm may be difficult to appreciate on gross examination. Multiple gastric nodules in the background of hypertrophic mucosa should raise the possibility of MEN1-associated gastric NETs (type 2 gastric ECL-cell neuroendocrine tumorigenesis). Individual lung NETs and TNETs are often indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. Multiple small pulmonary neuroendocrine microtumours (0.5–5 mm) can also be identified {26113081}. Adequate sampling of the non-tumorous duodenum, pancreas, stomach, and lung is often required because MEN1-associated tumours often show precursor lesions or associated microscopic tumours {23478233; 26113081}. Bilateral adrenal cortical nodules {21266030; 22084155} and rare phaeochromocytomas {22084155} can occur in patients with MEN1, but individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

MEN1-related parathyroid disease

Multiglandular parathyroid disease is the hallmark of MEN1-related primary hyperparathyroidism {15490065; 23933118; 25592387}. Because multiglandular parathyroid involvement can be asymmetrical and asynchronous, most patients with MEN1 syndrome are not subjected to the more common restricted surgical procedure {21713580; 22470073}. As a consequence, surgical pathologists may be asked to evaluate multiple specimens during the full neck and mediastinal exploration of a patient with MEN1. The role of the diagnostician during the intraoperative consultation for MEN1-associated hyperparathyroidism typically involves the distinction of the parathyroid glands from other structures that could be mistaken for a parathyroid gland by the surgeon {26163537}. Although the finding of multiple abnormal parathyroid glands generally results in a diagnosis of parathyroid hyperplasia, recent molecular data suggest that MEN1-related multiglandular parathyroid lesions are composed of multiple monoclonal proliferations, constituting multiple multiglandular microadenomas {26163537; 2568586}. Histologically, they show multinodular proliferations composed primarily of chief cells, followed by oncocytic and/or clear cells {26163537}. Unlike most sporadic parathyroid adenomas, MEN1-associated parathyroid proliferations lack the characteristic atrophic rim of non-lesional parathyroid tissue. Rim-like areas can occur in some glands; however, these regions are often cellular rather than being atrophic. Rarely, cystic changes can occur {23386389}. Nuclear atypia, necrosis, and prominent mitotic figures are uncommon. Glands may show stromal fibrosis and even irregular borders that can mimic the morphological features associated with parathyroid hyperplasia related to longstanding chronic renal failure or lithium exposure, atypical parathyroid tumour, and reactive changes associated with previous manipulations (e.g. FNABs, PTH washout, or therapeutic ethanol ablation) {17021134; 26163537; 26177319; 33740321}. Almost all MEN1-associated parathyroid neoplasms behave as benign tumours; carcinomas are extremely rare {22281890; 25557532}, and their diagnosis requires the unequivocal demonstration of invasion into adjacent organs, vascular invasion, lymphatic invasion, or perineural invasion, and/or the presence of metastases {21455204; 26163537; 26177319}. The parathyroid pathology of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 is histologically indistinguishable from that of MEN1. Patients presenting with MEN1-like parathyroid disease but lacking germline MEN1 mutation should be tested for germline CDKN1B mutation {23933118}.

MEN1-related pituitary disease

At the morphological level, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts {18300794}. PIT1-lineage pituitary adenomas / PitNETs are common in MEN1-related pituitary disease {34017065}; however, other PitNETs, including those of TPIT lineage (e.g. densely granulated corticotroph tumour, sparsely granulated corticotroph tumour, Crooke cell tumour) and SF1 lineage (e.g. gonadotroph tumour) can also occur {30215160; 18300794; 7479350; 33249439}. Plurihormonal tumours have been reported frequently in a series {18300794}; however, among functioning PIT1-lineage tumours, lactotroph tumours are the most frequent, followed by somatotroph and mammosomatotroph tumours {30637623; 26126205; 11836268}. Immature PIT1-lineage PitNETs (formerly known as poorly differentiated PIT1-lineage adenomas) can also occur in patients with MEN1 {26743473}; this is not surprising given the increased frequency of immature PIT1-lineage PitNETs in young adults {26743473; 29434339}. Multiple PitNETs of distinct cell lineages can also occur in patients with MEN1 {11059654; 11081204; 18300794; 30215160}, significantly more frequently than in patients without MEN1 {18300794}. Somatotroph or mammosomatotroph hyperplasia is an uncommon finding in MEN1 syndrome, but when it occurs in this setting, a GHRH-producing PanNET should be excluded {2887080; 18300794}. MEN1-associated PitNETs show an increased number of S100-positive folliculostellate cells, as compared with their sporadic counterparts. The significance of this finding is unknown.

MEN1-related pancreatic disease

The hallmark of MEN1-related pancreatic disease is the presence of multifocal PanNETs (> 5 mm) or a unifocal PanNET arising in the background of neuroendocrine microtumours (each 0.5–5 mm; also known neuroendocrine microadenomatosis) {16699310; 24718881}. Monohormonal cell clusters inside normal islets, as well as neuroendocrine microtumours (microadenomas), have been shown to be clonal, with loss of the wildtype MEN1 allele {17179192}. Such microtumours are rarely found in ducts {17179192}. At the morphological level, individual neoplasms are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. Most are plurihormonal {8898842; 16699310}. Glucagon has been the most frequently identified hormone, followed by PP, insulin, and somatostatin {24718881}. Glucagon-positive tumours may be cystic {20922407}. Rarely, other hormones (e.g. calcitonin, PTHrP) may be expressed {9920064; 21483254}. Gastrin is virtually never expressed. In cases of a MEN1-related hypoglycaemic syndrome, insulin-cell tumours can be the source of insulin excess. Very rarely, a PanNET may express a non-pancreatic hormone (e.g. GHRH causing ectopic acromegaly) {16699310}. Because MEN1 inactivation can occur in about 40% of sporadic PanNETs {33459926}, loss of menin expression cannot be used to distinguish MEN1 syndrome in the absence of multifocal neuroendocrine microtumours (microadenomatosis). However, global loss of nuclear menin expression may help rationalize germline screening for MEN1-related pathogenesis, especially when global loss of nuclear menin expression occurs in multiple PanNETs and microtumours [[Mete O, Hannah-Shmouni F, Kim R, et al. Inherited neuroendocrine neoplasms. In: Asa SL, La Rosa S, Mete O, editors. The spectrum of neuroendocrine neoplasia: a practical approach to diagnosis, classification and therapy. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2021. pp. 409–59.]].

MEN1-related duodenal disease

Multifocal duodenal NETs that arise in the background of neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia is the hallmark of MEN1-related duodenal disease. Hyperplastic gastrin and somatostatin cells are typically identified in the crypts and in the Brunner glands {15887103; 24718881}. Multifocal gastrin-producing duodenal NETs are the most common cause of Zollinger–Ellison syndrome {1678681; 1968616; 23478233; 24718881}. In almost all MEN1 patients with Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, the source of gastrin excess is multicentric duodenal NETs {1678681; 17312377; 17985091; 1968616}, typically in the mucosa and submucosa of the upper duodenum. The tumours are usually < 10 mm and may therefore be difficult to detect {18520436}. In as many as 80% of cases, the tumours give rise to large periduodenal and/or peripancreatic lymph node metastases, which were formerly interpreted as pancreatic gastrinoma primaries. Individual MEN1-related duodenal NETs look like sporadic ones. They show trabecular or pseudoglandular growth and are often associated with low-grade proliferative disease (grade 1). However, the multifocal nature of NETs and the presence of synchronous neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia often help distinguish MEN1-related disease from sporadic manifestations. Rare examples of MEN1-related duodenal NETs can be somatostatin-positive {15887103; 24718881; 23478233}.

MEN1-related gastric disease

The ECL-cell hyperplasia-to-neoplasia progression sequence due to autonomous gastrin secretion is the hallmark of MEN1-related gastric disease and defines type 2 ECL-cell NET pathogenesis {23478233; 25053544; 25740317}. Affected patients show multifocal ECL-cell NETs arising in the background of ECL-cell hyperplasia in the oxyntic mucosa {23478233; 25053544; 25740317}. Like in chronic atrophic gastritis (type 1 gastric ECL-cell neuroendocrine tumorigenesis), diffuse, linear, and micronodular ECL-cell hyperplasia can be seen in patients with MEN1; however, MEN1-related disease is distinguished by the presence of hyperplastic changes.

MEN1-related bronchopulmonary and thymic disease

Most MEN1 syndrome–associated bronchopulmonary neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are well-differentiated NETs that show either low-grade (typical carcinoid) or intermediate-grade (atypical carcinoid) proliferative features; however, neuroendocrine carcinomas (small cell and large cell types) have been reported {28785839}. At the morphological level, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts; evidence suggests that MEN1-related lung disease is enriched in multifocal neuroendocrine cell proliferations ranging from pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia to neuroendocrine microtumours (formerly called tumourlets; each 0.5–5 mm) and NETs (> 5 mm) {26113081; 33641055}. Therefore, the identification of the multifocal pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia-to-neoplasia sequence should alert the diagnostician to the possibility of an underlying genetic susceptibility. In an attempt to facilitate the genetic triaging in sporadic-looking presentations, global loss of nuclear menin expression has been suggested to be helpful in the setting of multifocal pulmonary NETs and microtumours {33641055}. The distinction of MEN1-related TNET from sporadic tumours may be difficult in the absence of a relevant clinical history or synchronous manifestations of MEN1-related states. However, the application of menin immunohistochemistry may be helpful in young individuals. Ectopic ACTH-related Cushing syndrome can occur in MEN1-related TNETs {21550948; 24452869}.

MEN1-related adrenal disease

Most MEN1-related adrenal lesions are benign cortical lesions that may be bilateral; they include adrenal cortical hyperplasia (e.g. paediatric-onset macronodular hyperplasia), cysts, and adrenal cortical adenomas. Adrenal cortical carcinomas may occur in just over 1% of patients with MEN1 {22084155}. The histology of carcinomas or other adrenocortical tumours is comparable with that of sporadic cases, and both sarcomatoid and myxoid subtypes of adrenocortical carcinoma have been described {31118348}. In addition to cortical lesions, phaeochromocytomas can occur in patients with MEN1, at a frequency of about 1% {23778871; 25494863}.

Other MEN1-related lesions

Many other entities have been reported to occur with increased frequency in patients with MEN1, although sufficient pathogenetic evidence has not been collected for all of these. This does not apply to cutaneous lesions, which have been firmly established as a component of MEN1 and include lipomas, angiofibromas, and collagenomas. These lesions are frequently multiple, and their combined occurrence may give a clue to the presence of MEN1 syndrome in such patients {15531478}. Another group of tumours that occur in MEN1 are CNS tumours, including meningiomas and spinal ependymomas {14871962; 23562332; 28238068}, but again, their histopathology does not differ from that of their sporadic counterparts. Furthermore, the following soft tissue lesions have been reported to be associated with MEN1 syndrome: leiomyoma {11549605; 15144407; 18729310; 32577074}, gastrointestinal stromal tumour {25022420}, deep-seated or visceral lipoma {25545711}, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour {25678969}. Female patients with MEN1 are at increased risk for breast cancer; this subgroup of patients has been characterized by a loss of heterozygosity at the MEN1 locus {25099597}. So far, follicular nodular disease and thyroid neoplasms do not seem to be part of the MEN1 spectrum, and their occurrence has been attributed to chance {25572387}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual endocrine tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline MEN1 testing is offered to (1) an index patient with a clinical diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome (based on two or more MEN1 syndrome–related tumours), (2) asymptomatic first-degree relatives of a patient with a pathogenic MEN1 variant (typically before the age of 5 years), (3) symptomatic first-degree relatives (with at least one MEN1-related tumour) of a proband with a pathogenic MEN1 variant, (4) patients with primary hyperparathyroidism–related multiglandular parathyroid disease or parathyroid adenomas in young individuals (< 30 years), and (5) patients with gastrinoma or multifocal PanNETs at any age {33249439}. In general, DNA sequence analysis (exons and splice junction mutations) and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification for duplication/deletion analysis (screening for larger alterations) are usually used in most clinical laboratories {33249439}. About 10% of patients with MEN1-related clinical states may test negative for pathogenic MEN1 variants {23933118}. A fraction of MEN1 mutation–negative patients may harbour pathogenic germline CDKN1B variants (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4) or other CDKI gene variants {23933118; 30990521}; however, the commonly used screening tools may also fail to capture some germline MEN1 variants, as well as somatic mosaicism {33249439}. Therefore, whole-exome sequencing or whole-genome sequencing may help reduce the fraction of mutation-negative patients on first-line diagnostic molecular analyses {33249439}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Clinical diagnostic criterion: at least two MEN1 syndrome–associated tumours; or Familial diagnostic criteria: at least one MEN1 syndrome–associated tumour and a first-degree relative with a confirmed diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome; or Genetic diagnostic criterion: germline MEN1 mutation

Staging

Malignant tumours are staged using the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging systems that are applied to sporadic manifestations.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with MEN1 are at increased risk of death, usually related to the disease and its complications {11038219; 19949948; 23645327; 33249439}. Mutation-negative patients with two out of three main MEN1 manifestations generally have a different phenotype and clinical course, with manifestations at an older age and a normal life expectancy {27842554; 32703679}. Some studies suggest the existence of high-risk MEN1 sequence variants. Mutation affecting the JUND interacting domain may be associated with an increased risk of death {23376981}, and sequence variants affecting the FOXN3 (CHES1) interacting domain may be associated with an increased risk of aggressive PanNETs {25210877}. However, further validation of these associations is required. Nevertheless, the well-established causes of high morbidity are TNETs, duodenal NETs, and PanNETs, along with rare cases of aggressive adrenal tumours {11038219; 19949948; 23645327; 24915123; 24389729}. Female sex, family history of MEN1, and a more recently assessed diagnosis using improved criteria are associated with a lower risk of death {19949948}. At the tumour level, the prognostic factors are the same as those for sporadic tumours; tumour stage, tumour size, and grade are important parameters {23645327}. MEN1-associated lung NETs are slow-growing tumours with a good prognosis {33135721}.
