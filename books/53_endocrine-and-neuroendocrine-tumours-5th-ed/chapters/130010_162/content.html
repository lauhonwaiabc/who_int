13.0.0.10 McCune-Albright syndrome

Definition

McCune–Albright syndrome (MAS) is a tumour disorder caused by somatic activating GNAS mutations resulting in mosaic disease with wide clinical variability and a broad spectrum of endocrine and non-endocrine manifestations.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

174800 McCune–Albright syndrome; MAS

ICD-11 coding

FB80.0 Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Related terminology

Not recommended: Albright syndrome; fibrous dysplasia (polyostotic and monostotic); Mazabraud syndrome.

Subtype(s)

The association of polyostotic fibrous dysplasia and intramuscular myxomas is categorized as Mazabraud syndrome.

Localization

MAS may involve any portion of the skin and/or skeleton. Endocrine manifestations involve the gonads, thyroid, pituitary, and adrenals.

Clinical features

The clinical spectrum of MAS is broad, ranging from asymptomatic findings to neonatal lethality. The phenotype reflects the distribution of GNAS mutations and the role of Gsα in mutation-bearing tissues. Penetrance is primarily determined by the number and location of mutant cells {3720010}. Hyperpigmented macules are apparent at or shortly after birth {31673695}. Fibrous dysplasia lesions manifest during the first few years of life and expand during childhood {17501668}. Most clinically significant bone lesions are detectable by the age of 10 years, and the final disease burden is close to established by the age of 15 years {17501668}. Endocrinopathies are generally apparent in childhood, with the exception of FGF23-mediated hypophosphataemia, which may wax and wane {23532406; 31673695}.

The presence of any feature of MAS should prompt evaluation for additional manifestations {31196103}. In individuals with isolated monostotic fibrous dysplasia, the identification of a GNAS mutation is required to establish the diagnosis {31196103}.

Precocious puberty is the most common endocrine manifestation in girls (accounting for ~80% of cases) {16789624; 22051789; 31673695; 31036049}. Precocious puberty is isosexual, due to peripheral estrogen and/or testosterone production {16789624; 22051789; 26228588}. One series revealed that the prevalence of gonadal involvement is equal between the sexes; however, precocious puberty is more frequent in girls than in boys (accounting for ~20%) {22745241}. In girls, precocity presents with episodic vaginal bleeding and breast budding, coinciding with the development of single or multiple estrogen-producing ovarian cysts {29988390}. Affected boys develop enlarged testes with or without excess testosterone production {22745241}. Ultrasonography is useful for screening gonads in MAS. Ovarian cysts may be either present or absent, owing to the episodic nature of their development. Testicular microlithiasis, hyperechoic lesions, hypoechoic lesions, heterogeneity, and focal calcifications can be identified in most boys irrespective of precocity {22745241; 30484079}.

Hyperthyroidism is the second most common endocrine manifestation in MAS {31673695}. A comprehensive literature review identified subclinical or clinical hyperthyroidism in as many as 66% of patients with MAS-related goitre {18349068; 22640971; 23006743}. Abnormal ultrasound findings have been reported in 54% of patients {18349068; 22640971}. Even in the absence of hyperthyroidism, affected patients have a higher triiodothyronine-to-thyroxine ratio, which is partially explained by cAMP-induced 5′-deiodinase activity {18349068}.

Growth hormone (GH) and prolactin excess gives rise to acromegaly/gigantism {24517150}. Most patients with MAS-related acromegaly have a normal height, which may be attributable to fibrous dysplasia, precocity, and/or hyperthyroidism, and may lead to delayed diagnosis {24517150}. Hyperprolactinaemia occurs in about 80% of patients with GH excess {24517150}. Diffuse pituitary enlargement, microtumour, macrotumour, and multifocal microtumours have been reported {24517150; 22564667}. Despite the presence of biochemical acromegaly, MRI fails to detect abnormalities in some cases {25248598; 24517150; 22564667}.

Adrenal Cushing syndrome typically manifests in the first year of life {20157193; 24012779} and affects 1.7–7.5% of patients {20157193; 15126527}. Weight gain with decreasing growth velocity is characteristic of Cushing syndrome in childhood; however, neonates may develop failure to thrive and severe, life-threatening illness {25425660; 2940600}. Spontaneous resolution has been described in some neonates {20157193}.

Renal phosphate wasting may lead to osteomalacia/rickets, with an increased risk of fractures and malformations {11341325; 32069220; 29924878; 29669167}. The phosphaturic factor FGF23 is released from fibrous dysplasia tissue {12952917; 24012779}; as a result, hypophosphataemia is typically seen only in patients with significant skeletal disease {11341325}.

Hyperpigmented skin macules and fibrous dysplasia are characteristic non-endocrine manifestations of MAS {31673695}. Less common manifestations include oral pigmentation {24671640}, gastrointestinal polyps {30124968; 3238850}, hepatobiliary and pancreatic neoplasms {24170100; 24898823; 28188442; 30628989}, and platelet dysfunction {18639732; 3238850}. An increased risk of breast cancer and other malignancies has been reported {33226445; 28856726}.

Epidemiology

MAS with multisystem involvement is rare. There are no reliable data establishing disease prevalence; however, estimates range between 1 case per 1 million and 1 case per 100 000 population {25719192}. In contrast, fibrous dysplasia (particularly as monostotic disease) is not rare; it has been estimated to account for as many as 7% of all benign bone tumours {25719192}.

Etiology

MAS results from postzygotic somatic activating (gain-of-function) mutations in Gsα, one of several transcripts encoded by GNAS at 20q13.2-q13.3 {1944469}.

Pathogenesis

GNAS is a complex locus with an imprinted expression pattern. Multiple gene products, including maternal, paternal, and biallelic-expressed transcripts, are derived from four promoters and 5′ exons that splice onto a common set of downstream exons (exons 2–13) {1944469}. Somatic GNAS mutations in MAS occur at one of two residues: R201 (accounting for > 95% of the mutations) {15126527} and Q227 (accounting for < 5%) {17493233}.

The major product of GNAS is Gsα, which is generated by the most downstream promoter (exon 1). Gsα is the ubiquitously expressed heterotrimeric G protein α-subunit, which couples seven-transmembrane receptors to the cAMP-generating enzyme adenylyl cyclase and is required for the intracellular cAMP response to hormones and other extracellular signals. The GNAS variants at R201 and Q227 disrupt the intrinsic GTPase activity of Gsα, causing constitutive activation and abnormal cAMP signalling {2549426}.

There are no known genotype–phenotype correlations {33512531}. Clinical presentation and disease severity are likely determined by the degree of mosaicism and by the location and extent of affected tissues.

Histopathology

MAS-related gonadal disease: Ovarian follicular cysts lined by granulosa cells are frequently identified in girls with MAS-related precocity {171361; 16789624}. A single case of borderline ovarian serous tumour {26321108} and another of virilizing sclerosing stromal tumour have been reported in MAS {23365131}. Testicular enlargement results from the maturation and growth of seminiferous tubules. Orchiectomy specimens reveal large foci of Leydig cell hyperplasia indistinguishable from Leydig cell tumours {22745241}. Sertoli cell proliferations (including Sertoli cell intraepithelial neoplasia), bilateral testicular germ cell tumours (including embryonal carcinoma), and testicular adrenal rests have also been reported {22745241}.

MAS-related thyroid disease: The thyroid in patients with MAS has multiple nodules that have been described as multinodular hyperplasia, papillary hyperplasia, or goitre; solitary nodules were interpreted as follicular adenoma with papillary architecture most frequently {18349068}. Like non-syndromic functioning thyroid nodules (which are typically associated with either TSHR-activating or GNAS-activating clonal mutations {10714366; 18694911; 10714365}, the solitary or multiple functioning nodules that occur in MAS appear warm or hot on scan {24701557} and can cause subclinical or clinical hyperthyroidism. Regardless of size, such nodules are called follicular adenomas with papillary architecture and are histologically characterized by benign follicular proliferations with intrafollicular centripetal papillary projections. Rare cases of inflammatory thyroiditis and thyroid carcinomas have been described {12970318; 33226445}.

MAS-related pituitary disease: This is heterogeneous and typically involves a subset of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells, including mammosomatotrophs and somatotrophs {26177314; 22564667; 26874696}. Some patients present with mammosomatotroph or somatotroph hyperplasia; others develop mammosomatotroph or somatotroph tumours {26177314; 6426154; 24517150; 22564667}. Although findings of the non-tumorous anterior pituitary were largely unavailable in some reports, evidence suggests a hyperplasia-to-neoplasia progression sequence and multicentric microtumours in extended resection specimens {22564667}. Given the heterogeneous nature of MAS-related pituitary disease, total hypophysectomy is required for surgical cure {11081212; 22564667; 28007843}.

MAS-related adrenal disease: MAS-related Cushing syndrome typically demonstrates bilateral nodular hyperplasia, which also encompasses presentations with bilateral primary bimorphic adrenocortical disease {21836496; 25425660; 24012779; 33534120}. Although primary bimorphic adrenocortical disease is not specific to MAS, it is histologically characterized by diffuse nodular hyperplasia juxtaposed with areas of atrophic cortex, resulting in a bimorphic appearance {21836496}. Hyperplastic foci are distinguished by strong α-inhibin and synaptophysin expression, whereas the thin atrophic cortex reveals strong CD56 expression and lack of zona reticularis {21836496}. Rare examples of bilateral adenomas have also been described in MAS {21836496}.

Cytology

The cytopathological features of the various neoplasms associated with MAS-related disease are indistinguishable from those of their sporadic counterparts, which are described elsewhere in this volume and in the other fifth-edition volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Documentation of a somatic activating mutation in GNAS confirms the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: at least two of the three clinical features listed in #42164Box 13.04 or monostotic fibrous dysplasia in a patient with demonstrated GNAS mutation.

Desirable: demonstration of GNAS mutation in addition to at least two of the three clinical criteria listed in #42164Box 13.04 .

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for individuals with MAS varies, depending on disease location and severity. Endocrinopathies persist throughout childhood and adulthood, with the exception of FGF23-mediated hypophosphataemia, which may worsen during periods of rapid linear growth and ameliorate in adulthood when fibrous dysplasia disease activity wanes {3167369; 30645769}. Endocrinopathies can generally be controlled with pharmacotherapies or surgical removal of affected glands {31673695; 31196103}.

Optimizing the treatment of endocrinopathies may improve outcomes in fibrous dysplasia. Untreated GH excess is associated with expansion of craniofacial fibrous dysplasia, resulting in macrocephaly, vision loss, and hearing loss {29672904; 27493082; 29192304}. Hyperthyroidism and hypophosphataemia increase fractures and malformations in patients with fibrous dysplasia {15005844; 29924878; 29669167}.

GNAS mutations in MAS are weakly oncogenic, and affected tissues likely carry a small increased risk of malignant transformation {33226445; 28856726}. Fibrous dysplasia rarely undergoes sarcomatous degeneration {8111708; 30809751}, and additional cancers (summarized above) have been reported. Radiotherapy and uncontrolled GH excess may increase the risk of malignant transformation {31673695}.

This disorder is never inherited, presumably because germline mutations are probably lethal {3720010}. Counselling should emphasize that although no pregnancy is at zero risk, the risk of recurrence appears to be no higher than in the general population.
