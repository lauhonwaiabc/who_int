13.0.0.8 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Definition

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant hereditary tumour syndrome caused by germline variants of the NF1 gene.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

162200 Neurofibromatosis, type 1; NF1

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.10 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Related terminology

Not recommended: von Recklinghausen disease; peripheral neurofibromatosis; von Recklinghausen’s neurofibromatosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

NF1 is characterized by multiple café-au-lait spots, axillary and inguinal freckling, multiple cutaneous neurofibromas, plexiform neurofibromas, Lisch nodules, optic nerve and other CNS gliomas, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours (MPNSTs), phaeochromocytoma (adrenal paraganglioma), duodenal and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), and choroidal freckling.

Clinical features

The presence of six or more café-au-lait spots supports a diagnosis of NF1 on clinical grounds {26564071}. The occurrence of multiple neurofibromas is one of the most typical clinical characteristics in both paediatric and adult patients with NF1. The presence of two or more neurofibromas or one plexiform neurofibroma further supports a diagnosis of NF1 {26564071}. Most are benign, but they can invade local structures, especially nerves, resulting in neurological manifestations. MPNST is rare, but neurofibromas often serve as precursor lesions, and the lifetime risk of transformation may be as high as 13% {26926675; 23036231}. Most skin lesions appear during early childhood. Café-au-lait spots occur in > 95% of patients and skin freckling in about 50% of patients {23246715}. Neurofibromas occur in about 40–60% of patients {9128932; 10204844}.

Various skeletal dysplasias (sphenoid wing, orbital, and long bone dysplasias) are common. Ciliary bone cysts and giant cell granulomas may also occur, but much less frequently {26564071}.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs) occur in 5–25% of patients with NF1 and are often multifocal {16461335}.

Growth hormone excess leading to clinical or subclinical gigantism or acromegaly is a rare but underrecognized phenomenon in patients with NF1 {30283094}. NF1-related optic pathway tumours, especially those involving the hypothalamus and sellar region, are the common source of the growth hormone / IGF1 excess in children {28631895; 30283094; 32294191}, which may be transient. Tumour-related GHRH secretion and/or absence of somatostatinergic inhibition are potential mechanisms in NF1-related growth hormone excess {30283094}. To date, no NF1-driven pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs)/adenomas have been defined.

Ampullary-type duodenal somatostatinomas are the most common neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) associated with NF1. Other duodenal/ampullary NETs and composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour (CoGNET; formerly known as gangliocytic paraganglioma) are present in about 5% of patients {20300877}. Very rarely, they can coexist {20300877; 2894342}. These tumours most commonly present with signs and symptoms related to the anatomical presence of a tumour rather than with somatostatin-related or other hormone-related clinical syndromes. CoGNETs do not produce any catecholamines, because of their lack of tyrosine hydroxylase. Commonly, patients present with weight loss, abdominal pain, and gastrointestinal bleeding presenting as melaena and resulting in anaemia (especially those with CoGNET), or with clinical signs or symptoms related to pancreatic and gallbladder and pancreatic dysfunction due to the obstruction of the Vater papilla or (very rarely) elevated somatostatin levels. NF1-related duodenal NETs usually occur in adults, with a mean age between 40 and 50 years {20300877}.

PanNETs have also been identified in patients with NF1 {33452231; 22824559}. Phaeochromocytomas (adrenal paragangliomas) {16787982} are seen in 7% of patients with NF1, whereas extra-adrenal paragangliomas are very rare {21051559; 31616500; 10524872; 16787982; 23551045; 2511319}. These tumours usually occur around the fourth or fifth decade of life {21147885}. About 10% of patients presenting with NF1-related phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas have multiple tumours. NF1-related phaeochromocytoma presents with a mature secretory phenotype characterized predominantly by the adrenergic biochemical phenotype (epinephrine [adrenaline] / metanephrine). Norepinephrine and normetanephrine may or may not be produced. NF1-related phaeochromocytomas present with classic symptoms and signs related to catecholamine excess, most commonly including hypertension, tachyarrhythmia, palpitations, sweating, nervousness, anxiety, and pallor {16112304}.

Epidemiology

NF1 has an incidence of 1 case per 2500–3000 births {19117870} and a prevalence of 1:4400 in Finland {10991696}. The highest prevalence was described in 17-year-old Israeli military recruits, reaching 1:1000 (0.94:1000 for boys and 1.19:1000 for girls) {7853369}.

Etiology

NF1 is caused by heterozygous germline variants of the tumour suppressor gene NF1 encoding neurofibromin (NF1) {17105749}. The second allele is typically inactivated in somatic cells that give rise to tumours.

Pathogenesis

Neurofibromin (NF1) is a RAS-GAP, and therefore NF1 is part of a group of human diseases that are collectively known as RASopathies {25877329}. Its inactivation due to mutations leads to increased signalling by the RAS oncogenes {25877329}. Neurofibromin (NF1) has multiple domains that interact and regulate multiple signalling pathways in addition to RAS signalling, such as the PKA pathway {9115203}.

Although patients with larger deletions of the gene and surrounding genomic material have more severe phenotypes, there are few other genotype–phenotype correlations among patients with classic NF1 and pathogenic NF1 sequence variants. This may be due to genetic modifiers on other chromosomal loci {25329635}.

Macroscopic appearance

NF1-associated neoplasms are macroscopically similar to their sporadic counterparts. Bilateral phaeochromocytomas (adrenal paragangliomas) may occur {31125973; 32410303}. NF1-related duodenal NETs are usually located in the ampulla and may be seen in association with synchronous non-neuroendocrine neoplasms {32425982}. The data on gross correlates of NF1-related PanNETs are limited; however, cystic change may occur {33708057}.

Histopathology

Endocrine manifestations in NF1

The histological growth pattern of ampullary-type duodenal NETs is typically tubular with psammoma bodies in the lumina in two thirds of the tumours. The psammoma bodies are composed of calcium apatite crystals {2565711}. Immunohistochemically, NF1-associated duodenal NETs express keratins, neuroendocrine markers (INSM1, synaptophysin, and chromogranin A), and somatostatin. The strong and diffuse labelling with somatostatin in patients with NF1 is in contrast to sporadic or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1–associated duodenal NETs {2476943; 18310290}. In patients with NF1, duodenal somatostatinomas may be combined with GISTs {18762695} and adenoma/adenocarcinoma {20065557; 32425982}. Duodenal CoGNETs can also occur in association with NF1 {20300877; 2894342}.

NF1-associated phaeochromocytomas do not differ from their sporadic counterparts that harbour somatic NF1 mutation, which occurs in about 20% of sporadic phaeochromocytomas {23756430}. However, NF1 phaeochromocytomas may be more frequently of the composite type {11904334} and may be associated with synchronous or asynchronous bilateral adrenal gland involvement {32410303; 31125973}, which may also include subcentimetre tumours. By immunohistochemistry, NF1-related phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas are positive for GATA3 and tyrosine hydroxylase, and negative for keratins and pseudohypoxia biomarkers, including α-inhibin and CAIX {33826547}. They lack the high microvessel density typical of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome–associated phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas.

At the morphological level, NF1-related PanNETs are largely indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. They express keratins and neuroendocrine differentiation markers (e.g. INSM1, chromogranin A, synaptophysin). Rare examples of functioning and non-functioning PanNETs have been documented in patients with NF1. (For literature reviews, see {36154786; 29912000; 38470548}.)

Other manifestations in NF1

Café-au-lait spots are histologically characterized by basilar hyperpigmentation with or without superbasilar melanosis. A minor melanocytic hyperplasia may be observed.

All subtypes of neurofibroma (reviewed in {22327363}) can be seen in patients with NF1. Localized cutaneous neurofibromas can affect the dermis or subcutis and show no site predilection. Like sporadic localized neurofibromas, the tumours are unencapsulated and round and are composed of a mixture of bland neoplastic Schwann cells and non-neoplastic perineural cells, entrapped axons, and fibroblasts, accompanied by a variable fibrous to myxoid matrix. Localized cutaneous neurofibromas do not bear a risk for malignancy. Diffuse cutaneous and subcutaneous neurofibroma is much less common and is characterized by a plaque-like tumour, usually in the head and neck region. Pseudomeissnerian corpuscles are frequent; about 10% arise in the setting of NF1. Malignant change is infrequent. Plexiform neurofibromas occur almost exclusively in patients with NF1. Involvement of multiple fascicles results in worm-like or rope-like tangles, referred to as a plexiform pattern. Many plexiform neurofibromas are present at birth or may continue to appear until adolescence. They occur in about 30% of patients with NF1. Plexiform neurofibromas and localized intraneural neurofibromas are precursor lesions of MPNST. MPNSTs are associated with a wide range of histological appearances. Pilocytic astrocytomas resemble their sporadic counterparts but may grow more diffusely.

GISTs in patients with NF1 belong to the group of wildtype GISTs without KIT/PDGFRA mutations {32063345}.

Cytology

The cytopathological features of the various NF1-associated neoplasms are indistinguishable from those of their sporadic counterparts, which are described elsewhere in this volume and in the other fifth-edition volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

NF1 defects are found in > 90% of individuals with a clinical diagnosis of NF1 {10862084}. Genetic testing should include various methods to test for splicing defects, deletions, large chromosomal and smaller gene rearrangements, and other mutations. The disease is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, and prenatal testing is routinely available for known carriers; however, about half of the patients have new (de novo) mutations, often due to paternal mosaicism. This complicates genetic counselling.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: the diagnostic clinical criteria have been described in a US National Institutes of Health (NIH) consensus conference [[National Institutes of Health. Neurofibromatosis. Conference statement. National Institutes of Health Consensus Development Conference. Arch Neurol. 1988 May;45(5):575–8.]] and are summarized in #24346Box 13.01 .

Staging

None

Prognosis and prediction

By the age of 8 years, 97% of children with NF1 clinically fulfil the diagnostic criteria, and by the age of 20 years, 100% do {10699117}. The phenotype, however, is highly variable {10625171}. About 15% of patients with NF1 develop brain tumours {2494565}. The lifetime risk for patients with NF1 of developing MPNST is reported as 8–13% {12011145}; early detection and resection are essential. Patients with NF1 are at 5–7 times greater risk than the general population of developing leukaemia, brain tumours, and breast cancer. The prognosis of NF1-associated NETs and phaeochromocytomas and/or paragangliomas is comparable to that of their sporadic counterparts.

Two or more of the following criteria must be met for the diagnosis:

1.

> 6 café-au-lait macules measuring > 5 mm in greatest diameter in prepubertal individuals and > 15 mm in greatest diameter after puberty

2.

> 2 neurofibromas of any type or 1 plexiform neurofibroma

3.

Axillary or inguinal freckling

4.

Optic glioma

5.

> 2 Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas)

6.

A distinctive osseous lesion, such as sphenoid wing dysplasia or thinning of the cortex of long bones (with or without pseudarthrosis)

7.

A first-degree relative (parent, sibling, or child) with neurofibromatosis type 1 diagnosed by the above criteria
