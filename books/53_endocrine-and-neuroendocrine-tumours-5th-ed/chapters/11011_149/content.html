11.0.1.1 Germ cell tumours in endocrine organs

Definition

The most common germ cell tumours in endocrine organs are teratomas – germ cell tumours defined by the presence of mature or immature tissues derived from all three germ cell layers.

Related terminology

Acceptable: benign teratoma; immature teratoma.

Not recommended: malignant teratoma (now classified as thyroblastoma).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Endocrine organ teratomas are rare and found in the thyroid, adrenal, and pituitary glands. To be considered of endocrine organ origin, they should be centred in, be contiguous with, or completely replace the organ {10699906; 28091891}.

Clinical features

Thyroid gland teratomas generally present as large neck masses that can cause airway compression and associated respiratory distress, particularly in the neonatal setting {10699906; 26611341; 15832097}. Most adrenal gland teratomas are found incidentally on imaging {31949356}. In both sites, imaging demonstrates a heterogeneous cystic and solid mass with variable amounts of fat and calcifications {32953615; 16091830}.

Epidemiology

Teratomas account for < 0.1% of thyroid or adrenal gland tumours {29194699; 10699906}. The majority of thyroid gland teratomas occur in neonates and young children, and most cases arise congenitally {10699906; 26611341; 15832097}. In contrast, the majority of adrenal gland teratomas are identified in adults {31949356; 31195014}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours generally have a smooth but lobulated exterior and a heterogeneous, multiloculated, solid to cystic cut surface and contain variable amounts of serous, mucinous, or haemorrhagic fluid.

Histopathology

Teratomas are defined by the presence of mature or immature tissues from all embryonic germ cell layers (endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm). Tumours display multiple cystic spaces lined by a variety of epithelial types, including simple or stratified squamous epithelium, frequently in association with pilosebaceous units or other adnexal structures, pseudostratified and ciliated respiratory epithelium, mucin-producing intestinal epithelium, and transitional epithelium. Virtually all teratomas also contain a significant component of neural tissue, encompassing both mature glial elements, retinal anlage, or choroid plexus or immature neuroectodermal tissue composed of small to medium-sized cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant fibrillary cytoplasm arranged in sheets and rosettes. A variety of mesenchymal elements, including skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, cartilage, and bone, can be identified. Solid organ parenchyma, including pancreatic, liver, or lung tissue, is uncommonly seen.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry is usually unnecessary for diagnosis, with the various teratoma components displaying staining characteristics of their constituent tissues {10699906; 8008695}.

Grading

Grading of endocrine organ teratomas is based on criteria for ovarian and sacrococcygeal teratoma, which rely on the quantity of immature neuroectodermal tissue {580884; 6165455; 3397045; 1260722; 5521737; 10699906}. Grade 0 teratomas are considered benign, containing only mature elements. Grade 1 and grade 2 teratomas are classified as immature, with grade 1 having immature neuroectodermal tissue in only 1 low-power (4×) field and grade 2 having > 1 but < 4 low-power fields containing immature foci. Classically, tumours with > 4 low-power fields of immature neuroectodermal tissue, mitoses, and/or pleomorphism have been considered grade 3 or malignant teratomas {1580935; 10699906; 30759052}. However, thyroid gland tumours that meet these criteria have been reclassified as thyroblastoma on the basis of recurrent DICER1 mutations and should no longer be regarded as true teratomas {27784193; 31917706; 32507920}. Rarely, somatic-type malignancies have been reported to arise within otherwise benign or immature teratomas {29137015}. Other germ cell tumours (e.g. yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma) are rarely reported in endocrine organs and are also malignant {31860425; 25528034; 16257799; 21793665}.

Differential diagnosis

Benign teratomas should be distinguished from other developmental cysts such as thyroglossal duct cyst, branchial cleft cyst, or duplication cysts on the basis of multiple tissue components. In limited sampling, immature teratomas can overlap with other primitive neoplasms such as Ewing sarcoma or neuroblastoma, but they can be correctly classified if mature elements are present.

Cytology

FNA is rarely performed but may reveal cystic acellular liquid {18323278; 24396669; 21793665}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

An absence of DICER1 hotspot mutations may help distinguish immature or benign tumours from malignant tumours (thyroblastoma), which typically harbour these mutations {31917706}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: involvement or replacement of the thyroid or adrenal gland by a proliferation of tissue from all three germ layers; usually mature tissues, but small foci of immature neuroectodermal tissues may be seen.

Desirable: absence of the DICER1 hotspot mutations seen in thyroblastoma (in selected cases).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Congenital thyroid gland teratomas can cause death due to airway compression and impaired development of other vital structures {7771726; 10699906; 26611341; 15832097}. Among benign and immature teratomas in endocrine organs, the quantity of immature tissue does not appear to correlate with outcome, and patients have an excellent prognosis in the absence of an overtly malignant component {10699906; 26611341; 15832097}.
