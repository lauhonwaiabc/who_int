7.0.1.1B Sympathetic paraganglioma

Definition

Sympathetic paraganglioma is a neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN) that develops from neural crest–derived progenitors in paraganglia associated with the prevertebral and paravertebral sympathetic chains, sympathetic nerve plexuses, and sympathetic nerve fibres.

ICD-O coding

8681/3 Sympathetic paraganglioma

8693/3 Composite paraganglioma

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.Y & XH4G21 Other specified neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of endocrine glands & Sympathetic paraganglioma

Related terminology

Acceptable: extra-adrenal paraganglioma.

Not recommended: extra-adrenal phaeochromocytoma; phaeochromocytoma.

Subtype(s)

Composite paraganglioma

Localization

Sympathetic paragangliomas occur predominantly in the abdomen, retroperitoneum, pelvis, and thorax, and occasionally in or near the cervical sympathetic ganglia.

Clinical features

See section Phaeochromocytoma . Some may be biochemically non-functioning, especially those with SDHB mutations. Surveillance of patients with hereditary predisposition should not rely solely on biochemical testing {18840642}.

Epidemiology

Paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas (PPGLs) are developmentally and functionally related {33833454; 28684471; 29807017; 30740044}, and because of their commonalities they are often grouped together in epidemiological studies. Sympathetic paragangliomas account for 10–18% of all PPGLs in population-wide studies consisting mostly of adult patients {29361475}. In the Netherlands {29361475} and the Republic of Korea {32207276}, which have national registries, the incidence rates are approximately 0.5 and 0.18 cases per 100 000 person-years, respectively. The overall prevalence of PPGL in the Republic of Korea was 2.13 cases per 100 000 population {32207276}. Patients typically present in their fifth or sixth decade of life (age range: 0–88 years) {29361475}. A slight female predominance {29361475} and an equal sex distribution {32207276} have both been reported.

Approximately 10–20% of phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas occur in paediatric patients {32432211}. In a 2020 French registry series of 81 patients aged < 18 years, there were 41 patients with phaeochromocytomas and 40 with paragangliomas. The median patient age in that cohort was 13.3 years (range: 4.6–18 years), and the M:F ratio was 1:1. Sixty-two per cent of tumours were in the abdomen, 12.5% were in the thorax, and a single tumour was in the pelvis {32432211}. In a comparable 2020 Italian registry series there were 28 patients with phaeochromocytoma and 22 with paraganglioma. The median patient age was 11.6 years, and the M:F ratio was 1.7:1. Tumour locations were the abdomen (72.2%), pelvis (13.6%), and mediastinum (4.5%) {32491270}. Both series also included tumours in the head and neck (22.5% and 9.1%, respectively), without specific indication of their precise locations or characteristics.

Etiology

Genetic factors

It is extremely likely that paediatric paragangliomas have a hereditary basis. In the 2020 French and Italian series, pathogenic variants were found in 78% and 59% of patients who underwent genetic testing, with the most common mutations being in SDHB or VHL {32432211; 32491270}.

Risk factors

There are no proven factors for sympathetic paragangliomas other than familial.

 

Pathogenesis

See section Phaeochromocytoma .

Macroscopic appearance

Sympathetic paragangliomas can appear identical to phaeochromocytomas and can be located close to the adrenal. Care should be taken during gross and microscopic examination to ascertain that their location is extra-adrenal, because a diagnosis of phaeochromocytoma would have different genetic and clinical implications {21262951}.

Histopathology

Paragangliomas are often indistinguishable from phaeochromocytomas microscopically. Some have cells with particularly clear cytoplasm, resembling head and neck paraganglioma, which can cause them to be mistaken for urinary bladder {14707870}, prostate {23766843}, or kidney {30116720} carcinomas. Immunohistochemical markers are the same as for phaeochromocytomas.

Cytology

FNAB is generally not advised {19958944}, but occasionally it is performed during the workup for other tumours, including those in the head and neck. Smears show a bloody background with single cells and discohesive small tissue fragments that can be acinar and resemble follicular cells of the thyroid. The cells have eccentric, sometimes spindle, cytoplasm that may contain fine red granules (Giemsa stain) and round to oval pleomorphic nuclei with prominent anisonucleosis and even, finely granular, speckled, neuroendocrine-type chromatin. Nucleoli can be prominent. Hyperchromatic nuclei and nuclear grooves may be seen {29575826}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

See section Phaeochromocytoma .

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Clinical: biochemical testing for metanephrines; structural imaging Pathology: diagnosis based on morphological criteria and immunohistochemistry as required, with no “benign” or “malignant” subcategories; all paragangliomas are considered to have metastatic potential

Desirable:

Clinical: biochemical testing including 3-methoxytyramine; structural imaging accompanied by functional imaging in appropriate clinical settings; routine genetic testing in all affected patients Pathology: routine use of immunohistochemistry for SDHB and other relevant biomarkers (see section Phaeochromocytoma)

Staging

See section Phaeochromocytoma .

Prognosis and prediction

For individual tumours, the prognostic and risk factors are the same as for phaeochromocytoma. However, location is itself considered a risk factor. Approximately 30% of abdominal paragangliomas may ultimately metastasize, versus about 8% of phaeochromocytomas {30893643}. It is difficult to ascertain the contributions of genotype versus anatomical site. Extra-adrenal abdominal paragangliomas often harbour SDHB mutations, which may be the major cause of their aggressive behaviour {30893643}.
