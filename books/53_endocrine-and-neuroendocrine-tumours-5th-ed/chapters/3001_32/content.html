3.0.0.1 Thyroid tumours: Introduction

Tumours of the thyroid gland are the most common endocrine neoplasms. The vast majority are derived from follicular epithelial cells that form the greater part of the gland. A much smaller number arise from calcitonin-secreting C cells, a component of the dispersed neuroendocrine system. Rarely, thyroid tumours are composed of both C cells and follicular cells. Tumours of parathyroid, thymus, and salivary glands can present as primary thyroid neoplasms because of their proximity to, and occasional location within, the gland. As in any organ, there can be primary tumours of stromal elements, and because the thyroid is a midline organ, germ cell tumours may arise.

Tumours of thyroid follicular cell derivation

Tumours of thyroid follicular cell derivation include benign adenomas, low-risk neoplasms, and malignancies that span the spectrum from minute, incidental findings to aggressive, rapidly lethal tumours (see #28677Table 3.01).

Benign

Benign neoplasms are classified as adenomas. The majority have simple microfollicular or macrofollicular architecture and exhibit a RAS-like molecular profile {25417114}. Some adenomas are composed of oncocytic cells (previously called Hürthle cells, although this is actually a misnomer and the term “Hürthle” is now discouraged {34012422}). A distinct form of adenoma has an intrafollicular papillary architecture sometimes reflecting a hyperfunctional status due to mutations that activate the cAMP pathway {27521067}. A common abnormality of the thyroid is the presence of multifocal benign nodules that span a hyperplasia–neoplasia sequence; morphological criteria cannot always distinguish hyperplastic lesions from neoplastic lesions in nodular disease of follicular cells in this disorder that has, by consensus, been designated by pathologists as thyroid follicular nodular disease. These lesions may give rise to clinically detectable thyroid enlargement, known as multinodular goitre, but they are also present in glands that do not qualify for this designation because the gland is not enlarged.

Low-risk

A category of low-risk neoplasms has been created to bridge the gap between benign and malignant. They include neoplasms that have been reported to give rise to metastases but have an extremely low incidence of spread. The designation “tumour”, rather than “carcinoma”, is intended to reduce the risk of overtreatment. These include tumours that have cytological features of malignancy but no evidence of invasive growth (non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm) {27078145}, tumours with uncertain invasiveness (uncertain malignant potential) {11493987; 19543912}, and tumours with striking nuclear features of papillary carcinoma and hyaline cytoplasm (hyalinizing trabecular tumour) {18813121}.

Malignant

Malignancies of thyroid follicular cells have been characterized at the molecular level, and it is clear that the majority of well-differentiated thyroid carcinomas fall into one of two categories. Tumours with a predominant expansile but not infiltrative growth pattern, often (but not always) clear encapsulation, and follicular architecture are classified as RAS-like tumours because of the high prevalence of RAS mutations and a common mRNA and protein expression profile {25417114}. They may have no nuclear atypia (follicular carcinoma), or they may have mild to moderate nuclear convolution and clearing of nucleoplasm (encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma); the degree of nuclear atypia is a highly subjective feature accounting for geographical and interobserver variability in the distinction of these two entities, and they may be merged in the next edition of the WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. These are the most differentiated carcinomas according to their morphology and iodide metabolism / functional thyroid differentiation expression profiles. Tumours with architectural abnormalities including papillary and/or infiltrative growth have a high frequency of BRAF p.V600E mutations (or RET or NTRK gene fusions) and are designated as BRAF p.V600E–like tumours {25417114; 36709221}. These BRAF p.V600E–like carcinomas usually have florid nuclear atypia, the hallmark of papillary carcinoma, and they frequently contain psammoma bodies. Although still histologically differentiated, the family of papillary thyroid carcinomas are less differentiated at the molecular level than follicular carcinoma or encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, being associated with impairment of iodide-metabolizing machinery and reduced radioactive iodine avidity {25417114}. Both RAS-like and BRAF p.V600E–like carcinomas can have high-grade features, i.e. tumour necrosis and/or mitotic activity, which correlate with additional molecular events conferring more aggressive behaviour. Thus, progression, associated with secondary genetic events (typically mutations of TP53, PIK3CA, PTEN, or TERT promoter), gives rise to high-grade follicular cell–derived carcinomas that may be RAS-like, such as most poorly differentiated (insular) thyroid carcinomas, or BRAF p.V600E–like, such as the high-grade papillary carcinomas. Therefore, a prognostically relevant classification of thyroid carcinoma of follicular cells must take into account differentiation and grade (mitotic activity and/or necrosis). All malignancies of follicular thyroid cells can have histological subtypes, but the most challenging are those with oncocytic cytology {34012422}. Oncocytic neoplasms can have genetic alterations characteristic of their corresponding non-oncocytic neoplasms (e.g. oncocytic papillary carcinomas may have BRAF p.V600E mutations) but in addition, mutations of mitochondrial genes that typically encode oxidative phosphorylation complex I subunits and genome haploidization with uniparental disomy are their defining molecular features {17517629; 30107175}. In this chapter, there is a separate discussion of oncocytic follicular carcinomas.

The most aggressive follicular-derived carcinoma is anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, a rapidly lethal malignancy. These tumours are by definition undifferentiated and therefore represent a diagnosis of exclusion. In most cases, the presence of a differentiated precursor lesion and mutations that are characteristic of thyroid carcinoma, such as RAS or BRAF p.V600E, combined with secondary genetic events (usually TP53 and/or TERT promoter mutation) provide evidence for the diagnosis {32284020}. Primary squamous cell carcinoma of the thyroid, listed separately in the previous edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs, is now considered a subtype of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. Previous reports of thyroid angiosarcoma and other high-grade mesenchymal tumours may represent misdiagnosed anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. However, there is also good evidence of primary thyroid angiosarcomas that appear to be geographically clustered {18766472}.

Tumours of thyroid C-cell derivation

Tumours of thyroid C-cell derivation are known as medullary thyroid carcinoma. This neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN) is not known to have a benign counterpart. RET and RAS mutations are the predominant drivers of medullary thyroid carcinoma, being detected in as many as 80–90% of cases {37842841}. RET mutation is inherited in the germline of patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia types 2A and 2B. C-cell hyperplasia is a precursor lesion in patients with familial predisposition {31605946; 33687689}.

Cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma

Cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma is an unusual thyroid tumour that may be either sporadic or a harbinger of familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome. Originally called a subtype of papillary thyroid carcinoma because of its papillary architecture, it was recently recognized as a distinct thyroid tumour because of its characteristic genetic features of WNT signalling alterations {29785019}. A recent study has shown a lack of thyroid follicular differentiation in these tumours, and they remain of uncertain histogenesis {34019236}.

Thyroblastoma

Thyroblastoma is an extremely rare embryonal high-grade thyroid neoplasm composed of primitive follicular cells, small cells, and mesenchymal stroma. As in other organs where blastomas arise, this malignancy is due to mutations in the DICER1 gene, which encodes an enzyme involved in microRNA processing {32507920}. Thyroblastoma was commonly previously classified as malignant (grade 3) teratoma of the thyroid gland.

Parathyroid tumours

Parathyroid tumours are occasionally intrathyroidal. These tumours are discussed separately in section Chapter 4: Parathyroid tumours.

Salivary gland tumours

Salivary gland tumours can arise in or around the thyroid. They include mucoepidermoid carcinoma and secretory carcinoma (previously known as mammary analogue secretory carcinoma). A recent report adds to this list a perithyroidal acinic cell carcinoma {32519264}. The previous category “mucinous carcinoma” is now regarded as a subtype of mucoepidermoid carcinoma. In contrast, the tumour known as sclerosing mucoepidermoid carcinoma with eosinophilia, although previously thought to be related to salivary gland–type mucoepidermoid carcinoma, is now recognized to be of uncertain histogenesis.

Thymic tumours

Thymic tumours that arise in or near the thyroid include thymoma, thymic carcinoma, and spindle epithelial tumour with thymus-like elements.

Other thyroid lesions

Other thyroid lesions include haematolymphoid, stromal, and mesenchymal tumours, as well as germ cell tumours that can arise in the thyroid. They are discussed in separate chapters. The thyroid is also a common site for metastatic malignancy, and this, too, is discussed in a chapter on metastatic disease.

Cytopathology

FNA plays an essential role in the management of patients with thyroid nodules {26462967; 31549998}. Which nodule to aspirate depends on the size and sonographic features of the nodule {26462967; 31549998}. Most malignant thyroid tumours can be diagnosed on cytology FNA material. Reporting follows established diagnostic criteria associated with different risks of malignancy to include benign, indeterminate, and malignant categories {21054822; 29091573}.

Ancillary markers

The application of ancillary immunohistochemical biomarkers is important to confirm the thyroid cell of origin (follicular vs C-cell derivation) and in the distinction between primary and metastatic tumours {29744727}. Immunohistochemistry using mutation-specific antibodies is becoming widely used in clinical practice to provide additional information on the pathogenesis and molecular classification of thyroid tumours. Molecular testing of FNA material is a useful adjunct for malignancy risk assessment in cases with indeterminate cytology {26462967}.
