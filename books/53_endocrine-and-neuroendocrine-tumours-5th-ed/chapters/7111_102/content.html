7.1.1.1 Neuroblastoma

Definition

Neuroblastoma is a peripheral neuroblastic tumour of neural crest origin containing proliferating neoplastic cells of various grades of neuroblastic differentiation with < 50% Schwannian stromal content.

ICD-O coding

9500/3 Neuroblastoma

9500/3 Undifferentiated neuroblastoma

9500/3 Poorly differentiated neuroblastoma

9500/3 Differentiating neuroblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2D11 & XH85Z0 Malignant neoplasm of adrenal gland, neuroblastoma & Neuroblastoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Undifferentiated neuroblastoma; poorly differentiated neuroblastoma; differentiating neuroblastoma

Localization

The adrenal gland is the most common primary site of neuroblastoma, and > 40% of tumours are found in the adrenal medulla and periadrenal locations. Other primary sites include the abdomen (25%), posterior mediastinum (15%), pelvic cavity (5%), and neck (~3–5%). Rare tumours are found in the paratesticular region, and 1% of patients are recorded to have metastatic disease with no detectable primary tumours {666647; 8591082; 15235064; 19415016; 25154816}.

Clinical features

A large primary adrenal neuroblastoma may cause abdominal distension. Adrenal neuroblastoma can also be detected before birth, by ultrasound scans during prenatal care. Metastases can cause lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, bone pain, and raccoon eyes (exophthalmia associated with distinct bruising and swelling around the eyes). Metastatic dissemination to the lung and brain is rare {18649370; 1913549}. CNS involvement, when present, often shows diffuse spreading along the meningeal surfaces.

Several paraneoplastic syndromes are associated with adrenal neuroblastoma. They include (1) WDHA syndrome (watery diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, and achlorhydria; also called Verner–Morrison syndrome) caused by the tumour secreting VIP {15051093}, (2) opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (dancing eyes and acute cerebellar ataxia, possibly via an autoimmune mechanism) {19430769}, (3) hypertension (due to catecholamine secretion or renal artery compression by the tumour) {15960647; 14694383}. Rare cases with constitutional mutations of PHOX2B have also been documented with congenital central hypoventilation and Hirschsprung disease {26375764; 12640453}. PHOX2B encodes a homeodomain transcription factor that promotes cell-cycle exit and neuronal differentiation; therefore, it plays a crucial part in the development of neural crest–derived autonomic neurons.

Epidemiology

Peripheral neuroblastic tumours are the third most common childhood neoplasm after leukaemias and brain tumours, and 80% of them are in the neuroblastoma category. They are the most common neoplasms during the first year of life, and the most common extracranial solid tumours in the first 2 years of life. In the USA, the prevalence of this disease is 1 case per 7 000 live births, and nearly 90% are diagnosed before 5 years of age (median age: 17.3 months). The disease is slightly more common in boys than in girls (M:F ratio: 1.2:1) [[Howlader N, Noone AM, Krapcho M, et al., editors. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2015. SEER Cancer Statistics Review, 1975-2012; based on November 2014 SEER data submission, posted to the SEER website in April 2015. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/archive/csr/1975_2012/.]]. A similar frequency has been observed in Europe {27823645}, China {32020595; 23927803}, and Japan {29378002; 29178401}. Familial neuroblastoma occurs as an autosomal dominant disorder with incomplete penetration {28674118} and seems to constitute approximately 1–2% of all neuroblastomas {14752801}. Neuroblastomas in patients with a hereditary predisposition are usually diagnosed at an earlier age (mean age: 9 months) than are sporadic cases (mean age: 17.3 months), and ≥ 20% of these patients have bilateral adrenal or multifocal primaries. A substantial fraction of familial neuroblastoma is caused by pathogenic germline variants in ALK {28458126}. PHOX2B pathogenic variants are also identified in a small number of inherited cases {26375764}.

Etiology

Neuroblastoma is caused by the transformation of neural crest cells due to genetic or epigenetic events during pre-migration, migration, and/or postmigration. Although a great deal has been discovered about the genetic and transcriptional regulation of neural crest development, the events leading to tumorigenesis remain poorly understood. The early age at onset of disease as well as the concept of in situ neuroblastoma {16010489} suggests that the tumour develops during fetal life. To date, however, there are no definite preconceptional or gestational factors accepted as consistently increasing the incidence of neuroblastoma.

Pathogenesis

MYC-family oncogene (MYCN and MYC) overexpression

The MYCN oncogene, located on chromosome 2p24.3, is considered a major oncogenic driver of neuroblastoma. Elevated LIN28B expression enhanced by MYCN is reported to contribute to neuroblastoma tumorigenesis. MYCN amplification is seen in approximately 20–25% of neuroblastoma cases. About 90% of MYCN-amplified tumours overexpress MYCN protein {29464082}. There are also neuroblastomas overexpressing MYC (c-myc) protein. In contrast to MYCN-overexpressing neuroblastoma, it is extremely rare to see gene amplification in MYC-overexpressing neuroblastoma {29426276}. In this regard, other molecular mechanisms for a higher level of MYC protein expression independent of genomic amplification have been reported, such as oncogene activation through enhancer hijacking by translocations, focal enhancer amplification near to the MYC coding region (8q24), or MK2-mediated OCT4 transcriptional activation of MYC {29284669; 32409685}. Tumours overexpressing MYC-family proteins (MYCN or MYC) are collectively named MYC-driven neuroblastomas {26035700}.

Abnormal maintenance/elongation of telomeres

Two mechanisms associated with telomere abnormalities that provide the tumour cells with infinite proliferating capability are reported in neuroblastoma: TERT overexpression and the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres (ALT) phenotype. TERT is the protein component of telomerase, and higher levels of expression are observed in both MYC-driven and non–MYC-driven neuroblastomas. In the former, TERT is the direct transcriptional target of MYC-family proteins {25893605}. In the latter, it is likely a result of long-range genomic rearrangements of TERT. TERT promoter hypermethylation may also be involved in its activating mechanism {26171145; 24337442; 28284778; 26523776}, whereas most of the tumours with ATRX loss due to ATRX mutation show the ALT phenotype {27760112}. The ALT phenotype is characterized by low TERT expression levels, and ATRX mutations occur almost mutually exclusively with genomic alterations of MYCN and TERT. Patients with ALT-positive neuroblastomas are significantly older at diagnosis than those with ALT-negative tumours {22416102; 33627664}. The ALT phenotype is also reported in rare neuroblastomas with loss of DAXX due to the gene mutation {25487495}.

ALK gene overexpression

As mentioned above, constitutive activating germline pathogenic variants in the oncogene ALK, located on chromosome 2p23, seem to account for most cases of hereditary neuroblastoma {18724359; 29374774}. About 6–10% of sporadic neuroblastomas carry somatic ALK-activating pathogenic variants, and an additional 3–4% have ALK amplification. Genomic rearrangements or amplification of ALK are observed in sporadic neuroblastoma only and have not been found in familial neuroblastoma thus far. ALK is a receptor tyrosine kinase with expression that is physiologically restricted mainly to the developing central and peripheral nervous system {29374774}. ALK mutations at hotspots (e.g. R1275, F1174, and F1245) lead to the activation of multiple signalling pathways, including the RAS/MAPK pathway and the PI3K/AKT pathway {29374774}. ALK mutations are single-nucleotide variants in the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD)-encoding region of the gene {28674118}. The ALK p.R1275Q variant occurs most frequently in familial neuroblastoma and is also one of the most prevalent pathogenic variants in sporadic neuroblastic tumours {28674118; 25517749}. By contrast, substitutions at F1174 and F1245, which are frequent mutations in sporadic neuroblastoma, have been detected rarely in familial cases and only in the context of severe neurocognitive defects and brainstem abnormalities {21972109}. ALK protein overexpression as a result of ALK mutation/rearrangement/amplification seems to be responsible for its oncogenic activity {26503946}. However, in mouse and zebrafish models, activated ALK has been shown to efficiently induce neuroblastoma development in combination with MYCN overexpression {22764207; 22789543; 22439933; 24811913}. By contrast, neuroblastoma did not develop or rarely developed in these models when activated ALK was expressed in the absence of a MYCN transgene. It is noted that the development of ALK-driven malignancies other than neuroblastoma has not been reported in children with germline ALK pathogenic variants.

Macroscopic appearance

Neuroblastomas can reach 100 mm in diameter and form a soft, grey-tan mass (often haemorrhagic), with or without foci of necrosis and calcification. The tumour mass can be well encapsulated or show invasive growth into the surrounding tissue. Biologically favourable primary neuroblastoma of the adrenal often pushes down the kidney without invasive growth. In contrast, biologically unfavourable neuroblastoma, especially with amplified MYCN oncogene, demonstrates direct invasion into the renal parenchyma.

Histopathology

Tumours in the neuroblastoma category are composed of neuroblastic cells forming groups or nests that are demarcated by thin fibrovascular septa with no or limited Schwannian proliferation {10421272}. The International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) defines three subtypes based on the grades of neuroblastic differentiation.

Neuroblastoma, undifferentiated subtype, is rare and requires supplementary studies such as immunohistochemistry and/or molecular tests to establish the diagnosis, because the proliferating cells have a uniformly primitive small blue round cell appearance without clearly recognizable neurite formation.

Neuroblastoma, poorly differentiated subtype, is defined as a tumour with a background of readily detectable neurites with or without Homer Wright rosette formation. Fewer than 5% of neuroblasts have cytomorphological features of differentiation towards mature neurons. This is the most common histological subtype in the neuroblastoma category. The conventional nuclear morphology of the neuroblasts in this subtype is often described as a salt-and-pepper appearance.

Neuroblastoma, differentiating subtype, is a tumour with usually abundant neurites, and where > 5% of tumour cells have the appearance of differentiating neuroblasts, characterized by synchronous differentiation of the nucleus (enlarged and eccentrically located, with a vesicular chromatin pattern and usually a single prominent nucleolus) and of the cytoplasm (eosinophilic/amphophilic and with a diameter at least twice that of the nucleus).

There are tumours with transitional histology between that of the differentiating subtype of neuroblastoma (Schwannian stroma–poor) and that of intermixed ganglioneuroblastoma (Schwannian stroma–rich; > 50% of the tumour tissue is composed of Schwannian stroma, presenting a ganglioneuromatous appearance). It is noted that undifferentiated / poorly differentiated tumours infrequently show unusual histological features (large, pleomorphic, fusiform, spindle, and pseudorhabdoid) focally or diffusely.

According to the INPC, mitotic and karyorrhectic activities are also evaluated, and the mitosis-karyorrhexis index (MKI; i.e. low, < 100/5000 cells; intermediate, 100–200/5000 cells; or high, > 200/5000 cells), is determined for the individual tumours. It is reported that MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma shows the characteristic histology of either the undifferentiated subtype or the poorly differentiated subtype with a high MKI. It is also reported that MYC-driven neuroblastoma overexpressing MYC-family protein (either MYCN or MYC) often demonstrates prominent nucleolar formation (nucleolar hypertrophy) that is considered a putative site of MYCN/MYC RNA synthesis and accumulation {29464082; 22744966; 23901000; 26035700; 15549714}. The histologically defined, rare, and very aggressive large cell neuroblastoma (LCN) is also included in this MYC-driven neuroblastoma group {14716776}. Although the nuclear morphology of LCN cells is often similar to that of differentiating neuroblasts, these two cell types should be strictly distinguished. The tumour cells in LCN have larger nuclei than conventional neuroblasts and their cytoplasm is similarly scant. In contrast, differentiating neuroblasts have abundant cytoplasm with a diameter at least twice that of the nucleus {18158560}.

Immunophenotype

An immunohistochemistry panel including neuronal markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin, PGP9.5, CD56, and neurofilament), so-called neuroblastoma marker (NB84) {9500774}, and neural crest markers (tyrosine hydroxylase and PHOX2B) {22790854; 25822764} is usually advised. Among these markers, PHOX2B is the most reliable for supporting a neuroblastoma diagnosis, even for the undifferentiated subtype {28986989}. Tyrosine hydroxylase immunostaining for metastatic neuroblastoma often fades after decalcification on bone marrow biopsy samples. Generally, a combination of positive staining for these markers along with negative staining for markers of other small round cell tumours is suggested. Certain MYC-driven neuroblastomas, especially those of LCNs, are reported to express the intermediate filaments of desmin {29426276}. S100 immunohistochemistry can stain some Schwannian stromal cells and putative Schwann cells (Schwannian blasts) in the fibrovascular septal tissue, especially in biologically favourable tumours in the neuroblastoma category {3886523}.

International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification

The INPC was established in 1999 by adapting the original Shimada classification {6589432} with minor modifications {10421273}, and it was then revised in 2003 {14601099}. According to the INPC, tumours in the neuroblastoma category are classified into favourable or unfavourable histology groups on the basis of age-dependent evaluation of the grade of neuroblastic differentiation (i.e. undifferentiated, poorly differentiated, or differentiating subtype) and mitotic and karyorrhectic activity (i.e. low, intermediate, or high MKI; see #26092Table 6.02). Tumours in the favourable histology group are within a framework of age-appropriate differentiation/maturation sequence and age-appropriate MKI class. As shown in #26092Table 6.02 , two age cut-off points – 18 months (548 days) and 60 months (5 years) – are introduced in the INPC. Prognostic classification by this system should only be performed on primary or metastatic tumours before treatment (chemotherapy, radiation). It is noted that MKI determination is not feasible or reliable on metastatic neuroblastoma in bone marrow biopsy samples.

Subgrouping of unfavourable-histology neuroblastomas

Recent advances in neuroblastoma research demonstrate that unfavourable-histology neuroblastomas are heterogeneous. Based on a molecular approach, four subgroups are proposed in the unfavourable-histology neuroblastomas: the MYC subgroup (overexpressing MYCN or MYC protein), the TERT subgroup (TERT overexpression due to genomic abnormalities), the ALT subgroup (ALT phenotype due to ATRX loss), and the null subgroup. These subgroups are molecularly and immunohistochemically identified by their protein markers, which could be potential targets for future precision therapy {31840131}.

Cytology

FNA or other cytopathological evaluation may not be representative and requires ancillary testing for a definitive diagnosis. Smears are highly cellular and show predominantly single cells along with discohesive tissue fragments showing moulding and occasionally a rosette-like architecture with pale, pink (H&E stain) or blue to grey (Giemsa stain) central fibrillary neuropil, which is diagnostic when present. The cells have high N:C ratios and oval to irregular nuclei with evenly dispersed granular salt-and-pepper chromatin and inconspicuous to small nucleoli, and they may show wispy cytoplasmic extensions. Apoptotic necrosis and chromatinic smearing are frequently present, and neuropil may be seen in the background with variable Schwannian stroma. Larger binucleated or multinucleated ganglion cells may be present. In the absence of the rosettes, immunohistochemistry is required to differentiate other tumours, including lymphomas, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and nephroblastoma {31231909; 14676638}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genomic/molecular characteristics significantly associated with tumorigenesis and/or the clinical behaviour of neuroblastomas include tumour DNA content and segmental chromosomal aberrations, MYCN oncogene amplification and MYC-family protein overexpression, abnormal maintenance/elongation of telomeres, and ALK gene abnormalities and protein overexpression (see Prognosis and prediction).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Confirming the diagnosis of neuroblastoma: histologically, the tumour is composed of neuroblastic cells forming groups/nests irregularly demarcating thin fibrovascular septa with no or limited Schwannian stromal development; tumour nuclei typically show a salt-and-pepper appearance, whereas MYC-driven neuroblastoma characteristically demonstrates nucleolar hypertrophy; diagnosis is supported by immunohistochemistry using neuronal markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD56, etc.) and neural crest markers (PHOX2B, tyrosine hydroxylase) Distinguishing favourable-histology and unfavourable-histology tumours according to the INPC: the prognostic distinction is made by age-linked evaluation of histological factors – the grade of neuroblastic differentiation (undifferentiated, poorly differentiated, or differentiating) and the MKI (low, intermediate, or high)

Desirable (as available): molecular/genomic testing including MYCN oncogene status and MYC-family protein expression (immunohistochemistry), telomere maintenance–associated gene (TERT, ATRX) status, and ALK oncogene status; DNA index and segmental chromosomal aberrations for prognostic prediction.

Staging

There are two widely used clinical staging systems: the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS; stages 1, 2A, 2B, 3, 4, and 4S) {3199170; 8336186} and the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS; stages L1, L2, M, and MS) {19047290}. The INSS is based on the postsurgical evaluation of the disease status, whereas the INRGSS depends on the presurgical determination of the presence or absence of image-defined risk factors. Many collaborative and independent clinical studies have started using INRGSS, which eliminates the surgical approach for determining clinical staging (see #26097Table 6.03).

Prognosis and prediction

Besides clinical stage, age at diagnosis {23255319; 19047291; 34319759}, and histopathology (INPC), the genomic/molecular properties of the individual tumours have significant prognostic effects for patients with neuroblastomas. They are summarized below.

Tumour DNA content and segmental chromosomal aberrations

Tumours presenting with numerical whole-chromosome copy-number gains without structural abnormalities (hyperdiploid, DNA index > 1) are associated with an excellent prognosis {6738617; 16116152}. Hyperdiploidy presumably results from chromosome-segregation failure during mitosis. In contrast, tumours in the diploid range (DNA index = 1) are associated with a poor clinical outcome. Flow cytometric analysis for the determination of the DNA index has been used as a simple and semi-automated method of measuring total cell DNA in neuroblastomas. However, the prognostic significance of ploidy determined by flow cytometry seems to be lost in children aged > 2 years, possibly because tumours with a DNA index > 1 in older children typically have a number of structural abnormalities.

Various segmental chromosomal aberrations are reported as indicators of a poor clinical outcome in neuroblastomas {8608986; 16306521; 20145112; 20558371; 10379019; 18923191; 19536264}. Loss of heterozygosity of chromosome 1p has been reported in 25–35% of cases, and most of the deletions are located distal to 1p36. Distal 1p likely harbours tumour suppressor genes or genes controlling differentiation of neuroblastic cells. A relationship between loss of heterozygosity of chromosome 1p and MYCN amplification has been described. Loss of heterozygosity of chromosome 11q has been identified in 35–45% of cases; 11q23 is the most common site for deletion. Chromosome 11q deletions are often associated with older patients and rarely occur in combination with MYCN amplification. Trisomy 17q (17q gain) may be the most prevalent genetic abnormality in neuroblastomas, and it has been detected in about 60% of cases. Although the gain can occur independently, it frequently occurs as part of an unbalanced translocation between chromosomes 1 and 17. The 17q breakpoints vary but most frequently occur at 17q22-qter. Chromosome 1q gain is also reported in neuroblastomas.

The prognostic effects of the segmental chromosomal alterations (i.e. 1p deletion, 11q deletion, and 17q gain) were tested in neuroblastomas without MYCN amplification; 11q deletion and 17q gain were associated with poor survival in a univariate analysis. A segmental profile (i.e. the presence of 1p deletion and/or 11q deletion and/or 17q gain, rather than a single and specific genetic alteration) adds prognostic information to patient age and clinical stage, according to a multivariate analysis {22976801}.

MYCN oncogene amplification and MYC-family (MYCN/MYC) protein overexpression

It is well known that MYCN amplification is associated with advanced-stage disease and aggressive clinical behaviour {20558371; 6719137; 4047115}. A large region including the MYCN locus becomes amplified as extrachromosomal double minutes, and it may be linearly integrated into a chromosome as homogeneously staining regions. By FISH, MYCN status is determined as amplified (> 4-fold increase in the MYCN signal number compared with the reference probe located chromosome 2), gain (with increased signals but < 4 times as many vs reference signals of chromosome 2), or non-amplified. It is recommended to report the extent of amplification (i.e. > 4–10 times, > 10–30 times, or > 30 times), and on the type of amplification (i.e. double minutes or homogeneously staining regions). In the past few years, heterogeneous MYCN intratumoural amplification has been appreciated in association with a diverse aggressive phenotype {26119945}.

Recent studies support the notion that MYCN protein overexpression, rather than gene amplification, is critical for determining aggressive tumour behaviour {22744966}. There are also neuroblastomas overexpressing MYC (c-myc) protein. As mentioned above, it is extremely rare to see gene amplification in MYC-overexpressing neuroblastoma {29426276}.

Tumours overexpressing MYC-family protein (MYCN overexpression detected in ~20% or MYC overexpression detected in ~10% of neuroblastomas of the undifferentiated or poorly differentiated subtypes) are collectively named MYC-driven neuroblastomas {26035700}. Those MYC-driven neuroblastomas are highly aggressive {23901000} and histologically characterized by prominent nucleolar formation (nucleolar hypertrophy). Because of the presence of prominent nucleoli, MYC-driven neuroblastoma can be morphologically distinguished from other conventional neuroblastomas, which have salt-and-pepper nuclei.

Abnormal maintenance/elongation of telomeres

Abnormalities of telomere maintenance have been suggested to be the hallmark of high-risk neuroblastoma, discriminating these cases from spontaneously regressing low-risk tumours {30523111}. Both TERT overexpression and the ALT phenotype are reported to be predictive of reduced patient survival {23702928; 26466568; 30523111}. Elevated levels of TERT expression and enzymatic activity are associated with either MYCN amplification or rearrangements of the TERT locus {26171145; 28284778; 26523776}. Most of the ALT phenotype is associated with ATRX protein loss, which is due to ATRX gene mutations leading to recombinogenic telomeres {22416102; 30523111; 33627664}.

ALK gene abnormalities and protein overexpression

Genomic ALK alterations causing protein overexpression {18724359; 18923523; 18923524; 2650394} have been reported to be associated with a poor prognosis in patients with neuroblastoma {22585002; 26503946}. However, the prognostic significance of ALK overexpression has been controversial. For example, the clinical outcomes of patients within the same family bearing a germline ALK mutation are different and not always poor {28674118}, and spontaneous regression has been documented in ALK-mutated neuroblastomas {30523111}. It is therefore likely that, in addition to ALK mutations, further genomic alterations (e.g. MYCN amplification, TERT rearrangement, or ATRX mutation) might be required for oncogenic development and full malignant transformation of neuroblastoma cells in both familial and sporadic tumours {30523111}. Regairaz et al. reported that the expression of ALK and its active form, phosphorylated ALK, was immunohistochemically detected in many neuroblastomas, independently of ALK mutations/amplification {26687816}. The therapeutic effect of ALK inhibitors in ALK-mutated neuroblastoma is currently under investigation in clinical trials {24166094}.

Risk grouping system

Historically, there have been several risk grouping systems proposed by different cooperative groups, such as the International Society of Paediatric Oncology Europe Neuroblastoma Group (SIOPEN), the Society for Paediatric Oncology and Haematology (GPOH), and the Children’s Oncology Group (COG). Currently, two systems – the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS) and the COG risk grouping system – are widely accepted; both are based on a combination of predictive factors, such as clinical stage, patient age at diagnosis, histopathology, and genetic and genomic/molecular properties. The COG risk grouping system has been used for the purpose of patient stratification and protocol assignment in clinical trials {23255319; 12773750}. Patients in the low-risk group are often followed by observation (with no chemotherapy or radiation) after surgery or even biopsy alone. Patients in the intermediate-risk group are treated with surgery or biopsy followed by non-aggressive chemotherapy. Patients in the high-risk group are treated with intensive chemotherapy, surgery, radiation therapy, bone marrow / haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and biologically based therapies. The reported 5-year survival rates for non–high-risk and high-risk patients are 90% and 50%, respectively {23255319; 34319759}.

The INRGSS has been designed to facilitate the comparison of risk-based clinical trials conducted in different regions and countries, and it distinguishes very low-risk (> 85% event-free survival), low-risk (75–85% event-free survival), intermediate-risk (50–75% event-free survival), and high-risk (< 50% event-free survival) patients {19047291}.
