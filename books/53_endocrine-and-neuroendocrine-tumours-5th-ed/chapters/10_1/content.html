1.0 Chapter 1: Introduction to endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours

The fifth edition of the WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours is a timely sequel to the previous editions. It fully reflects the landscape of endocrine pathology, which is constantly evolving owing to new insights into etiology and pathogenesis and with newer methods of diagnosis, molecular and genetic testing, imaging, and management. The text incorporates not only the traditional endocrine organs but also a chapter on neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) in non-endocrine organs. This new WHO classification largely adopts the 2018 WHO / International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) terminology framework for NENs in various anatomical sites {30140036}. This achieves consistency in classifying this family of endocrine tumours in a comprehensive, organized, and reproducible manner. The chapter on thyroid neoplasia has undergone extensive reorganization; and the sections on the adrenal cortex outline the advances in our understanding of steroid hormone–producing cells. The understanding of tumour syndromes is important in this field where genetic predisposition is so common.

Like in other volumes in the series, each entity has a clear and concise definition and a list of pertinent essential and desirable diagnostic criteria to ensure a unified diagnostic approach, to facilitate accurate data collection for tumour registries and further improve the quality of translational research. Special emphasis is placed on diagnostic and predictive biomarkers, including molecular analysis and immunohistochemistry.

Neuroendocrine neoplasms

NENs are derived from two main neuroendocrine cell groups: epithelial and neuronal/paraneuronal.

Pituitary NENs

The term “pituitary neuroendocrine tumour” (PitNET) is the preferred nomenclature for pituitary NENs, formerly known as pituitary adenomas {34017065; 33866514}. This change is also accompanied by updated coding, aligned with other neuroendocrine tumours (NETs). PitNETs are well-differentiated NETs that reflect the cytogenesis of the various hormone-producing adenohypophysial cells. These tumours have a low frequency of metastatic spread, but they can have significant morbidity and mortality due to local invasion. When metastatic, the term “metastatic PitNET” is endorsed instead of “pituitary carcinoma”. However, this edition still preserves the nomenclature of “pituitary carcinoma” and “PitNET / pituitary adenoma” as acceptable, with a view to facilitating a smooth transition to the next edition of the WHO classification where it is likely that the terms “pituitary carcinoma” and “pituitary adenoma” will be considered redundant. It also remains to be determined whether primary poorly differentiated pituitary NENs exist that would qualify for classification as neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) based on the WHO/IARC framework. Unlike for most NENs, there are no formal grading and staging systems applied to PitNETs. The detailed histological classification of PitNETs models the classification of NENs based on cell lineage, cell type, and related characteristics by combining the routine use of transcription factors and hormones with the use of other biomarkers, including but not limited to keratins (especially CAM5.2) and Ki-67. This approach facilitates risk stratification and also predicts the differential response to therapies among various subtypes of PitNETs {24144285; 26177314; 29434339; 32813227; 34017065}.

The addition of hypothalamic NENs expands the spectrum of sellar endocrine neoplasia, and the clarification of tumours such as spindle cell oncocytomas, granular cell tumours, and sellar ependymomas as subtypes of pituicytoma allows a clear distinction between neuroendocrine and glial-derived neoplasms in this region.

Parathyroid NENs

Even though parathyroid glands are neuroendocrine tissues, no effort has been made to classify parathyroid NENs as NETs, on the basis that truly benign lesions (adenomas) are very common, and biologically malignant neoplasms (parathyroid carcinomas) are very rare.

A focus of this edition has been to clarify issues in the nomenclature of hyperplasia versus neoplasia. An abnormal parathyroid gland is often enlarged and cellular, and it typically exceeds 8 mm and 60 mg {26163537}; however, cellular parenchyma is not always a pathological sign. The importance of clinical, laboratory, and genetic findings in achieving accurate diagnoses of parathyroid disorders is emphasized. Traditionally, multiglandular disease has almost always been classified as “parathyroid hyperplasia”. However, primary multiglandular parathyroid disease is usually due to multiple adenomas arising in the setting of a germline susceptibility syndrome, and the terminology has now been updated to reflect this. For example, multiglandular disease arising in the setting of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 is now classified as multiple adenomas rather than primary hyperplasia. With this approach, true hyperplasia in primary hyperparathyroidism is now an extremely uncommon manifestation that is being questioned, and true parathyroid hyperplasia is restricted to secondary hyperparathyroidism.

The diagnostic criteria of parathyroid carcinoma, which may be considered more analogous to a well-differentiated NET than a true NEC, remain unchanged. However, the role of biomarkers has been expanded. The diagnostic term “atypical parathyroid tumour” has been introduced to replace the older nomenclature of “atypical parathyroid adenoma”, and it is noted that the great majority of atypical parathyroid tumours not associated with worrying clinical, molecular, or immunohistochemical features do not recur after simple excision. Practical information, including but not limited to what can simulate atypical features, is also provided, along with the crucial role of immunohistochemistry in the workup of atypical parathyroid tumours and parathyroid carcinoma.

Paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas

Non-epithelial NENs are paragangliomas, including intra-adrenal paragangliomas known as phaeochromocytomas. Their postulated origin from neural crest–like Schwann cell precursors is new information. The occurrence of these tumours in various anatomical locations (e.g. liver, lung, pancreas, thyroid) correlates with dispersed microscopic paraganglia in close association with components of the autonomic nervous system {30217041}. The importance of employing immunostains for GATA3 and tyrosine hydroxylase is emphasized in the diagnosis of paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas (PPGLs). The distinction between PPGL and epithelial NEN is of clinical significance, because the clinical management of these tumours is different. Another important feature of particular importance is the distinction of metastatic PPGLs from synchronous multifocal primary tumours that may occur due to the high frequency (≥ 40%) of germline predisposition in PPGLs. The recognition of genomic alterations {23478233; 25379023} in subcentimetre adrenal medullary nodules supports the concept of microphaeochromocytoma {24476517}; therefore, the current WHO classification restricts medullary hyperplasia only to the diffuse expansion of the adrenal medulla. The presence of diffuse medullary hyperplasia in association with (micro)phaeochromocytomas should prompt investigation of germline susceptibility (e.g. RET, MAX, TMEM127, and SDHB) {25379023; 25800244; 27838885}. The routine use of molecular immunohistochemistry is encouraged in PPGLs. All PPGLs have metastatic potential, and therefore they are all considered malignant. While acknowledging them, the current WHO classification does not endorse the scoring systems that have been introduced to predict the risk of metastasis, because no single system has consistently proved to have strong predictive performance. Instead, members of the multidisciplinary endocrine oncology team are encouraged to use dynamic risk stratification and clinical correlation to estimate the recurrence risk of PPGLs. The features associated with increased risk include somatic, epigenetic, and/or germline alterations; various other factors such as anatomical tumour location, large tumour size, and immature catecholamine profiling (absence of elevated metanephrine / epinephrine [adrenaline] levels); and the Ki-67 proliferation index {10526289; 28162975; 30217041; 31678991; 32062700; 32877928; 33768452}.

Pancreatic NENs

The classification of epithelial pancreatic NENs (PanNENs) remains largely unchanged. However, the diagnostic category of “neuroendocrine microadenoma” is being replaced with “neuroendocrine microtumour”, reflecting their very low-risk metastatic potential {30003325} while keeping consistency with the NEN terminology framework. Advances made in understanding the tumorigenesis of pancreatic epithelial NENs are reflected in individual entity sections. Particularly, the small indolent insulinomas are distinguished from their aggressive metastatic counterparts by ARX expression and by the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres phenotype, ATRX or DAXX loss, and CDKN2A loss, similarly to non-functioning NETs {32103422; 32590616}. In this classification, the role of immunohistochemical biomarkers has been emphasized, with a particular emphasis on keratin and hormone immunohistochemistry, and the list of functional categories now includes PPomas. The concept of NET-to-NEC progression remains to be further studied.

NENs in non-neuroendocrine organs

The topic of NENs in non-neuroendocrine tissues is addressed for the first time in this fifth edition. There is an important discussion of the various biomarkers that are used to define neuroendocrine differentiation. The use of nonspecific immunohistochemical biomarkers alone (e.g. CD56, CD57, and NSE) is discouraged. The expression of classic neuroendocrine differentiation markers (e.g. chromogranin A) may be variable in NECs {9712424; 27529763} and in NETs with selective expression of chromogranin B (e.g. L-cell NETs of the rectum and appendix {7599788; 27529763}). Although negative synaptophysin expression makes the possibility of a NEN unlikely, it is not a specific marker, and the use of INSM1 (the nuclear transcription factor that regulates neuroendocrine differentiation) is useful in the diagnostic workup of neoplasms with synaptophysin and focal or absent chromogranin A expression {29438167; 31857137; 32813226; 32388776; 33786701}.

The arbitrary cut-off point of 30% of each component to support a diagnosis of mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN) has been challenged. Although this distinction is even more difficult in small biopsy specimens from metastases (e.g. liver core biopsies), immunohistochemically confirmed and morphologically distinct tumour components should be recognized and reported irrespective of their extent.

Advances made in the understanding of NENs with mixed epithelial and neuronal differentiation are reflected in updated terminology. The tumour formerly known as “gangliocytic paraganglioma” is now renamed “composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour” (CoGNET; see section Composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour), a diagnostic term better reflecting its composition and biology. Similarly, the tumours previously called “cauda equina paragangliomas” are biologically, genetically, and morphologically different from PPGLs arising elsewhere {32668278; 32892244}. Further studies are awaited, and it is anticipated that in the future, CNS paraganglioma may be better classified as a form of NET.

Thyroid neoplasia

The data obtained from molecular studies have provided an opportunity to streamline and simplify the classification of thyroid neoplasia. There is still work to be done to elucidate the pathogenesis of the common multifocal benign proliferative lesion that is clinically known as sporadic nodular goitre but is recognized to be an admixture of hyperplastic and neoplastic proliferations. The term “thyroid follicular nodular disease” is now used to describe this entity.

Non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features (including oncocytic and subcentimetre subtypes), thyroid tumours of uncertain malignant potential, and hyalinizing trabecular tumours are now assigned to a low-risk follicular cell–derived neoplasia category to reflect their extremely low risk of metastatic spread or structural recurrence.

At the genomic level and by their clinical behaviour, follicular thyroid carcinoma and the invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma are identical malignant follicular cell–derived thyroid neoplasms with RAS-like molecular profiles. The degree of nuclear alterations varies among these tumours; however, the threshold for nuclear alterations varies among experts. Therefore, the current WHO classification separates these two lesions with an expectation that in the next edition it may be possible to coalesce follicular thyroid carcinoma and encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma into a single diagnostic category of follicular-patterned differentiated thyroid carcinoma. (Although the term “variant” is now usually reserved for genetic alterations, it remains part of the entity name for invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma.)

In this classification, papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is defined as a class of BRAF-like neoplasms. The diagnosis still requires either papillary or solid/trabecular architecture, or infiltrative growth in follicular-patterned tumours. The various subtypes are discussed in detail and their clinical importance is reviewed. Tall cell change is defined using the rigid criterion of a height-to-weight ratio of ≥ 3:1. Papillary microcarcinomas require subtyping, similarly to PTCs exceeding 10 mm.

The term “Hürthle cell” is recognized as a misnomer, and the use of the term “oncocytic” is encouraged for tumours with this cytological alteration. The follicular cell–derived adenomas and carcinomas with extensive (> 75%) oncocytic changes are discussed separately to emphasize their specific features in terms of behaviour, molecular profile, and radioactive iodine avidity; special attention is given to recognizing the high-grade features that should prompt reclassification into a high-grade category that reflects a greater risk of adverse behaviour.

High-grade follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma is a new category introduced in this edition. It includes follicular cell–derived carcinomas with high-grade features based on mitotic count and tumour necrosis in the absence of anaplastic dedifferentiation. These tumours are further subtyped as high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma (e.g. high-grade PTC, high-grade follicular thyroid carcinoma, high-grade oncocytic carcinoma) and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinomas. The relevant message for pathologists is the importance of mitoses and necrosis even in differentiated thyroid carcinomas.

Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma remains the least common but most aggressive form of follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinomas. A particular emphasis is placed on the diagnosis of exclusion of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma from other high-grade malignancies, especially in the absence of expression of PAX8 and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1). Formerly listed as a distinct tumour category, primary squamous cell carcinoma of the thyroid is now considered to be a subtype of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma.

The tumour formerly known as cribriform-morular variant of PTC is no longer classified as a subtype of PTC. It is a tumour of uncertain cytogenesis with unique morphology, genetics, and immunohistochemical features that include diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic β-catenin expression, absence of diffuse PAX8 expression and thyroglobulin expression in the non-morular component, and a lack of TTF1 along with coexpression of CD5 and CK5 in morulae {34019236}. Its histogenesis awaits further clarification, therefore it has not yet joined the groups of tumours of thymic or salivary gland differentiation that also occur in the thyroid gland.

Medullary thyroid carcinoma is the classic NEN of the thyroid, and the importance of Ki-67 as well as tumour necrosis and mitotic count in this lesion is now emphasized.

In this classification, thyroblastoma is officially recognized as an extremely rare and underrecognized embryonal high-grade thyroid neoplasm that is linked to DICER1 variants. It consists of primitive thyroid follicular cells, small cells, and primitive mesenchymal stroma {32507920}.

Adrenal cortical lesions

Structure, function, and morphology correlations are reflected in the classification of adrenal cortical lesions {29594118; 31672302; 33534120}. CYP11B2 immunohistochemistry–based histopathology of primary aldosteronism (HISTALDO) classification {32717746} is recommended in the diagnostic workup of adrenalectomy specimens with primary aldosteronism. In light of advances made in translational molecular biology, the spectrum of cortical neoplasia has been expanded to reflect sporadic nodular adrenal cortical disease and bilateral micronodular and macronodular adrenal cortical disease, because they are all composed of genetically modified cells {29594118; 31672302; 33534120}. The only true adrenal cortical hyperplasia is bilateral diffuse adrenal cortical hyperplasia in the context of ACTH-dependent disease, including congenital adrenal hyperplasia.

In addition to well-established multifactorial schemes, the reticulin algorithm and Helsinki scoring systems have expanded the list of available tools to classify adrenal cortical neoplasms. The importance of venous angioinvasion stands out as a diagnostic and predictive feature of adrenal cortical carcinomas {28877067}. Special emphasis is given to the role of immunohistochemical biomarkers, including paranuclear dot-like IGF2 overexpression in the distinction of adrenal cortical carcinoma from adrenal cortical adenoma {28877067; 29542002}. In addition to mitotic tumour grading (based on the cut-off point of 20 mitoses/10 mm2), the crucial role of Ki-67 as a diagnostic and predictive biomarker in adult and paediatric adrenal cortical carcinoma is discussed {24434900; 25559399; 28877067; 30401946; 33443677}. Evidence suggests that the diagnosis and prognostication of adrenal cortical carcinoma is possible using molecular diagnostics {27165744}.

Endocrine tumour syndromes

The spectrum of endocrine tumour syndromes continues to grow rapidly. This edition provides important updates on the known genetic tumour syndromes as well as incorporating newly described genetic tumour syndromes. Changes include a unified terminology for succinate dehydrogenase–deficient tumour syndromes based on both clinical and molecular features. Whereas the term “Carney triad” was previously defined clinically, it is now defined by dual clinical and molecular criteria as a succinate dehydrogenase–deficient tumour syndrome where no germline SDH mutation has been identified. The use of older terminology such as “Carney–Stratakis dyad” (the clinical association of familial succinate dehydrogenase–deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour and paraganglioma) is now discouraged in favour of nomenclature based on identification of the mutated gene {30877234}. DICER1-related disease has been significantly expanded, and the new multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 (due to germline pathogenic MAX variants) and MAFA-related familial insulinomatosis are now defined and discussed in detail. The familial follicular cell–derived thyroid neoplasia in syndromes is well defined; however, the non-syndromic familial follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma remains to be clarified.

A lack of family history and age are not reliable criteria to argue against inherited endocrine tumours / NETs, because de novo manifestations that simulate sporadic disease are not uncommon. Moreover, technical limitations in detecting pathogenic variants using next-generation sequencing panels in a small fraction of patients should be kept in mind. Whole-exome sequencing or even whole-genome sequencing can reduce the fraction of mutation-negative individuals on first-line diagnostic molecular analyses {33249439}.

Technical and methodological issues
Mitotic counts and the Ki-67 labelling index

In general, the fifth edition requires reporting of mitotic count (either conventional or phosphohistone H3–assisted) using mm2 rather than HPF to provide reproducibility among practices {34079071}. This will also ensure a smooth transition to upcoming digital pathology practice. Eyeballing alone for the Ki-67 proliferation index is discouraged, and pathologists should normally provide the labelling index using an automated image analysis nuclear algorithm or formal manual count. The methodology used to assess mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index should be clearly defined in pathology reports.

Molecular immunohistochemistry

Molecular immunohistochemistry is an invaluable tool for pathologists to acquire insights into disease cytogenesis and pathogenesis. However, reliable prediction and prognostication requires technically sound methodology, accurate reagents, and rigorous validation protocols. The importance of using monoclonal antibodies has been emphasized with respect to pituitary hormones and CEA, and different antibody clones, such as those used to localize TTF1 and PAX8, can have differing sensitivities and specificities. Good immunohistochemistry can also serve as a screening tool for inherited disease when prompted by identification of precursor lesions and/or clinical features, and it is of particular value in assessing genetic variants of uncertain significance.

Angioinvasion

The subject of angioinvasion is a controversial but critical one in endocrine pathology. Endocrine organs are rich in vessels and are decorated with fenestrated endothelium, providing the basis for potential artefact. As shown in adrenal cortical carcinoma {28877067}, follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinomas {21804527}, medullary thyroid carcinoma {23150029}, parathyroid carcinoma {22290780; 23001705; 31708875}, and NETs {26936845; 34283399}, angioinvasion characterized by the presence of intravascular tumour cells admixed with thrombus/platelets reliably predicts a high risk of tumour spread. The use of CD61 immunohistochemistry can assist the recognition of intravascular tumour cell–platelet aggregates by highlighting platelets when the thrombus is subtle {31218593}. In follicular cell–derived malignancies, the number of vessels involved has been discussed and a cut-off point of 4 vessels has been applied in some risk-stratification schemes {16534796; 23327839; 26482605}; however, there is no scientific clarity or multi-institutional validation on how to count vessels and whether adjacent vessels represent a single focus or multiple foci. Furthermore, opinions vary among experts on the relevance of this requirement. Specifically in the thyroid gland, because of the lower risk of negative outcomes associated with locoregional lymph node metastasis, the distinction of vascular channels (angioinvasion) from lymphatics (lymphatic invasion) remains important.

Open questions and future vision

The endocrine system, while composed of three distinct lineages of cells with different embryogenesis, structure, and function, remains the site of some of the most indolent and some of the most aggressive neoplasms. The role of genetic predisposition and the identification of molecular subtypes of sporadic tumours has shed light on the often-unique cellular mechanisms underlying neoplastic transformation. Nonetheless, there remain many unanswered questions.

In light of current advances in the field of endocrine pathology, it is not far-fetched to anticipate that the following will be most likely addressed in the next edition of the WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours: (1) validation of biomarkers that truly distinguish NET from NEC, and data proving whether there is progression from NET to NEC; (2) diagnostic criteria that will distinguish MiNEN from non-neuroendocrine tumours with neuroendocrine differentiation or amphicrine features; (3) evidence of a poorly differentiated pituitary NEC; (4) development of a TNM staging system for pituitary tumours; (5) clarification as to the existence of a poorly differentiated NEC of the parathyroid gland; (6) determination of whether medullary thyroid carcinoma is better classified as a NET rather than a NEC, within the NEN terminology framework; (7) the etiology and pathogenesis of composite epithelial and neuronal tumours arising in the pituitary, duodenum, and cauda equina; and (8) the role of the environment in the epigenetic changes that are so common in endocrine tumours / NETs.
