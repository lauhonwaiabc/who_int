6.2.1.2 Adrenocortical nodular disease

Definition

Adrenocortical nodular disease comprises a group of distinct pathological entities characterized by benign adrenal cortical nodular proliferations.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

5A70.Y Other specified Cushing syndrome

Related terminology

Acceptable: adrenal cortical nodular disease; cortical nodular disease; nodular adrenocortical disease.

Not recommended: cortical nodular hyperplasia; adrenocortical nodular hyperplasia; micronodular adrenocortical hyperplasia; macronodular adrenocortical hyperplasia.

Subtype(s)

Sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease; bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease (isolated micronodular adrenocortical disease [i-MAD] and primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease [PPNAD]); bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease

Localization

The micronodular and macronodular forms of adrenocortical nodular disease involve both adrenal glands, whereas sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease may manifest as unilateral or bilateral adrenal gland involvement {31672302; 33534120}.

Clinical features

Sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease often consists of one or more incidentally discovered non-functioning adrenocortical nodule(s) that measure < 10 mm and can occur in all age groups {31672302; 33534120} [[Duan K, Giordano TJ, Mete O. Adrenal cortical proliferations. In: Mete O, Asa SL, editors. Endocrine pathology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2016. pp. 602–27.]]. They may be found on imaging, at autopsy, or during the histopathological examination of adrenalectomy specimens for other reasons {20969508; 31672302}.

Individuals with bilateral micronodular or macronodular adrenocortical disease usually have variable degrees of endogenous Cushing syndrome {25425660; 29594118; 31672302; 33534120; 33776926; 33209819}. Bilateral micronodular adrenocortical nodular disease occurs in children and young adults (often in those aged < 30 years) {33776926; 31912137}, with a female predominance {19293268; 32209819}. PPNAD, a distinct subtype of bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease, is a characteristic endocrine manifestation of Carney complex {4010501; 31912137} and has been reported in about 57% of patients with Carney complex {31912137}. Because PPNAD can occur in the absence of Carney complex (isolated PPNAD [i-PPNAD]) {12213893; 33534120; 25425660}, the term “c-PPNAD” has been applied to PPNAD associated with Carney complex {33776926; 25425660; 29594118}. i-MAD is a very rare subtype of bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease that is distinguished from i-PPNAD on the basis of histopathological characteristics {25425660; 29594118; 33776926; 32115357}. Both PPNAD and i-MAD show variable degrees of hypercortisolism, including cyclic Cushing syndrome, and may exhibit a paradoxical response to a dexamethasone suppression (Liddle test) with an increase in 17-hydroxycorticosteroid and urinary free cortisol levels {19383776; 33776926; 31912137; 29594118}. Unlike children with Cushing syndrome due to other causes {25425660}, children with PPNAD may exhibit normal growth {32209819; 17955016}.

Bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease (formerly known as primary bilateral macronodular adrenal cortical hyperplasia or ACTH-independent macronodular adrenocortical hyperplasia) is more frequent in adults; however, paediatric manifestations also exist {33776926; 25425660; 29594118; 33534120}. In a large series, the age at onset in adults ranged from 30 to 75 years (mean: 53 years) and there was a female predominance {25853793}. ARMC5-related macronodular disease has no sex predominance {25853793}. Fewer than about 10% of individuals with bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease may have biochemically non-functioning disease, and the remainder show a variable degree of Cushing syndrome {25853793}. ARMC5-related bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease often has a significantly higher level of cortisol secretion than ARMC5-wildtype disease and an absence of non-functional manifestations {25853793}. Childhood-onset bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease often shows more pronounced Cushing syndrome {29594118; 25425660}.

Imaging

On imaging studies, massive bilateral adrenal gland enlargement is the hallmark of bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease {1653280; 15026592; 31672302}. CT and MRI findings in bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease are variable and may underestimate the presence of bilateral adrenal gland involvement {31912137; 31672302}. Half of all affected patients may have an abnormal imaging finding {31912137}, including multiple bilateral small bead-like nodules {15026592}. Functional imaging studies using iodocholesterol (NP-59) scintigraphy/PET help identify the bilateral nature of the disease in ACTH-independent Cushing disease with normal CT {26390100}.

Epidemiology

Adrenal incidentalomas are reported in about 5% of people who undergo imaging studies {21618498; 16699294; 20969508}. Although not all adrenal incidentalomas are of cortical origin, they may be underreported {20969508}. Therefore, the prevalence of sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease in surgical and radiological series with complete endocrine workup remains to be determined. Nevertheless, sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease is more frequent than bilateral micronodular and macronodular adrenocortical disease. Bilateral micronodular and macronodular subtypes of adrenocortical disease account for up to 2% of cases of endogenous Cushing syndrome {32209819; 33776926}.

Etiology

The etiology of sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease is largely unknown. However, cigarette smoking may be a risk factor, because the prevalence of both benign and malignant adrenocortical neoplasms is higher in individuals with a history of tobacco use {32871402}. Virtually all cases of bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease and a significant fraction of cases of bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease are caused by genetic susceptibility (see Pathogenesis) {33534120; 29594118; 33776926}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic small adrenocortical nodules (as small as 3–5 mm) may be clonal {17890895; 23478233}. Therefore, the term “sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease” has replaced “cortical nodular hyperplasia” for incidental non-functioning cortical nodules measuring < 10 mm {33534120} [[Pakbaz S, Mete O. Adrenal cortical neoplasia: from histology to molecular biology. Diagn Histopathol. 2019 May;25(5):178–89. doi:10.1016/j.mpdhp.2019.02.004.]]

In bilateral nodular adrenocortical disease, germline susceptibility is followed by a somatic second hit involving the other allele. Bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease is linked to pathogenic germline variants leading to the activation of the PKA pathway {33534120; 29594118}, including PRKAR1A (the most frequent alteration in c-PPNAD and i-PPNAD), PRKACA alterations (i-PPNAD and i-MAD), PDE11A (i-MAD), PDE8B (i-MAD), and 2p16 CNC2 locus alterations (PRKAR1A-wildtype c-PPNAD) {33776926; 29594118}. The discovery of a pathogenic germline MYH8 variant has also expanded the spectrum of isolated bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease in early infancy {33109698}.

Because bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease consists of multiple independent clonal proliferations {33534120; 29594118; 24283224; 33151900}, the traditional terminology of “primary bilateral macronodular adrenal cortical hyperplasia” is no longer appropriate {33534120; 29594118; 31672302}. The pathogenesis of bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease has been linked to several alterations, including germline pathogenic ARMC5 variants (most frequent cause, 25–55% of cases) {24283224; 25853793}, MEN1 (in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1) {8678766; 31482957; 32783015}, FH (in hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer) {15741255; 19509103}, APC (in familial adenomatous polyposis) {20978149; 19509103}, and somatic GNAS mosaicism (in McCune–Albright syndrome) {27512387; 32783015; 29594118}, as well as rare PDE11A {19509103; 27625633; 29594118}, PDE8B {27625633; 29594118}, and EDNRA {23754170} variants. In addition, several additional complex mechanisms including aberrant G protein–coupled receptor expression {15380809; 19719763}, dysregulation of ectopic hormone receptors or their expression {19719763; 9888584; 18209887; 31572300}, aberrant intra-adrenal ACTH production {24283225; 12843140; 29594118}, and increased PRKAR2B expression are described in a subset of cases of bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease {16772351}.

Pangenomic characterization of benign adrenocortical lesions identified an ovarian gene expression signature (FOXL2, CYP19A1, PTHLH) and global CpG hypermethylation in ARMC5-related bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease {33151900}. Specific microRNA signatures also distinguish PPNAD from cortisol-secreting adenomas {33151900}.

Macroscopic appearance

Sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease appears as randomly distributed small (< 10 mm) yellow to brown cortical nodules {31672302}. Adrenal glands show no significant enlargement in bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease. Gross examination can identify bilateral small cortical nodules (often < 5 mm) that are pigmented in PPNAD and non-pigmented in i-MAD {25425660; 29594118}. Bilateral irregular adrenal gland enlargement due to multiple cortical nodules is a characteristic finding of bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease {31672302}. In adults, the mean weight for both adrenal glands has been reported as 81 g; however, an individual adrenal gland can weigh > 120 g in individuals with bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease {25853793}. ARMC5-driven macronodular disease is associated with an increased number of nodules (> 5 nodules in 75%), leading to a significantly increased adrenal size and weight compared with ARMC5-wildtype macronodular disease {25853793}.

Histopathology

With the exception of size, sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease is indistinguishable from non-functioning adrenocortical adenomas. There can be more than one nodule per gland, and both adrenal glands may be involved. When they occur in adrenals from patients with functioning adenomas, their distinction from subcentimetre functioning adrenal cortical adenomas requires the use of steroidogenic enzyme immunohistochemistry (e.g. CYP11B2, CYP11B1, CYP17) {29542002; 1542635} (see section Adrenal cortical adenoma).

Bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD and i-MAD) is characterized by multiple small nodules (< 10 mm) that often range in size from 1 to 4 mm {31672302; 29594118}. Cortical micronodules are composed of compact (lipid-poor) adrenocortical cells and are typically located in the zona fasciculata or in the intersection of the zona reticularis and zona fasciculata {29594118}. PPNAD is distinguished from i-MAD by the presence of pigmented nodules and internodular cortical atrophy in PPNAD {33534120; 33776926; 32115357}. Rarely, myelolipomas {27875378}, adrenal cortical adenomas (> 10 mm) {21442380; 27875378}, and adrenal cortical carcinomas {22112809; 22112814} have been described in c-PPNAD.

Bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease is characterized by bilateral multiple clear cell–rich nodules, each > 10 mm with small lipid-poor cortical cells dispersed among large clear cells {33534120; 29594118; 25425660; 8008746}. Internodular cortical atrophy is identified in some adrenal glands {33776926}. Patients with McCune–Albright syndrome–related Cushing syndrome can have diffuse and/or macronodular adrenocortical hyperplasia. A subset of infants with McCune–Albright syndrome may present with adrenal glands that have a bimorphic pattern of diffuse and nodular adrenocortical hyperplasia with alternating areas of cortical hyperplasia and atrophy (also known as primary bimorphic adrenocortical disease; see section McCune–Albright syndrome) {21836496}.

Cytology

Not applicable

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline testing for pathogenic variants is considered in light of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For sporadic nodular adrenocortical disease: non-functioning benign adrenal cortical nodules measuring < 10 mm For bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease: bilateral multiple pigmented (PPNAD) or non-pigmented (i-MAD) compact cell adrenocortical nodules measuring < 10 mm (often < 5 mm); internodular cortical atrophy (in PPNAD); Cushing syndrome For bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease: bilateral marked adrenal gland enlargement with multiple benign clear cell–rich adrenocortical nodules measuring > 10 mm; Cushing syndrome

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Although adrenocortical nodular disease is a benign disorder, uncontrolled hypercortisolism due to bilateral nodular forms of adrenocortical disease (especially in infants/neonates) and additional pathologies associated with genetic tumour syndromes are important prognostic factors.
