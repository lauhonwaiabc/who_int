3.2.3.3 Papillary thyroid carcinoma

Definition

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is a malignant tumour of follicular cell derivation characterized by distinct nuclear features. PTC diagnosis requires either papillary or solid/trabecular architecture, or invasive growth in follicular-patterned tumours.

Related terminology

Acceptable: papillary thyroid adenocarcinoma; infiltrative follicular subtype of papillary thyroid carcinoma (for infiltrative follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma).

Subtype(s)

Infiltrative follicular variant of PTC; oncocytic PTC; classic PTC; encapsulated classic PTC; diffuse sclerosing PTC; solid/trabecular PTC; Warthin-like PTC; clear cell PTC; spindle cell PTC; PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like/desmoid-type stroma; tall cell PTC; hobnail PTC; columnar cell PTC

Localization

PTC can arise anywhere in the thyroid gland in its orthotropic and ectopic locations {28078619}. It can also occur in thyroglossal duct cyst and teratomas including struma ovarii {25577275; 26374222; 27704385; 32744438}.

Clinical features

PTC usually presents as a painless thyroid nodule with or without associated cervical lymphadenopathy. Dysphagia and stridor due to local compression on the trachea and oesophagus, as well as voice complaints, can be encountered in as many as 20% of patients. Rare cases of PTC will initially present as distant metastasis (usually lung or bone) {26462967; 27965276}. PTC in paediatric patients shows high rates of bilateral and multifocal disease, regional lymph node metastasis, and an increased risk of pulmonary metastasis {25231453; 25900731}.

In most cases of PTC, thyroid function tests are within normal limits. Extremely rare cases of PTC can be associated with hyperthyroidism (autonomously functioning tumour or arising in a background of toxic nodular goitre) {26462967; 27965276}. On radionuclide scanning, PTCs are typically hypofunctioning (cold) as compared with the surrounding thyroid parenchyma {11370536; 26462967; 33660459}.

A typical case of PTC will appear on thyroid ultrasound as a solid and hypoechoic nodule with microcalcifications / echogenic foci and poorly defined margins. Cystic elements can occur within a solid nodule. Doppler ultrasound may show disorganized and increased vascularity. Ultrasound examination can also confirm cervical lymph node involvement by PTC. Additional imaging modalities employed in the detection and staging of PTC include CT, MRI, and FDG PET-CT. These help to identify the extent of extrathyroidal invasion, lymphadenopathy and metastatic foci not visualized by ultrasound (mediastinum, posterior to trachea, skull base), and distant metastasis especially from non–radioiodine-avid tumours {11370536; 26462967; 32190531; 32544875; 33040848; 33660459}.

Epidemiology

PTC is the most common endocrine malignancy in both the adult population and the paediatric population. In adults, it accounts for 80–85% of all thyroid malignancies, and in children it accounts for 90% {32895503}.

The incidence of PTC has been increasing worldwide in recent decades across all age groups and in both sexes; the reported rates are related to screening and diagnostic practices {33220765; 33662332; 33662333; 33837603}. According to recent data, in the USA, the incidence rates of PTC have almost quadrupled between 1975 and 2016, with an average annual increase of 3.30%, compared with a decrease of 0.02% for follicular thyroid carcinoma and a decrease of 0.41% for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma {27418023}. The extreme example of increased incidence rates of PTC comes from the Republic of Korea, where there was a 15-fold increase between 1993 and 2011 {25672579; 27571228}. Similar but more subtle trends have been reported from Italy, France, and Australia. Approximately half of all diagnosed PTC cases measure ≤ 10 mm, and despite the increasing incidence of PTC, the mortality rate remains stable. However, some authors have also reported an increase in the number of large PTCs; this remains to be validated as a true increase in incidence {27571228; 31368412; 31934555; 33662333}.

Etiology

PTC commonly occurs as a sporadic tumour. However, familial forms are being increasingly recognized {29239041; 32192806; 32553450; 33025555}. Exposure to radiation during childhood is the most extensively studied and well-documented environmental factor in the development of PTC {21723770; 23014067; 30104523}. The factors that increase the risk of developing PTC after radiation include female sex, radiation therapy for childhood cancer, and a family history of thyroid cancer.

High levels of dietary iodine have also been implicated in the development of PTC, because of the increasing rates in the dietary iodine–sufficient regions of the world {29067516; 28513893}. Other risk factors studied in the development of PTC include exposure to specific chemical compounds found in volcanic regions, polybrominated diphenyl ethers, obesity, hormonal reproductive factors, and hereditary syndromes {26438396; 27571228; 29239041; 30104523; 32192806; 32553450; 33025555}.

As mentioned, most PTCs (> 90%) are sporadic and approximately 5–10% are familial. Familial PTCs have been divided into two groups: syndromic with a preponderance of non-thyroidal tumours such as familial adenomatous polyposis, designated as syndromic familial non-medullary thyroid carcinoma; and those with PTC as the main tumour, designated as non-syndromic familial non-medullary thyroid carcinoma {27807061; 29239041; 32192806; 33495912; 33025555} (see section Chapter 13: Genetic tumour syndromes associated with endocrine lesions and tumours).

Pathogenesis

The majority of molecular alterations seen in PTC are somatic mutations that probably occur in the early stage of pathogenesis. Interestingly, PTCs show a low somatic tumour mutation burden (~0.41 non-silent mutations/Mb), the lowest among all cancer types {25417114; 26030480; 32231639; 33799953; 33572167}.

The most frequent driver events are either point mutations or gene rearrangements, mostly involving the MAPK pathway. The most common molecular alterations in PTC involve somatic mutations in BRAF and in RAS genes (mainly NRAS) and RET fusions. BRAF p.V600E mutations are common in classic PTC and in subtypes showing a papillary architecture; this is in contrast to RAS mutations and the rare BRAF p.K601E in follicular-patterned tumours {16434896; 16007166; 25417114} (see section Invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma).

BRAF p.V600E mutations are the most common (98–99%) of all BRAF mutations in PTC. The prevalence of BRAF mutations varies with different histological subtypes of PTC and is highest in tall cell PTC (TCPTC) {25417114; 33651322; 33260892; 33224863}.

The infiltrative follicular variant of PTC shows BRAF p.V600E mutations, and some cases may show RET translocations and NTRK and ALK fusions {20526288; 25736029; 30900082; 29046324}.

BRAF mutations have been reported in oncocytic PTC (OPTC). Somatic and germline mutations of NDUFA13 (GRIM19) and RET rearrangements have also been reported in limited case series {15902486; 33901345; 30694737; 20924280; 15841082}.

BRAF p.V600E mutations have been found in some cases of Warthin-like PTC (WLPTC) {25983754; 26191315}.

Molecular studies have shown that radiation-associated solid/trabecular PTC (SPTC) commonly harbour NCOA4::RET rearrangements, as compared with non–radiation-associated cases demonstrating CCDC6::RET and ETV6::NTRK3 fusions. BRAF p.V600E mutations are rare in SPTC {11717536; 16021577; 19370421; 26784937}.

Molecular analyses have shown that the spindle cells in PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma show CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutations, and the epithelial component frequently harbours BRAF p.V600E mutations {31327063; 31938411; 32291398}.

BRAF p.V600E mutations are common in TCPTC (~80% of cases), and some cases have shown TERT promoter mutations. Loss of heterozygosity at chromosome 1 (D1S243) and the TP53 mutation have been reported in TCPTC, although not consistently {11745186; 12182066; 27008708; 8957493; 12114668}.

Mutations involving TERT promoter genes occur in approximately 10% of PTCs {25417114}. The two most common TERT promoter mutations in PTC are NC_000005.9:g.1295228G>A (TERT promoter p.C228T) and NC_000005.9:g.1295250G>A (TERT promoter p.C250T); these are considered as secondary pathogenic mutations in PTC {27184112; 28231576; 32816325; 32774993; 33240806}.

Less common molecular alterations in PTC include mutations in HRAS, KRAS, PPM1D, CHEK2, and MAP2K1 (MEK1), and fusions in BRAF, PPARG, THADA, LTK, MET, and FGFR2. NTRK fusions (NTRK1 and NTRK3) can be detected in 3–5% of PTCs {33487086; 31539879; 26784937; 31407636; 26668120; 29281951; 33923728}.

Fusions involving RET occur in PTC {10882153; 26868437; 29281951}. The prevalence of RET rearrangement in papillary carcinoma varies significantly among various geographical regions. In sporadic tumours, CCDC6::RET is the most common form of RET rearrangement (60–70%), followed by NCOA4::RET (20–30%). A strong association between radiation-induced papillary carcinoma and expression of RET rearrangement has been found. In PTC occurring among children affected by the Chernobyl nuclear accident, NCOA4::RET was found to be the most common form of rearrangement, followed by CCDC6::RET {23436219; 26668120; 33978172}. The other molecular targets involved in the pathogenesis of radiation-associated PTC involve primary chromosomal rearrangements causing gene fusions in NTRK and other oncogenes {21411301; 29165687; 33888599; 31480712}.

Alterations in copy number, gene expression, and microRNA expression have been reported in PTC {32559147; 33091426}.

Although the pathogenesis of syndromic familial non-medullary thyroid carcinoma is well known (e.g. germline mutation in APC for familial adenomatous polyposis), the genetic basis of non-syndromic familial non-medullary thyroid carcinoma remains poorly understood {33025555; 32192806; 27807061; 33495912} (see section Non-syndromic familial follicular cell–derived thyroid tumours).

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of PTC can be quite variable. Most variations in the gross appearance of PTC are related to subtypes and are discussed in their descriptions. Typically, PTC measures more than 10–15 mm, often averaging 20–30 mm, although large tumours can also occur. The tumour nodule is usually firm and white in colour, with an infiltrative appearance. Focal intratumoural calcification is a common feature; however, in some cases it may be extensive or (rarely) associated with ossification. Because of extensive sclerosis, some PTCs may grossly resemble a scar. Cystic formation of varying degrees is not uncommon in PTC; rarely, some lesions may be entirely cystic. Necrosis (in the absence of a prior needle biopsy) is not a feature of PTC and suggests transformation to high-grade follicular cell–derived carcinoma or undifferentiated carcinoma {3967175; 21455196; 10897019}.

Histopathology

The hallmark features of classic (conventional) PTC are the papillae and the nuclear features. The neoplastic papillae contain a central thin core of fibrovascular (occasionally just fibrous) tissue lined by one or (occasionally) several layers of tumour cells with crowded oval nuclei. The papillae may be long, straight, or arborizing; arranged in a parallel regimented fashion, short and stubby; or tightly packed, imparting a solid appearance to the tumour on low magnification. The cores of the papillae are usually composed of loose connective tissue and thin-walled vessels of various sizes. It is rare to encounter cases of PTC showing exclusively papillary formations. Most PTCs will show interspersed neoplastic follicles containing thick colloid and lined by cells with nuclear features similar to those of the cells lining the papillae {6660352; 3967175; 3843693; 16627919; 21455196}.

The tumour cells of PTC show a distinct set of nuclear features that can be categorized as changes in size and shape, irregularities of the membrane, and chromatin characteristics {14758551}. The nuclei are larger and more elongated or oval in shape than normal follicular cell nuclei and often show overlapping. The nuclear envelope shows marked remodelling, which is evident from irregular contours, nuclear pseudoinclusions, and/or prominent intranuclear grooves {6660352; 3843693; 3967175; 21455196}. The characteristic nuclear pseudoinclusions seen in PTC are due to deep cytoplasmic invaginations into the nucleus, often eccentrically placed, lightly acidophilic, and round in shape, with sharp outlines due to the peripheral condensation of chromatin. Intranuclear grooves are another characteristic feature of PTC. However, these can occur in other non-neoplastic lesions and neoplasms of the thyroid {3428064; 16760576; 16830963}. The other characteristic feature of PTC nuclei is an empty appearance of the nucleoplasm. This feature has been variously described as optically clear, empty, or ground glass {534382}.

The cytoplasm of the tumour cells is generally slightly eosinophilic. Mitotic figures are usually rare or absent {4043953} (excluding foci of reactive changes due to preoperative FNAB). A focal solid growth pattern can be seen in classic PTC and does not indicate a different subtype or high-grade transformation {16760576; 29239042; 32632840}.

Psammoma bodies, round calcific concretions that exhibit concentric lamination, are found in 40–50% of cases of PTC {7401638; 13663006}. They are commonly seen in the PTC subtypes with papillary growth patterns {7401638; 16627919}. True psammoma bodies are seen within the main tumour mass, in associated tumour stroma, and within lymphatic channels {7401638; 33138175}. Psammoma body–like calcifications of the colloid (pseudopsammoma bodies) can be encountered in benign conditions such as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, toxic goitre {4337189; 3473869; 9678732}, and non-papillary follicular-patterned and oncocytic follicular tumours, and they should not be considered diagnostic of PTC {12520702; 16760576; 30911738}.

Focal or extensive squamous differentiation can be encountered in PTC (in ~15–45% of cases) {3204680; 3967175}. Desmoplasia, in either the central or the peripheral zone of the tumour, is seen in PTC; encapsulated tumours will show dense sclerosis in the areas of capsular invasion {3967175}. Scattered lymphocytes are common (rarely, there is extensive infiltrate) at the invasive edges of the tumour {3967175; 21455196; 21765238; 26280320}. Cyst formation of varying proportions may occur in PTC. Most cases of cystic PTC will show readily identifiable papillary formations lined by tumour cells. However, in other instances the papillae can be few in number and lined by attenuated, flattened, benign-appearing epithelium {3967175; 20024942; 21455196}.

PTC invades the thyroid gland lymphatics, which accounts for its high incidence of regional node metastases {3967175; 21455196}. PTC can also present as multifocal tumours within the same gland {30851890; 30592330}. Multifocality of PTC can be due to intrathyroidal lymphatic spread from one tumour source or multifocal primary tumours {22434481; 29384841; 27071483; 33045819}.

Vascular (blood vessel) invasion is rare in PTC and can be identified in up to 7% of cases {12109784; 14992733; 15798466; 26482605}. It is more often seen in encapsulated PTC or TCPTC {12109784; 14992733; 15798466; 21088998; 26482605; 30352403}. Regional lymph node metastases are extremely common (≥ 80%) at the initial presentation of usual papillary carcinomas {27965276; 28405952; 29329789; 32595391}. Histological grading is of no use in papillary carcinoma because > 95% of these lesions are low-grade {20225604}. See also section High-grade follicular cell–derived non-anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, .

PTC measuring ≤ 10 mm has been termed PTC-microcarcinoma (also termed occult sclerosing carcinoma, occult papillary carcinoma, or incidental PTC). Although not endorsed, this same terminology has been applied by some authors to tumours measuring up to 15 mm {9766259; 16670460; 16005155; 19041007; 19475551; 31627846}. These lesions are common and are often encountered during autopsy or in thyroidectomies for benign thyroid disease, completion thyroidectomy, and thyroid removal during surgery for carcinoma of a neighbouring organ (laryngeal carcinoma). The incidence of PTC-microcarcinoma has been reported to be as high as 36% in meticulously sectioned thyroid specimens. Because of their small size, these lesions can be missed on gross examination. By histology, a typical case of microcarcinoma will show an infiltrative scar-like pattern with neoplastic elements either at the periphery or embedded within. Some cases are completely encapsulated by a thick fibrous capsule, which may be focally calcified. Histologically, the tumours may be totally follicular-patterned or demonstrate papillary areas as well; they can also occur as other histological subtypes of PTC {23745671; 24126547; 24356791}. Overall, the prognosis of PTC-microcarcinoma is excellent. However, lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis have been reported, almost always at presentation {14615819; 32804453; 9752488; 23745671; 27749574}. Aggressive clinical behaviour in PTC-microcarcinoma is associated with specific PTC-subtype histology, vascular invasion, location within the thyroid gland (peripheral vs central), molecular profile, and family history of PTC {10426589; 21876434; 32233872; 28289666; 31627846; 33971214}. In 2003, during a meeting held in Porto, Portugal, some authors proposed renaming papillary microcarcinomas as papillary microtumours if they lack aggressive clinicopathological features {14615819}. More recently, Aliyev et al. defined papillary microtumour on the basis of the following exclusion criteria: (1) patient aged < 19 years, (2) multiple tumour foci measuring in aggregate > 10 mm when all foci diameters are added, and (3) aggressive histological features (extrathyroidal extension, angioinvasion, tall and/or hobnail cells) {32804453}. This approach, named the updated Porto proposal, has been shown in one study to identify those papillary microcarcinomas with extremely low malignant potential {32804453}.

It is recommended that papillary microcarcinoma should not be considered a distinct subtype, because size alone is not a sufficient criterion to consider it a subtype. For this reason, the current classification requires the subtyping of papillary microcarcinomas (e.g. classic microcarcinoma).

Immunohistochemistry

By immunohistochemistry, PTC cells express thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), PAX8, thyroglobulin, and cytokeratins (pancytokeratin, AE1/AE3, CK7, and CAM5.2) {29744727}. They are negative for CK20, calcitonin, and neuroendocrine markers {29744727}. Mucicarmine positivity of varying proportions can be encountered in PTC {3277907; 18384214; 22156465; 32632840}. Immunostains for HBME1, galectin-3, CK19, and CD56 have been shown to help differentiate between PTC and benign lesions with either papillary architecture and/or atypical nuclei {12665648; 14870775; 21222366; 22888980; 27153840; 30317844}. However, none of these markers is specific and may be seen in benign lesions {16722927; 20551283; 27153840}, as well as in low-risk follicular-patterned tumours including non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features {27153840}. Immunostaining for BRAF p.V600E using the VE1 clone is highly sensitive and specific for the detection of the mutation in PTC {22997209}. To be considered positive, the staining should be cytoplasmic and diffuse throughout the tumour (≥ 90% staining) {22997209; 32358715; 31300997}. Other potential biomarker immunostains employed in the diagnosis of PTC include mutation-specific antibodies to RAS, and antibodies to RET, ALK, and pan-TRK, which are overexpressed in tumours with oncogenic fusions {29633575; 32980422; 33960437}.

Histological subtypes of PTC

Encapsulated PTC

Encapsulated PTC is defined as classic PTC enveloped by a thick fibrous capsule, which may be intact or infiltrated by tumour with or without extension into the surrounding thyroid parenchyma {21088998; 26003547; 28554832; 33261309}. It accounts for approximately 10% of all cases. The differential diagnosis includes follicular adenoma with papillary architecture and papillary hyperplastic nodule {21088998; 26003547; 28554832}.

Infiltrative follicular variant of PTC

After classic PTC, the follicular variant of PTC (FVPTC) is the second most common histological subtype of PTC {2134378; 21088998; 21593347; 27732333; 27845971}. It exclusively or almost exclusively shows a follicular growth pattern {2134378; 27845971; 7920009}. Two major forms are known: infiltrative FVPTC and invasive encapsulated FVPTC {20526288; 26003547; 21088998}. The majority of follicular PTCs are encapsulated FVPTCs, which are RAS-like tumours; these are described in section Invasive encapsulated follicular variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma . Infiltrative FVPTC is quite rare and clinically behaves like classic PTC {20526288; 26003547; 29343212}. Despite the almost exclusive follicular-patterned growth in the primary site, features that suggest that a tumour is a papillary carcinoma include clear nuclei, psammoma bodies, and sclerosis in the foci of infiltration. In addition, some of these tumours are accompanied by multicentric tumour foci of classic PTC within the same gland and cervical lymph node metastasis, with some showing papillary architecture (classic PTC features) {20526288; 21088998; 26003547; 31671409}.

Oncocytic PTC

Oncocytic changes can occur in PTCs with a follicular growth pattern as well as in those with a solid pattern. The term “OPTC” (or “pure OPTC”) is reserved for those very rare neoplasms composed of well-developed papillae (and rare follicles) all lined by oncocytic cells with nuclear features of PTC {9013831; 30215297; 30325491; 34012422}. The nuclei can be similar to those of papillary carcinoma, although in a few studied cases, large nucleoli reminiscent of those seen in oncocytes were seen. OPTC should be distinguished from TCPTC. In contrast to OPTC cells, TCPTC cells have a height that is at least three times their width and a polarized nucleus (in a basal or apical position).

Warthin-like PTC

WLPTC can present as a well-circumscribed or infiltrative tumour and shares morphological similarities with Warthin tumour of the salivary gland. It is composed of papillae lined by oncocytic cells, with the papillary cores rich in lymphocytes and plasma cells. It almost always occurs in a background of well-developed to severe chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (Hashimoto thyroiditis) {7793479; 10923082; 23661355; 33520545}. The neoplastic cells resemble the metaplastic follicular cells in the thyroiditis so the neoplasm is sometimes overlooked at low magnification. Transition to TCPTC can occur in WLPTC and it is usually found at the invasive edge of the tumour.

Clear cell PTC

Clear cell changes occur in both benign and malignant thyroid lesions and are usually associated with other usual features, such as oncocytic changes {4061729; 3957248; 9590722; 28293986}. Histologically, clear cells can grow in papillae or solid sheets and often show typical PTC nuclei {1132876; 4061729; 28293986; 33848035}.

Metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma, intrathyroidal parathyroid tumours, and clear cell medullary thyroid carcinoma are the main differential diagnoses; immunostains for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), chromogranin, calcitonin, PTH, or renal cell carcinoma markers may be required {28293986}.

Diffuse sclerosing PTC (DSPTC)

DSPTC is a rare subtype of PTC, representing only about 3% of all PTCs {25577570; 28183787; 32488657}. It is characterized by diffuse involvement of a single lobe or the entire thyroid gland with extensive lymphatic permeation, dense sclerosis, numerous psammoma bodies, and associated chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis. Tumour appears as solid nests and papillary formations with squamous metaplasia. Some cases may show a large focus of classic PTC. The lymphatics are extensively involved by psammoma bodies with or without accompanying viable tumour cells {25577570; 32134428}. This tumour is common in children and young adults, and it represented 10% of PTCs seen in children exposed to radioactive iodine after the Chernobyl accident {25351557; 27314225}. Compared with other PTCs, there is a higher incidence of potentially actionable gene rearrangements, usually involving RET, NTRK3, NTRK1, ALK, or BRAF, and a relatively lower incidence of the BRAF p.V600E mutations, which are found in about 20% of cases {37716505; 38847983}.

Solid/trabecular PTC

SPTC represents 1–3% of all adult PTCs. It is common in children and was reported in > 30% of children after the Chernobyl accident {11717536; 27882585; 29351089; 31983699; 33838489}. A diagnosis of SPTC requires the presence of a solid, trabecular, or nested growth pattern in > 50% of the tumour mass {29239042}. The stroma separating the tumour sheets and nests usually shows thin and delicate fibrovascular bands; however, some cases may show foci of dense sclerosis. A few well-formed neoplastic papillae and abortive follicular groups can occur. The tumour cells show mostly rounded nuclei with other features of PTC {29509280; 31983699; 33838489}. Because of its growth pattern, SPTC should not be mistaken for aggressive high-grade follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma. In contrast to the latter, SPTC lacks tumour necrosis and a high mitotic rate (see also section High-grade follicular cell–derived non-anaplastic thyroid carcinoma).

PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like/desmoid-type stroma

The hallmark diagnostic feature of this very rare (< 1% of all adult PTCs) subtype of PTC is characterized by abundant cellular stroma resembling nodular fasciitis, desmoid fibromatosis, or other myofibroblastic processes {16627919; 25663334; 27713418; 28794344; 32291398}. The epithelial tumour cells embedded within the stroma usually form cords, tubules, and compressed papillae, and show nuclear features of PTC. Varying degrees of squamous metaplasia can occur in these tumours. The stroma can be cellular, show myxoid change, or be densely collagenous, like the stroma seen in scar/keloid and desmoid tumours {27713418}. The stromal cells do not demonstrate nuclear atypia. PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma should be distinguished from spindle cell anaplastic carcinoma arising in PTC and the paucicellular subtype of anaplastic carcinoma. Immunohistochemical analysis should help in arriving at the correct diagnosis. The stromal cells in PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma are negative for cytokeratins and show expression of desmin, SMA, β-catenin (nuclear accumulation), and cytoplasmic SOX11 {27713418; 30223266; 31327063; 32291398}. The epithelial cells express TTF1, PAX8, and thyroglobulin {27713418; 28794344}.

Spindle cell PTC

Thyroid spindle cell lesions are rare {16830961}, and spindle cell metaplasia in PTC is even rarer. Spindle cell areas may be focal or diffuse in PTC, but this subtype is characterized by a predominance (> 50%) of spindle cells with nuclear features of PTC. Their presence does not modify the prognosis of the tumour, but a case of spindle cell transformation carrying a poor prognosis has been reported {14692805}; however, data are generally lacking. Spindle cell areas are made up of cytologically bland, elongated cells arranged in bundles, with less-pronounced nuclear clearing or pseudoinclusions than in classic PTC {32632840}. Spindle cells do not show increased mitotic activity or necrosis. They are immunoreactive for pancytokeratin and markers of thyroid lineage (thyroglobulin, TTF1, and PAX8) {21297445; 26722568; 32632840}, but negative for calcitonin/chromogranin, confirming their follicular origin. These cells are not associated with post-FNA reactive change. The main differential diagnoses include PTC with fibromatosis stroma, anaplastic carcinoma, medullary carcinoma, and spindle epithelial tumour with thymus-like elements.

Tall cell PTC

By definition, TCPTC is composed of cells whose height is at least three times their width and that show abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm with a prominent cell membrane {30565013}. The typical nuclear features of PTC, especially intranuclear pseudoinclusions, are easily found. Tall cells should represent ≥ 30% of the PTC cells to make the diagnosis of TCPTC {29020884; 29239042; 29351089; 37864666}.

TCPTC shows an exuberant and tightly knitted growth pattern of elongated follicles, giving rise to a tram-track appearance. The differential diagnosis of TCPTC includes thyroid tumours with papillary architecture and oncocytic features, such as WLPTC, OPTC, and rare cases of oncocytic carcinoma with papillary architecture. Like other differentiated follicular-derived carcinomas, these tumours express TTF1, PAX8, and thyroglobulin. A high Ki-67 labelling index (≥ 5%) has been reported in TCPTC {27807709}.

Hobnail PTC (HNPTC)

HNPTC is an aggressive subtype of PTC, which derives its name from the peculiar feature of its tumour cells having enlarged nuclei that bulge from the apical surface (hobnail appearance). This subtype is defined by the presence of > 30% hobnails and accounts for < 1% of PTCs {19956062; 23036367; 29019044; 29239042; 29351089; 32554948; 33761111}. There is a female predominance and a mean age of > 55 years at diagnosis. These tumours usually have a complex papillary or micropapillary architecture, although areas of follicular architecture may be present. Nuclear features of PTC are present; however, there is often more pleomorphism than is typically seen in classic PTC. High-grade features are often present (i.e. frequent mitoses and/or tumour necrosis), and thus many HNPTCs qualify as high-grade follicular cell–derived carcinomas {33143568}. Vascular invasion and gross extrathyroidal extension are frequently present, and there is a high rate of lymph node and distant metastases detected either at the time of initial presentation or with disease progression.

These tumours are extremely rare in pure form and should be generously sampled and carefully examined, because they are usually intermixed with areas showing progression to high-grade differentiated, poorly differentiated, and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma {24417340; 25321328; 28009606}.

HNPTC generally has an immunohistochemical profile similar to that of classic PTC; however, the Ki-67 proliferation index is often > 5% and a subset of tumours overexpress p53 {19956062}. Molecular analysis has shown that most tumours harbour BRAF p.V600E mutations {28062544; 28423545; 29179638; 26339365; 24417340}, although RET rearrangements have been reported {24417340}. Additionally, TP53 mutations, TERT promoter mutations, and PIK3CA mutations are common {28062544; 28423545; 29179638}. Additional genes with alterations reported include CTNNB1, EGFR, AKT1, ATM, ARID2, and NOTCH1 {28062544; 31811787}.

Columnar cell PTC (CCPTC)

CCPTC is a rare PTC subtype, composed of columnar cells with pale eosinophilic to clear cytoplasm, and prominent nuclear pseudostratification. Subnuclear vacuoles may be present that impart an appearance reminiscent of secretory endometrium. Nuclear clearing and grooves are evident in some tumours but subtle in others. Pseudoinclusions are generally absent. The tumour is hypercellular, with areas of crowded elongated follicles admixed with thin papillae. Morphologically, these tumours can be distinguished from TCPTC by their typical cellular features. Because of the unusual morphology of CCPTC, metastases from other sites may be considered (e.g. endometrial or colorectal primaries). Like other PTCs, these tumours are positive for PAX8, TTF1, and thyroglobulin. Additionally, as many as 55% have been shown to be positive for CDX2 {22523209; 23488912}. The prognosis of this subtype depends on its encapsulation and infiltrative status {9477108}. Infiltrative CCPTCs are usually large (often > 40 mm), invasive tumours with frequent gross extrathyroidal extension {3341287; 3940424; 21358618; 22523209; 33783022}. These tumours often have high-grade features (necrosis and increased mitotic count) and many can be classified as high-grade follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinoma {33783022}. Distant metastases are frequently present at the time of initial diagnosis or develop with disease progression.

Cytology

Smears are usually moderate to highly cellular and demonstrate complex papillary structures with fibrovascular cores, rounded papillary fragments without cores, crowded monolayered sheets showing nuclear overlapping and often moulding, and 3D groups with cellular swirls in a background of watery or ropy (chewing-gum like) colloid. The background may also contain calcific debris, macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, and stromal fragments. The individual tumour cells are enlarged and elongated or oval, with well-defined cytoplasm. The diagnostic feature of PTC is the typical nuclei, which are elongated, with thickened membranes and often a pleomorphic shape; pale, powdery, even chromatin (Pap); grooves (often delicate and more easily seen on Pap); and pseudoinclusions. The one or more nucleoli are small and eccentrically placed {6255716; 7539202; 15192947; 16951672}.

Cytopathological features of PTC subtypes

FNAB diagnosis of FVPTC can be challenging in some cases, because of the paucity of the nuclear features of PTC. FVPTC shows enlarged follicular cells arranged in monolayer sheets and microfollicles in a background of thin and thick colloid {11421549; 15192947; 27338864; 30315491}. Thin colloid can be abundant in cases containing an appreciable number of enlarged, dilated follicles {9484634; 11074641}. The intranuclear grooves in FVPTC are delicate and do not traverse the entire length of the nucleus {11074641; 9930143}, and intranuclear pseudoinclusions can be scarce, especially in liquid-based cytology {9930143; 11074641; 11391817}. Diffuse sclerosing PTC shows ball-like tissue fragments of tumour cells with or without flat sheets and papillary fragments, consisting of cells with well-demarcated squamoid dense cytoplasm and nuclear features of PTC, although inclusions and grooves may be scant. A hobnail outer margin to the tissue fragments, squamous metaplasia, and unilocular vacuoles in the cytoplasm are frequent. Psammoma bodies along with specks of coarse calcifications and calcific debris are numerous. A brisk lymphocytic infiltrate is always seen closely associated with tumour cell fragments and in the background {23781895; 24474727; 25542934; 27666595}. The diagnosis of WLPTC can be difficult in FNAB preparations because of the presence of a heavy lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate into the fibrovascular stalks and oncocytic cells of papillary tissue fragments and in the background chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis. Oncocytic tumour cells with nuclear features of PTC are present {9850656; 21645139; 31513354}. TCPTC smears show elongated cells with distinct cytoplasmic borders, granular cytoplasm, and elongated variably sized nuclei with readily identified PTC features including relatively frequent pseudoinclusions and more prominent central nucleoli {12203860; 19373912; 24021475; 30582297}. Intranuclear inclusions can be multiple and variably sized within the same nucleus, giving rise to a soap-bubble appearance {12203860}. HNPTC smears tend to be highly cellular, with a bloody background and scant colloid with micropapillary tissue fragments consisting of elongated cells with polarized eccentric nuclei and tapering cytoplasm attached to the tissue fragment, leading to a teardrop or comet-like appearance {23913779; 26339365; 31150164}. Similar single cells are present. Multiple intranuclear inclusions may also result in a soap bubble–like nuclear appearance similar to that seen in TCPTC. CCPTC can have cytopathological features that overlap with those of classic PTC, showing marked cellularity, with papillary tissue fragments covered by elongated cells with pseudostratified oval nuclei, which are more hyperchromatic and show infrequent to absent nuclear pseudoinclusions and grooves {28374549}. Necrosis and mucin are not seen in the background.

The cytopathological features of other rare subtypes of PTC have been described in the literature {8842184; 10578985; 28224716; 27666595}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Currently, molecular genetics is not required for the histopathological diagnosis of PTC but is increasingly being relied upon for clinical decision-making, particularly for surgical planning in cases with indeterminate cytology as well as for additional decision-making surrounding treatment goals (inoperable tumour with possibilities for neoadjuvant treatment) {33813868; 33300952; 33910629}. Currently, platforms for preoperative and postoperative molecular testing are either commercially available or developed in laboratories in academic medical centres. Preoperative testing determines the need for surgery. Postoperative molecular testing typically is for the purposes of subclassification and/or the documentation of targetable/actionable mutations {31821746; 33471700; 30811896}. Germline screening for genetic susceptibility is considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a carcinoma of follicular cell derivation characterized by a set of distinct nuclear features, with either papillary or solid growth or invasive features.

Staging

PTC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems, incorporating age at diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

PTC is typically associated with long-term survival; survival rates are 96% at 5 years, 93% at 10 years, and > 90% at 20 years. Overall, mortality rates for PTC are 1–6.5%, and reported overall recurrence rates are 15–35%, with tumour recurrence occurring either in the tumour bed or cervical neck lymph nodes, or rarely at distant sites {28782127; 28754228; 30192723; 31672296}.

The prognosis in PTC is influenced by the clinical extent of the disease; the 10-year survival rate is > 99% for stage I disease and drops to < 50% for stage IV disease. Poor prognostic factors in PTC include older age at diagnosis, male sex, large tumour size, and gross extrathyroidal growth. Pathological variables associated with a more guarded prognosis include vascular invasion (specifically venous invasion) {21804527; 28071623; 28754228; 28782127; 30192723; 33291668; 33633983}.

Although some studies found that non-syndromic familial non-medullary thyroid carcinoma-PTCs have a similar behaviour to sporadic PTCs {20954815}, other publications reported a higher risk of multifocality, extrathyroidal invasion, lateral lymph node involvement, and recurrence in the familial cases {22136209; 24132694} (see also section Syndromic familial follicular cell–derived thyroid tumours, and section Non-syndromic familial follicular cell–derived thyroid tumours).

Complete excision of a PTC that is confined to the thyroid gland, including central and cervical lymph nodes, is an important determinant of outcome. Incomplete surgical resection and a tumour with gross extrathyroidal extension and extranodal extension of lymph node metastases is associated with increased recurrence. Various professional societies have devised dynamic risk-stratification schemes, which take into account clinical, histopathological, and follow-up data, to care for patients with PTC {23575762; 26462967; 30561068; 31549998; 32269182; 32588461}.

Early studies suggested that BRAF p.V600E mutations are associated with an aggressive clinical course; however, this was predictive only in univariate and not multivariate analysis, and the association may fall away when adjusted for aggressive clinicopathological factors {23571594; 2357158; 25024077}. Therefore, the BRAF p.V600E mutation status of a tumour will most probably not alter clinical management; for example, BRAF-mutant and BRAF-wildtype subcentimetre papillary thyroid carcinomas are treated similarly. Some studies have also identified TERT promoter mutation alone as an independent prognostic variable that may play a role in distant metastasis. PTCs with coexistent BRAF p.V600E and TERT promoter mutations were shown to have worse disease-specific survival than those with either mutation alone {27581851; 33562809; 33776938; 33651322}. Although these molecular markers are not currently recommended for the postoperative prognostication and management of PTC {26462967}, they can provide valuable targets for those PTCs that are refractory to conventional treatment protocols.

Children with PTC have larger primary tumours, more regional nodal metastasis, and a higher rate of distant disease at presentation than adults with PTC {29030676}. In a comparative study of 190 children and 4242 adults with PTC, 75% of the children presented with regional nodal metastasis, 17% with extrathyroidal extension, and 5% with distant disease, whereas in the adults, regional nodal disease, extrathyroidal extension, and distant metastases were found in 40%, 9%, and 2%, respectively {29030676}. Despite presenting with more extensive disease than adults, children with PTC have similar recurrence rates and a much lower mortality {29030676}. The difference in mortality is particularly striking in patients with distant metastasis at presentation, where the 20-year disease-specific mortality rate is 63% in adults and 0% in children {29030676}.

Encapsulated PTC carries an excellent prognosis, with survival rates nearing 100% {3887295; 14870778; 28114862}.

The prognosis for diffuse FVPTC appears to be poor {8838340; 11956824; 22431868; 26239193; 29239042}.

OPTC can metastasize to lymph nodes, but too few cases diagnosed according to the above criteria have been systematically studied to understand their prognosis and clinical outcomes {31273353; 31343615; 31410542}.

WLPTC has an excellent long-term prognosis, similar to that of low-risk PTC {10923082; 23661355}.

The clear cell subtype has a prognosis similar to that of PTC without cell clearing.

DSPTC is often associated with extrathyroidal extension, extensive cervical lymph node involvement, and distant metastasis {27349273; 28183787; 29875289; 31605177; 32488657; 33779552; 37716505}. Although DSPTC has a lower disease-free survival than classic PTC, its mortality rate is similar to that of classic PTC {31605177}. Molecular analyses of DSPTC have revealed a high rate of potentially actionable rearrangements in RET, NTRK3, NTRK1, ALK, and BRAF {37716505; 38847983}. BRAF p.V600E mutations are reported in 20% of cases {9414185; 17464312; 25501013; 29108474; 30830105; 32488657; 37716505; 38847983}.

Higher rates of less favourable clinicopathological features have been reported in SPTC. In adults, the prognosis of SPTC appears to be less favourable than that of classic PTC {11717536; 27882585; 29351089; 29509280; 31983699; 33838489}.

Typically, TCPTCs occur at an older age than classic PTCs; are large and often associated with extrathyroidal extension; and show mitotic activity, vascular invasion, and locoregional and distant metastasis more often than classic PTCs {18925842; 27008708; 27480016; 28528434; 28529577; 28009980}. TCPTCs can follow an aggressive clinical course, with a lower 5-year disease-specific survival rate than classic PTC, and they probably represent a significant proportion of radioiodine-refractory thyroid carcinomas. This aggressive clinical course in TCPTC is probably related to its association with poor prognostic variables: old age, large tumour size, extrathyroidal extension, and high mitotic rate {37864666}. Local tumour recurrences and distant metastasis (even to unusual sites) are not uncommon in TCPTC. Even though a diagnosis of TCPTC should be reserved for tumours with TCPTC features constituting ≥ 30% of the tumour, tumours with a smaller TCPTC component (especially > 10% but < 30%) can pursue an aggressive clinical course {17696836; 26225942; 27008708; 27480016; 28009980; 28528434; 28529577; 31115855; 37864666}. Therefore, it is recommended to document focal tall cell change (< 30% of the tumour) in PTC. Dedifferentiation of TCPTC to the spindle squamous subtype of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma has been described {1722043; 21309723}.

HNPTC is an aggressive PTC subtype, with most patients demonstrating rapid disease progression and many dying of the disease within 5 years. It is important to note that hobnail cells can be seen in classic PTC with an indolent course, sometimes in > 30% of the tumour {31811787}. In contrast to HNPTC, these hobnail-like PTCs occur in younger patients, have a low mitotic rate, and lack gross extrathyroidal extension and secondary pathogenic mutations {31811787; 32632840}. A diagnosis of HNPTC without the presence of aggressive clinicopathological features (e.g. a high mitotic count) should therefore be rendered with caution {31811787}.

Most patients with CCPTC demonstrate rapid disease progression, and many die of the disease within 5 years {3341287; 3940424; 8912824; 21358618; 33783022}. The encapsulated/circumscribed form of CCPTC has limited invasion. In contrast to infiltrative CCPTC, these circumscribed tumours have been shown to have an indolent clinical course {8912824; 9477108; 21358618; 30174490} and may also lack secondary molecular alterations associated with aggressive clinical behaviour {33783022}.
