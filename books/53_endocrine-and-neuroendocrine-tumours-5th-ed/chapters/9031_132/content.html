9.0.3.1 Benign vascular tumours

Definition

Benign vascular tumours encompass a variety of haemangiomas, lymphangiomas, and vascular malformations. These tumours are characterized by proliferation of mature blood vessels or lymphatics.

Localization

Benign vascular lesions have been described in the thyroid {1119698; 11327620; 19500020; 2774858}, parathyroid {12114803; 20063187; 21286857; 30242327}, adrenal glands {30041023}, and pituitary {7411202; 8865015; 29189199; 33332201}.

Clinical features

Adrenal vascular tumours are unilateral, and patients present most commonly with abdominal, flank, or back pain (in about half of cases) and non-refractory hypertension (in about a quarter of cases) {30041023}. Sellar haemangiomas often mimic a non-functioning neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET) and present with headache, hypopituitarism, and (rarely) pituitary apoplexy {29189199}. Cranial nerve palsies can be seen when lesions involve the cavernous sinus {29189199}. Thyroid haemangiomas tend to present as an asymptomatic thyroid nodule {24701513; 29245224}. Occasionally, fast growth and calcification may mimic thyroid carcinoma {29245224}. Of the 5 reported parathyroid haemangiomas, 3 have been found incidentally during surgery for parathyroid adenoma causing hyperparathyroidism, 1 case presented with acute unilateral neck swelling and pain with swallowing {21286857}, and 1 case was a retropharyngeal tumour found incidentally on MRI {30242327}.

Epidemiology

Benign vascular tumours of endocrine organs are rare, with most cases reported in the adrenal {30041023; 1417444}, followed by the thyroid, pituitary, and parathyroid glands. Patients of any age can be affected, with congenital cases described {25130945}. In one series of 17 adrenal haemangiomas, the mean age at diagnosis was 65 years (range: 48–87 years) {30041023}. In a series of adrenal lymphangiomas, the mean age at presentation was 42 years (range: 28–56 years) {21315417}.

Etiology

Many haemangiomas and lymphangiomas are probably non-neoplastic vascular malformations. Most are solitary and sporadic. Congenital vascular lesions of the thyroid have been documented, including arteriovenous malformations and infantile haemangiomas {25130945}.

Pathogenesis

Lymphangioma / lymphatic malformations have been linked to mutations in FLT4 (VEGFR3) {12543715; 9817924} and FOXC2 {11078474}.

Macroscopic appearance

Haemangiomas tend to present grossly as red or dark-brown spongy tumours. Phleboliths may be seen. Adrenal lymphangiomas show multiloculated cysts without papillary excrescences, filled with non-viscous, clear, yellow to brown fluid {21315417}.

Histopathology

Haemangiomas of endocrine organs tend to be of cavernous, venous, and capillary types {29245224}, with other subtypes reported rarely. All haemangiomas show multiple vascular channels but differ in their calibre, wall thickness, and size. Cavernous haemangiomas are composed of multiple thin-walled, large-diameter, blood-filled channels lined by endothelial cells lacking atypia and multilayering, and separated by scant connective tissue stroma. Venous haemangiomas consist of veins of various sizes, often with thick muscular walls, frequently containing thrombi and occasionally phleboliths. Venous haemangiomas with widely dilated vessels can mimic cavernous haemangioma. Arteriovenous malformations show a variety of vessels of different sizes, including veins and arteries, and occasionally lymphatics. Veins typically predominate. Thrombosis and calcification can be seen. Lymphangiomas show dilated thin-walled vascular channels with empty-appearing lumina and occasional small, delicate valves. Adrenal haemangiomas and lymphangiomas may contain fat tissue {18376767} and in rare cases may coexist with other adrenal tumours such as cortical adenomas {31558958}. In the thyroid gland, vascular tumours must be distinguished from papillary endothelial hyperplasia secondary to FNA {2001162}, which can occasionally resemble cavernous haemangioma {1417444}, and from papillary endothelial hyperplasia within or around the capsule of thyroid nodules {18766472}. The latter has been associated with the production of lymphangiogenic factors by proliferating follicular cells {20042405}.

Histochemistry

Elastin stains may help to distinguish between arteries, which contain elastic lamina, and veins, which usually do not.

Immunohistochemistry

Endothelial cells in haemangiomas and vascular malformations show immunoreactivity for CD34, CD31, FLI1, and ERG. They are negative for D2-40, keratin, and KSHV/HHV8. Infantile haemangiomas are positive for GLUT1, which distinguishes this subtype from other haemangiomas, although positive staining can also be seen in arteriovenous malformations {21697701}. Lymphatic endothelial cells show characteristic podoplanin immunoreactivity recognized by D2-40.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a tumour-forming proliferation of vascular channels lined by endothelial cells lacking atypia.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Benign vascular lesions are cured with complete surgical resection, but completeness of removal may not be achievable in certain locations such as the pituitary or with extensive tumours. Recurrences are common in incompletely resected cases {26061261}.
