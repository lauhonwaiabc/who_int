2.1.1.4 Thyrotroph PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Thyrotroph pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is a well-differentiated PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with thyrotroph differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8272/3 Thyrotroph PitNET/adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: thyrotroph tumour.

Not recommended: TSH-producing PitNET/adenoma; TSH-secreting PitNET/adenoma; thyrotropin-secreting PitNET/adenoma; thyrotrope PitNET/adenoma; thyrotroph cell PitNET/adenoma; thyrotropinoma; TSHoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Most thyrotroph tumours present as macrotumours, commonly invading inferolaterally into the cavernous sinuses. Ectopic cases have been reported in the nasopharynx and sphenoidal sinus {8875757; 25069990; 31704334}.

Clinical features

Thyrotroph tumours may be associated with hyperthyroidism (central form), hypothyroidism, or (rarely) euthyroidism {22697380}. A subset of tumours may present as non-functioning tumours with signs and symptoms of mass effect, including headaches and visual deficits {31668712}.

Biochemical investigations

Primary hyperthyroidism is characteristically associated with undetectable serum TSH levels, in contrast to central hyperthyroidism associated with a thyrotroph PitNET/adenoma and non-suppressible serum TSH excess called syndrome of inappropriate secretion of TSH (SITSH). In cases of longstanding primary hypothyroidism, thyrotroph hyperplasia causing increased serum TSH excess is most common; however, rare examples of a thyrotroph PitNET/adenoma may also arise within this clinical setting. An increased ratio of serum α-subunit to TSH is also supportive of a thyrotroph tumour.

Neuroimaging

Typical neuroimaging findings include a large, invasive, contrast-enhancing tumour, often growing downward through the sellar floor and into the clivus, as well as laterally into the cavernous sinuses {33329389}.

Epidemiology

Thyrotroph tumours are among the rarest pituitary tumours, accounting for < 1% of all PitNETs/adenomas {22697380; 22869255; 28566440; 31175617}. Despite some variability among series, there is no clear male or female predominance {22869255; 25237847; 31400861}.

Etiology

In most cases, the etiology of these tumours is unknown, although rare examples are thought to result from chronic stimulatory feedback associated with longstanding hypothyroidism {934699; 10417962; 15640908; 32080117}. There have also been rare reports of thyrotroph tumours encountered in tumour predisposition syndromes, such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 and familial isolated pituitary adenoma (FIPA) due to germline AIP mutation {9425388; 11765049; 33543431}.

Pathogenesis

Like other PIT1-lineage tumours, thyrotroph PitNETs were recently shown to have an epigenetic signature associated with diffuse DNA hypomethylation, transposable element expression, and chromosomal instability; in terms of their transcriptome, they clustered more closely with plurihormonal PIT1-lineage tumours than with somatotroph tumours {31883967}.

Macroscopic appearance

The vast majority of thyrotroph tumours are large and invasive but have no distinctive macroscopic features otherwise. Variable degrees of fibrosis and calcification are seen in a subset. One series reported 74% of tumours being firm elastic or hard in consistency and 82% being macrotumours {25237847}.

Histopathology

Thyrotroph tumours generally grow in diffuse and sinusoidal patterns, with variable fibrosis and (occasionally) psammomatous calcification. Tumour cells are spindled to polygonal with chromophobic cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders. Cases associated with hypothyroidism occasionally show thyrotroph hyperplasia in the adjacent non-neoplastic anterior pituitary.

Immunohistochemistry

There is diffuse PIT1 expression, and TSH positivity is extensive. The fraction of TSH-positive tumour cells often varies from case to case and regionally within the same tumour. There is variable staining for α-subunit and GATA3, as well as extensive SSTR2A expression {24182563; 28434012; 30390035; 32416325}.

Cytology

Similar features to other PitNETs

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no molecular tests required to diagnose these tumours. Germline screening for inherited tumour syndromes should be considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a PitNET with diffuse PIT1 positivity; extensive TSH positivity; absence of other pituitary hormone staining.

Desirable: immunoreactivity for GATA3 and α-subunit.

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour extension based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}.

There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

Like for other large PitNETs/adenomas, surgery plays an important therapeutic role. Gross total resection was achievable in only about 45% in one series, because of the invasive tendencies of these tumours; however, all TSH-secreting PitNETs were lumped together in this study, so it is unclear if this is true in the pure thyrotroph tumours {31668712}. Somatostatin analogues are effective in reducing TSH secretion in > 90% of patients, although detectable tumour shrinkage is seen in only roughly one third of cases {12675505; 19945025; 24783044}.
