7.0.3.1 Composite phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas

Definition

Composite phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas are tumours with combined features of phaeochromocytoma or paraganglioma and a developmentally related neurogenic tumour such as ganglioneuroma, ganglioneuroblastoma, neuroblastoma, schwannoma, neurofibroma, or malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST).

Localization

Composite phaeochromocytoma is more common in the right adrenal gland, with a right-to-left ratio of 1.4:1. Four bilateral tumours have been reported, accounting for 3.6% of all composite phaeochromocytomas. These comprise 3 composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuromas {11904334; 8338194; 12114771} and a composite phaeochromocytoma-MPNST {8669538}; 3 of these 4 patients had neurofibromatosis type 1 {8669538; 8338194; 12114771}.

Composite paragangliomas are most often found in the retroperitoneum (accounting for nearly half of the cases) {28752484; 27704704; 23587063; 23001299; 22687375; 20829582; 20409235; 19889235; 19598001; 17013491; 2721353; 21092109; 19251979; 32357642; 1300605} and the urinary bladder {19829717; 16143037; 14708645; 12114785; 30263028; 31966391}. Other reported sites include the spinal cord region {15892009; 6291307; 20515359; 5026551; 23532344}, sacrum {25855915}, mediastinum {15806520}, and carotid body {30222023}.

Clinical features

A quarter of composite phaeochromocytomas are incidental findings on radiological investigations (CT or MRI). Nevertheless, > 80% of composite phaeochromocytomas show increased catecholamine metabolites in serum or urine. About three quarters of patients with composite phaeochromocytomas are symptomatic, most often with signs and symptoms of catecholamine excess, such as paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, headache, or sweating {22440998; 20350228}. There are also patients with abdominal pain due to mass effect or haemorrhage of the tumour, and patients presenting with a palpable mass {24641930}. About 10% of patients have WDHA syndrome (watery diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, and achlorhydria) due to VIP production {22660994; 22440998; 20350228; 20216072; 16027147}. All cases were phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroma with secretion of VIP from the ganglioneuroma component.

More than half of the composite paragangliomas arising from the retroperitoneum or urinary bladder are associated with signs caused by catecholamine excess, such as increased catecholamine metabolites in urine, and paroxysmal hypertension, palpitation, headache, etc. In contrast, composite paragangliomas arising from the carotid body, spinal cord region, or sacrum are rarely hormone-producing. Patients with composite paraganglioma of the urinary bladder commonly have urinary symptoms including haematuria, dysuria, and micturition-induced hypertensive crises {19829717; 16143037; 14708645; 12114785; 30263028; 31966391}. A quarter of composite paragangliomas are incidental findings on radiological investigations (CT or MRI). One patient presented with WDHA syndrome due to VIP production {23587063}.

Epidemiology

Composite phaeochromocytomas are uncommon, with > 130 cases reported in the literature. Approximately 75% of the composite tumours are phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuromas, which has a female predilection (M:F ratio: 1:1.5). They are more commonly seen in the sixth decade of life, with a median age of 53 years (range: 12–86 years) {22440998; 28752484}. The other types are composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroblastoma and composite phaeochromocytoma-neuroblastoma, each accounting for slightly more than 10% of cases of composite phaeochromocytoma. Both have a male predilection (M:F ratios: 2:1 and 3:1, respectively). Composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroblastoma occurs in patients with a median age of 37 years (range: 14–73 years) {3922614; 14506975}, whereas composite phaeochromocytoma-neuroblastoma is seen in patients with a median age of 42 years (range: 5–59 years) {28864906; 11257670}. Rarely, composite phaeochromocytoma-MPNST is noted; reported in 6 patients with a median age of 39 years (range: 13–48 years) {17727489; 3468715} and no sex predilection. In addition, a composite phaeochromocytoma–neuroendocrine carcinoma has been reported {10583935}.

Composite paragangliomas are rare, with 35 cases reported. There is no sex predilection. More than 75% are composite paraganglioma-ganglioneuromas, which are usually seen in adult patients (median age: 51 years, range: 29–81 years). Half of the composite paraganglioma-neuroblastomas occurred in the paediatric age group {19251979}. The 3 cases of composite paraganglioma-ganglioneuroblastoma were in adult patients {31966391; 32357642; 23532344}.

Etiology

Most composite phaeochromocytomas are sporadic, with no known predisposing factors. In approximately 20% of tumours there are underlying hereditary syndromes. The most common genetic predisposition is neurofibromatosis type 1, accounting for approximately 70% of the hereditary cases {33651160}. Other than this, 3 composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuromas and 1 composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroblastoma were noted in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A {28760150; 24641930; 8990146; 9568520}. In addition, 3 composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuromas associated with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {19864235; 18031321; 16483185} and 1 associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B have been reported {18706367}.

Most composite paragangliomas are sporadic, with no known predisposing factors. However, 2 retroperitoneal composite paragangliomas associated with neurofibromatosis {28752484; 22687375} have been reported, as well as 1 associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B {27704704} and 1 with SDHB deletion {19251979}.

Pathogenesis

Because of the rarity of composite phaeochromocytoma, the pathogenesis is largely unknown. However, it is known that both composing tumour types ultimately derive from neural crest progenitor cells, for which developmental plasticity has been shown {28684471; 33833454}.

Both components of composite paraganglioma also have a common embryogenic origin in the neural crest.

Macroscopic appearance

The phaeochromocytoma component is brown with variable haemorrhage, fibrosis, central degenerative changes, or cystic change. The ganglioneuroma component is often white and firm, and the ganglioneuroblastoma or neuroblastoma components may reveal necrotic or haemorrhagic areas {12114771; 20216072}.

The paraganglioma component is brown, haemorrhagic, and cystic, whereas the neural component is white and solid {21092109; 30222023}.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of composite phaeochromocytoma requires the presence of well-developed cytological and architectural features of both phaeochromocytoma and another neuroblastic tumour type. A reasonable guide is that ≥ 5% of the tumour should be composed of each tumour type, so the presence of scattered neuron-like cells or a little Schwannian stroma is not sufficient.

The phaeochromocytoma component is composed of chromaffin tumour cells with basophilic granular cytoplasm. The most common non-phaeochromocytoma component is ganglioneuroma, characterized by mature ganglion cells in a fibrillary Schwannian stroma–rich background {25984524}. The ganglion cells stain positive for NFP (which is negative in chromaffin cells). VIP immunohistochemical staining may be identified in the ganglioneuroma component in patients with WDHA syndrome {20350228; 22440998}. Schwann cells stain for S100.

The other neurogenic components are neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma, or MPNST. A neuroblastoma component shows neuropil-type stroma with small blue round cells. This component is positive for PGP9.5 (phaeochromocytoma is negative for PGP9.5) {28864906}. A ganglioneuroblastoma component shows a mixture of immature and maturing neurons, neuropil, and Schwann cells. No MYCN amplification was detected in composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroblastoma {19864235; 27980262}. An MPNST component contains a malignant spindle cell component with nuclear hyperchromasia, which forms fascicles in a myxoid stroma and shows frequent mitotic figures. Compact cellular nodules are also present. The spindle cells are positive for S100. Rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation has been identified {19561238}.

The diagnosis of composite paraganglioma requires the presence of well-developed cytological and architectural features of both paraganglioma and another neuroblastic tumour type. The presence of a few isolated ganglion cells or a small amount of Schwannian stroma in an otherwise typical paraganglioma does not warrant classification as a composite tumour. Again, a reasonable guide is that ≥ 5% of the tumour should be composed of each tumour type. The most common non-paraganglioma component is ganglioneuroma, followed by neuroblastoma {30263028; 21092109; 19251979}. Paraganglioma-ganglioneuroblastoma has been reported {31966391; 32357642}. A case with melanin pigmentation has been reported {14708645}. VIP immunohistochemical staining may be identified in the ganglioneuroma component {2721353}.

Cytology

FNA has been reported in a composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroma showing the expected mixture of phaeochromocytoma (cells with granular cytoplasm) and ganglioneuroma (spindle cells with elongated nuclei and ganglion-like cells with Nissl granules) {3468715}.

FNA has been reported in a retroperitoneal tumour showing the expected mixture of paraganglioma (cells with granular cytoplasm) and ganglioneuroma (spindle cells with elongated nuclei and ganglion-like cells with Nissl granules) {2721353}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Most cases of composite phaeochromocytoma are sporadic, but clinical or molecular screening for NF1 {33651160}, RET {28760150; 24641930; 8990146; 9568520; 18706367}, VHL {18706367}, or MAX {32508744} mutations may be appropriate in selected cases.

Most cases of composite paraganglioma are also sporadic, but clinical or molecular screening for NF1, RET, or SDH gene mutations may be appropriate in selected cases {28752484; 22687375; 30222023; 19251979; 27704704}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Composite phaeochromocytoma

Essential: the presence of ≥ 5% of histologically recognizable components of phaeochromocytoma and neurogenic tumour(s) such as ganglioneuroma, ganglioneuroblastoma, neuroblastoma, or MPNST.

Composite paraganglioma

Essential: the presence of ≥ 5% of histologically recognizable components of paraganglioma and neuroblastic tumour(s) such as ganglioneuroma, ganglioneuroblastoma, or neuroblastoma.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of composite phaeochromocytoma is dependent on the highest-grade component present. Approximately 13% of composite phaeochromocytomas metastasize. Overall, 60% of these patients died of the disease, with a median survival time of 2 years. In composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroma, 6% of cases metastasize, whereas in composite phaeochromocytoma-ganglioneuroblastoma/neuroblastoma, metastases are more common and present in a quarter of cases. Composite phaeochromocytoma-MPNSTs are high risk and often metastatic at presentation. Common sites of metastases include lymph nodes {12114771; 17727489}, the retroperitoneum {3194994; 17727489}, and the contralateral adrenal gland {12114771}. Distant metastases have been reported in the liver {12114771; 3667875}, bones {11257670; 8042583; 17727489}, and lung {11257670; 17727489}.

Approximately 10% of composite paragangliomas metastasize. To date, all metastatic tumours have arisen in male patients with composite paraganglioma-neuroblastoma {30263028; 21092109}. The sites of metastases were lymph nodes, bones, liver, lung, and skin.
