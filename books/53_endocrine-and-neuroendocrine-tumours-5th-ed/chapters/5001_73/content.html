5.0.0.1 Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction

Terminology

Like other neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), pancreatic NENs (PanNENs) express synaptophysin and usually chromogranin A. PanNENs are malignant and are separated into well-differentiated epithelial NENs, called pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), and poorly differentiated epithelial NENs, designated pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (PanNECs), which can be either small cell or large cell type. PanNETs measuring < 5 mm, previously called neuroendocrine microadenomas, are now called neuroendocrine microtumours, in recognition that, very rarely, they may exhibit lymph node metastases. Neoplasms composed of a neuroendocrine component combined with a non-neuroendocrine component are called mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs).

Epidemiology

The relative frequency of NENs among all pancreatic neoplasms is 2–5%, and their estimated incidence is < 1 case per 100 000 person-years {19997980; 18508996}. Autopsy studies that consider microtumours show a higher incidence {2070707} (see section Non-functioning (non-syndromic) pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours). PanNETs make up 11% of all well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) throughout the body {18515795; 19997980}. Risk factors include a family history of NENs, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, and diabetes {26487581}.

Clinical features

PanNETs show no substantial sex difference and occur over a wide age range, with the highest incidence found in patients aged 30–60 years. PanNECs tend to affect older patients, overlapping with the prevalence of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. The incidence of PanNETs has steadily increased over the past 40 years {19997980; 21719641; 26562558}, probably as a result of the increasing use of cross-sectional abdominal imaging.

PanNETs have traditionally been separated on the basis of hormonally mediated symptoms, into functioning and non-functioning types. Functioning PanNETs present with characteristic hormonal paraneoplastic syndromes, and the names of the corresponding neoplasms reflect this clinical functionality, such as insulinoma, glucagonoma, gastrinoma, and VIPoma. Less common functioning PanNETs produce syndromes due to serotonin, ACTH, GHRH, PTHrP, or CCK secretion {22442262; 23514309; 24905065}. Clinically non-functioning PanNETs (NF-PanNETs) may also produce various peptide hormones and bioamines, such as PP, somatostatin, and chromogranins, which either are secreted at levels insufficient to cause symptoms or by themselves do not give rise to a clinical syndrome. These substances can be detected in serum assays or by immunohistochemistry. In some cases, production of these hormones may explain clinical symptoms that are not classic for the defined functioning PanNET types. Thus, “functionality” is somewhat difficult to define precisely. To standardize the approach, the current WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours retains the notion of clinical functionality to define the functioning PanNET types, with presenting symptoms aligning with those well recognized to reflect the abnormal secretion of specific hormones by the neoplasm.

Patients with NF-PanNETs usually have nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal pain or nausea. PanNETs in the head of the pancreas may cause jaundice due to biliary compression. In ≥ 15% of patients, NF-PanNETs are identified incidentally on abdominal imaging studies performed for other reasons {6258500}. Finally, 40% of patients are asymptomatic until they develop metastases, and even then the symptoms may be relatively mild. Like other digestive NENs, larger PanNETs have a higher risk of metastasis {32839579}.

In the past, functioning PanNETs accounted for 60–85% of all PanNETs, with insulinomas being the most frequent type (accounting for as many as 70% of cases), followed by gastrinomas, glucagonomas, VIPomas, and other functioning PanNETs. Recent data show that NF-PanNETs outnumber functioning NETs and account for > 60% of all cases {22261872; 26742109}.

PanNECs and MiNENs are only exceptionally associated with a hormonal syndrome and present similarly to exocrine carcinomas of the pancreas, including commonly with manifestations of metastatic disease.

Genetic syndromes

In 10–20% of cases, PanNETs arise in the context of hereditary syndromes, including (in order of decreasing frequency) multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome, tuberous sclerosis, neurofibromatosis type 1, glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasm syndrome (Mahvash disease), MAFA-related familial insulinomatosis, Lynch syndrome, and familial atypical mole–malignant melanoma syndrome (see the dedicated chapter on inherited tumour syndromes) {25695890; 26365060}. Germline mutations in DNA repair genes (MUTYH, CHEK2, and BRCA2) have been described in patients with apparently sporadic PanNETs {22251937; 28199314; 33459926}. PanNECs and MiNENs do not show these associations {28199314}.

Updated WHO classification

PanNENs are classified according to their degree of differentiation (well-differentiated vs poorly differentiated), and they are graded according to the mitotic rate and Ki-67 labelling index as grade 1 (G1), grade 2 (G2), or grade 3 (G3) (see #31938Table 7.01). Despite sharing neuroendocrine differentiation, the well-differentiated and poorly differentiated groups are not closely related and represent genetically distinct categories of neoplasms. The 2010 WHO classification [[Bosman FT, Carneiro F, Hruban RH, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of the digestive system. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2010. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/13.]], using the parameters above, effectively stratified groups predictive of patient survival. In the years since its publication, however, it became clear that some PanNENs with the histological features of PanNET (i.e. well differentiated) have a Ki-67 proliferation index > 20%. This is particularly common in liver metastases developing in the course of the disease. According to the 2010 WHO criteria, these histologically bland but more proliferative tumours would be classified as PanNECs. Although these tumours have a worse prognosis than G2 PanNETs, their behaviour is less aggressive than that of PanNECs {25723112; 26113608}. They show features associated with PanNETs (i.e. hormone expression, and association with hormonal and genetic syndromes) and usually lack the genetic abnormalities found in poorly differentiated PanNENs (e.g. changes in expression and mutation of TP53 and RB1) {22251937; 24503751}. They can also retain the mutations characteristic of PanNETs (e.g. MEN1, DAXX, or ATRX). Finally, histological progression from G1 or G2 PanNETs to high-grade epithelial NENs was observed, strongly suggesting that the high-grade neoplasms are closely related to their lower-grade predecessors. From a clinical perspective, patients with high-grade PanNENs as defined by the 2010 WHO classification whose neoplasms had a Ki-67 proliferation index < 55% had a lower response rate but better survival than patients with neoplasms that had a Ki-67 proliferation index > 55% when treated with first-line platinum-based chemotherapy as used for the treatment of poorly differentiated epithelial NENs {24771552}. Therefore, a novel tumour category, called G3 PanNET, was included in the 2017 WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs. These tumours retain a well-differentiated histological pattern and have a Ki-67 proliferation index > 20% and usually (but not always) < 55% (see #31939Table 7.02). In the fifth-edition WHO classification of digestive system tumours, the G3 designation applied to PanNECs was dropped, because these neoplasms are high-grade by definition [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]]. This updated classification has been validated on a large surgical series {30300897}.

The phenomenon of grade progression in PanNETs is increasingly recognized and is associated with poor patient outcome {31658459}. Higher-grade regions can occur within primary PanNETs, between the primary and the metastases, or among different metastases. In essentially all reported case, the higher-grade (G2 or G3) component is well differentiated. There is a possibility that bona fide poorly differentiated PanNECs could arise through progression of a well-differentiated PanNET, but this phenomenon has yet to be convincingly reported. Instead, PanNECs often have associated exocrine precursors (e.g. pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia) or a component of exocrine carcinomas.

No upper limit has been defined for the mitotic count or the Ki-67 proliferation index of a G3 PanNET, but the lower limits, respectively, are > 20 mitoses/2 mm2 or Ki-67 > 20%, by definition. In many G3 PanNETs, it is only the Ki-67 index that exceeds this threshold. The Ki-67 proliferation index of PanNECs is by definition > 20%, and most also have > 20 mitoses/2 mm2. Primary G3 PanNETs have intact TP53 and RB1 in 96% and > 99% of cases, respectively. Occasionally, however, G3 PanNETs can develop a TP53 mutation during progression {29596894}.

PanNECs have poorly differentiated histological features (of either small cell or large cell type), often show p53 nuclear accumulation, lose RB1 expression, lack insulin gene enhancer protein ISL1 expression, and retain expression of DAXX and ATRX {22251937; 23503646}. Genetically, PanNECs may show similarities to ductal adenocarcinomas, because PanNECs frequently harbour KRAS mutations, which characterize ductal adenocarcinomas {27262570; 29596894}. In the absence of non-neuroendocrine tumour morphology, they may also express ductal lineage markers such as EMA (MUC1) and CEA {29596894}.

Studies in large PanNET cohorts have shown that patients with G2 tumours have a significantly higher risk of progression when 5% is used as the Ki-67 proliferation index cut-off point, instead of 3% {8912819; 20305616; 22525418}. However, the evidence of differences in clinical management based on this higher cut-off point is insufficient to justify changing it at this time. It is important to recognize that Ki-67 is a continuous variable in PanNETs, with higher rates predicting an increasingly poor prognosis regardless of the cut-off points used to place tumours into large groups. Therefore, establishing a single cut-off point to dichotomize the prognosis is inherently problematic. Reporting the actual Ki-67 index in the pathology report is critical for clinical management.

The terminology for mixed neoplasms was changed in the 2017 (fourth) edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs and the 2019 (fifth) edition of the WHO classification of digestive system tumours, from mixed adenoneuroendocrine carcinoma (MANEC) to MiNEN, which is retained in the current edition. There are two reasons for this change. First, the complex neoplasms that are usually poorly differentiated in both components may occasionally be well differentiated; and second, they may contain a non-neuroendocrine component other than adenocarcinoma. The term “neuroendocrine” is the only one maintained from the previous terminology, because the neuroendocrine component is the constant of these mixed tumours.

The category of hyperplastic and preneoplastic lesions included in the 2010 (fourth) edition of the WHO classification of digestive system tumours was abolished in the 2017 (fourth) edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs because PanNEN precursor changes have not been clearly identified in association with sporadic neoplasms. They are described in the settings of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {22461457}, and glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia {24718881; 25695890; 26365060}.

Definition of PanNET

PanNET is a well-differentiated NEN of low, intermediate, or high grade, composed of cells with minimal to moderate atypia, displaying organoid patterns, often lacking necrosis, and expressing general markers of neuroendocrine differentiation (diffuse/intense chromogranin A and synaptophysin staining) and hormones (usually intense but not necessarily diffuse), either orthotopic or ectopic to the pancreas. Immunoexpression of keratins is usually present and can help distinguish PanNETs from paragangliomas. Most PanNETs express peptide hormones or bioamines in highly variable proportions. Functioning PanNETs generally demonstrate expression of the hormone responsible for the clinical syndrome. Although routine staining of NF-PanNETs for hormones is not mandated, it can be helpful in some clinical situations to explain less specific hormone-mediated symptoms. Based on their proliferative activity, PanNETs are graded as G1 (< 2 mitoses/2 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index < 3%), G2 (2–20 mitoses/2 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index of 3–20%), or G3 (> 20 mitoses/2 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index > 20%) (see #31939Table 7.02). In cases associated with a clinical hormonal syndrome, PanNETs are categorized as insulinoma, glucagonoma, somatostatinoma, gastrinoma, VIPoma, serotonin-producing tumour (with or without carcinoid syndrome), ACTH-producing PanNET, etc.

Definition of PanNEC

PanNEC is a poorly differentiated, high-grade epithelial NEN, composed of highly atypical small cells or intermediate to large cells expressing markers of neuroendocrine differentiation (diffuse/faint synaptophysin staining as well as faint/focal chromogranin A staining) and usually no hormones. PanNECs lack expression of acinar cell markers such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, or carboxyl ester hydrolase (detected by BCL10). Based on their proliferative activity (> 20 mitoses/2 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index > 20%), PanNECs are considered high-grade neoplasms but are not specifically graded (see #31939Table 7.02).

Definition of MiNEN

MiNEN is not a specific diagnostic entity; rather, it is a conceptual category to encompass mixed neoplasms with a neuroendocrine component combined with a non-neuroendocrine component (typically ductal adenocarcinoma or acinar cell carcinoma). Each component should be histologically and immunohistochemically recognizable and account for ≥ 30% of the tumour cell population. This admittedly arbitrary threshold is intended to avoid including exocrine carcinomas with scattered neuroendocrine cells, or NENs with scattered exocrine marker expression, in the MiNEN category, on the basis that this minor extent of divergent differentiation is not thought to have an impact on the clinical behaviour of the neoplasm. When a minor cell population does not exceed the 30% threshold, its presence can be mentioned but does not affect the diagnostic categorization. One potential exception is the occurrence of a minor component of small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) within an exocrine carcinoma. Given the potential for SCNEC to dictate the prognosis, even when present only focally, it is suggested that this component be mentioned whenever it is recognized.

The requirement that both components are morphologically recognizable excludes some carcinomas from the MiNEN category. For instance, many mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas have relatively uniform morphology, and the presence of both acinar and neuroendocrine differentiation can only be demonstrated by immunohistochemistry. Only rarely do mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas demonstrate morphologically separate components, qualifying as MiNENs.

Both components of MiNENs are usually high-grade (G3), but occasionally one or both components may be G1 or G2. Therefore, each component should be graded on the basis of the respective grading system for that component. Of particular importance is the grading of the neuroendocrine component, because it seems that the Ki-67 proliferation index of this component drives the prognosis of the MiNEN {29592868}. Because MiNEN is a conceptual category rather than a discrete entity, individual diagnoses indicating the specific cellular components should be applied (i.e. mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma, mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma, etc.; see section Mixed ductal adenocarcinoma–neuroendocrine neoplasms, and section Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma).

Reporting of PanNENs

PanNENs could be NET or neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) based on histology and immunohistochemistry. PanNETs are graded as G1, G2, or G3. PanNECs are defined as small cell or large cell type. Hormone expression assessment by immunohistochemistry should be provided upon clinical request. See the sections that follow for the tumour type–specific requirements.

Cytology

The features of PanNET are similar to those of NETs of other organs. Smears usually show a high cellularity, with plentiful dispersed cells and a variable number of loosely cohesive sheets and tissue fragments consisting of thin branching capillaries associated with similar cells. The cells are round or (often) plasmacytoid with low N:C ratios and abundant pale granular cytoplasm, which may contain granules (Giemsa stain), and they have uniform round to oval nuclei with coarse, granular chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli (salt-and-pepper nuclei). There may be a granular background, and acinar or pseudorosette architecture may be present. Necrosis may suggest a higher-grade NET, but distinction requires cell-block or biopsy material. Mitotic figures are usually not identified. Grading of NF-PanNETs in cytological specimens can underestimate the true grade because of limited material {25044931; 25493252; 29044913}.

Molecular genetics

MEN1 is somatically inactivated in about 40% of PanNETs. This gene codes for menin, a tumour suppressor protein with diverse functions. In addition, approximately 45% of PanNETs showed mutation in either DAXX or ATRX. The function of DAXX and ATRX proteins is to maintain chromatin remodelling at telomeric and pericentromeric regions. DAXX and ATRX mutations are strongly associated with the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres pathway for telomere maintenance, as well as with chromosomal instability. DAXX and ATRX mutations and the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres status are strong indicators of a poor prognosis in PanNET {34269919}. About 15% of PanNETs have alterations in mTOR pathway genes, such as TSC2 and PTEN. The mTOR pathway is important because it is a potential therapeutic target. There are also PanNETs with HIF1A and VHL alterations {19690016; 21252315; 25441311; 25695890; 26303708; 26365060; 26413978; 28199314}. Germline alterations affecting MEN1, VHL, NF1, GCGR, and MAFA are found in PanNETs associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia, and insulinomatosis, respectively {29339498}. Some of these PanNETs have distinctive morphologies. Mutations in VHL result in dysregulation of the HIF pathway, and PanNETs that arise in patients with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome often have clear cell morphology {11342771}. Mutations in GCGR alter glucagon receptor signalling and lead to glucagon cell hyperplasia and subsequent neoplasia, but the mechanism through which these alterations occur is not yet clear {25695890}. Missense mutations of MAFA in familial insulinomatosis cause the development of familial multifocal trabecular insulin-producing PanNETs {29339498}. PanNETs usually lack mutations in the genes most commonly altered in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, such as KRAS, TP53, and SMAD4. Mutations in RB1 are nearly absent in PanNETs. The mutation status of PanNETs does not relate to PanNET Ki-67 grades. By contrast, recent evidence indicates that expression profiling and LINE1 methylation status of PanNETs correlate with Ki-67 grade, the switch from G1/G2 to G3 associating with profound transcriptome changes {33922803}.

In PanNECs, only somatic genetic alterations have been identified. These include mutations of cell-cycle regulatory genes, such as TP53, RB1, and CDKN2A, whereas mutations in chromatin-remodelling genes, such as MEN1, ATRX, and DAXX, do not occur {22251937; 29596894}. In addition, PanNECs may show KRAS mutations identical to those that occur in conventional ductal adenocarcinomas {22251937; 28455360; 29596894}, suggesting a relationship with ductal neoplasia that also accounts for mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma, a type of MiNEN.

The molecular genetic features of MiNENs have not been thoroughly characterized but appear to correlate with each specific neoplastic component.

Staging (TNM)

Epithelial PanNEN staging, which is presented in section TNM staging of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours , follows the eighth (2017) editions of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]] and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging manual [[Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]], which rely on macroscopic assessment of site, tumour size, and metastases. This approach largely corresponds to that of the European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) classification {22525418}.

Prognosis and prediction

In contrast to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and NECs, PanNETs are low-grade malignancies with 10-year survival rates of about 40–50% {16030115; 27663587; 30081149}. Surgical resection markedly improves survival rates (see section Non-functioning (non-syndromic) pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours). In contrast, patients with PanNECs, which are fast-growing, rarely survive 1 year {24503751}. The prognosis of MiNENs is also usually poor, because most cases include a component of poorly differentiated NEC.

The WHO classification (PanNET vs PanNEC) and grading scheme for PanNETs (G1–G3) are highly useful for predicting prognosis, both for primary neoplasms and for metastases. Given the potential for grade progression in PanNETs, reassessment of the grade of metastases is important to predict the time to progression. The criteria that predict the risk of tumour progression of PanNENs are summarized in the TNM staging table at the beginning of this volume. The listed criteria (in particular the Ki-67 index {8912819; 22525418}) significantly correlate with the behaviour of a given epithelial PanNEN {18059224}. However, predicting the outcome of a PanNET often requires long clinical follow-up because metastasis may occur many years after resection of the primary.

Prognostic immunohistochemical markers other than Ki-67 (e.g. CK19, KIT, CD99, CD44, and p27) have been reported {15316313; 18059224} (see also section Non-functioning (non-syndromic) pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours), but these have not been validated in large cohorts and are not in routine diagnostic use. Recent genetic studies in PanNETs showed that cases with DAXX or ATRX mutations, particularly G2 PanNETs, had a worse clinical outcome than those with wildtype DAXX and ATRX {27407094; 27663587; 28199314; 28371511; 28591701}. Furthermore, primary PanNETs with loss of H3 p.K36me3, loss of ARID1A expression, and/or CDKN2A deletion reportedly have a poorer prognosis {29486199}, but further investigations are required in order to confirm the clinical relevance of these genetic abnormalities.
