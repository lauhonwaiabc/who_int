7.0.1.1A Adrenal medulla and extra-adrenal paraganglia tumours: Introduction

Two groups of tumours with complementary but overlapping epidemiology occur in the adrenal medulla and extra-adrenal paraganglia: neuroblastic tumours (mostly neuroblastoma and ganglioneuroblastomas), usually in infants and children; and paragangliomas, usually in adults. Phaeochromocytomas are part of the paraganglioma family of tumours and can therefore be addressed as adrenal (sympathetic) or intra-adrenal paraganglioma. Although a neural crest origin for both groups of tumours has long been accepted, advances in developmental biology, including single-cell RNA sequencing and lineage tracing, have provided a new understanding of the derivation of normal chromaffin cells and sympathetic neurons. New developmental concepts may help to explain distinct characteristics associated with tumour location for both paragangliomas and neuroblastomas, as well as age and genotype associations and the existence of composite tumours {33833454}. A study performed in 2017 in mice showed that while sympathetic ganglia were populated predominantly by direct migration of progenitors from the neural crest, the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla {28684471} and extra-adrenal paraganglia {30740044} arose from nerve-associated progenitors termed Schwann cell precursors. These Schwann cell precursors are derived from neural crest cells that remain in place near the dorsal root ganglia and then reach their destinations by migrating along nerves. Subsequent studies in 2021 showed a more complex situation in humans, with a variety of bridge cells and developmental transitions between neuronal and chromaffin cell precursors {33833454}. In both species, Schwann cell precursors provide a source of neural crest–like cells for the expansion and diversification of sympathoadrenal tissues after direct migration is no longer possible because of body enlargement and the fact that the neural crest itself no longer exists.

The morphological classification of neuroblastomas, ganglioneuroblastomas (nodular and intermixed), and ganglioneuromas has not changed since the previous edition (see #28070Table 6.01). Advances with regard to prognostically and therapeutically relevant molecular abnormalities continue to be achieved. Notably, mechanisms of telomere maintenance have been studied and partly elucidated, including TERT overexpression and alternative lengthening of telomeres {30523111; 33627664}.

The previous edition of the WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs addressed the fact that all phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas should be considered malignant. This is continued in the new edition. Consequently, the adjectives “benign” and “malignant” are no longer recommended and can be replaced by a concept of risk stratification. Although several systems have been proposed to predict the risk of metastasis, no individual system is endorsed for routine use. The previous edition also noted that small adrenal medullary lesions historically known as hyperplastic nodules should be considered neoplasms, because they have been shown to carry the same clonal molecular abnormalities as larger tumours {23478233; 25379023}. As a result, the term “microphaeochromocytoma” has been put forward, analogous to microtumours elsewhere in the body, such as the thyroid and pancreas.

The new edition places increased emphasis on the importance of biomarkers. Given the complexity of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), it is often not sufficient to classify a lesion using chromogranin and S100 immunohistochemistry alone. The recognition that paragangliomas can occur in unusual locations, including primary lesions in lung and liver, makes this even more critical {30217041}. The importance of applying stains for GATA3 and tyrosine hydroxylase in diagnosis is emphasized, and the value of Ki-67 in predicting metastasis is now recognized.

Emphasis on hereditary disease has also increased. Paragangliomas are characterized by a very high incidence of genetic predisposition {28162975; 29239044; 33433885}. Awareness of this phenomenon has led to the discovery of new genes implicated in their tumorigenesis and new syndromes with novel associated neoplasms {28162975}. The concept of molecular immunohistochemistry provides the pathologist with tools for predicting the genetic basis of these neoplasms and for avoiding the pitfall of diagnosing multifocal primary tumours as metastatic disease {29239044; 33433885; 33768452}.
