3.2.3.1 Follicular thyroid carcinoma

Definition

Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) is a malignant well-differentiated follicular cell–derived neoplasm that has invasive growth and lacks nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) and high-grade features.

ICD-O coding

8330/3 Follicular thyroid carcinoma

8335/3 Minimally invasive follicular thyroid carcinoma (capsular invasion only)

8339/3 Encapsulated angioinvasive follicular thyroid carcinoma

8330/3 Widely invasive follicular thyroid carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2D10.0 Follicular carcinoma of thyroid gland

Related terminology

Acceptable: follicular carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Minimally invasive follicular thyroid carcinoma (capsular invasion only); encapsulated angioinvasive follicular thyroid carcinoma; widely invasive follicular thyroid carcinoma

Localization

FTC can occur anywhere in the thyroid, in ectopic locations and in association with struma ovarii {8253550; 19696610; 26374222}.

Clinical features

FTC occurs predominantly in adults (mean age at diagnosis: 45–50 years at diagnosis) and is rare in children {10897019; 20373982; 23616135; 26041024; 26131690; 32098591}. Most patients present with a painless thyroid mass that is detected on self-examination, routine physical examination, or incidentally on imaging. As many as 25–35% of FTCs are > 40 mm {23360496; 32098591}; however, only a subset of these tumours produce dysphagia, hoarseness, or stridor. Regional lymph node enlargement at presentation essentially does not occur with FTC. Approximately 5–10% of patients with FTC have distant metastases at diagnosis {23616135; 32098591}. In some of these patients, the metastases may be the presenting symptom, especially in patients with bone metastases at diagnosis. Very rare examples of FTC are associated with hyperthyroidism {11842375; 31798874}. On ultrasound, the majority of FTCs are indistinguishable from follicular adenoma (FA). Most FTCs are solitary, well-defined, solid, homogeneous, ovoid, isoechoic or hypoechoic nodules, with or without lobulated margins {16361145; 31899594}.

Epidemiology

While an increase in PTC has driven a global rise in thyroid cancer over the past four decades, the absolute incidence of FTC has been relatively stable {17289376; 23410185; 32098591}. The incidence of FTC in the USA in 1980–2009 was 0.88 cases per 100 000 person-years, with the rate in women about 2.2 times as high as that seen in men {23360496}. FTC currently accounts for approximately 5–15% of thyroid carcinomas, although this percentage varies geographically, in part because of a variable rise in PTC across countries {23410185; 25597380; 26041024; 27388603; 28362912; 29544469}.

Etiology

Iodine deficiency is considered a risk factor, based on studies demonstrating a higher incidence of FTC and an increased FTC:PTC ratio in iodine-deficient areas compared with iodine-sufficient areas and series demonstrating a decrease in FTC incidence with iodine supplementation {3969810; 9920375; 26146517}. FTC incidence has also been reported to be increased after exposure to ionizing radiation {1385648}, although it is possible that some, or even most, of these FTCs may actually represent the follicular variant of PTC.

Although the majority of follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinomas are sporadic, almost 10% are hereditary {21190444}. FTC can occur in the setting of syndromes predominantly defined by extrathyroidal manifestations, including PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (which includes Cowden syndrome), DICER1 syndrome, Werner syndrome, and Carney complex {29239041; 33495912}. Cowden syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder most frequently caused by germline mutations in the tumour suppressor gene PTEN. In addition to the numerous adenomatous nodules and FA that are often seen in thyroids from patients with Cowden syndrome {21190448; 21921783}, approximately 25% of patients with Cowden syndrome have FTC that may present in childhood (for additional features of Cowden syndrome, see the section on Cowden syndrome) {21956414; 32228164; 33495912}. DICER1 syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder with reduced penetrance caused by heterozygous inactivating germline DICER1 mutations {29343557}. People with DICER1 syndrome have a 16-fold increased risk of thyroid cancer, with FTC accounting for a significant subset of these cancers, some of which present in childhood {24617712; 28323992; 32228164}. FTC can also occur in people with Werner syndrome, an autosomal recessive disease characterized by premature aging and cancer predisposition caused by biallelic WRN mutations {20301687}. Thyroid neoplasms occur in approximately 15% of patients with Werner syndrome {23573208}. In Japanese patients with Werner syndrome, FTC has been reported to account for 48% of thyroid malignancies {10189141}. About a quarter of patients with Carney complex, an autosomal dominant disorder most often caused by germline mutations in PRKAR1A, have thyroid tumours (including FA, follicular adenomatosis, and toxic adenomas) and 2.5% have thyroid carcinomas {19293268}. In a cohort of patients with Carney complex who underwent thyroid surgery, it was reported that approximately a quarter had FTC {29635258}. A family history of thyroid carcinoma is identified in 3.7% of patients diagnosed with FTC in the USA {10897019}.

Pathogenesis

The most common somatic mutations in FTC are RAS point mutations, which are reported in as many as half of all FTCs, with an overall rate of approximately 30% in published studies {33224857}. NRAS mutations are most common, followed by HRAS and KRAS mutations {25708358; 25999051}. Most NRAS and HRAS mutations involve codon 61, whereas most KRAS mutations involve codon 12/13 {25999051}. PAX8::PPARG rearrangements occur in 10–40% of FTCs {23738683}, with the frequency showing more geographical variability than is seen with RAS mutations {25708358}. RAS mutations and PAX8::PPARG rearrangements are generally mutually exclusive. Compared to RAS-mutant FTCs, FTCs with PAX8::PPARG rearrangements have been reported to occur in younger patients and to be smaller in size but with more overt invasion {12651598; 12727991}. Rare CREB3L2::PPARG gene fusions have also been reported in FTC {18757431}. EIF1AX mutations are identified in about 5% of FTCs and may co-occur with RAS mutations {26911375; 31077238}.

FTCs also harbour mutations and copy-number gains in genes involved in the PI3K/PTEN/AKT pathway. PIK3CA mutations and copy-number gain are found in approximately 8% and 30% of FTCs, respectively {17317825; 17426084}. Inactivating PTEN mutations are identified in approximately 10% of FTCs {17317825; 17426084}. Alterations in the PI3K/PTEN/AKT pathway are more frequent in FTC than in FA {17426084}.

A small subset of FTCs harbour somatic DICER1 or somatic NF1 mutations {29726952}. FTCs with DICER1 mutations may have a macrofollicular architecture {31701808; 34008223}. TERT promoter mutations are found in approximately 15% of FTCs (often in combination with a RAS mutation), with NC_000005.9:g.1295228G>A (228C>T) as the predominant form of this mutation {23766237; 27528624; 31077238}. TERT promoter mutations are enriched in FTC with distant metastases (found in approximately half) {30557173; 32282345}. FTC can also rarely have activating TSHR mutations {29516685}. In contrast to oncocytic carcinoma, conventional FTC generally lacks TP53 mutations {23979959}.

Macroscopic appearance

The vast majority of FTCs are grossly encapsulated, representing either minimally invasive FTC or encapsulated angioinvasive FTC {15022277; 21144693; 23616135}. Most FTCs are > 20 mm {23360496}; however, tumour size cannot be used to differentiate FTC from FA. FTCs are usually solid and tan/grey on cut surface. The capsule of minimally invasive FTC and encapsulated angioinvasive FTC can vary in thickness, although it is generally thicker in FTC than in FA {1373582; 21455197}. Gross evidence of invasion in encapsulated FTC is rare. A diagnosis of widely invasive FTC is based on tumour grossly invading through the thyroid parenchyma, often in a multinodular pattern. Widely invasive FTC can grossly invade extrathyroidal tissue. In some of these cases, some of the tumour nodules represent large tumour thrombi within vessels. Widely invasive tumours tend to be larger than minimally invasive FTCs {24741369; 26041024}. It should be noted that widely invasive FTCs are very rare tumours. Many tumours previously diagnosed as such probably represent poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC).

Histopathology

The diagnosis of FTC requires capsular and/or vascular invasion. FTC is divided into three subtypes: (1) minimally invasive (capsular invasion only), (2) encapsulated angioinvasive (venous invasion present with or without capsular penetration), and (3) widely invasive. Although the distinction between FTC and FA is seemingly straightforward, it is recognized that there is debate over what constitutes capsular invasion {11169933; 16798933} and vascular invasion {21804527}, and there are issues with interobserver reproducibility {32236857}.

Minimally invasive FTC: To render this diagnosis, most authors require full-thickness capsular penetration. Tumour that has invaded through the capsule often shows mushroom-shaped growth. The leading edge of the tumour may induce fibrosis, which can complicate assessment of invasion; however, the fibrosis at the leading/invasive edge of the tumour is beyond the contour of the tumour capsule. For cases with foci of invasion into but not through the capsule, serial/deeper sections should be considered, because full-thickness invasion may be identified on deeper sections. In a subset of cases, tumour is present outside the capsule but the focus of capsular invasion connecting the focus to the main mass is not present on the section examined. To be considered evidence of invasion, the focus being considered must have the same cytomorphology as that seen in the main mass. Also, the background thyroid should be considered. If there is a background of multiple adenomatous nodules, caution is advised and serial/deeper sections should be considered to confirm that the focus is contiguous with the main mass. Capsule rupture due to surgical manipulation or tissue grossing, capsular disruption secondary to prior biopsy, and curling of tumour beyond the capsule at tissue edges should not be equated with invasion. Capsular disruption secondary to FNA can often be recognized by the presence of haemosiderin-laden macrophages, inflammatory cells, and fresh haemorrhage.

Encapsulated angioinvasive FTC: Most authors define vascular invasion as the invasion of vessels within the tumour capsule or beyond, with intravascular tumour attached to the vessel wall, or admixed with fibrin or covered by endothelium. In some cases with vascular invasion, the tumour is seen directly invading into the capsule and into a vessel within the capsule as a polypoid protrusion within the lumen. Foci of tumour that only push up against vessels should not be considered foci of vascular invasion because this can frequently be seen at the tumour capsule interface or in the context of tumour buds within the capsule that are located next to vessels. Additionally, foci resembling vascular invasion that are located within the tumour or tumour floating within a vessel lumen (without associated endothelium) are not diagnostic of vascular invasion. There are also vascular alterations, including pseudoangiosarcomatous changes (Masson lesion) and endothelial proliferations, that may be associated with the needle tract from the prior FNA and can mimic vascular invasion {9796729}. The presence and extent of vascular invasion is prognostically relevant {15022277; 23327839; 26482605}. Tumours with limited invasion of vessels (< 4 foci) have a better prognosis than do those with extensive vascular invasion (≥ 4 foci of invasion). Therefore, foci of vascular invasion should be counted (with foci in adjacent vessels counted separately). ERG or CD31 immunohistochemistry may be considered, to highlight vascular endothelial cells {20205756; 29744727}. Vascular invasion represents venous invasion, regardless of the vessel size; moreover, consistent with the fact that FTC rarely spreads to lymph nodes, lymphatic invasion is essentially not seen with FTC {20205756}. In one study, vascular invasion was diagnosed only when the intravascular tumour was associated with fibrin or invaded the vessel wall {21804527}. These stringent criteria resulted in a higher risk of distant metastasis and a diminished impact of the extent of vascular invasion in prognosis, highlighting the importance of fibrin-associated tumour thrombi {21804527}. In that study, the number of foci of vascular invasion did not impact the outcome {21804527}. In cases with questionable capsular or vascular invasion, a diagnosis of follicular tumour of uncertain malignant potential can be rendered. However, this category should be used sparingly.

Widely invasive FTC: The capsule of widely invasive FTC may be entirely obliterated or only focally intact. Widely invasive FTCs show extensive invasion of the thyroid and often of extrathyroidal soft tissues. Vascular invasion is often prominent, but alone it does not categorize an FTC as widely invasive.

The cytoarchitectural features of FTC are similar to those of FA. The tumour can have a microfollicular, normofollicular, or macrofollicular growth pattern. Solid or trabecular growth can occur (alone or in combination with follicular-patterned growth); however, it is important that in tumours showing these growth patterns, a diagnosis of PDTC should be excluded (see section High-grade follicular cell–derived non-anaplastic thyroid carcinoma). For all cases, the tumour should be examined for mitoses. The mitotic count should be < 3 mitoses/2 mm2 in areas of solid or trabecular growth and < 5 mitoses/2 mm2 if the tumour has an entirely follicular architecture (see section High-grade follicular cell–derived non-anaplastic thyroid carcinoma). Tumour necrosis should not be seen in FTC, but infarct-like necrosis (often secondary to preoperative FNAB) is permissible. FTC also lacks the nuclear features of PTC. Instead, the nuclei are round and have coarse chromatin. All potential FTCs should be examined for nuclear features of PTC to exclude a diagnosis of the encapsulated follicular variant of PTC. The stroma of FTC can vary from scant to hyalinized or myxoid. Occasionally, lipomatous metaplasia is present within the tumour. Extensive intratumoural fibrosis (often with associated calcification), a finding that has been associated with FTC with distant metastases at diagnosis, can variably obscure evidence of invasion {30557173}. Although it is difficult to identify vascular invasion in such lesions, it seems logical that venous involvement was present at some time during the evolution of the tumour.

Histological patterns

Other histological patterns of FTC are extremely rare. Clear cell FTC, the most frequent non-conventional pattern of FTC, is defined as having > 50% clear cells. Clear cell change can occur in a range of benign and malignant thyroid tumours {2829386; 4061729; 3957248; 30370813; 30931661} and occurs secondary to an accumulation of glycogen, lipids, thyroglobulin, or vesicles of apparent mitochondrial derivation {3957248; 4061729}. A diagnosis of malignancy is rendered according to the criteria used for conventional FTC. Clear cell FTC can have areas of solid architecture {4061729}; in such cases, progression to PDTC should be excluded. Because clear cell FTC is a rare tumour, other tumours with clear cytoplasm should be excluded, including metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma, and parathyroid tumours. Thyroglobulin staining can be focal or weak in clear cell FTC {3061922}; therefore, the combined use of thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and PAX8 may be more helpful diagnostically. The prognostic significance of clear cell change has not been established, because of the rarity of the tumour. Like patients with conventional FTC, some reported patients have no further evidence of disease after resection, whereas others die of disease progression {2829386; 3957248}. Clear cell FTCs have molecular alterations similar to those seen in conventional FTC, with RAS mutations and PAX8::PPARG rearrangements reported {2829386}. Malignant oncocytic tumours are discussed separately in the section on oncocytic carcinoma (see section Oncocytic carcinoma of the thyroid gland).

The signet-ring cell pattern of FTC is characterized by cells with cytoplasmic vacuoles with eccentrically displaced nuclei {2996373; 4061729; 24068558; 31699114}. The cytoplasmic vacuoles are similar to follicular lumina and have an accumulation of thyroglobulin {2996373}. The nuclei are generally uniform and bland. These tumours must be distinguished from metastatic adenocarcinomas with signet-ring cell morphology. Other extremely rare lesions are FTC with a glomeruloid pattern, characterized by follicles with round to oval epithelial tufts growing within them, mimicking a renal glomerulus {18602667}; and spindle cell FTC {18788926}, characterized by cells arranged in intersecting fascicles. Differentiating spindle cell FTC from anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is essential. To render a diagnosis of spindle cell FTC, pleomorphism, marked mitotic activity, and tumour necrosis must be absent.

Immunohistochemistry

FTC is positive for CAM5.2, EMA, and CK7 and negative for CK20 {2428725; 10981870}. These tumours also have strong nuclear staining for the transcription factors PAX8 and TTF1 {10981870; 21552115}. The cytoplasm and associated colloid of FTC is positive for thyroglobulin {10981870}. There are no immunohistochemical stains that reliably distinguish FTC from FA. Ki-67 immunohistochemistry may be helpful when considering a differential diagnosis of PDTC. Consistent with the low mitotic count of FTC, the Ki-67 proliferation index is < 5% in the majority of FTCs, whereas most PDTCs have a higher Ki-67 proliferation index {20562850}.

Cytology

Benign and malignant follicular-patterned lesions cannot be distinguished using thyroid FNA. The cytological diagnosis of follicular neoplasm or suspicious for follicular neoplasm reflects the limitations of thyroid cytology, because the diagnosis of FTC is based on the demonstration of capsular and/or vascular invasion.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In preoperative fine-needle biopsies, the presence of RAS mutations and PAX8::PPARG rearrangements raise the possibility of FTC {30175060}. However, these genetic events are not specific, being encountered in FA and encapsulated follicular variant of PTC. There is currently no role for molecular pathology in the diagnosis of FTC in the postoperative setting.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: follicular cell–derived tumours lacking nuclear features of PTC and lacking high-grade features; capsular and/or vascular invasion; widely invasive FTCs have grossly apparent invasion through the thyroid parenchyma and (typically) harbour extensive vascular invasion.

Staging

FTC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems, incorporating age at diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

Extent of invasion significantly impacts survival for patients with FTC. Disease-free survival rates of 97%, 81%, and 45% at 40 months have been reported for minimally invasive, encapsulated angioinvasive, and widely invasive FTC, respectively {21144693}. For the rare minimally invasive FTC that results in death, distant metastases are usually present at the time of initial diagnosis {26482605; 21144693}. For encapsulated angioinvasive FTC, the extent of vascular invasion has been shown to be prognostically significant, with an odds ratio of 13.7 reported for extensive vascular invasion in a multivariate analysis of 10-year cause-specific survival {23327839}. For M0 patients with minimally invasive or encapsulated angioinvasive FTC, an age of ≥ 55 years and a tumour size of > 40 mm have been shown to be prognostic for decreased disease-free survival in multivariate analyses {33237449; 26041024; 23327839}. The presence of distant metastases is the most important prognostic feature for FTC. The most common sites of distant metastases are lung and bone {24420337}, with bone metastases more common in patients with metastatic disease at the time of initial diagnosis, and lung metastases more common in patients who are M0 at initial diagnosis {30557173}. The prognosis appears to be similar for patients who present with or subsequently develop distant metastases {30557173}. TERT promoter mutations have been shown to be associated with more aggressive clinical behaviour in FTC, including a more advanced stage at presentation, distant metastases, and increased disease-specific mortality {24476079; 26971368; 27528624}. Increased mutation burden has also been associated with a worse prognosis in FTC {29726952}.
