9.0.3.3 Glomangioma and glomus tumour

Definition

Glomangioma and glomus tumour are mesenchymal neoplasms composed of cells with features of the modified smooth muscle cells of the normal glomus body.

ICD-O coding

8711/0 Glomus tumour, benign

8711/1 Glomangiomatosis

8711/1 Glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential

8711/3 Glomus tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Y Glomangioma

Related terminology

Glomus tumour

Acceptable: glomangioma; glomangiomyoma.

Malignant glomus tumour

Acceptable: glomangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Oncocytic glomus tumour {3038728}; epithelioid glomus tumour {7672784}; myxoid glomus tumour {8065987}; signet ring glomus tumour {1317998}; hyalinized glomus tumour {19940754}; symplastic glomus tumour {11145243}; glomangiomatosis {11145243}; glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential {11145243}; benign glomus tumour; malignant glomus tumour {11145243}; glomuvenous malformation

Localization

Glomus tumours arise most commonly in the skin and soft tissues, especially in the distal extremities {11145243; 14080348; 20530151}; however, they can also rarely occur in visceral locations, including endocrine organs. Cases have been reported in the pituitary {6097000; 15952045; 21912963; 31948309; 32918169}, thyroid {25544269; 31039649}, and pancreas {12009105; 33141292}, but not in the adrenal or the parathyroid.

Clinical features

Cutaneous glomus tumours typically present as solitary, painful, pink to blue nodules, whereas glomuvenous malformations are typically larger and more common in patients with multiple or familial lesions {15689436}. The 6 cases reported in the pituitary presented with headache, decreased visual acuity, intermittent diplopia, bitemporal hemianopia, and panhypopituitarism {6097000; 32918169; 15952045; 21912963; 31948309}. All had suprasellar extension, with 4 cases also involving the cavernous sinus {32918169}. The 2 reported thyroid cases presented initially as painless nodules, with 1 case progressing to a painful tumour {25544269; 31039649}. The pancreatic cases presented with postprandial epigastric and back pain, weight loss, episodic precordial pain, and vomiting {12009105; 33141292}.

Epidemiology

The pituitary cases occurred in patients aged 8–72 years; 3 patients were male and 3 were female {6097000; 32918169; 15952045; 21912963; 31948309}. The 2 reported thyroid cases occurred in men aged 55 and 68 years {25544269; 31039649}. The pancreatic cases occurred in a 17-year-old girl and a 46-year-old woman {12009105; 33141292}.

Etiology

Multiple familial glomus tumours are caused by inactivating mutations in GLMN (encoding glomulin), inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern {15689436}.

Pathogenesis

Inactivating mutations in GLMN (1p22.1) are associated with familial glomus tumours inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern {15689436}. Multiple digital glomus tumours have also been linked to neurofibromatosis type 1 {19738042; 23849732; 25426397}. NOTCH gene fusions were identified in about half of the cases, with NOTCH2 rearranged in most (88%) and NOTCH3 and NOTCH1 in a minority {23999936; 32604167}. In about three quarters of these fusion tumours, the gene partner was the microRNA gene MIR143 {32604167}. NOTCH fusions were seen in both benign and malignant glomus tumours and in both superficial and visceral cases {23999936; 32604167}. BRAF p.V600E mutations have been detected in 6% of glomus tumours, including 3 (12%) of 24 malignant tumours {28834810}.

Macroscopic appearance

Glomus tumours tend to be grossly well circumscribed, even in malignant cases {33141292}. Reported sellar cases were described as firm and rubbery {32918169}.

Histopathology

Tumours are composed of small, uniform, round or polygonal cells with a central nucleus and amphophilic or eosinophilic cytoplasm. Cell borders may be well delineated or indistinct. Predominantly oncocytic {3038728}, epithelioid (large cell) {7672784}, and signet-ring cell {1317998} subtypes have been described. The cells are typically arranged in nests surrounding capillaries and small vessels. Myxoid or hyalinized stroma may be seen in some cases {8065987; 19940754}. Most tumours are solid, but a vascular component predominates in glomangiomas, glomangiomatosis, and glomuvenous malformations, with small nests of glomus cells present in vessel walls {19267531}. Haemangiopericytoma-like vessels may also be seen {9591720}. Spindle cells resembling smooth muscle are seen in a minority of cases (glomangiomyomas). The symplastic subtype displays degenerative nuclear atypia lacking mitotic activity and necrosis {11145243; 19602065; 25051107}. Malignant glomus tumours show atypical mitotic figures and/or a combination of high nuclear grade and mitotic activity (≥ 5 mitoses/10–11 mm2) {11145243; 22228777; 27772590}. They can arise from a benign precursor and may show leiomyosarcoma- or fibrosarcoma-like components.

Immunohistochemistry

A typical glomus tumour shows diffuse reactivity for SMA, with the majority of cases also showing MSA (recognized by HHF35), vimentin, pericellular collagen IV, caldesmon, SIRT1, and laminin staining. TLE1 expression has been reported in one study {28632567}. Synaptophysin expression may be present, a confounding factor in the differential diagnosis with neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) {31948309; 11859201}. Tumours are negative for keratins, chromogranin A, CD56, S100, and KIT (CD117). CD34 highlights the endothelial cells but may be focally positive in the neoplastic glomus cells {11145243; 25614464}. The Ki-67 labelling index in the pituitary cases ranged between 10% and 30% {32918169}.

Cytology

Cytological reports of glomus tumours are infrequent and mostly describe cutaneous and superficial lesions. One report of a visceral glomus tumour described groups of cohesive, uniform, small, round to polygonal cells with scant cytoplasm, indistinct cell borders, and round, hyperchromatic nuclei with homogeneous chromatin {12768637}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

About half of glomus tumours harbour NOTCH gene fusions, with NOTCH2 being involved most commonly (88%) and MIR143 being the most common fusion partner (76%) {32604167}; however, these fusions are not entirely specific for glomus tumours because they have also been demonstrated in myopericytoma and angioleiomyoma {32604167}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: small, round to polygonal glomoid cells with central nuclei and diffuse SMA expression.

Desirable: pericellular collagen IV expression.

Staging

Malignant cases are staged by the staging criteria appropriate to the site in which they arise.

Prognosis and prediction

Most glomus tumours are benign and are cured by complete resection. Malignant glomus tumours are highly aggressive, with frequent metastases, often resulting in the death of the patient {11145243}. Deep or visceral location is one of the proposed criteria for the diagnosis of glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential {11145243}, which would indicate that all endocrine organ glomus tumours have uncertain malignant potential. In line with this, among the 5 pituitary cases with available follow-up, all 5 recurred and 2 patients died of the disease {32918169}. The 2 reported glomus tumours of the thyroid also behaved in a malignant fashion, with the patients developing metastases and dying from their disease, one at 30 months and one at 6 months after their initial diagnosis {25544269; 31039649}.
