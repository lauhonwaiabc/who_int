12.0.1.2 Metastasis to the thyroid gland

Definition

Metastases to the thyroid gland are tumours that involve the thyroid gland secondarily by haematogenous spread from primary sites in other organs.

ICD-O coding

Code according to histology of metastasis

ICD-11 coding

2E0Y & XA8RK3 Malignant neoplasm metastasis in other specified sites & Thyroid gland

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Metastases to the thyroid gland arise in either or both thyroid gland lobes or the isthmus. They are most commonly a nodule but may be dispersed throughout the gland, and they are preferentially located at the periphery {17371476; 32897542}.

Clinical features

Clinical symptoms are the most common mode of presentation {22313412}, often in the form of a palpable neck mass {25907117; 27873099}. Dysphonia and dysphagia may occur {17371476}. Patients are typically euthyroid, with very rare cases of hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism due to parenchymal destruction by the metastatic process {11575859; 16714841; 17371476}. Twenty-five per cent of cases are found incidentally {22313412}. In clinical series, metastases most commonly originate from kidney tumours (40–50% of all metastases {12404280}), followed by lung tumours, breast tumours, colorectal tumours, upper gastrointestinal tract tumours, melanoma, and sarcoma; the order of frequency of these other primary sites varies among different studies and when sex is taken into consideration {16983577; 19031929; 31645015; 32897542}. Rare primary sites include the uterus and bladder {18446450; 22937279; 32665867}.

Epidemiology

Metastases to the thyroid are identified in 1.9–25% of autopsy cases {22313412}, but their prevalence in surgical pathology practice is much lower (< 0.1%) {12404280}. They occur in 0.36–2.1% of resected thyroid malignancies {15256229; 16983577; 17371476; 32897542}. There is a slight female preponderance (M:F ratio: 1:1.4) {22313412}. The mean and median ages at diagnosis are within the seventh decade of life {15256229; 16983577; 17371476; 32897542}. Metastases may be synchronous (20% of cases) but are usually metachronous, detected several years (median: 53 months) after the diagnosis of the primary tumour {22313412}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The high vascularity of the thyroid gland predisposes to haematogenous dissemination. Interaction of the parenchymal microenvironment with the properties of specific tumour cell types is also important for the successful growth of tumour deposits, as demonstrated by the predominance of the thyroid gland as the recipient of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) {12404280; 32761606; 32897542}.

Macroscopic appearance

The metastases can be unifocal or multifocal {32375670; 32897542}. Those of RCC are well circumscribed or encapsulated, are bright yellow on cross-section, and can be as large as 150 mm {12404280}. Diffuse bilateral involvement of the gland may occur.

Histopathology

Clear cell RCC is the most frequent histotype {12404280; 16983577; 32897542}. Metastatic neoplastic cells can grow into the interstitium, form nodular masses, or develop within pre-existing thyroid nodules (tumour-to-tumour metastasis) {10420227}. Metastatic lung carcinoma can be mistaken for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, and RCC for Hürthle cell or papillary thyroid carcinoma {32897542}. Metastatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs; including small cell neuroendocrine carcinomas [SCNECs] and large cell neuroendocrine carcinomas [LCNECs] from the lung and elsewhere, but also lung neuroendocrine tumours [NETs]) must be distinguished from medullary thyroid carcinoma {9236831; 17371476; 32897542}. Clinical history, histological clues (e.g. glandular lumina packed with red blood cells in RCC), and immunohistochemistry (e.g. absent calcitonin and CEA immunoreactivity in metastatic NENs, but positive reactions in medullary thyroid carcinoma) are important for the correct diagnosis. Importantly, direct extensions from other organs (i.e. larynx, oesophagus) should be excluded.

Cytology

FNA can accurately identify the metastasis in 74–81% of cases {22313412; 25369542}. High-grade nuclear atypia (often present) and tumour necrosis can help exclude common primary thyroid gland tumours {25369542}. Immunohistochemistry is needed if nuclear atypia is mild and to exclude anaplastic thyroid carcinoma {25369542}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing may have a role in identifying the primary site of origin of the tumour or in guiding therapy, depending on the tumour type.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: deposits of non-thyroidal malignant cells within the thyroid gland parenchyma.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system for the primary tumour metastatic to the thyroid

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year overall survival rate ranges from 31% to 58% after thyroidectomy {18578610; 19031929; 32897542}, depending on the tumour type. Patients with unifocal thyroid involvement and oligometastatic disease have better outcomes {12404280; 19031929; 27873099; 32897542}.
