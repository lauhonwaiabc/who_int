3.2.1.3 Oncocytic adenoma of the thyroid gland

Definition

Oncocytic adenoma (OA) is a benign, non-invasive, encapsulated, follicular cell–derived neoplasm composed of > 75% oncocytic cells.

ICD-O coding

8290/0 Oncocytic adenoma of the thyroid gland

ICD-11 coding

2F37.Y & XH5SM2 Other specified benign neoplasm of endocrine glands & Follicular adenoma, oxyphilic cell

Related terminology

Not recommended: Hürthle cell adenoma; oxyphilic adenoma; follicular adenoma, oncocytic variant; follicular adenoma, Hürthle cell type.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

OA can occur anywhere in the thyroid. It has also been reported in ectopic locations, such as in association with thyroglossal duct cysts and arising from thyroid tissue within the mediastinum {3220776; 10613966}.

Clinical features

The median age at diagnosis of OA is 50–55 years {12461336; 16453320; 31038598; 31751003; 33792557}. They are uncommon in children {19355826}. OAs usually present as painless, palpable thyroid masses or are incidentally detected on physical examination or imaging studies, although a small subset of large OAs are associated with compressive symptoms. On ultrasound, OAs are typically solitary, well-demarcated, solid, ovoid nodules with smooth borders, variable echogenicity (they may be either isoechoic to hypoechoic, or hyperechoic), and no associated calcifications. The sonographic features of OA and oncocytic carcinoma (OCA) show considerable overlap, so benign and malignant tumours usually cannot be differentiated on ultrasound {31038598}. Most patients with OA are biochemically euthyroid. On radionuclide scan, OAs show less radioiodide avidity than the adjacent thyroid parenchyma (they are “cold” nodules). OAs show significantly higher focal FDG uptake than non-oncocytic follicular adenomas and can be mistaken for primary or secondary malignant thyroid tumours {24948789; 27221817}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of OA is difficult to assess, because many studies combine OA with follicular adenoma and there is histological overlap between OA and oncocytic adenomatous nodules. However, OAs have been reported to account for about 10–15% of thyroid nodules with a preceding indeterminate FNA diagnosis {16604553; 22731672}. OAs are more frequently diagnosed in women than in men {12461336; 16453320; 31751003}.

Etiology

There are no known exogenous risk factors for OA. Rarely, OA can be seen in the context of tumour syndromes, including Cowden syndrome (most frequently associated with a germline PTEN mutation) and Carney complex (most frequently associated with a germline PRKAR1A mutation). Although most benign neoplasms that occur in these syndromes represent follicular adenomas, OA can be rarely seen {10594284; 9215269}.

Pathogenesis

The genomics of OA is distinct from that of follicular adenoma. OAs are characterized by alterations in the mitochondrial genome; specifically, disruptive mutations in mitochondrial DNA complex I subunit genes {16778181; 17517629; 30107175; 30107176}. These mitochondrial DNA mutations affect the majority of copies of the mitochondrial genome (near-homoplasmy) and lead to global mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction. OAs also harbour mutations in NDUFA13 (GRIM19), a nuclear gene that contributes to a component of the complex I subunit {15841082}. The mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction of OA is thought to underlie the mitochondrial accumulation that characterizes these tumours. Copy-number alterations have been reported in OA; however, the rate has been reported to be lower than in OCA (38% for OA vs 81% for OCA) {33792557}. In OCA, near-genome-wide haploidization as a consequence of widespread whole-chromosome loss is often found, with subsequent genome endoreduplication. Allelic state analysis in a small series showed that only a small proportion of OAs showed similar features, and to a far lesser extent. Furthermore, a larger fraction of OAs seem to show whole-chromosome gains as if being the reciprocal mirror of OCA {29066502}. Interestingly, in oncocytic cell hyperplasia no such abnormalities are seen {29345728}. A subset of OAs have RAS and EIF1AX mutations (together present in about 10% of tumours). The rate of these mutations is similar to that seen in OCAs, although in contrast to OCAs, in OAs these mutations occur in tumours without copy-number alterations {33792557}. TERT promoter mutations and TP53 mutations, found in a subset of OCAs, are very rare in OA {31293091; 33792557}. Additionally, OAs lack the fusions found in follicular adenomas, such as PAX8::PPARG rearrangements and THADA::IGF2BP3 fusions.

Macroscopic appearance

OAs are usually solitary, round or oval encapsulated nodules, with a mean size of 20–30 mm {16453320; 31038598; 31751003}. Larger oncocytic nodules have a higher rate of malignancy than smaller nodules {12461336; 33792557}. For example, oncocytic tumours measuring > 40 mm have been reported to have double the malignancy rate of those < 40 mm {12461336}. However, OAs can be well over 40 mm, and therefore size cannot be used to differentiate between OA and OCA {31038598}. The cut surface of OA is usually homogeneous and is tan to mahogany brown. Oncocytic neoplasms are prone to haemorrhage and infarction secondary to FNA, and these secondary changes may be grossly apparent.

Histopathology

OAs are encapsulated, follicular-patterned tumours that lack capsular or vascular invasion. The capsule of OA ranges from thin to thick, although it is generally thinner than that of OCA. OA can have a microfollicular, normofollicular, or macrofollicular growth pattern. It can also have solid or trabecular growth, although these growth patterns are more frequent in OCA. OAs comprise > 75% oncocytic cells. Oncocytic cells are characterized by abundant granular eosinophilic cytoplasm, sharp cytoplasmic borders, and round to oval nuclei with an even chromatin pattern and prominent nucleoli. In contrast to other tumours that may have granular eosinophilic cytoplasm (e.g. oncocytic, tall cell, and Warthin-like subtypes of papillary thyroid carcinoma [PTC]), there is a complete loss of cell polarity in OA {20002768}. Moreover, at an ultrastructural level, although these PTC subtypes may be rich in mitochondria, the accumulation of mitochondria is less than that seen in OA. The nuclei of OA cells are usually round; however, nuclear contour irregularities and even nuclear grooves (raising the diagnostic consideration of PTC) may be present. However, unlike PTC, which has small, peripherally located nucleoli, OA characteristically has larger, centrally placed nucleoli. OAs often show focal nuclear atypia, with scattered cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei, consistent with endocrine atypia. Mitoses are usually rare (< 3 mitoses/2 mm2). OAs are prone to infarction induced by prior FNA; however, coagulative tumour necrosis is absent. Necrosis secondary to FNA is usually associated with fibrosis, haemorrhage, granulation tissue formation, and haemosiderin-laden macrophages. FNA can also result in capsular disruption that must be differentiated from true capsular invasion, and it can induce vascular changes that can mimic vascular invasion {9796729}. Some OAs have calcifications within follicles that are often associated with colloid and should not be confused for psammomatous calcifications found in PTC. The stroma of OA is often scant, but it can be oedematous, myxoid, or hyalinized. In some cases of OA with extensive hyalinization, the stroma may be calcified and even ossified showing cancellous bone. A lymphocytic infiltrate may be present in OA (usually denser at the periphery) that occurs in a background of Hashimoto thyroiditis. Lipomatous metaplasia can occur in OA.

The histological distinction between OA and an oncocytic hyperplastic nodule is straightforward when the tumour is solitary and has a well-defined capsule; however, the distinction is less clear when there is a background of hyperplastic nodules with adenomatous and oncocytic change. In some cases, histological distinction between a clonal OA and a hyperplastic nodule with oncocytic change is not possible.

The distinction between OA and OCA is based on the histological evaluation of the tumour capsule. Therefore, the capsule should be thoroughly sampled. Serial/deeper sections should be considered to resolve foci of questionable capsular or vascular invasion. If deeper levels do not allow for the definitive classification of a tumour as OA or OCA, a diagnosis of oncocytic tumour of uncertain malignant potential can be rendered (see section Thyroid tumours of uncertain malignant potential). However, this category should be used sparingly.

A subset of OAs have a papillary architecture that may be focal or extensive. In contrast to PTCs, these tumours lack nuclear clearing, peripheral margination of chromatin, and pseudoinclusions (although nuclear contour irregularities may be present). Additionally, psammomatous calcifications are absent. For oncocytic tumours with a papillary architecture, malignancy is based on the same criteria as are used for conventional oncocytic neoplasms.

Non-invasive, encapsulated, follicular cell–derived neoplasms composed of < 75% oncocytic cells are considered follicular adenomas with oncocytic features {1636432; 23543667}.

Immunophenotype

OAs have an immunohistochemical profile similar to that of other differentiated thyroid tumours. They are positive for PAX8 and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and negative for calcitonin {2428725; 10981870; 21552115}. The cytoplasm and associated colloid of OA is positive for thyroglobulin {10981870}. There are no immunohistochemical stains that reliably distinguish OA from OCA.

Cytology

Smears are moderately cellular and show oncocytic cells as sheets, as tissue fragments of nested oncocytic cells separated by transgressing vessels, and as single cells. The oncocytic cells constitute more than 90% of epithelial cells, in distinction to the common finding of focal metaplastic, oncocytic change frequently associated with cystic degeneration in follicular nodules. Thin colloid is usually absent, chronic inflammatory cells are scant, and intracytoplasmic lumina can be seen. The cells have abundant coarsely granular cytoplasm and uniform central round nuclei with prominent single nucleoli {16453320}. Although cytopathological features favouring OCA over OA (and other benign conditions with oncocytic metaplasia) have been reported, including small cell dysplasia, large cell dysplasia, nuclear crowding, and prominent cellular dyscohesion {18232004}, ultimately OA and OCA have overlapping features and cannot be definitively distinguished on FNAB, because the distinction is based on capsular and vascular invasion, similar to the problematic distinction between follicular adenoma and follicular carcinoma. Diagnostic pitfalls for OA include oncocytic, Warthin-like, and tall cell subtypes of PTC, which also have cells with abundant granular cytoplasm and a variable number of nuclear pseudoinclusions and grooves {30668897}, as well as the oncocytic subtype of medullary thyroid carcinoma {28802094} and autonomously functioning follicular adenomas {33901345}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Oncocytic neoplasms (adenoma and carcinoma) have a low prevalence of mutations in genes that are commonly altered in non-oncocytic thyroid tumours {30107175; 30107176}. Therefore, a negative result obtained from molecular analysis performed on preoperative cytology samples should not influence the decision for thyroid surgery, especially when limited molecular panels {31821746} are employed.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a non-invasive, encapsulated tumour; > 75% oncocytic cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

OAs are benign tumours that do not recur if completely excised.
