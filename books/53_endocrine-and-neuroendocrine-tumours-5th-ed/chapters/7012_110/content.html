7.0.1.2 Parasympathetic paraganglioma

Definition

Parasympathetic paraganglioma is a well-differentiated non-epithelial neoplasm derived from paraganglion cells of the autonomic nervous system.

ICD-O coding

8682/3 Parasympathetic paraganglioma (head and neck paraganglioma)

8693/3 Composite paraganglioma

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.Y & XH5LK3 Other specified neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of endocrine glands & Parasympathetic paraganglioma

Related terminology

Acceptable: head and neck paraganglioma.

Not recommended: glomus tumour; carotid body tumour; glomus caroticum; glomus jugulare; glomus tympanicum; chemodectoma.

Subtype(s)

Composite paraganglioma

Localization

Tumours are typically associated with the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves. Although “parasympathetic” and “head and neck” are generally considered synonyms, head and neck lesions may include sympathetic paragangliomas associated with the cervical sympathetic chains {16782577; 20310044; 24652566; 33100776} or arise from paraganglia with mixed sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation {11889402}.

Tumours develop around the carotid body (40–60%), middle ear (jugulotympanic paraganglia; 30%), vagal nerve trunk in or near the nodose ganglion (10%), sympathetic chain (4%), and (rarely) in the larynx, thyroid gland, parathyroid gland, mandible, parotid gland, nasopharynx and paranasal sinuses, sella turcica, orbit, and clivus {9236830; 20890271; 30217041; 28299533}. Bilateral/multifocal paragangliomas account for 10–40% of cases {28162975; 28321772}.

Clinical features

Carotid body paragangliomas usually present as asymptomatic mass lesions. Middle ear paragangliomas present with pulsatile tinnitus, hearing abnormalities, or a sensation of aural fullness. Large or infiltrative tumours and most vagal paragangliomas are associated with cranial nerve symptoms {29202495}. Horner syndrome and first bite syndrome are associated with sympathetic paragangliomas {33100776; 24652566; 16782577}. Increasingly, tumours are discovered by screening families with known genetic predisposition syndromes. These patients require lifelong surveillance and management tailored to specific genotypes.

Only about 4% of head and neck paragangliomas are biochemically functional, producing predominantly dopamine and/or 3-methoxytyramine. Occasionally, norepinephrine or normetanephrine is elevated {31168271; 22431860}, but elevated metanephrine/epinephrine levels usually indicate a concurrent sympathetic paraganglioma/phaeochromocytoma {29299512}.

Paragangliomas are detected with the highest sensitivity by PET-CT. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT is most commonly available in North America, and other methods use 68Ga-DOTATOC or 68Ga-DOTANOC. This technique has the added advantage of predicting the efficacy of somatostatin-based therapies, including peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) {25554089; 29450723}. Other techniques, such as iobenguane (metaiodobenzylguanidine; 131I-MIBG) scan and SSTR scintigraphy with 111In-pentetreotide, have significant limitations but may be useful if other studies are unavailable {22399235; 18632829}.

Epidemiology

Paragangliomas represent < 0.5% of head and neck tumours, with an estimated incidence of 1 case per 30 000–100 000 person-years {29299512}. They can occur at any age, from childhood to old age, with a mean in the sixth decade of life {29299512}. In contrast to their sympathetic counterparts, there is a definite female predominance (M:F ratio: 1:1.7) {11701678}. Paediatric cases are very rare, mostly reported in the teenage years. About 9–23% of paragangliomas in this population are found in the head and neck {32432211; 32491270}.

Etiology

Genetic factors

Head and neck paragangliomas have a hereditary predisposition of ≥ 40%, approaching 85% of patients of all ages tested in some series {29299512; 37010365}. As many as two thirds of carotid body paragangliomas may be associated with SDH mutations {37010365}. Consequently, there is a high probability of synchronous or metachronous multicentric paragangliomas, which may be confined to the head and neck or occasionally include sympathetic paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas {29299512; 15774781}. The most common mutations in paragangliomas are in SDHD (46.5%), followed by SDHB (30.2%), and SDHC (16.3%) {29299512}. SDHD mutation is associated predominantly with single or multifocal tumours in the head and neck, with a lower probability of phaeochromocytomas and thoracoabdominal sympathoadrenal paragangliomas. SDHB is more often associated with thoracoabdominal tumours {22584701}. Paragangliomas in the head and neck are rarely associated with mutations of VHL, RET, or NF1 {22584701}. A rare, non-hereditary, genetic association is Carney triad. Most cases of Carney triad show downregulation of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) through site-specific hypermethylation of SDHC {28739378}, which has recently also been reported in both parasympathetic and sympathetic paragangliomas without other components of the triad {29126304}. Carney–Stratakis dyad, consisting of hereditary SDH-deficient paraganglioma and gastrointestinal stromal tumour, is a component of multiple paraganglioma syndromes caused by germline mutations within one of the genes encoding SDH subunits {28739378} and is not a distinct diagnostic entity.

Risk factors

For carotid body paragangliomas in particular, tumour incidence is increased and the M:F ratio is as high as 1:9 in some populations living at high altitudes, probably reflecting effects of hypoxia as a phenotypic modifier in patients with SDHD {12811540; 29681642} or SDHB {20592014} mutations. An association with congenital cyanotic heart disease has also been reported {26961564}. Although the SDHD mutation can be transmitted by either a male or female carrier, tumours usually occur only after paternal transmission, causing skipped generations {26067997}.

Pathogenesis

Familial paragangliomas are autosomal dominant disorders in which a germline mutation is followed by a second hit inactivating the wildtype allele {33059362}.

Macroscopic appearance

Parasympathetic paragangliomas are typically encapsulated, pink to tan, firm nodules. There may be areas of haemorrhage, especially after preoperative embolization, which is frequently employed in head and neck sites.

Histopathology

Tumours exhibit a variable Zellballen architecture composed of chief cells with abundant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm with slightly to moderately atypical nuclei, and peripherally located sustentacular cells, usually difficult to detect in H&E sections. The stroma is highly vascular. Mitotic figures are usually rare.

Composite paraganglioma, composed of paraganglioma admixed with ganglioneuroma, is at most very rare in the head and neck. Only one case has been reported and in that example ganglion cells retained immunoreactivity for SDHB, which was lost in the paraganglioma. The pathogenesis was therefore unclear {30222023}.

Chief cells express markers of neuroendocrine differentiation, including INSM1 {30358615}, synaptophysin, and chromogranin A, as well as nuclear GATA3. Catecholamine-synthesizing enzymes, tyrosine hydroxylase and DBH, are expressed in 32% and 11%, respectively, of head and neck paragangliomas, including tumours considered non-functioning because of undetectable catecholamine metabolites {25677368}. Chief cells are almost always non-reactive for keratins {31442521}. Sustentacular cells express S100, SOX10, and (uncommonly) GFAP {32548761}. Loss of reactivity for SDHB is associated with mutations of SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, and SDHAF2 {19576851}. Ancillary markers of pseudohypoxic tumours are imperfect, but α-inhibin is expressed more commonly in SDH- related and VHL-related tumours {33826547} and CAIX is expressed strongly in VHL-associated tumours {31383958; 23257898}. Paragangliomas express SSTRs {14602858}, and SDH-deficient paragangliomas have been reported to demonstrate stronger staining for SSTR2A and SSTR3 {19936639; 25554089}.

Numerous primary and metastatic tumours constitute the differential diagnosis in head and neck regions, including epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the larynx {28321772}, parathyroids {27406876}, pituitary {29749133}, middle ear and temporal bone, and thyroid (medullary carcinoma) {28493102}. Olfactory neuroblastoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma may also need consideration.

Negativity for keratins distinguishes paragangliomas from most but not all epithelial NENs and depends in part on antibody selection, because some epithelial tumours possess a limited repertoire of keratins {33098891}. Conversely, focal aberrant keratin expression can occur, very rarely, in paragangliomas {31442521; 2467285}. Such cases must be approached with particular caution, using a battery of markers. Although GATA3 is a useful biomarker of paragangliomas, it is also expressed in normal and neoplastic parathyroid and pituitary {30217041; 32303954; 27529763; 24145643; 32193825}. Paragangliomas can also express peptide hormones including calcitonin {28493102; 27529763; 29744727}. Although medullary thyroid carcinomas can also focally stain for tyrosine hydroxylase, they are typically negative for GATA3 and positive for CEA and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) {28493102; 29744727}. Sustentacular cells, identified by S100 or SOX10, are not specific for paraganglioma {27529763; 32632839}.

Cytology

Smears usually show isolated tumour cells and small tissue fragments suggestive of microfollicles. The cells are often large, polygonal, and ovoid or elongated, with abundant granular, often eccentric, cytoplasm that is eosinophilic (H&E stain) or pale (Pap) or which may contain fine red granules (Giemsa stain). The nuclei are round to oval, with speckled chromatin typical of neuroendocrine differentiation and inconspicuous nucleoli. Nuclear grooves and intranuclear pseudoinclusions may be seen. Scattered cells may have larger, pleomorphic nuclei with prominent nucleoli and clumped chromatin, and similar stripped nuclei can be seen. The differential diagnosis includes medullary carcinoma of the thyroid. Cell-block material can be used for immunohistochemistry and shows neuroendocrine markers {31168271; 10945911; 20715670}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a nested/Zellballen pattern by histology, with round, hyperchromatic nuclei with stippled chromatin; SDHB immunohistochemistry to assess hereditary risk.

Desirable: neuroendocrine differentiation by immunohistochemistry, GATA3 expression, and negative keratins; relevant germline genetic evaluation.

Staging

There is no TNM system as published by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC), but International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) and other expert-initiated reporting guidelines have been introduced {24476517; 32407815}.

Prognosis and prediction

Head and neck paragangliomas have a low metastatic potential, with only a 4–6% risk of metastasis for those in the carotid body and 2% for those in the middle ear {28321772}. The main putative risk factor for metastasis is hereditary mutation of SDHB {24169168; 31194233}. Metastases can occur after many years, especially in patients harbouring SDHB mutations, where they may be difficult to distinguish from metachronous disease {31194233}. However, locally invasive tumours can have a very aggressive course if unresectable {32548761}. Patients with familial paragangliomas require lifelong surveillance for multicentric disease.
