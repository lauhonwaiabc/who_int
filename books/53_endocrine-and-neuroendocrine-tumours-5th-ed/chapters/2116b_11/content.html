2.1.1.6B Null cell PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Null cell pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is a PitNET that shows no evidence of adenohypophysial differentiation by immunohistochemistry for pituitary hormones and the transcription factors PIT1, SF1, and TPIT.

ICD-O coding

8272/3 Null cell PitNET/adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: pituitary transcription factor and hormone–immunonegative PitNET/adenoma.

Not recommended: immunonegative PitNET/adenoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Null cell PitNETs are sellar tumours with frequent suprasellar and parasellar growth, sometimes with cavernous and/or sphenoidal sinus extension.

Clinical features

Like patients with other non-functioning PitNETs, patients with null cell tumours usually present with symptoms of mass effect, including visual disturbance, headaches, and various degrees of hypopituitarism. They show no clinical symptoms of hormonal excess apart from occasional mild hyperprolactinaemia due to stalk section effect. A few cases are detected because of a neurological deterioration associated with pituitary apoplexy {31401793}.

Neuroimaging

Null cell tumours are usually large masses, often with suprasellar extension and compression of the optic chiasm; they are more likely to invade the cavernous sinus than comparable clinically silent gonadotroph tumours {31401793}.

Epidemiology

There are limited epidemiological data regarding null cell PitNETs as they are currently defined. With the complementary use of antibodies to pituitary transcription factors and anterior pituitary hormones, the proportion of null cell PitNETs has been reduced to < 5% {29434339; 33396509}. One study has shown a reduction to < 1% {26481628}. In a recent study, no tumour was classified as a null cell PitNET in a cohort of 171 well-characterized PitNETs {33852418}. These data support the previous anticipation that the number of null cell PitNETs/adenomas will diminish with methodological improvements {7456308}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

There are no known genetic or epigenetic factors involved in the pathogenesis of transcription factor and hormone–immunonegative PitNETs. The majority of previously diagnosed null cell adenomas probably represent silent, hormone-negative gonadotroph or corticotroph PitNETs that could not be recognized by using immunohistochemistry for pituitary hormones and transcription factors. This has also been suggested by recent molecular genetic studies {31883967; 33908609}.

Whether null cell tumours are pathogenetically distinct from their gonadotroph or corticotroph counterparts needs to be determined. Further molecular genetic studies are needed to show whether a proportion of tumours diagnosed as null cell tumours represent true null cell PitNETs originating from pituitary stem cells that show no signs of differentiation towards mature pituitary cell lineages.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of null cell tumours does not differ from that of other PitNETs. Null cell PitNETs are usually macrotumours. They are mainly solid tumours; however, cystic or haemorrhagic changes can occasionally be identified.

Histopathology

In general, the histopathology of null cell PitNETs does not differ from that of silent gonadotroph or corticotroph tumours. Occasionally, oncocytic change can be seen in the tumour cells. Neuroendocrine markers and synaptophysin, and usually also chromogranin A, are positive. However, immunostains for SF1, TPIT, PIT1, and all adenohypophysial hormones are negative.

Immunohistochemistry

When a tumour fulfils the criteria for null cell PitNET, the use of an expanded set of antibodies to transcription factors involved in pituitary cell differentiation, such as GATA2 {11910101}, GATA3 {30390035}, ER {9171231}, and insulin gene enhancer protein ISL1 {11910101}, is recommended. Positivity for GATA3 raises the possibility of paraganglioma. In such cases, the identification of keratin reactivity would support a PitNET, but these tumours are not always positive for keratins; therefore, tyrosine hydroxylase may be helpful for further distinction. Immunopositivity for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), CDX2 {20602288}, or serotonin {28719461} argues against the diagnosis of PitNET.

Differential diagnosis

Differential diagnosis from rare metastatic neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) is critically important and needs an extensive immunohistochemical panel. Most, but not all, NETs that are metastatic to the pituitary have an appropriate clinical history of a known extracranial primary, unique immunohistochemical features, and transcription factor marks of the tumour’s primary site of origin, as well as a generally a higher proliferation index than most PitNETs. In the rare patients with null cell PitNETs, imaging workup such as PET-CT to search for NET in other organs should be considered by the multidisciplinary team {31112312}. The important differential with a primary sellar paraganglioma is discussed above (see Immunohistochemistry).

Cytology

The cytological features of null cell tumours are identical to those of other PitNETs.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no molecular tests required to diagnose these tumours. Germline screening for inherited tumours should be considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a PitNET/adenoma with a lack of immunoreactivity for pituitary-lineage transcription factors and pituitary hormones.

Desirable: additional immunostains to rule out sellar paraganglioma and other neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs).

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour invasiveness based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}.

There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

There are data indicating that null cell PitNETs may be more invasive at the time of diagnosis, and associated with a more aggressive clinical course and shorter progression-free survival, than silent gonadotroph tumours {31401793}. However, data are based on limited case numbers, and no molecular genetic data have been provided to confirm a lack of pituitary cell lineage differentiation.
