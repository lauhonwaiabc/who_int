3.2.1.2A Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture

Definition

Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture is a benign, non-invasive, encapsulated follicular cell–derived neoplasm that is characterized by an intrafollicular papillary architecture, lacks nuclear features of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), and is often associated with autonomous hyperfunction.

ICD-O coding

8330/0 Follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture

ICD-11 coding

2F37.Y & XH0LM0 Other specified benign neoplasm of endocrine glands & Follicular adenoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: hyperfunctioning thyroid adenoma; toxic thyroid adenoma.

Not recommended: papillary thyroid adenoma; papillary hyperplastic thyroid nodule.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These neoplasms can occur anywhere in the thyroid gland. They may also arise in ectopic thyroid tissue, including satellite thyroid tissue (i.e. parasitic thyroid nodules) in the perithyroidal soft tissues.

Clinical features

Solitary autonomous hyperfunctioning follicular adenomas with papillary architecture occur predominantly in women and were initially described in young women at the age of menarche {6660352}. Most patients have subclinical hyperthyroidism and present with a thyroid mass; however, 15–60% of patients present with symptoms of hyperthyroidism, including cardiac signs and symptoms, nervousness, muscle weakness, heat intolerance, and weight loss {1130435; 4166326; 4167139; 8279452}. The rate of overt hyperthyroidism increases with the size of the adenoma {1130435; 4166326; 4167139; 8279452} and with iodine deficiency {11434717; 2040867; 21357288}. Thyroid hormone levels are variably elevated and, therefore, TSH levels are variably suppressed {4166326; 8279452}. On ultrasound they can be solid and isoechoic to hypoechoic with increased vascularity {33772338}. These neoplasms usually demonstrate increased radioiodide uptake compared with the adjacent gland (they are “hot”).

Epidemiology

These neoplasms have been reported to account for approximately 3% of thyroid nodules {8279452}. However, it should be acknowledged that the incidence of these tumours is difficult to assess because they are associated with a variable rate of clinical hyperthyroidism that is impacted by the rate of iodine deficiency {21357288}. Additionally, cases of hyperthyroidism attributed to “toxic nodular hyperplasia” actually represent a clonal hyperfunctioning follicular adenoma arising in multinodular goitre {9467563}.

Etiology

The etiology of these lesions is usually unknown. A few have arisen in the setting of thyroid FND (multinodular goitre) {9467563} and some occur in patients with McCune–Albright syndrome, Carney complex, and DICER1 syndrome {2229316; 29474644; 29635258}.

Pathogenesis

These neoplasms are caused by genetic alterations that stimulate thyroid hormone synthesis and secretion as well as follicular cell proliferation. Activating TSHR mutations are detected in as many as 70%, whereas GNAS mutations are found in a small subset {8413627; 11434721; 16990652}. These mutations result in the activation of adenylyl cyclase, which leads to increased intracellular cAMP, which, in turn, produces unrestrained stimulation of thyroid follicular cell function and proliferation {9297624}. Additionally, almost 30% of these tumours harbour an EZH1 mutation in combination with a TSHR or a GNAS mutation {27500488}. EZH1 mutations have been shown to result in increased histone H3 trimethylation and increased proliferation of thyroid cells. In patients with McCune–Albright syndrome, germline mosaicism for activating mutations in GNAS are implicated {2229316; 15126527}. In Carney complex, germline inactivating mutations in PRKAR1A result in constitutive activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway through loss of regulation of the catalytic subunits of PKA {30428497}. The pathogenesis of tumours lacking TSHR, GNAS, or PRKAR1A mutations is not established.

Macroscopic appearance

These neoplasms are usually well-delineated thyroid nodules that may have a distinct capsule. The cut surface is usually beefy-red with colloid translucence. There may be foci of haemorrhage and cystic degeneration, especially if the lesion has been biopsied before resection. In McCune–Albright syndrome, the tumours may be multiple.

Histopathology

These tumours have a characteristic morphology. They are usually cystic and show a mixture of follicular and papillary architecture that is neither random nor complex. Instead, the overarching theme is one of large follicles with complex papillary infoldings of the lining epithelium, with broad papillae showing an organized centripetal orientation and oedematous cores with embedded follicles. The formation of Sanderson polsters (subfollicles within follicles) is common. The colloid may appear bubbly and often has peripheral scalloping, consistent with the rapid resorption of colloid for active hormone synthesis. The follicular epithelial cells lining the papillae are crowded. If there is haemorrhage, the epithelial cells usually show a striking accumulation of haemosiderin, reflecting active uptake of colloid. The cells are often columnar and have variably vacuolated cytoplasm; oncocytic metaplasia has been reported in a few cases. The nuclei, which are often basally located, are round and uniform in size and shape. They lack clearing and peripheral margination of chromatin. Intranuclear grooves and pseudoinclusions are not seen. There may be focal nuclear atypia around sites of degeneration, but this is considered to be reactive and should not alter the diagnosis. Psammoma bodies are not seen in these tumours. The presence of nuclear enlargement, clearing, contour irregularities, grooves, pseudoinclusions, or psammoma bodies should prompt a diagnosis of PTC {6660352; 21563322; 23599721; 24891128; 30559722}. These neoplasms generally lack immunoreactivity for HBME1, galectin-3, and BRAF p.V600E (VE1); CK19 can be focally positive, especially in cases that have undergone preoperative biopsy {20181018; 25041837; 27904595; 29099711}.

Cytology

FNA specimens are usually cellular and show a monotonous population of follicular cells, some with oncocytic change, arranged in non-branching papillary groups in a background of thin colloid and macrophages. The cells usually have readily identifiable prominent nucleoli, especially those with oncocytic change. Rare cases of this neoplasm on FNA can show intranuclear grooves and nuclear chromatin clearing. Because of the papillary architecture and atypical nuclear features, some cases are mistaken for PTC {18996814; 21563322; 24891128}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

TSHR, DICER1, and GNAS mutations can be detected in FNA specimens of these neoplasms diagnosed as atypical or suspicious for PTC. This molecular profile usually triggers investigations to confirm the possibility of a hyperfunctioning nodule {25209362; 33460435; 33901345}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an encapsulated thyroid neoplasm; follicular epithelial cells with an organized intrafollicular papillary architecture, with subfollicle formation; broad papillae with oedematous cores; no nuclear atypia of PTC; no capsular invasion; no psammoma bodies.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Like conventional follicular adenomas, these are benign tumours that do not recur once resected.
