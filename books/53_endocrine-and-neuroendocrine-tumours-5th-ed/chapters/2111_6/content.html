2.1.1.1 Somatotroph PitNET/adenoma

Definition

Somatotroph pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET)/adenoma is a well-differentiated PitNET composed of PIT1-lineage adenohypophysial cells with somatotroph differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8272/3 Somatotroph PitNET/adenoma

8272/3 Densely granulated somatotroph tumour

8272/3 Sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures

Related terminology

Acceptable: somatotroph tumour.

Not recommended: growth hormone–positive PitNET/adenoma; growth hormone–producing PitNET/adenoma; growth hormone–producing tumour/adenoma; growth hormone tumour/adenoma; acidophil tumour/adenoma; somatotropinoma.

Subtype(s)

Densely granulated somatotroph tumour; sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour

Localization

The majority of somatotroph tumours are located in the sellar region and show variable extension into adjacent structures including but not limited to cavernous sinuses, bone, and sinonasal mucosa. Extrasellar ectopic somatotroph tumours (e.g. clivus, sphenoidal sinus) and those arising in teratomas (e.g. ovarian teratoma) occur rarely {15347217; 22990736; 24119925; 32504302; 32632841}.

Clinical features

Most somatotroph tumours are biochemically active and cause acromegaly and/or gigantism; however, rare silent forms have been reported {25716461; 26481628}. The clinical diagnosis is often delayed; the median delay from first symptoms to diagnosis was 8 years in male patients and 2 years longer in female patients {28733467}. Headache, visual field deficits, mild hyperprolactinaemia, hypopituitarism, and pituitary apoplexy have been described in invasive somatotroph macrotumours {22697380; 22903574; 24251123; 25356808}.

Irrespective of functional status, pituitary tumours of somatotroph differentiation are divided into two clinicopathologically relevant histological subtypes on the basis of their density of growth hormone (GH)-containing secretory granules and the organization of their cytoplasmic keratin filaments using low-molecular-weight cytokeratin reactivity patterns: (1) densely granulated somatotroph tumours and (2) sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours. Importantly, the size of the tumour and the age at diagnosis vary with tumour type {34067494}. Compared with their sparsely granulated counterparts, which are more often large and invasive at presentation, densely granulated somatotroph tumours are smaller, less frequently invade the cavernous sinus, and rarely compress the optic chiasm {25556181}. On average, patients with densely granulated somatotroph tumours are 50 years of age at diagnosis, whereas those with sparsely granulated tumours present 10 years earlier {22200580; 23288882; 24925503; 25129651; 29434339}. The delay may be due to the less rapid and less aggressive growth associated with densely granulated tumours {34067494}.

Biochemical investigations

Virtually all patients with gigantism and/or acromegaly have increased serum GH and IGF1 levels. Serum IGF1 levels are relatively stable, whereas GH concentrations fluctuate. Oral glucose intake suppresses GH secretion in healthy individuals, but patients with functioning somatotroph tumours lack the characteristic full suppression. Therefore, the biochemical diagnosis of GH hypersecretion is based on an IGF1 level above an established age- and sex- reference range and confirmed by an oral glucose tolerance test where GH level fails to be suppressed below 0.4 µg/L {31766255; 25356808}. High IGF1 levels have been reported in pregnancy and the late adolescent period {25356808}.

When an ectopic source of GHRH is suspected as the cause of acromegaly {3145190; 8126126; 22983831}, plasma GHRH over the threshold of 250–300 ng/L has been shown to have excellent specificity {22442262; 23122576}. In addition, the absence of GH response to exogenous GHRH may help distinguish GHRH-related acromegaly from a primary somatotroph tumour, because there is typically no response in ectopic acromegaly {23122576}.

Neuroimaging

Because of the insidious nature of acromegaly, most somatotroph tumours are macrotumours (> 10 mm) at the time of diagnosis {7934807; 25905322; 25556181}; rarely, microtumours can be difficult to identify on conventional MRI studies {20610592}. A contrast-enhanced sellar MRI study with T1- and T2-weighted sequences is important in the evaluation of acromegaly {25356808}. Radiopathological correlations suggest that densely granulated tumours tend to be hypointense on T2 sequence {21722151; 22066905; 25556181} and responsive to somatostatin analogues {8548053; 16118335; 31766255; 22697380; 23288882}. In contrast, sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours are usually hyperintense on T2 sequence {21722151; 25556181; 22066905; 22697380; 33074402} and tend to be resistant to first-generation somatostatin analogue therapy {8548053; 16118335; 24144285; 26177314; 31766255}.

The normal pituitary shows physiological somatostatin uptake on functional studies (e.g. 68Ga-DOTATATE or 68Ga-DOTATOC PET-CT), complicating assessment of intrasellar tumours. In aggressive somatotroph tumours with an unresectable progressive extrasellar component that fails conventional therapies, functional imaging studies may be considered to rationalize the possibility of somatostatin-based peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) {26636388}.

A normal or bulky pituitary gland without gadolinium enhancement should prompt GHRH measurement and a search for a GHRH-secreting neoplasm, because this may indicate hyperplasia rather than neoplasia. In young patients with normal MRI and acromegaly, X-linked acrogigantism should also be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of acromegaly is 125–137 cases per 1 million population {16968795; 23239049; 26423473}. Gigantism due to GH excess is rare and typically occurs during childhood or puberty, before the closure of the epiphysial plate {25356808; 25905378}. Somatotroph tumours can arise in all age groups {29434339}; however, in acromegaly the median age at diagnosis is 45.2 years, with a slight predominance (54.5%) in women {28733467}. As indicated above, the age at diagnosis differs between the two morphological subtypes. At the time of diagnosis, men were significantly younger than women in two series {28733467; 31677093}.

Traditionally, somatotroph tumours were reported to account for about 10–15% of all resected PitNETs {19400692; 26522017; 25084775}. In a large Canadian series, somatotroph tumours accounted for about 12% of surgically resected PitNETs {29434339}, with almost equal numbers of densely and sparsely granulated subtypes. In several series, the incidence of GH-secreting pituitary tumours is higher (25–35%), but this figure is not limited to pure somatotroph tumours {30481158; 32154573}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown, except for those associated with genetic syndromes (see Pathogenesis) {33433883; 33805450}.

Pathogenesis

Most somatotroph tumours are sporadic and caused by somatic and epigenetic alterations {31883967; 33433883}. Somatic activating mutations of GNAS (GNAS1), which encodes the Gsα subunit of G proteins that mediate the signalling of seven-transmembrane-domain G protein–coupled receptors including GHRH, have been identified in as many as 40–60% of sporadic somatotroph tumours (traditionally called gsp mutations), typically in adult patients and frequently in densely granulated somatotroph tumours {8766942; 21722151; 23288882}. Postzygotic mosaic somatic activating GNAS mutations have been also implicated in McCune–Albright syndrome–related pituitary disease {22564667; 23478233; 24517150; 26177314}. These mutations result in constitutive activation of the cAMP/PKA pathway that leads to increased intracytoplasmic cAMP levels, GH hypersecretion, and expression of the α-subunit of glycoprotein hormones in somatotrophs. Although the G protein–mediated high cAMP levels may explain the clinical responsiveness of densely granulated somatotroph tumours to somatostatin analogues, not all densely granulated somatotroph tumours harbour these mutations. Therefore, the histological subtype of a somatotroph tumour is considered to be a better predictor of somatostatin responsiveness than GNAS status {23288882}. Some authors reported that differential SSTR expression between densely and sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours may also account for the response to somatostatin analogues {23847328; 26586796}. Somatic GHR mutations that alter GH autoregulation and STAT signalling have been described in sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours {17671221; 24272682}.

AIP-related tumours are more commonly identified in young patients and are more frequently sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours {20685857; 23371967; 28220018; 32604740}. Somatic GNAS mutations were not identified in AIP-related PitNETs {26186299}, suggesting mutually exclusive aberrant signalling. An acromegalic patient with germline AIP mutation has been reported to harbour a mixed gangliocytoma and sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour {23674160}.

Epigenetic alterations involving RB1, DAPK1 (DAPK), CDKN2A (encoding p16), GADD45G, RHBDD3 (PTAG), THBS1 (TSP1), RASSF1 (RASSF1A), FGFR2, MGMT, CASP8, TP73, and the gene(s) encoding p14 have been described in somatotroph tumours {24367530}. However, most of these alterations are not specific to somatotroph tumours {24367530}. Downregulation of several microRNAs targeting HMGA1, HMGA2, and E2F1 was also identified {22564666}. Initially isolated in rat pituitary GH4 cells {9092795}, the pituitary tumour transforming gene PTTG1 (PTTG) has been shown to have higher mRNA levels in somatotroph tumours than in non-functioning tumours {12590639}. Subsequent studies demonstrated that PTTG expression was indeed higher in various hormone-secreting invasive PitNETs {22822048}.

Transcriptome enrichment analyses identified high interferon signatures in somatotroph tumours that were also reflected in specific microRNA clusters, including but not limited to the MEG3 cluster and miR-148a {31883967}. GNAS-driven tumours were associated with a combination of hypomethylation and chromosomal changes {31883967}. Genomic imbalances were previously described at chromosomes 10q, 11q, and 13q {9062489; 10571521; 16388084}. Using whole-genome sequencing, no recurring mutations were seen, but an enrichment of sequence variants affecting the calcium- and ATP-related pathways, as well as chromosomal losses on chromosomes 1, 6, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, and 22, and chromosomal gains at chromosome 12 and the X chromosome, were documented {26280510}.

Dysregulation of FGFRs has been shown to play a role in the tumorigenesis of PitNETs {16857743; 22822048; 24272682}. Ikaros, a chromatin remodelling factor that plays an important role in the development of hypothalamic GHRH neurons {16467156}, has been shown to target FGFR4 promoter activity in somatotrophs {12937159}. The FGFR4-R388 polymorphism plays a role in the correlation between tumour size and hormone excess in somatotroph tumours {22174695}.

Genetic susceptibility

About 20% of cases of childhood gigantism and 8–11% of cases of acromegaly in young individuals have been associated with mutation in AIP, and in at least half there is a positive family history of familial isolated pituitary adenoma (FIPA) syndrome {20685857; 23371967; 25184284; 25248598; 26186299}. At least 80% of cases of very early onset paediatric gigantism (age < 5 years at diagnosis) have been linked to X-linked acrogigantism due to a microduplication containing the gene GPR101 {25470569; 26574647}. Patients with pituitary GH excess may also be encountered in the setting of syndromic presentations, such as Carney complex, McCune–Albright syndrome, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 {26187128; 33805450}. In contrast to lactotroph tumours, somatotroph tumours have rarely been encountered in association with germline pathogenic SDH gene variants {28220018; 30155846}, and they can be seen in association with paraganglioma/phaeochromocytoma {30155846}. Rare examples of somatotroph tumours have been reported in association with germline MAX mutations (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5) {29535143}. A patient with a densely granulated somatotroph tumour was reported to harbour a TMEM127 variant (c.245-10C>G) {25695889}.

Macroscopic appearance

Like other PitNETs, somatotroph tumours are white to grey and distinct from the normal pituitary gland; they are generally soft, explaining the relative preservation of normal pituitary function. Large tumours show variable degrees of invasion into adjacent anatomical structures {25014437}. Tumours from patients with pituitary apoplexy exhibit haemorrhagic necrosis. Posttreatment tumours may be firm, owing to variable stromal fibrosis.

Histopathology

In both subtypes, the tumour is positive for GH (details below), diffusely positive for PIT1, and negative for other PIT1-lineage hormones such as prolactin and TSH-β, as well as other pituitary-lineage transcription factors (TPIT, SF1, ERα, GATA3, GATA2) and hormones (ACTH, FSH-β, LH-β) {29434339; 29453601}.

Rarely, somatotroph tumours occur in association with gangliocytomas {23674160; 28079576; 31635149}, pituitary hyperplasia, or a distinct synchronous PitNET {10698050; 11081204; 15212641; 20506337; 23512282; 30215160}.

Densely granulated somatotroph tumours

These tumours are composed of cells that have abundant eosinophilic (acidophilic) cytoplasm, which correlates with their dense content of GH-containing secretory granules. Ultrastructurally, the tumour cells contain abundant large secretory granules measuring 400–600 nm [[Asa SL, Perry A. Tumors of the pituitary gland. Rockville (MD): American Registry of Pathology; 2020. (AFIP atlas of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 1).]].

Immunohistochemistry

Densely granulated somatotroph tumours are positive for PIT1 (diffuse nuclear staining), GH (diffuse and strong cytoplasmic staining), and the α-subunit of glycoprotein hormones (often diffuse and strong) {29434339}. Low-molecular-weight cytokeratin (CAM5.2 or CK18) usually exhibits predominant perinuclear cytoplasmic reactivity {18629656; 22697380; 24144285; 26177314; 29434339; 33098891}. These tumours are negative for CK19 and CK20 {33098891}.

The term “intermediate-type somatotroph tumour” has been proposed for a subset of densely granulated somatotroph tumours that display occasional (< 70% of the tumour cells and usually only scattered) fibrous bodies, which are defined as spherical juxtanuclear keratin aggregates. These fibrous bodies are usually associated with abundant cytoplasmic keratin filaments, unlike the situation in sparsely granulated tumours. Because intermediate-type somatotroph tumours were found not to be biologically different from densely granulated somatotroph tumours, most experts consider them within the morphological spectrum of densely granulated somatotroph tumours {18629656; 22697380}.

Sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours

These tumours are composed of loosely cohesive cells with chromophobic to pale eosinophilic cytoplasm. Variable nuclear pleomorphism including multinucleated cells can be seen. One of the characteristics of these tumours is the presence of abundant fibrous bodies, which may be recognized on H&E-stained slides, as the nuclei are typically indented by these pale globular eosinophilic structures. Ultrastructurally, sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours contain only a few secretory granules, which range in size from 100 to 250 nm. Fibrous bodies are spherical aggregates of intermediate filaments with trapped secretory granules and endoplasmic reticulum [[Asa SL, Perry A. Tumors of the pituitary gland. Rockville (MD): American Registry of Pathology; 2020. (AFIP atlas of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 1).]].

Immunohistochemistry

Consistent with the sparse granulation pattern, immunoreactivity for GH is variable and ranges from weak to focal or patchy reactivity {29434339}. Unlike densely granulated tumours, these tumours lack α-subunit expression {7509101; 1446651; 22697380; 29434339}. Because the extent of GH expression can be very scarce or even absent in some sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours, the identification of diffuse fibrous bodies (see below) and the absence of α-subunit, prolactin, and TSH-β can help distinguish sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours from immature PIT1-lineage pituitary tumours.

In sparsely granulated tumours, low-molecular-weight cytokeratins reveal characteristic fibrous bodies in the vast majority (> 70%) of the tumour cells {18629656; 22697380; 26177314; 29434339}, with scant or no cytoplasmic positivity outside these structures. A recent study of keratin profiling of PitNETs showed that CK18 expression is less intense, with more delicate staining in fibrous bodies than CAM5.2 {33098891}. Like all PIT1-lineage pituitary tumours, these tumours are negative for CK19 and CK20 {33098891}, and unlike densely granulated somatotroph tumours, they show epigenetic downregulation of E-cadherin {12665652; 15281343; 17873891}, a feature that explains their discohesive growth.

In contrast to densely granulated tumours, the majority of sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours have a Ki-67 labelling index > 3% {29434339}.

From a pathogenetic perspective, the application of molecular immunohistochemistry tools may be used to rationalize further germline screening in select tumours as discussed in the introduction to this chapter. Somatotroph tumours with cytoplasmic vacuoles may benefit from SDHB immunohistochemistry {22170724; 24625421; 33805450}, because a loss of SDHB immunohistochemistry has been regarded as a surrogate biomarker of SDH-driven neoplasms {30421319}.

The diagnosis of a sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour in a young patient should prompt genetic testing for AIP-related pathogenesis (FIPA syndrome) {23371967}; however, AIP immunohistochemistry is not recommended, because it is associated with poor sensitivity in the detection of AIP mutations {19556287}, and this protein is epigenetically downregulated in sporadic tumours.

Although routine SSTR immunohistochemistry is not required for the management of patients with somatotroph tumours, staining for SSTR2 and SSTR5 has been shown to predict responsiveness to somatostatin analogues {28176162; 30346538; 32375119}.

Differential diagnosis

An important differential diagnosis in patients with acromegaly and gigantism is hyperplasia. The distinction between neoplasia and hyperplasia relies on the demonstration of a disrupted reticulin framework in tumours, whereas hyperplasia typically features an expanded but preserved reticulin framework {22697380} usually associated with trapped non-tumorous adenohypophysial cells of all types. The presence of somatotroph or mammosomatotroph hyperplasia in association with a neoplasm may be a harbinger of a GHRH-producing neoplasm or genetic conditions such as Carney complex, McCune–Albright syndrome {7584515; 22564667; 24012779; 24517150; 26177314}, FIPA {21450940}, or X-linked acrogigantism {25470569}. Somatotroph hyperplasia is uncommon in patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, but when it occurs in this setting, it is frequently caused by a GHRH-producing pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (PanNET) {18300794; 2887080}. Nonetheless, it should be noted that hyperplasia is only rarely encountered in surgical pituitary specimens. Similarly, the association of GHRH-producing phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma and pituitary acromegaly has been reported in a patient with germline pathogenic MAX variant (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5) {33367756}. A rare GHRH-producing pulmonary neuroendocrine tumour (NET) metastatic to the pituitary associated with somatotroph hyperplasia and adenomatous transformation has also been reported {16968791}.

Cytology

Functioning somatotroph tumours usually do not require intraoperative consultation; however, when performed, it should be done by a smear that yields an even cellular spread. Densely granulated somatotroph tumours have abundant acidophilic granular cytoplasm; sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours have distinctive fibrous bodies identifiable on smears.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There are no molecular tests required to diagnose somatotroph tumours. Germline screening for inherited somatotroph tumours should be considered on the basis of clinicopathological findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Densely granulated somatotroph tumours

Essential: PIT1 positivity; diffuse GH expression; absence of other pituitary cell differentiation; perinuclear low-molecular-weight cytokeratin staining; acidophilic cytoplasm on H&E-stained sections.

Desirable: α-subunit expression.

Sparsely granulated somatotroph tumours

Essential: PIT1 positivity; variable GH expression; absence of other pituitary cell differentiation; abundant fibrous bodies (> 70% of the tumour cells) using low-molecular-weight cytokeratin stains; absence of acidophilic cytoplasm on H&E-stained sections.

Desirable: absence of α-subunit expression.

Staging

There is no formal staging system that has been universally applied for pituitary/sellar neoplasms. A CT-era approach based on tumour size and patterns of invasion was proposed in 1973 by Hardy [[Hardy J. Transsphenoidal surgery of hypersecreting pituitary tumours. In: Kohler PO, Ross GT, editors. Diagnosis and treatment of pituitary tumors. International Congress Series No. 303. Amsterdam (Netherlands): Exerpta Medica; 1973. pp. 179–98.]]. Subsequently, a standardized assessment of parasellar tumour invasiveness based on MRI was published by Knosp in 1993 {8232800}. There are no pathology-standardized reporting templates yet endorsed by international pathology organizations. A pathology reporting template for pituitary tumours was introduced in 2011 {21526962}, followed by the European Pituitary Pathology Group (EPPG) proposal in 2019 {31578606}.

Prognosis and prediction

Uncontrolled GH and/or IGF1 secretion causes significant morbidity in patients with functioning somatotroph tumours. Although the first-line treatment option for somatotroph tumours is surgical resection {25014437}, cure often cannot be achieved, particularly in large tumours and those with cavernous sinus invasion {25014437}. Therefore, the completeness of tumour resection and degree of extrasellar tumour extension, the histological subtype (densely granulated vs sparsely granulated somatotroph tumour), tumour growth rate (e.g. residual tumour growth on imaging studies), and response to medical therapy are all important prognostic factors {22822048; 24144285; 26177314; 30657362; 33074402}.

The accurate histological subtyping of a somatotroph tumour is of clinical significance, because pharmacological GH antagonism with pegvisomant is considered in patients with sparsely granulated tumours, which often lack satisfactory responsiveness to somatostatin analogues {24144285; 24925503; 26177314}. The tumour subtype on histopathology that correlates with T2 hyperintensity on MRI studies {21722151; 22066905; 25556181} and the status of SSTR2/5 expression are well-recognized predictors of responsiveness to somatostatin analogues {28176162; 30346538; 32375119}.
