9.0.3.2B Angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is a highly malignant tumour showing endothelial differentiation.

ICD-O coding

9120/3 Angiosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B56.Y & XH6264 Angiosarcoma, other specified primary site & Haemangiosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: malignant haemangioendothelioma; haemangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Tumours primary to endocrine organs are often centred in the organ rather than involving the periphery of the organ secondarily.

Clinical features

Angiosarcoma of endocrine organs predominantly affects older patients, often those in their sixth or seventh decade of life {8279629; 29264447; 29909901}. Patients with thyroid angiosarcoma are more commonly female, whereas adrenal angiosarcoma affects both sexes similarly {8279629}. Primary thyroid angiosarcoma may present as painless thyroid enlargement, or as a rapidly enlarging neck mass with compressive symptoms of dysphagia or dyspnoea. Paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia, transient increase of blood sugar, and leukocytosis are not uncommon {26408676; 29909901}. Patients with primary thyroid angiosarcoma often have a history of goitre {29909901}. Primary adrenal angiosarcoma presents as an abdominal mass with or without pain, fever, weight loss, and weakness {8279629}. Angiosarcoma involving the pituitary gland can be associated with panhypopituitarism and intracranial hypertension {29264447}. Primary angiosarcoma of the pancreas may present with nonspecific symptoms, abdominal epigastric pain, and nausea, and it can mimic acute pancreatitis {25541144; 33032040}. Pancreatic angiosarcoma has been reported as an incidental finding in an individual undergoing imaging for metastatic breast cancer {30631836}.

Epidemiology

Angiosarcoma, the most common primary thyroid sarcoma, is a rare tumour {26408676}. Older series have reported a relative high incidence in Alpine regions of Switzerland, Italy, and Austria, in association with endemic goitre {29909901}. Adrenal angiosarcomas are very rare, without well-defined epidemiological characteristics. Sarcomas account for < 0.1% of all pancreatic malignancies, and fewer than 10 primary pancreatic angiosarcomas have been reported {18665093; 25541144; 27228987; 29858730; 30631836; 33032040}. An exceptional example of pituitary angiosarcoma has also been reported {29264447}. With so few angiosarcomas reported in endocrine organs other than the thyroid, well-defined epidemiological characteristics for angiosarcomas at these sites have yet to be established.

Etiology

Iodine deficiency is a likely etiological factor for primary thyroid angiosarcoma, at least for cases that arise in longstanding goitres in areas where the goitre is endemic {29909901}.

Pathogenesis

Primary thyroid angiosarcoma is a distinct entity. Although historically it was debated whether primary thyroid angiosarcoma was an angiomatoid subtype of anaplastic thyroid carcinoma rather than a true sarcoma with endothelial differentiation, thyroid angiosarcoma and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma are now regarded as two distinct and unrelated entities. Unlike anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, angiosarcomas of the thyroid harbour few mutations, and in particular they lack mutations of TP53 and/or TERT {30723294}. Because of the rarity of primary angiosarcomas of other endocrine organs, little is known about their pathogenesis and underlying genetic abnormalities.

Macroscopic appearance

Thyroid angiosarcomas may appear deceptively circumscribed, but they are invariably infiltrative lesions. Extension into adjacent structures is common {1696070}. The cut surface is haemorrhagic and variegated, with cystic and solid areas which may be necrotic. Thyroid angiosarcomas range in size from 30 to 120 mm {8917714; 30723294}. Adrenal angiosarcomas range from relatively small to massive, haemorrhagic masses {8279629}. Angiosarcomas of the pancreas are variably sized and may involve any part of the gland {18665093; 25541144; 27228987; 29858730; 30631836; 33032040}.

Histopathology

Angiosarcomas primary to endocrine organs have the same general features as angiosarcomas at any other anatomical site. They may be well-differentiated and vasoformative, or poorly differentiated, with chiefly epithelioid or spindled morphology. Diffusely infiltrative growth into surrounding non-neoplastic tissues is the rule. Although some cases have relatively subtle degrees of nuclear atypia, most are obviously malignant, with high nuclear grade and easily identified mitotic figures. Tumours with epithelioid differentiation display cells with abundant eosinophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm, large nuclei with well-defined nuclear membranes, and prominent nucleoli. They are common, and particularly well characterized in the thyroid gland {1696070; 29909901; 18766472}. Predominantly epithelioid angiosarcomas may be easily confused with primary epithelial malignancies at all endocrine locations {29264447; 29340997}. Angiosarcoma of the thyroid may have surrounding areas of multinodular goitre and must be distinguished from reactive vascular proliferation and anaplastic carcinoma {18766472; 30723294}. Adrenal angiosarcomas have been misdiagnosed as adrenal cortical carcinomas and must also be distinguished from metastatic tumours {26472162}.

Immunohistochemistry

Angiosarcomas express the panendothelial markers CD31, CD34, FLI1, von Willebrand factor (factor VIII–related antigen), and ERG, and they may be positive for the lymphatic marker podoplanin (D2-40). Expression of low-molecular-weight keratins is seen in approximately 50% of angiosarcomas, typically those with epithelioid features {1696070; 8279629; 25541144; 29264447; 29340997; 30723294}. Angiosarcomas of the thyroid lack expression of thyroid-related markers (thyroglobulin, thyroid transcription factor 1 [TTF1], PAX8). Expression of MYC (c-myc) may occasionally be present but is not helpful in the distinction of angiosarcoma from non-vascular malignancies at endocrine sites {30723294}. For the differential diagnosis, a panel of immunohistochemical markers including those of endothelial lineage, organ-specific markers (e.g. TTF1, pituitary hormones, pancreatic hormones), and endocrine/neuroendocrine markers (e.g. SF1, synaptophysin, chromogranin A) is essential.

Cytology

FNAB of angiosarcoma may be highly cellular, and there is often a bloody and variably necrotic background. Cells are arranged in loosely cohesive groups and can be spindled, plasmacytoid, or epithelioid with abundant cytoplasm; scattered binucleated cells with prominent nucleoli may be seen. Mitotic figures may be present. The diagnosis of angiosarcoma should be made with great caution in patients who have undergone prior FNAB, because biopsy-related reactive stromal changes can closely mimic a malignant vascular tumour {18766472; 28514786}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant vascular neoplasm showing histological and/or immunohistochemical evidence of endothelial differentiation.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system is used.

Prognosis and prediction

Primary angiosarcomas of endocrine organs are highly aggressive tumours, with rapid growth, extensive local invasion and infiltration of adjacent organs, regional and distant metastasis, and a poor outcome with death often within 6 months, although cases with long survival have been reported {18766472; 26408676; 29909901}.
