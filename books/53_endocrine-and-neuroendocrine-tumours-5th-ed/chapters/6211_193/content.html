6.2.1.1 Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Definition

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a proliferation of adrenal cortical cells caused by mutations in genes involved in the enzymatic steps of steroid production in the adrenal cortex.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

5A71.01 Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Related terminology

Not recommended: adrenogenital syndrome.

Subtype(s)

21-hydroxylase deficiency (classic type and non-classic type); 11β-hydroxylase deficiency; 17α-hydroxylase deficiency; 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase deficiency; P450 oxidoreductase deficiency; congenital lipoid adrenal hyperplasia; P450 cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme deficiency

Localization

Adrenal glands become enlarged if untreated. Adrenal cortical neoplasms and myelolipomas may also occur. Testes can become enlarged by adrenal cortical rest–related proliferations. In rare instances, adrenal rests occur in the ovaries or retroperitoneum, and other unusual places along the adrenal embryonic and gonadal pathways may be affected {34095993; 28032206; 33809106}.

Clinical features

Infants with classic CAH, the most severe form of CAH, are usually identified by neonatal screening (elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone) and may have ambiguous genitalia (female), enlarged genitalia (male), virilization, poor feeding, weight loss, dehydration, hyponatraemia, and hyperkalaemia {32966723; 28576284; 29718004}. Because of the complexity of the CYP21A2 locus, genotyping is not used for first-line screening {31333583; 32966723}.

Mutations in CYP21A2, which encodes the enzyme 21-hydroxylase, cause 95–99% of cases of CAH, resulting in hypocortisolism and (in 75% of cases) hypoaldosteronism. Hypoaldosteronism results in the inability to retain water and salt (salt wasting), which can result in hypotension, shock, and even death due to adrenal crisis. A variable degree of hyperandrogenism is present in all patients and can result in ambiguous external genitalia in female patients and normal or enlarged genitalia in male patients. The simple virilizing form of CAH is generally not associated with overt salt wasting.

The prevalence of adrenal cortical tumours and myelolipomas in CAH is approximately 30% {33471666; 32339029}. Of the adrenal tumours, 25% are myelolipomas, and > 90% of individuals with these have undiagnosed or poorly managed CAH {33471666}. The overall prevalence of myelolipomas in CAH may be increased, and they tend to occur at a younger age {33471666}. Testicular adrenal rest tumours (TARTs) occur in a subset of patients and vary based on the severity of the underlying CYP21A2 mutation {33524003}. A recent summary of 33 original articles reported that the prevalence of TARTs ranges from 14% to 86%, depending on genotype. The prevalence of TARTs was related to the severity of disease, with the highest prevalence in the most severely affected individuals, and with the overall prevalence of TARTs being 37% of male patients with CAH {30882882}. Only a few TARTs are reported in non-classic CAH {30882882}.

Non-classic CAH is much more common and milder than classic salt-wasting CAH or classic simple virilizing CAH. Classic CAH is identified in infants, whereas individuals with non-classic CAH may not be symptomatic until childhood or adulthood, if ever. Those presenting in childhood may have precocious puberty and an increased growth rate, and those presenting later may have amenorrhoea or oligomenorrhoea, hirsutism, and decreased fertility {32966723}. Gynaecomastia can be the first presentation in male patients with non-classic CAH {28582566}.

In 10% of individuals with CAH, there is disruption of CYP21A2 and TNXB, resulting in CAH-X syndrome, in which features of both CAH and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome may be present, because TNXB encodes tenascin X {31666125; 29734195; 33332743}.

Epidemiology

Classic CAH occurs in 1 in 14 000–18 000 people, with a ratio of salt-wasting to simple virilizing types of 2.7:1 {9521938}, whereas non-classic CAH occurs in 1 in 200 people {30272171; 28541281}.

Etiology

CAH is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by inactivating germline mutations in genes encoding enzymes in the adrenal cortical steroidogenesis pathway {20981249; 33961029}.

Pathogenesis

In CAH, the disruption of adrenal cortical steroidogenesis due to enzyme defects results in the aberrant production of adrenal cortical hormones (see #27942Table 5.01). The most commonly affected gene is CYP21A2, located on chromosome 6p21.33 {33961029}, and it accounts for 95–99% of mutations in all patients with CAH {20981249; 33961029}. Mutations in this gene are most commonly caused by complex intergenic recombinations involving misalignments with the closely positioned CYP21A1P pseudogene {33961029; 7479886}. CYP21A2 encodes 21-hydroxylase, an enzyme responsible for the conversion of progesterone to deoxycorticosterone in the mineralocorticoid synthesis chain as well as the formation of 11-deoxycortisol from 17-hydroxyprogesterone in the glucocorticoid production chain. Biallelic mutations will therefore severely affect the adrenal production of both aldosterone and cortisol, and an accumulation of 17-hydroxyprogesterone will lead to an increase in the production of androgens, causing virilization. The reduction in cortisol causes an impeded negative feedback at the pituitary level, leading to a compensatory rise of ACTH and hyperplastic adrenals. Other genes associated with CAH are rare and include several other genes in the cortisol production chain, such as CYP11B1 (encoding 11β-hydroxylase), HSD3B2 (encoding 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase), and CYP17A1 (encoding 17α-hydroxylase) {33961029}.

Increased screening may contribute to the increased detection of adrenal tumours in people with CAH compared with the general population; however, the stimulatory effects of ACTH have been hypothesized, given the fact that many of these tumours arise in the background of severe cases with poor hormonal control {33961029; 32339029; 21689130}.

Macroscopic appearance

The adrenal glands are enlarged (10–15 g in children, 30–35 g in adults) and may have a cerebriform or convoluted surface due to cortical hyperplasia from trophic stimulation by ACTH {2973986}. CAH-associated adrenal cortical tumours appear similar to sporadic tumours but the background adrenal cortex may be hyperplastic. TARTs appear as yellow, firm, round to oval, sharply delineated nodules, often multinodular with fibrous bands, frequently located near or in the mediastinum testis, and bilateral {30882882}.

Histopathology

The adrenal glands (and accessory adrenal cortical tissue) may show varying histological features depending on whether or not the patient was treated with exogenous corticosteroids. If untreated, the adrenal glands show marked bilateral cortical compact cell hyperplasia characterized by expansion of the adrenal cortex with a lipid depletion pattern due to a zona reticularis–like compact cell predominance that imparts a dark colour to the adrenal glands [[Lack EE. Chapter 2: Congenital adrenal heterotopia, hyperplasia, and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome. In: Tumors of the adrenal glands and extraadrenal paraganglia. Washington, DC: American Registry of Pathology; 2007. (AFIP atlas of tumor pathology, series 4; fascicle 8).]]. Incomplete steroid replacement may result in a mixture of columns of zona fasciculata–like lipid-rich cells intermixed with lipid-depleted compact cell glands [[Lack EE. Chapter 2: Congenital adrenal heterotopia, hyperplasia, and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome. In: Tumors of the adrenal glands and extraadrenal paraganglia. Washington, DC: American Registry of Pathology; 2007. (AFIP atlas of tumor pathology, series 4; fascicle 8).]].

TARTs are composed of sheets and nests of large vacuolated cells with granular pink cytoplasm, often containing lipofuscin pigment, with round to oval nuclei and small nucleoli similar in appearance to those of Leydig cells {33961029; 22157069; 29850650; 30882882}. TARTs and Leydig cell tumours cannot be reliably differentiated by histomorphology, although Reinke crystalloids, which may be seen in Leydig cell tumours, are absent in TARTs {30882882}. TARTs are more often bilateral than testicular Leydig cell tumours are (77% vs < 10%) {30882882}. In addition to the expression of adrenal cortical steroidogenesis enzymes (CYP11B1 and CYP21A2), an absence of INSL3 expression and an abundance of DLK1 may help distinguish TART from Leydig cell tumours {25609776}; however, these biomarkers are not widely available in most clinical laboratories. Therefore, a clinical history of CAH and assessment of the testicular parenchyma may be helpful.

Cytology

Not applicable

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genetic testing is considered to accurately determine the specific mutation in affected individuals.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Clinical: physical and laboratory findings confirming the diagnosis of CAH, including hypocortisolism and hypoaldosteronism in association with elevated 17-hydroxyprogesterone for classic 21-hydroxylase deficiency, and cosyntropin stimulation test for non-classic or milder subtypes (see #27942Table 5.01). Pathological: adrenal gland enlargement with variable degrees of compact cell hyperplasia in the appropriate clinical context and with the typical laboratory findings.

Desirable: genetic testing for pathogenic variants of genes encoding steroidogenic enzymes.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Neonatal screening has decreased infant mortality and morbidity in CAH. CAH is treated to replace deficient hormones, reduce excessive androgens, and prevent adrenal crisis (commonly triggered by an infection). Exogenous glucocorticoids may result in a complication of iatrogenic Cushing syndrome {26045561}. Bilateral adrenalectomy with glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy, most often used to control hyperandrogenism/virilization and/or Cushing syndrome {29554355}. Deaths may occur due to adrenal crisis. Non-classic CAH is comparatively mild, not life-threatening, and treated symptomatically as required.
