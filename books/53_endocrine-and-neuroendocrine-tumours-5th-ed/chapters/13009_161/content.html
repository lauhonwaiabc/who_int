13.0.0.9 Carney complex

Definition

Carney complex (CNC) is an autosomal dominant tumour predisposition syndrome whose cardinal features include spotty skin pigmentation, myxomatosis, secretory endocrine tumours, and pigmented schwannomas.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

160980 Carney complex, type 1; CNC1

ICD-11 coding

5A70.Y Other specified Cushing syndrome

Related terminology

Not recommended: NAME syndrome (naevi, atrial myxoma, myxoid neurofibromas, and ephelides); LAMB syndrome (lentigines, atrial myxoma, mucocutaneous myoma, and blue naevus).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The majority of patients with CNC have multisystem disease involving endocrine organs (e.g. pituitary, thyroid, adrenal) and non-endocrine organs (e.g. skin, mucosae, heart, gonads, peripheral nerve sheath, bone).

Clinical features

For most patients with CNC, the disease takes a variable but indolent course, with various manifestations (see below and #28583Table 13.06) accumulating as the patient ages. Around one third of patients had no identifiable family history {2690418}. Patients with CNC typically manifest pigmentation before puberty; this is usually the earliest physical manifestation, and it may become less prominent in patients as they age {27943004}. Myxomatosis (including cardiac myxomas) and endocrine manifestations are often observed, beginning in the teenage years, although the endocrine manifestations may be subtle {32893266}.

Skin pigmentation (lentiginosis and pigmented naevi): Lentigines are pigmented cutaneous macules with variable appearances. They are typically located on the central face but may involve the lips and other mucosal surfaces, such as the inside of the mouth, the sclera, and the genitalia {27943004}. Pigmented or blue naevi are seen on the extremities and trunk, and less commonly on the head and neck {9711672}.

Myxomas: Cardiac myxomas in CNC are usually multifocal and are found in any cardiac chamber {24618615; 32893266; 24088910}. By the age of 30 years, about 35% of female patients and 45% of male patients have one myxoma, and recurrence is more common in female patients {32893266}. Myxomas may also occur in other sites, including the skin, external auditory canal, earlobe, eyelid, mucocutaneous boundaries, and breast {8116795; 15368482; 21880053}.

Primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD): PPNAD causes ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome, and cortisol levels may paradoxically increase in response to dexamethasone {19383776; 33776926; 31912137}. Patients may present with typical or cyclical symptoms of hypercortisolism {25425660}. The adrenal glands are not notably enlarged on imaging studies. CT and MRI findings may underestimate the presence of bilateral adrenal gland involvement {31912137; 31672302}. About 50% of affected patients may show an abnormal imaging finding {31912137}, including bilateral multiple bead-like small nodules (2–5 mm) {15026592}.

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET) / pituitary adenoma: CNC-related pituitary involvement may manifest with acromegaly (growth hormone excess) or with symptoms from mixed growth hormone and prolactin secretion {31877737; 33805450; 23315333; 33433883}. Patients may present with multiple pituitary adenomas / PitNETs or with pituitary hyperplasia {27509071; 31877737; 33433883}.

Thyroid nodules and cancer: Thyroid nodules are observed in 30–60% of patients with CNC {9215269; 19293268}. CNC joins the small group of dominantly inherited diseases, including Cowden syndrome, DICER1 syndrome, and hereditary familial polyposis, in which non-medullary thyroid neoplasia occurs. However, in patients with CNC, follicular thyroid carcinoma is more common than papillary thyroid carcinoma {18948764; 21455198; 29635258}. Like in other populations with thyroid nodules, cancers are diagnosed in about 10% of these nodules {19293268; 29635258}.

Gonadal tumours (large cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumour [LCCSCT] and ovarian cystadenoma or cystadenocarcinoma): LCCSCT occurs in a small percentage of male patients with CNC and presents as bilateral testicular masses {17848226; 11832717}. The tumours may have palpable calcifications and cause infertility. Ovarian lesions in CNC range from ovarian cysts to serous cystadenomas and, rarely, to malignant tumours including endometroid and mucinous adenocarcinomas {11095480}.

Nerve sheath tumours (psammomatous melanotic schwannoma): Psammomatous melanotic schwannomas are peripheral nerve sheath tumours that often affect the posterior spinal nerve roots, the alimentary tract (especially the oesophagus and stomach), bone, and skin. The majority of the tumours are symptomatic, causing either neurological symptoms or mass effect {2305928; 9711672}.

Osteochondromyxomas: These are bone tumours that tend to appear early in life in the craniofacial or long bones, and they may exhibit either a lytic or blastic appearance on imaging {11176065}.

Other manifestations: Tubular adenomas, ductal adenomas, myxomas, and myxoid fibroadenomas of the breast, as well as intraductal mucinous tumours of the pancreas and fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinomas, may also be seen in adults with CNC {19238031; 29222914}.

Epidemiology

CNC is a rare disease, with < 1000 patients described in the literature, and its true incidence and prevalence are unknown. An M:F ratio of 1:2 was documented in a large series of 353 patients {2690418}. The median age of detection is about 20 years of age, although patients have been identified as early as 2 years of age and as late as the fifth decade of life {25905341}.

Etiology

CNC is a genetic condition involving germline pathogenic mutation of PRKAR1A or, less commonly, of PDE8B or PDE11A {25905341; 26130139}. Most patients present in their teenage years, although diagnosis can occur in the very young or in older individuals. Cascade testing from clinically identified index cases decreases the age at diagnosis for patients with this inherited disease.

Pathogenesis

In vitro and in vivo studies have determined that tumours in patients with CNC are caused by activation of the PKA signalling pathway {12424709}. In many involved endocrine tissues, this signalling abnormality may result in the hormone hypersecretion that is seen clinically {31877737; 33534120; 33433883}. Because PKA functions in a tissue-specific fashion, tumours are limited to those tissues where this signalling pathway promotes growth {31877737; 33534120; 33433883; 20451576}.

Macroscopic appearance

In PPNAD, the adrenal glands show no enlargement but the gross examination can highlight bilateral small pigmented cortical nodules (often < 5 mm) {25425660; 29594118}. Pituitary adenomas / PitNETs of somatotroph cell lineage are soft with a white to grey cut surface. Pigmented melanotic schwannomas have a black, brown, or blue appearance and are usually encapsulated and solid or spongy. LCCSCTs may exhibit microcalcifications or macrocalcifications at the time of removal. Other tumours in CNC have a macroscopic appearance similar to that of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

PPNAD is characterized by bilateral cortical micronodules (often < 5 mm) enriched in compact (lipid-poor) adrenocortical cells, typically located in the zona fasciculata layer or in the intersection of the zona reticularis and zona fasciculata layers. CNC-related PPNAD is indistinguishable from isolated PPNAD at the histological level. However, PPNAD is distinguished from isolated micronodular adrenocortical disease (a rare form of bilateral micronodular adrenocortical disease) by the presence of pigmented nodules and internodular cortical atrophy {33534120; 33776926; 32115357}. Rarely, myelolipomas {27875378}, adrenal cortical adenomas (> 10 mm) {21442380; 27875378}, and adrenal cortical carcinomas {22112809; 22112814} have been described in the setting of CNC-related PPNAD. Cortical micronodules are positive for SF1, synaptophysin, α-inhibin, and melan-A {6329005; 24805858}.

PitNETs / pituitary adenomas are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts; however, most CNC-related PitNETs are often PIT1-lineage tumours with somatotroph or mammosomatotroph cell differentiation, often with densely granulated tumour cells {3343883; 31877737; 25592387}. The rare occurrence of hyperprolactinaemia in CNC has an unknown etiology but may be related to a possible lactotroph cell pathology {25592387; 33805450}. In CNC, the detailed documentation of other PitNET subtypes using modern classification tools is lacking. Pituitary hyperplasia of somatotroph or mammosomatotroph cells, as well as multifocal PitNETs, can be identified in CNC {27509071; 31877737; 33433883}. Individual tumours are subtyped according the criteria defined in the relevant sections within this volume.

Thyroid nodules typically appear as adenomatous nodules and follicular adenomas, including those with intrafollicular centripetal papillary growth (follicular thyroid adenoma with papillary architecture) that can manifest with hyperthyroidism {29635258; 22514108; 19293268}. Thyroid cancers may exhibit papillary or follicular histology {19293268; 21455198; 29239041} and are usually multicentric and bilateral, but they are not different to their sporadic counterparts (see #28575Box 13.02).

LCCSCTs show various patterns, varying from solid to trabecular. An intratubular or in situ component may be present. The tumour cells are large, with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and mitotic figures are uncommon. Distinctly laminated calcospherites are a common finding. Leydig cell tumours and adrenocortical rest tumours may also be seen {4010501}.

Lentiginosis exhibits localized hyperpigmentation of the basal layer epidermal keratinocytes and melanocyte hyperplasia. Other lesions may show coarse melanin granules in the epidermis. Hyperplasia of the epidermis with prominent rete pegs may also be present. Other pigmented naevi are poorly circumscribed, located in the dermis with two types of melanocytes: one heavily pigmented, globular, and fusiform; and the other lightly pigmented, polygonal, and spindle {8772778}. The melanocytes are usually located along the dermal collagen bundle.

Cardiac myxomas are hypocellular lesions with scattered polygonal and spindle tumour cells in a myxoid matrix. Extracardiac myxomas exhibit a similar appearance, with scattered polygonal cells, stellate cells, and spindle cells in a myxoid matrix. Capillaries are usually prominent. The cutaneous lesions, when in contact with the overlying epidermis, may induce epidermal proliferation into the dermis, which becomes incorporated into the myxoma.

Pigmented melanotic schwannoma presents microscopically as a well-circumscribed but incompletely encapsulated tumour composed of spindle and epithelioid cells with melanin pigment deposition, psammoma bodies, and adipose tissue. The spindle cells are arranged in interlacing fascicles and show whorling and occasionally nuclear palisading. They are usually positive for S100, SOX10, and vimentin, and negative for GFAP, actin, and keratin. Ultrastructural examination usually shows cells with elongated processes, continuous basal lamina, and melanosomes, as well as premelanosomes and intercellular long-spacing collagen.

Osteochondromyxomas exhibit a polymorphic histology, including areas of polygonal, stellate, or bipolar cells. Immature osteoid is present, with increased numbers of osteoclasts, indicating rapid bone remodelling. Areas of hyaline fibrosis may also be observed.

Cytology

The cytopathological features of the various neoplasms related to Carney complex are indistinguishable from those of their sporadic counterparts, which are described elsewhere in this volume and in the other fifth-edition volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

CNC can be diagnosed by the detection of pathogenic germline mutation of PRKAR1A or, less commonly, of PDE8B or PDE11A. Molecular diagnosis also includes gene copy testing for PRKACA or PRKACB {26130139}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

See #28574Box 13.03, .

 

Essential: at least two major criteria must be present to establish the diagnosis of CNC, and their occurrence must be confirmed biochemically, histologically, or by imaging, as indicated. In the presence of one supplemental criterion, a single clinical manifestation is sufficient to set the diagnosis.

Major diagnostic criteria:

Typical skin pigmentation (lentiginosis, or multiple blue or epithelioid blue naevi) Myxomas (cutaneous, mucosal, breast, or cardiac) Specific endocrine tumours or endocrine hyperactivity (PPNAD, PitNET / pituitary adenoma leading to acromegaly or evidence of acromegaly, LCCSCT or characteristic calcification on ultrasound, thyroid cancer or multiple thyroid nodules in young patients) Any of the other rare tumours, including pigmented melanotic schwannoma, osteochondromyxoma, and breast adenomas

Supplemental criteria:

An affected first-degree relative Pathogenic inactivating germline PRKAR1A mutation

Desirable (in patients with a clinicopathological diagnosis of CNC):

Germline pathogenic PRKAR1A sequence variants Germline pathogenic PDE8B or PDE11A sequence variants in PRKAR1A-wildtype patients Gene copy testing for PRKACA or PRKACB in PRKAR1A-wildtype patients

Staging

Eighth-edition Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours, like for their sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

Most of the tumours associated with CNC are benign and slow-growing. The exceptions to this notion are the schwannomas, which can behave more aggressively, although not nearly as aggressively as melanomas, with which they can be confused radiologically owing to their striking melanotic pigmentation. The other tumour type that can cause catastrophic acute consequences is cardiac myxoma, although this is a feature of these tumours in general and is not due to malignancy per se. In families and/or patients with multiple cardiac myxomas, complications from open heart surgery may also lead to a decrease in overall lifespan.
