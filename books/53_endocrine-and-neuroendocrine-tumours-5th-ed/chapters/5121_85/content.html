5.1.2.1 Pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma

Definition

Pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma (PanNEC) is a poorly differentiated, high-grade malignant epithelial neoplasm with diffuse neuroendocrine differentiation. Small cell and large cell subtypes occur.

ICD-O coding

8246/3 Neuroendocrine carcinoma, NOS

8041/3 Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

8013/3 Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C10.1 & XH0U20 Neuroendocrine neoplasms of pancreas & Neuroendocrine carcinoma, NOS

2C10.1 & XH9SY0 Neuroendocrine neoplasms of pancreas & Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

2C10.1 & XH0NL5 Neuroendocrine neoplasms of pancreas & Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: high-grade (poorly differentiated) neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC); large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC); small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC); small cell carcinoma.

Not recommended: high-grade neuroendocrine tumour.

Subtype(s)

Large cell PanNEC; small cell PanNEC

Localization

The majority of PanNECs occur in the head of the pancreas {24503751}.

Clinical features

The presentation of PanNECs is similar to that of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas {8789136; 24503751; 25723112; 26113608} and includes back pain, jaundice, and/or nonspecific abdominal symptoms {17383419; 22967994}. In contrast to well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), PanNECs are usually clinically non-functioning {4350960}. Serum calcitonin level may be elevated in some patients {29063495}. SSTRs (in particular SSTR2) usually are not expressed {28059098}, which may be helpful in distinguishing PanNECs from PanNETs, because functional imaging studies such as SSTR scintigraphy (111In-pentetreotide [Octreoscan]) and 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT {25786743; 33433884; 33459926} are often negative in PanNECs. However, FDG PET-CT is usually positive {25786743}.

Epidemiology

PanNECs are uncommon and represent < 1% of all pancreatic neoplasms and no more than 2–3% of all pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNENs) {6268272; 18508996; 20702725; 25723112}. Most of the patients are in their late fifties, and there is a slight male predilection (M:F ratio: 1.4:1) {22967994; 24503751; 25723112; 26113608}.

Etiology

The genetic syndromes associated with PanNETs (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, etc.) are not associated with PanNECs.

Pathogenesis

The most frequently mutated gene in PanNECs is TP53, followed by RB1 and, to a lesser extent, KRAS, BRAF, APC, and others {22251937; 24292676; 27482646; 28059098; 29596894; 33433884}. KRAS mutations, which are very common in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, have been observed in about 40% of PanNECs, and their presence supports the pathogenetic relationship between PanNECs and non-neuroendocrine pancreatic carcinoma (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) {27262570; 29596894}. The potential for well-differentiated PanNETs to transform to poorly differentiated PanNECs could be considered, and anecdotal cases exist.

The following specific genetic alterations common in PanNETs are not features of PanNECs: alterations in MEN1, as well as in DNA damage repair (MUTYH, CHEK2, BRCA2), chromatin remodelling (ARID1A, SMARCA4), telomere alteration (DAXX/ATRX), and PI3K/mTOR signalling (EWSR1 fusions, PTEN, HIF1A/EPAS2) pathways (see section Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction) {22251937; 28199314; 33459926}.

Macroscopic appearance

PanNECs present as large (mean size: 40 mm, range: 20–180 mm), relatively circumscribed, solid tumours that are typically fleshier than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas. They tend to be white-grey or yellow, and often reveal vague nodularity. Haemorrhage is common, and necrosis may be seen {24503751}.

Histopathology

The histology of PanNECs is similar to that of their counterparts in other organs, particularly their counterparts in the lung {20664477; 24430597; 24503751; 25723112}. These poorly differentiated carcinomas are subdivided on the basis of cell size into large cell and small cell subtypes.

The large cell PanNEC (LCNEC) is more common (60%) and is characterized by relatively uniform round to polygonal cells with amphophilic cytoplasm, large nuclei, vesicular chromatin, and (in some cases) prominent nucleoli. Diffuse large irregular nests, and trabecular and peripheral palisading growth patterns, often intermingled in various proportions, are seen in most of these carcinomas {24503751; 25723112; 32298138} [[Lloyd RV, Osamura RY, Klöppel G, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/554.]].

The small cell PanNEC reveals diffuse sheets of small to intermediate cells with finely granular chromatin, high N:C ratios, inconspicuous nucleoli, prominent nuclear moulding, and crush artefact [[Lloyd RV, Osamura RY, Klöppel G, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/554.]].

In both subtypes, mitotic activity is brisk (often > 20 mitoses/2 mm2) and there is extensive tumour necrosis. The Ki-67 proliferation index is by definition > 20%, and commonly 60–80%, particularly in the small cell subtype {22967994; 24503751; 25723112}. The Ki-67 index tends to be lower in the large cell subtype.

PanNECs can be associated with non-NEC components, in particular ductal adenocarcinoma or very rarely acinar cell carcinoma. When each component accounts for ≥ 30% of the tumour, the term “mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma”, a type of mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN), is applied {24503751} (see section Mixed ductal adenocarcinoma–neuroendocrine neoplasms, and section Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma).

Immunohistochemistry

Demonstration of neuroendocrine differentiation is required to confirm the diagnosis of PanNEC {24503751; 25723112; 20664477; 24430597; 27529763; 30140036; 33999752} [[Lloyd RV, Osamura RY, Klöppel G, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/554.]]. Synaptophysin is the most sensitive marker. In contrast, chromogranin A is a highly specific marker, but its sensitivity is relatively low, and its expression can be limited in the small cell subtype, where the cytoplasm contains fewer secretory granules {986582}. Moreover, the expression of these neuroendocrine markers may be focal or show a dot-like staining pattern {24503751; 27529763}. INSM1 may be expressed in neoplasms with variable synaptophysin or focal/negative chromogranin A expression {28849340; 31857137; 31786484; 32813226; 33786701}. The use of nonspecific biomarkers alone (e.g. CD56, CD57, and NSE) is discouraged.

Differential diagnosis

An important mimic of PanNEC, especially of the large cell subtype, is acinar cell carcinoma, because of its monotonous appearance, high proliferative rate, and common expression of neuroendocrine markers (at least focally). In fact, the diagnosis of large cell PanNEC should not be established without immunohistochemical evaluation for acinar differentiation markers (e.g. trypsin, chymotrypsin, BCL10) {33786701}. In one study, 15% of cases originally classified as PanNEC proved to be mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas on further analysis {24503751}.

Metastatic carcinoma to the pancreas should also be excluded and requires clinical correlation {15057558}. Immunohistochemical analysis may not be helpful in this regard, given the common expression in PanNECs of otherwise organ-specific transcription factors, such as thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), CDX2, and insulin gene enhancer protein ISL1 or PDX1{22046491; 23503646}.

The crushed and moulded tumour cells of the small cell subtype may resemble lymphoma. Immunohistochemistry is helpful for the diagnosis. Other small round cell tumours, such as the Ewing family of tumours, may also involve the pancreas and need to be distinguished. Immunolabelling for CD99 can be helpful because most Ewing-family tumours show staining {12170091; 15166674}.

Cytology

The features of PanNEC are similar to those of other organs {26837492}. Smears are hypercellular and show sheets of small cells with very high N:C ratios, poorly defined cytoplasm, hyperchromatic angulated and elongated nuclei with uniformly distributed fine salt-and-pepper chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, nuclear moulding, numerous mitotic figures, and apoptotic bodies. Nuclear crush artefact and necrotic debris in the background are prominent, along with stripped nuclei and a variable number of intact single tumour cells. The carcinomas should be differentiated from other malignancies, including other carcinomas, melanomas, and lymphomas; immunohistochemical studies on cell blocks may be helpful {29044913; 27529763; 29451738}. Their distinction from well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) can be also be challenging. Proliferation index analysis may serve as an adjunct {24021213; 29451738}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

PanNECs show abnormal p53 expression and/or complete loss of RB1 expression in 95% and 74% of cases, respectively, which correspond to intragenic mutated TP53 and RB1 genes; PanNECs lack mutations in DAXX, ATRX, and MEN1 {22251937; 29596894}. Therefore, genomic profiling can be useful in the distinction of PanNECs from PanNETs in selected cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a keratin-positive poorly differentiated epithelial neoplasm of small to intermediate or large cells; neuroendocrine morphology; a mitotic count of > 20 mitoses/2 mm2; Ki-67 proliferation index > 20%; expression of neuroendocrine markers.

Desirable: abnormal p53 expression; loss of RB1 expression.

Staging

The pathological staging of PanNECs is based on the eighth edition (2017) of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]] for carcinomas of the exocrine pancreas.

Prognosis and prediction

PanNECs are highly aggressive malignant neoplasms {22967994; 24420597; 24503751; 25723112}. Advanced stage at presentation is common {17383419; 22967994; 26113608}; therefore, chemotherapy is often the primary therapeutic approach {20664477; 27759713; 33999752}. The aggressive behaviour of PanNECs warrants consideration of adjuvant therapy after surgery, even for locoregional disease, and although definite data are lacking, platinum and etoposide are recommended {22967994; 25723112; 33999752}. However, even with resection and platinum-based therapy, the median survival time is very short (< 1 year), and < 25% of patients survive beyond 2 years {22967994; 24503751; 33565789; 33999752}. In addition, PanNECs with other carcinoma components (e.g. ductal adenocarcinoma) appear to be similarly aggressive, emphasizing the importance of recognizing the PanNEC component in such cases.

There are no data to support the use of somatostatin analogue therapy in PanNECs. Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors is currently under investigation in extrapulmonary NECs {33999752}.
