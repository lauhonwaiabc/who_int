2.5.3 Brown tumour of hyperparathyroidism

Definition

Brown tumours are benign spindle cell neoplasms with intermingled osteoclastic giant cells occurring in the context of hyperparathyroidism.

ICD-O coding

XXX

ICD-11 coding

XXX

Related terminology

Not recommended: osteitis fibrosa cystica.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most commonly affected bones include the ribs, clavicles, pelvis, and long bones. Craniofacial involvement occurs in approximately 4% of cases, most frequently affecting the mandible (70% of cases) {32192952}. Brown tumours may present as solitary or multifocal lesions and radiologically can mimic metastatic disease {38795187}.

Clinical features

Patients can be asymptomatic or experience pain. In rare instances, bone deformities or pathological fractures may occur {37627135}. If hyperparathyroidism is severe, other clinical features such as proximal muscle weakness, renal calculi, constipation, and neuropsychiatric disorders including anxiety and depression may be present {25166047}. Serum calcium levels are elevated.

Imaging

Lesions may be single or multiple. Radiographically, brown tumours are typically well-defined medullary osteolyses with non-sclerotic margins and usually minimal reaction in the adjacent bone or periosteum {32148487}. There may be a multilocular appearance with trabeculation {32148487} and ground-glass attenuation {33506077}. Expansile remodelling, focal cortical thinning, and occasional cortical breach can be seen {20549204}. Lesions heal with sclerosis {8456657}. CT and MRI show heterogeneous lesions consisting of cystic, haemorrhagic, and solid areas {20549204}. Fluid-fluid levels may be seen {20549204}. Isotope studies including planar bone scan, SPECT/CT, and PET/CT may show skeletal features of metabolic disease with focal uptake, but distinction from metastases on scintigraphy alone can be challenging {17237612}.

Epidemiology

The occurrence of brown tumours in hyperparathyroidism reported in case series ranges from 1.5% to 3%, with women being affected 2–3 times more commonly than men {32192952; 39958142; 37627135; 38795187}. Because of early diagnosis and treatment of hyperparathyroidism, brown tumours have become rare in most high-income countries {23349562; 38795187}.

Etiology

Hyperparathyroidism is required but not sufficient to develop brown tumours. The endocrine stimulus appears to act as a second hit following activating variants in the MAPK signalling pathway.

Pathogenesis

The molecular pathogenesis of brown tumours is incompletely understood. Pathogenic KRAS variants have been detected in 8 of 14 (57%) jaw lesions, with 6 of 8 (75%) variants occurring at codon 12 {32492752; 36040039}. Also, in the peripheral and axial skeleton, KRAS hotspot variants have been identified in 9 of 15 (62%) cases, predominantly affecting codon 146 (5 of 9, 55%) {36040039}. No other pathogenic variants (particularly FGFR1 variants previously detected in giant cell granuloma of the jaws and non-ossifying fibroma of bone, as well as TRPV4 variants detected in giant cell granuloma of the jaws) have been detected in any of the 29 sequenced cases {32492752; 36040039; 30549028; 30385747}. Consistent with activation of the MAPK signalling pathway, the mononuclear cells of brown tumours have shown immunohistochemical phosphorylated ERK1/2 positivity {32492752}.

Macroscopic appearance

The lesional tissue is fleshy and haemorrhagic, resulting in the eponymous brown appearance.

Histopathology

Brown tumours typically consist of monomorphic and cytologically bland spindle cells arranged in a haphazard and sometimes storiform pattern. They contain varying amounts of osteoclastic giant cells, haemorrhage, and siderophages and show a particularly well-vascularized background. Osteoclastic giant cells are usually small and tend to cluster in areas of haemorrhage. The morphology is reminiscent of giant cell granuloma of the jaws, non-ossifying fibroma of bone, and solid ABC. Higher-grade cytological atypia is absent.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

KRAS variant testing is usually not required in the typical context but might be considered in equivocal cases to rule out solid ABC.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: monomorphic spindle cells in a storiform pattern; osteoclastic giant cells; haemorrhage; elevated PTH levels.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Brown tumours tend to behave clinically indolent and usually regress after normalization of PTH levels.
