2.0.1 Bone tumours: Introduction

Epidemiology and etiology

Among the wide array of human neoplasms, primary tumours of bone are relatively common; however, clinically relevant bone neoplasms are infrequent.

Incidence

The true incidence of benign bone tumours is unknown, although older radiographic studies have suggested that a substantial proportion of the population might actually have indolent lesions. Non-ossifying fibroma is the most common bone tumour. Cartilaginous lesions are relatively common incidental findings on knee MRI and have an estimated prevalence of 2.8% {25994192}. As a result of the improved sensitivity of radiological scans, the reported incidence of benign and low-grade cartilaginous tumours (atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1 [ACT/CS1]) has increased over the past decade {30217294}. In contrast, bone sarcomas are rare, accounting for only approximately 0.2% of all neoplasms. Comparison of the incidence of bone and soft tissue sarcomas indicates that clinically significant soft tissue neoplasms are approximately 10 times more common than their bone counterparts. Bone cancer age-standardized incidence rates (1993–2017) range from 0.53 to 2.72 cases per 100 000 person-years for female patients and 0.90–4.28 for male patients {40512321}. The overall annual reported incidence of bone sarcomas in both sexes is about 0.68–0.75 cases per 100 000 population {38858229}. The incidence rates of specific bone sarcomas are age-related. Osteosarcoma, which is the most common malignant primary bone tumour with an incidence of 3–7 cases per 1 million person-years, has a bimodal age distribution {32400205; 33630918}. Most cases develop between the ages of 15 and 25 years (55% of cases), with a second peak in adults aged > 40 years {40203873; 39966776; 39966776}. This second peak is usually related to other risk factors such as prior radiation treatment and Paget disease. Ewing sarcoma is less common than osteosarcoma (2.3 cases per 1 million person-years), and most patients are < 30 years old. In contrast, the incidence of chondrosarcoma and chordoma increases from adolescence onwards {21184634}.

Predisposing lesions

Although most primary bone malignancies appear to arise de novo, it is increasingly apparent that some develop in association with benign precursor lesions or in other pathological conditions of bone. Chondrosarcoma can be secondary to a pre-existing benign osteochondroma or enchondroma. Malignant transformation of other benign or intermediate bone tumours, such as fibrous dysplasia or giant cell tumour of bone, has been described but is rare {18560851; 40077813}. For all of these, additional molecular alterations often leading to genetic instability and complex chromosomal aberrations are hypothesized to occur on top of the defining gene alteration in the pre-existing condition {21804604; 40077813}. Osteosarcoma can occur in elderly patients with Paget disease of bone in < 1% {15018182}. Radiation-associated sarcoma of bone is estimated to occur in 0.03% of patients receiving radiotherapy {12057168}, with osteosarcoma being the most frequent tumour type {1365680; 11162693}. In addition, osteosarcoma can develop secondary to bone infarction, or rarely, in chronic osteomyelitis {26397350; 30112253; 12623051}.

Genetic predisposition

Constitutional genetic variants affecting the risk for bone tumour development range from mutations with high penetrance, causing disorders that typically follow traditional Mendelian patterns of inheritance, to SNPs that only mildly influence the function of the proteins encoded by the genes involved {29719630}. Individual genetic variants with high penetrance for bone tumour development are considered to be rare. Well-known examples include mutations in TP53 (see section Li–Fraumeni syndrome) and RB1 {3587888; 23674091}, both of which predispose to osteosarcoma. TP53 germline variant testing is recommended for patients aged < 46 years with osteosarcoma, who have at least one first- or second-degree relative with a TP53 core tumour (breast cancer, soft tissue sarcoma, osteosarcoma, CNS tumour, adrenocortical carcinoma) before 56 years of age. In addition, patients with osteosarcoma with another TP53 core tumour should be tested, irrespective of the family history {34500044}. However, large-scale sequencing studies have recently shown that > 50% of patients with sarcoma in general carry pathogenic germline variants and that the presence of multiple variants is associated with an earlier age at diagnosis {27498913}. More specifically, in approximately 25% of patients with osteosarcoma, irrespective of the family history, a highly penetrant germline variant can be found, also in genes not previously linked to osteosarcoma such as CDKN2A, MEN1, VHL, POT1, APC, MSH2, and ATRX {32191290}. These alterations are important to identify because they may affect response to treatment or predict an increased risk for developing other tumours, requiring genetic counselling or screening of at-risk individuals {26580448}. Non-inherited, postzygotic variants result in mosaicism and can increase the risk of tumour development in the affected parts of the body, as exemplified by IDH1 and IDH2 mutations causing enchondromatosis (see section Enchondromatosis) and GNAS mutations in polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, McCune–Albright syndrome, and Mazabraud syndrome (see section McCune–Albright syndrome).

Clinical features

Bone tumours present in a number of different ways, including with pain, swelling, pathological fracture, and neurovascular compromise, or as an incidental finding on radiological studies.

Although most benign bone tumours are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, the most common presenting symptom (when present) is a dull, intermittent aching pain. However, a previously asymptomatic individual may present with a pathological fracture (e.g. secondary to a simple bone cyst). Osteoid osteomas typically present with night pain that is relieved by anti-inflammatory agents. Spinal localization may cause scoliosis. Osteochondromas typically present as a painless lump, whereas chondroblastomas commonly present with severe pain. Clinical signs are often nonspecific. Swelling and tenderness over the affected bone are the most common findings. Periarticular lesions (e.g. chondroblastoma and juxta-articular osteoid osteoma) can irritate the joint, with resulting joint effusion and stiffness.

Malignant tumours usually present with progressively worsening, non-mechanical pain, often with a pain-free (lucent) interval {10819277}. At the time of diagnosis, many patients experience night pain that interrupts sleep. Swelling usually becomes apparent once the cortex is breached and the tumour starts to grow beneath the periosteum or extend through it. Most malignant bone tumours arise in a metaphysial location. However, Ewing sarcoma and adamantinoma are usually diaphysial. The presence of swelling in a limb (in particular around the knee) associated with pain (in particular non-mechanical or night pain) should always lead to further investigation. There is a broad differential diagnosis for patients presenting in this manner, including benign nocturnal limb pains of childhood, osteomyelitis, benign and malignant bone tumours, metastases, and haematological disorders. A simple algorithm based on the most likely diagnosis for a patient of a particular age has been advocated {21037340}. This is based on the fact that metastatic carcinoma is uncommon in individuals aged < 35 years, whereas the likelihood of a bone tumour being a metastasis from a known or undiagnosed carcinoma increases after that age {21705575}.

As many as 43% of malignant bone tumours arise around the knee, but in patients aged < 20 years, this rises to 56% (see #57003Table 4.01). Therefore, for any child or adolescent presenting with pain and/or swelling around the knee that does not resolve, a diagnosis of a primary bone tumour should be considered and investigated. The second most common site is the pelvis, which is the most common site for both Ewing sarcoma and chondrosarcoma. Delays in diagnosis of pelvic tumours are frequent, because symptoms are nonspecific and often of considerable duration {10199269}. Referred pain to the leg or knee is not infrequent, and it is not unusual for patients to have been investigated before diagnosis without the possibility of a bone tumour being considered. Night pain, again, is often the key that should alert the clinician to the possibility of a bone tumour being present.

Pathological fractures in malignant bone tumours are often associated with pre-existing pain or discomfort in the limb. The fracture is often caused by minimal force. Awareness of the possibility of a neoplastic cause for the fracture is essential to prevent inadvertent internal fixation that jeopardizes clinical outcome {18937020}. Imaging manifestations combined with the history may raise suspicion for an underlying malignant primary bone tumour. Blood tests are usually not helpful in diagnosing bone tumours. An elevated alkaline phosphatase level is seen in about 46% of patients with osteosarcoma and is a poor prognostic sign {11748477}. Raised LDH, ESR, and C-reactive protein may be seen in Ewing sarcoma, also being poor prognostic indicators {10623687}.

Biopsy

Obtaining a diagnosis for a symptomatic patient as quickly as possible is essential, but delays in diagnosis are frequent {22004638}. All patients with suspected locally aggressive or malignant bone tumours should be discussed at a multidisciplinary team meeting, physically or remotely, preferably at a sarcoma reference centre that includes an expert radiologist, pathologist, orthopaedic surgeon, and oncologist, involved in the patient’s diagnosis and treatment {30285218; 21253474; 34500044}. Local staging investigations should be completed before a biopsy. A biopsy can alter the imaging features, making interpretation and diagnosis less accurate. Imaging before the biopsy improves the diagnostic yield. A reliable histological diagnosis often cannot be made without prior correlation with radiographic imaging {22555180}. The biopsy of a suspected tumour should be performed at a reference centre for bone sarcomas. Planning of the surgical approach to the biopsy site and tract, as well as the diagnostic approach, should involve the orthopaedic surgeon who will perform the surgery {30285218}. There is evidence that treatment in centres with a high volume of bone tumours and the presence of an experienced multidisciplinary team positively affects outcome {21030381; 29117335}. The pathologist must be informed of the patient’s age and the exact anatomical site and size of the tumour. This information should be correlated with the imaging. Additionally, it is important to know about the presence of other lesions, possible underlying bone disease, and family history to provide an informed diagnostic opinion. In case the biopsy was performed outside a reference centre, expert pathological review in a sarcoma reference centre is highly recommended {34500044}.

Tissue processing

All tissues, whether from a biopsy, curettage, or resection of a suspected bone tumour, should be processed in a manner that allows molecular studies to be undertaken successfully. Decalcification is usually necessary but can be detrimental to nucleic acids; therefore, the collection of fresh snap-frozen tissue is encouraged to preserve DNA integrity and to allow subsequent molecular assessment {34500044}. Also, immunohistochemistry can be affected by acid-based decalcification {31768625}. As an alternative to harsher decalcification methods, the use of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is strongly recommended, as it provides better tissue stainability, preserves epitopes for immunophenotyping, and enables molecular analyses. Additionally, non-calcified portions of the tumour should be fixed in buffered formalin alone before paraffin embedding. For resection specimens of malignant bone tumours, a complete, representative slab of the tumour, usually through its largest dimension in the longitudinal axis, as guided by the radiological images, should be embedded for microscopy in a grid manner {34500044}. This is especially relevant to assess response after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in Ewing sarcoma and osteosarcoma.

Ancillary testing

While in the past bone tumour pathology heavily relied on morphology, with the advent of next-generation sequencing, specific molecular alterations have been identified in various bone tumours. Some of these can also be detected by specific immunohistochemical markers, including overexpression of GRM1 in chondromyxoid fibroma {35650682}; FOS in osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, and epithelioid haemangioma {31768625}; or the specific variants in the genes encoding histone protein H3.3 in giant cell tumour of bone {28505000} and chondroblastoma {26844533}. There is controversy and confusion about whether to include the first methionine of the coding sequence (the start codon) in the count when describing the mutations in the H3.3-encoding genes H3-3A and H3-3B. In this volume we chose to adopt the abbreviated recommended protein nomenclature, with the historical nomenclature in parenthesis, e.g. G35W (G34W).

For molecular analysis, it is imperative to safeguard either non-decalcified, short EDTA decalcified, or fresh frozen tissue with preserved nucleic acids. Molecular testing for diagnosis plays an important role in translocation-driven tumours such as Ewing sarcoma, especially when presentation is unusual. From a therapeutic perspective, current evidence does not support the routine clinical use of broad next-generation sequencing testing for all patients with sarcoma because of the small number of actionable alterations and the limited evidence of clinical benefit achieved with matched treatment {41100097}. Therefore, this should be considered on an individual, case-by-case basis.

Reporting

For surgical specimens, tumour size and local extent of spread, site, status of surgical margins, and percentage of pathological response to preoperative chemotherapy should be described {34500044; 36464647}, as well as whether the resection margins are either clear (R0) or microscopically (R1) or macroscopically (R2) involved. Histological response in Ewing sarcoma and osteosarcoma has prognostic value and should be documented as the percentage of viable tumour / percentage of histological response (including necrosis, fibrosis, and calcification).

Use of clinical imaging
Diagnosis

For bone tumour diagnosis, correlation of the histological and radiological findings are crucial. Imaging contributes to making a specific diagnosis by depicting the macroscopic anatomy of tumours, surrounding host reaction, location, and relationship with surrounding normal anatomical structures. Certain tumours occur in a characteristic location in the skeleton. Adamantinoma and osteofibrous dysplasia have a strong predilection for the anterior cortex of the tibia. In long bones, epiphysial lesions include chondroblastoma, giant cell tumour, and clear cell chondrosarcoma. Several tumours have a predilection for sites of rapid growth and involve the metaphysial regions, often around the knee joints, such as osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, osteochondroma, and enchondroma (#57011Table 4.02). Some tumours have a predilection for the bone surface or cortex, such as osteoid osteoma, osteochondroma, periosteal chondroma, and parosteal and periosteal osteosarcomas {29025191; 29025191}.

Bone tumours usually produce matrix that can be classified as bone, cartilaginous, or fibrous. The term “mineralization” refers to calcification of the matrix {22024290}. Margins and periosteal reaction of a bone tumour reflect the growth rate and biological behaviour of the tumour. Slow-growing tumours have circumscribed margins with a narrow zone of transition, and a solid and/or thick periosteal reaction. Aggressive and rapidly growing tumours have indistinct margins with a wide zone of transition and an aggressive, usually interrupted, periosteal reaction, such as onion-skin, spiculated, sunburst, or hair-on-end periosteal reaction, or a Codman triangle. These types of periosteal reactions are seen not only in rapidly growing tumours but also in other non-neoplastic aggressive lesions such as osteomyelitis.

Radiological techniques

Radiography (of the entire bone in two planes) is the initial imaging examination in the workup of bone sarcomas and is usually the most specific for establishing a diagnosis {29025191; 27070373}. CT can be helpful in complex anatomical regions, such as the spine and pelvis, showing matrix mineralization, periosteal reaction, and cortical destruction {31163509; 29025191}. Mineralization in and around tumours is best demonstrated by CT {29025191}. CT is additionally used for percutaneous biopsy guidance {34573895; 33531215}. MRI is the imaging modality of choice for local staging, treatment evaluation, and detection of recurrences {29025191; 34500044; 32370967}. Whole-body MRI or 18F-FDG PET/CT can be used to screen for metastases or multifocal bone tumours {33635285; 31016339}. Limitations of 18F-FDG PET/CT are relatively poor spatial resolution and the background activity of normal bone marrow, especially in children and adolescents. For these reasons, MRI is more sensitive and specific than 18F-FDG PET/CT in diagnosing metastases of Ewing sarcoma {31016339}. It is also mandatory that the primary tumour has high 18F-FDG uptake. Bone scintigraphy has been replaced by MRI and 18F-FDG PET/CT {23995264}. Radiographs can be used to assess posttreatment complications, such as fracture, prosthetic loosening, or non-union, and to detect recurrence. As radiographs cannot exclude recurrence, which usually occurs in the soft tissues, gadolinium chelate–enhanced MRI, with metal artefact reduction techniques when hardware is present, is used for local surveillance {36436964}.

More functional information such as glucose metabolism (18F-FDG PET/CT), cell density (diffusion MRI), vascularization, necrosis, and interstitial pressure (all three on gadolinium chelate–enhanced MRI) can add information for monitoring the effect of neoadjuvant therapy. Dynamic gadolinium chelate–enhanced MRI and 18F-FDG PET/CT correlate well with histopathological evaluation and survival {38893075; 37201958}. MRI and 18F-FDG PET/CT using radiomics obtained before the start of neoadjuvant therapy have been shown to correlate well with histopathological response {36178349; 35778618; 38151381}.

Radiomics is an emerging method mainly used on radiographs, CT, and MRI, to assist in diagnosis of bone tumours, monitoring response to neoadjuvant therapy, and prediction of survival, recurrence, and therapy response {41072272; 32154773}. With the increase of high-quality studies and increased uniformity, or correction of acquisition protocols, radiomics will become an important enhancer of radiological accuracy {35986808; 37499847}.

Terminology used to reflect biological potential

Bone tumours are now divided into four categories: benign, intermediate (locally aggressive), intermediate (rarely metastasizing), and malignant, which were defined in previous editions of the WHO Classification of Tumours series. The distinction between these categories can be somewhat arbitrary and subject of debate, especially in tumours that show a broader histological and biological spectrum (e.g. conventional central and peripheral cartilaginous tumours). The intent of the classification is to specify which lesions would be best managed by regular surveillance without surgical removal, which need local treatment such as curettage, and which require wide en bloc resection.

Benign: Most benign bone tumours have a limited capacity for local recurrence. Those that do present with clinical complaints or cause deformity can be resected or treated intralesionally. If they recur, they do so in a non-destructive fashion and are almost always readily cured by complete local excision/curettage.

Intermediate (locally aggressive): Bone tumours in this category often recur locally and may be associated with an infiltrative and locally destructive growth pattern. They do not appear to have the potential to metastasize but typically require local treatment and are often cured with an intralesional resection or a curettage. Wide excision with a margin of normal tissue is not indicated, but application of a local adjuvant to ensure local control is common. Of note, tumours may behave differently depending on their localization. For example, epithelioid haemangioma of bone is considered to be locally aggressive, whereas epithelioid haemangioma of soft tissue is considered a benign tumour. Also, well-differentiated cartilaginous tumours in the flat bones need more aggressive treatment as compared with those in the long bones, which is reflected in the use of chondrosarcoma grade 1 and atypical cartilaginous tumour for histologically similar lesions in different locations.

Intermediate (rarely metastasizing): Bone tumours categorized as intermediate are often locally aggressive (see above). However, there is well-documented evidence of such lesions occasionally giving rise to distant metastases. Such metastases occur in < 2% of cases and are not predictable morphologically. These lesions usually metastasize to the lung. The prototypical example in this category is giant cell tumour of bone. It is important to note that the intermediate categories do not correspond to intermediate histological grade (grade 2) in a bone sarcoma. Treatment can be curative when performed intralesionally with adjuvants or by marginal resection.

Malignant: In addition to the potential for locally destructive growth and recurrence, bone sarcomas have a substantial risk of distant metastasis, ranging in most instances from 20% to almost 100%, depending on histological type and, if applicable, histological grade. Some (but not all) histologically low-grade sarcomas have a metastatic risk of only 2–10%. Examples in which progression to high-grade sarcoma can be seen include parosteal osteosarcoma and low-grade central osteosarcoma. Moreover, in some tumours, malignancy is not defined by morphology; instead, size is used as the most important discriminating feature. For instance, for the progression of osteochondroma to secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma grade 1, a debatable size cut-off point of 2–3 cm for the cartilage cap is used {12782876; 35193636}, since other generally accepted criteria are lacking {22555180}. Likewise, for periosteal chondroma and periosteal chondrosarcoma, in the absence of cortical invasion, a size cut-off point of 5 cm is used. While scientific evidence is currently lacking and the use of size to define malignancy might appear counterintuitive and suboptimal, no better defining criteria are presently available.

Management

Treatment of bone tumours must be tailored to the lesion’s biological potential, as defined in the four WHO categories, and is best delivered in specialized multidisciplinary sarcoma reference centres, with a demonstrated impact on survival in several studies {31168580; 33852793}. For benign lesions, many can be managed conservatively with regular surveillance only, particularly when asymptomatic and radiologically stable. When intervention is indicated, local excision or curettage is usually sufficient, as recurrence is uncommon and typically non-destructive {41010632}. Intermediate, locally aggressive tumours often require more rigorous local control because of their infiltrative growth and tendency for local recurrence, though they lack metastatic potential. Marginal or intralesional excision supplemented with local adjuvants such as cryotherapy, burring, or cement is frequently employed to minimize the risk of residual or recurrent disease {24599207; 30811352; 22880716}. Intermediate, rarely metastasizing tumours necessitate a balanced, often multimodal approach that considers both local control and the small but real risk of distant spread {38033413; 38160684; 30217294}. Intralesional curettage or marginal resection may be appropriate in many cases, with closer follow-up and consideration of wider excision in cases of recurrence {39713913}.

For malignant primary bone sarcomas, such as osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and high-grade chondrosarcoma, treatment is typically multimodal. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is indicated for chemosensitive tumour types, i.e. high-grade osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and other high-grade sarcomas of the bone, to reduce tumour burden, enhance pseudocapsule formation, and address micrometastatic disease {34500044; 34818481}. It should not be proposed for high-grade conventional or dedifferentiated chondrosarcomas, for which the impact of chemotherapy has never been demonstrated and is very uncertain in advanced phase {34500044}. After neoadjuvant chemotherapy, definitive local treatment usually consists of wide or radical surgical resection, complemented by adjuvant chemotherapy for high-grade osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and other high-grade sarcomas of the bone, except for chondrosarcoma. Postoperative radiotherapy administration depends on the histological subtype, the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and the quality of the margins (R0, R1, R2). Radiotherapy plays an important role in Ewing sarcoma, either preoperatively to facilitate resection or postoperatively to improve local control. It may also be the sole local treatment modality when the tumour is localized but not resectable or resectable with severe mutilation {34500044}.

The cytotoxics used for the neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatment depend on the age and histotypes. Doxorubicin, cisplatin (CDDP), ifosfamide, and etoposide are used in most regimens for osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and high-grade sarcoma except for chondrosarcoma. High-dose methotrexate is used in osteosarcoma arising in young children and adults {34818481; 34500044; 33497548}. For conventional grades 2 and 3 chondrosarcoma, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma, the role of neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy is not proven {34500044}.

Localized disease in chondrosarcoma is generally treated with a wide resection, as adjuvant therapies have not yet proven to improve outcome outside metastatic patients. Cytotoxic chemotherapy is not proposed for low-grade surface osteosarcomas or low-grade chondrosarcomas, unless high-grade areas (dedifferentiation) are observed in the case of surface osteosarcoma.

The sequence and extent of local and systemic treatments are determined through multidisciplinary discussion in a multidisciplinary tumour board. The medical treatment of bone sarcoma should be conducted only in reference centres and prospective clinical studies whenever possible {31168580; 33852793}.

Principles of grading and staging
Grading

Bone tumours vary widely in their biological behaviour. Histological grading is an attempt to predict the behaviour of a malignant tumour on the basis of histological features. There is no generally accepted grading system for bone sarcomas, and the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) grading system used in soft tissue sarcomas has never been validated for bone tumours {11346874; 24378389}. In bone sarcomas, the histological type often determines clinical behaviour and therefore also the grade (see #57004Table 4.03). For example, Ewing sarcoma, mesenchymal chondrosarcoma, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma are always considered high-grade, whereas parosteal osteosarcoma is a low-grade sarcoma. The exceptions to this include conventional chondrosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma of bone. For conventional central and peripheral chondrosarcoma, the grading system as proposed by Evans et al. {890662} is widely used. However, grading according to Evans et al. does not seem to reliably predict the biological behaviour of chondrosarcomas in the phalanx {10547545; 14599838} or the larynx {15167045}. Also in periosteal chondrosarcoma, histological grading does not seem to correlate with outcome {25648524}. Adamantinoma and conventional chordoma are malignant neoplasms but are not graded. The clinical significance of histological grading is limited by interobserver variability {18852676; 17908885}. Guidelines for reporting and grading of bone tumours are available {30285218; 20367293; 22613930; 36464647}.

Staging

There is no generally accepted system for the staging of bone sarcomas {34500044}. Enneking and coauthors {7449206; 3456859} described a staging system for musculoskeletal tumours, which is used by orthopaedic surgeons because it helps determine the surgical treatment. The Enneking stage is determined by tumour grade, extent beyond the surgical compartment, and the presence of metastases. Limitations include the fact that tumour size and site are not taken into account, which constitute additional prognostic factors {20352387; 29781819}.

The TNM staging system, as described by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), is based on evolving prognostic evidence; at present for bone tumours, the ninth edition is applicable [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]]. For the appendicular skeleton, trunk, skull, and facial bones, an 8 cm cut-off point distinguishes between T1 and T2 tumours. Skip lesions are defined as T3. Most bone tumours primarily metastasize to the lung and are classed as M1a (stage IVA). Metastases to bone or other sites (M1b) have a worse prognosis (stage IVB). Metastases to regional lymph nodes, which are uncommon, are also stage IVB. Bone tumours of the axial skeleton have a worse prognosis than those of the appendicular skeleton. Consequently, since the eighth edition of the TNM system, a special T classification for spine and pelvic tumours is included. In the spine, the classification is based on five segments (see <>TNM staging of tumours of bone). However, in the pelvis, the T classification is determined by tumour size (cut-off point: 8 cm) and tumour extent into four defined segments. There is no defined stage for malignant bone tumours of the spine or pelvis.

Outline to the sixth edition
Chondrogenic tumours

Cartilaginous tumours are the most frequent bone tumours in general. Their diagnosis can be challenging and sometimes impossible without radiological correlation. A multidisciplinary approach for diagnosing these lesions is therefore essential. In the fifth edition (2020), the terms “atypical cartilaginous tumour” and chondrosarcoma, grade 1 were further defined: cartilaginous tumours in the appendicular skeleton (long and short tubular bones) should be diagnosed as atypical cartilaginous tumours, and the term “chondrosarcoma, grade 1” is reserved for tumours of the axial skeleton, including the pelvis, scapula, and skull base (flat bones), reflecting the poorer clinical outcome of these tumours at these sites {29462035; 30217294}. Using different terminology for tumours with identical histomorphology could be considered counterintuitive and therefore controversial. However, this terminology has the dual benefit of being simple in application and reflecting the worse outcome for tumours at these sites, where there is a need for more extensive surgery {29462035}. As a consequence, this terminology is further endorsed in the current classification. In this sixth edition, malignant chondroblastoma with H3.3 K37M (K36M) mutation is now clearly recognized.

Osteogenic tumours

Benign osteogenic tumours include osteoma, osteoid osteoma, and osteoblastoma. Osteosarcoma is the most common malignant bone sarcoma, and multiple types as well as morphological subtypes are distinguished, on the basis of location as well as morphology. No changes were made in their classification as compared with the fifth edition.

Osteoclastic giant cell–rich tumours

While many bone tumours can contain osteoclasts, they are a defining feature in giant cell tumour of bone, aneurysmal bone cyst, and non-ossifying fibroma. Brown tumours of hyperparathyroidism, which were shown to be neoplastic when KRAS variants were found to be necessary for their development, in addition to elevated PTH levels {32492752; 36040039}, are now also included as a separate entity. With regard to the annotation of histone gene mutations, we chose to exclude the first codon from the coding sequence. While undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma or osteosarcoma with H3.3 G35W (G34W) mutations show similar molecular and methylation profile and thus may represent malignant giant cell tumours of bone {28545165; 28899740; 28505000; 29241742; 40157506}, studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm this association and direct clinical management.

Notochordal tumours

Notochordal tumours include benign notochordal cell tumour, conventional chordoma (including chondroid chordoma), dedifferentiated chordoma, and poorly differentiated chordoma. No changes were made in their classification as compared with the fifth edition.

Other tumours of bone

In this sixth edition, osteofibrous dysplasia (OFD)-like adamantinoma has been renamed as atypical OFD and is now considered a locally aggressive subtype of OFD. This better reflects the non-malignant behaviour of atypical OFD and should avoid overtreatment of these tumours in young patients. The diagnosis can nevertheless be difficult and interobserver variability is high. TFCP2-rearranged rhabdomyosarcoma is a novel type of rhabdomyosarcoma with a predilection for the (maxillofacial) bones, and it is now included as a new bone tumour entity.
