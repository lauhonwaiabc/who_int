2.7.13 Bone metastases

Definition

Bone metastases are tumours involving bone as a result of haematogenous spread from malignancies at distant sites.

ICD-O coding

Multiple codes ending in /6, depending on the primary source

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Any site can be involved. Metastases to the axial skeleton (especially vertebrae) but also pelvis, ribs, sternum, and skull predominate. The metaphyses of the femur and proximal humerus are also commonly involved. Lung cancer is the most common primary malignancy that metastasizes to acral sites {33650515}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Pain is usually the first presenting symptom and is nearly always present. Skeletal-related events include pathological fracture, need for radiotherapy (these two are the most common), need for surgery, spinal cord compression, and hypercalcaemia. Hypercalcaemia is more common with osteolytic metastasis and can also be paraneoplastic {24782453}. Metastases can be the first manifestation of cancer, and a primary tumour cannot always be identified (unknown primary).

Imaging

Plain radiographs are often used initially in patients presenting with pain. Metastases can be lytic (lung, breast, thyroid, kidney), sclerotic/osteoblastic (prostate, breast, neuroendocrine tumour/carcinoma), or mixed (breast, lung, gastrointestinal tract). Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, bisphosphonates, denosumab, and hormonal therapy can cause formerly invisible osteolytic metastases to become visible because of therapy-induced sclerosis. Whole-body MRI (including T1, fluid-sensitive, and diffusion sequences) and 18F-FDG PET/CT provide the advantage of whole-body imaging; with state-of-the-art equipment and protocols, accuracies are similar and have improved over time {31016339; 35099603; 39735112; 40132471; 35809098}. MRI has the advantages of low cost, short scan time (30 min), and no exposure of the patient to radiation. MRI provides superior anatomical imaging, and 18F-FDG PET is dependent on glucose metabolism by the tumour; both modalities are imperfectly sensitive, and performing both techniques can add value in challenging cases. Tc-99m methylene diphosphonate (MDP) and HDP-based skeletal scintigraphy highlights areas of increased osteoblastic activity but less sensitive than MRI or 18F-FDG PET. 18F-NaF PET/CT is based on the same physiological principle and is more sensitive. Increasingly, tumour-specific radiolabelled imaging agents have become available {34191163}. Specific examples relevant to bone metastases include PSMA and choline for prostate cancer, MIBG for neuroblastoma, and DOTATATE/DOTATOC for neuroendocrine tumours; many of these molecules can be chelated to alternative radioligands for the purpose of radionuclide therapy.

Epidemiology

A few studies have been published providing population-based estimates for prevalence and incidence rates of patients with bone metastases {33276151; 38497290}. In the SEER Program database, patients with bone metastasis at disease presentation had a mean age of 67 years. Excluding multiple myeloma, the most common primary sites in descending order included lung (51% of all patients with bone metastasis), prostate (16%), breast (14%), kidney (5%), colorectum (2.5%), pancreas (2.4%), liver and bile duct (1.8%), oesophagus (1.7%), stomach (1.6%), urinary bladder (1.5%), and oral cavity/pharynx (0.8%); melanoma and sarcoma represent 1% and 0.8% of tumours, respectively {33276151}. Among lung primaries, most are adenocarcinoma (64%), followed by small cell carcinoma (20%) and squamous cell carcinoma (17%) {32395526}. In patients < 20 years, sarcoma and neuroblastoma predominate.

Etiology

Bone metastases result from metastatic spread of a primary malignancy elsewhere in the body.

Pathogenesis

Metastases tend to involve the haematopoietic stem cell niche, thus the predilection for the axial skeleton. In osteolytic metastases, the RANK-RANKL signalling pathway is aberrantly activated, including neoplastic secretion of PTHrP and interleukins, leading to extensive bone resorption {38474093}. Osteoblastic metastases are characterized by enhanced WNT signalling, due in part to reduced activity of the WNT signalling inhibitor DKK1 {21593787}. Bisphosphonates have a direct pro-apoptotic effect on osteoclasts and inhibit bone resorption; denosumab, a monoclonal antibody to RANKL, disrupts the ligand's interaction with RANK-expressing osteoclasts. These agents can delay skeletal-related events, reduce morbidity, and improve quality of life in patients with bone metastases {24782453}.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteoblastic metastases can be hard because of the formation of sclerotic bone. Osteolytic metastases are typically composed of friable, haemorrhagic tissue. Metastasis from melanoma can be darkly pigmented.

Histopathology

The morphology of metastases mirrors that of the primary tumour. Osteoblastic metastases can contain abundant reactive woven bone formation and must be distinguished from osteosarcoma. Haemorrhage, fibrosis, and osteoclast-like giant cell reactions can obscure the metastatic cells.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry is useful to support tumour lineage and site of origin {32205473}. See #55653Table 4.04. Ideally, immunohistochemistry (and molecular pathology, if indicated) is performed on non-bony tumour tissue not requiring decalcification. Nuclear antigens are more susceptible to adverse impact from decalcification than those expressed in the cytoplasm or membrane.

Differential diagnosis

Primary vascular tumours, especially those with epithelioid cytomorphology, are frequently keratin-positive and can be mistaken for carcinoma. Although CD138 is typically used as a marker of plasmacytic differentiation (e.g. to support a diagnosis of multiple myeloma), it is expressed in as many as 50% of carcinomas. Polyclonal PAX8 cross-reacts with other PAX-family transcription factors (e.g. PAX5), and B-cell lymphomas staining for polyclonal PAX8 can be misdiagnosed as renal cell carcinoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing should be guided by the primary tumour type and local availability of targeted therapies. In addition to tumour-specific targets, panels should include tumour-agnostic biomarkers (e.g. microsatellite instability; tumour mutation burden; mutations such as BRAF p.(Val600Glu) [V600E], FGFR1/FGFR2/FGFR3; and targetable fusions such as NTRK1/NTRK2/NTRK3, FGFR1/FGFR2/FGFR3, RET) {38834388}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant growth distant from primary site; correlation with clinical history and imaging.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry (in selected cases) to support tumour lineage and site of origin.

Staging

Bone metastasis represents Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage IV disease.

Prognosis and prediction

Although a number of prognostic scoring systems have been developed, primary tumour histology and performance status have the greatest impact on survival {26866742; 29064040}. In a population-based study of 36 000 newly diagnosed patients with breast cancer in Denmark, 5-year survival was 76% for patients without bone metastasis, 8.3% for those with bone metastasis, and 2.5% for those with bone metastasis and skeletal-related events; the median survival in these last two categories was 16 and 7 months, respectively {21461730}.
