1.5.13 Angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is a high-grade malignant neoplasm demonstrating endothelial differentiation, with variable vasoformative, epithelioid, or spindle cell phenotypes.

ICD-O coding

9120/3 Angiosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B56.Y & XH6264 Angiosarcoma, other specified primary site & Haemangiosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: haemangiosarcoma; lymphangiosarcoma; malignant haemangioendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Cutaneous angiosarcoma is the most prevalent form and occurs predominantly in the head and neck region. The skin of the breast is a frequent site for radiation-induced angiosarcoma. Other sites include breast parenchyma; soft tissue; bone; and viscera such as liver, heart, and spleen {9630175}. In bones, the tumour shows a wide skeletal distribution with preferential involvement of tubular bones, especially the femur, followed by the pelvis, axial skeleton, and trunk {39142183; 38545583; 22993209}. One third to half of angiosarcomas of bone are multifocal, affecting contiguous (64%) or distant bones (36%) {21323951; 12766574; 34742485; 40492503}.

Clinical features

Angiosarcomas constitute a heterogeneous group that can be primary or secondary to certain precipitating factors (chronic lymphoedema, irradiation, or exposure to toxic chemicals [vinyl chloride]). Cutaneous angiosarcoma usually presents in older patients (mean: ~70 years) as raised papules, nodules, or purplish-red plaques {20537949}. Soft tissue angiosarcomas present as poorly defined, rapidly growing, often painful masses, occasionally associated with acute haemorrhage. In bone, pain is the most common symptom, which can be associated with a palpable mass or bone fracture {32914587; 38237880; 33851644}. Approximately one third of patients have other symptoms, such as anaemia, coagulopathy, or persistent haematoma {9630175}.

Bone imaging

Angiosarcoma of bone typically presents as multifocal well-defined osteolytic lesions, with cortical destruction and soft tissue extension, but without periosteal reaction {40492503; 20828961}. Multifocal disease may be regional (around a joint) or widespread. MRI shows a heterogeneous lesion and may include fluid-fluid levels in cystic areas, marked enhancement, and extensive reactive changes seen on T2-weighted images {40492503; 20828961}. High 18F-FDG uptake with osteolytic destruction on 18F-FDG PET/CT is characteristic, although false-negative results have been reported {38089865; 40492503; 37443509}. Increased tracer uptake on PSMA PET/CT mimics metastases of prostate cancer {34172597}.

Epidemiology

Angiosarcoma is rare, and very few population-based studies provide estimations of its prevalence and incidence using nationwide population-based studies. Incidence estimates vary from 0.18 to 0.33 cases per 100 000 person-years {40121268; 38607625; 33403092}. Cutaneous angiosarcoma is the most prevalent, with male predominance and peak incidence in the seventh decade of life {40121268; 33403092; 38607625}. Cutaneous angiosarcomas secondary to breast radiation occur in older patients, while primary breast angiosarcomas show a predilection for younger female individuals. Angiosarcoma of bone (~4% of all angiosarcomas) mostly occurs in adult patients, with a peak incidence in the sixth to seventh decades of life and a slight male predominance {21323951; 38237880; 12766574; 32914587; 32960834; 40492503}. Angiosarcoma is rare in children {18987547; 1409148}.

Etiology

The etiology is generally unknown; however, for a minority, well-established risk factors for development have been identified. A subset develops on the background of chronic lymphoedema (Stewart–Treves syndrome). Radiation-associated angiosarcomas occur in radiotherapy fields, most frequently secondary to breast cancer. Exposure to certain chemicals, such as vinyl chloride, is directly related to the incidence of liver angiosarcoma {4134297; 30474170}. Smaller numbers occur in association with implanted foreign material (including synthetic grafts), in the vicinity of arteriovenous fistulas, in pre-existing benign lesions (e.g. haemangioma/vascular malformation, fibrous dysplasia), in regions of prior trauma or surgery, syndromes (e.g. neurofibromatosis and Maffucci syndrome), bone infarction, or chronic osteomyelitis {3052791; 21659738; 10460655; 12360047; 9840390; 10594289; 38237880; 32271190; 33851644; 38701315}.

Pathogenesis

The majority of angiosarcomas have a complex and diverse genomic landscape, including both intragenic mutations as well as arm-level copy-number alterations. They show consistent upregulation of angiogenesis and vascular-specific receptor tyrosine kinases encoding genes (including TIE1, TEK, KDR [VEGFR2], and FLT4 [VEGFR3]) {19723655; 18041747}. MYC amplification (8q24) occurs in the vast majority of secondary angiosarcomas (associated with irradiation or chronic lymphoedema), and less frequently in primary angiosarcomas {20949568; 20008140; 24329959; 22121953}. A subset of secondary angiosarcomas is associated with FLT4 (VEGFR3) coamplification at 5q35 and is associated with a poor prognosis {26735859; 20949568}. KDR and PLCG1 variants are mutually exclusive and occur in subsets of both primary and secondary angiosarcomas, particularly in breast and bone/visceral sites, regardless of MYC status {26735859; 40492503}. About 40% harbour recurrent somatic mutations involving angiogenic signalling pathways (e.g. in KDR, PTPRB, and PLCG1). Other less common mutations occur in ATRX, PTPRB, RAS, ARID1A, CRKL, PIK3CA, TP53, FLT4, and TIE1 {19723655; 22648906; 24633157; 26735859; 26403419; 39367667}, indicating tumour heterogeneity and possible population differences. KIT overexpression has been reported in soft tissue angiosarcoma {23599148}. Primary cutaneous angiosarcomas are more frequently associated with ultraviolet (UV)-light mutation signatures and high tumour mutation burden {33016928; 31562204}. POT1 alterations are reported predominantly in cardiac angiosarcomas {26403419}. In the bone, karyotypic and comparative genomic hybridization studies have defined two molecular groups: one with few or no gross aberrations and the other with numerous genetic aberrations consisting of chromosomal losses, gains, and high-level amplifications, such as 2q and 17q {25231439; 34742485}. The PI3K/AKT, RB1, and TGF-β pathways are involved in angiosarcoma of bone, whereas overexpression of p53 or MDM2 is absent {23599148}.

Macroscopic appearance

Angiosarcoma is usually a large, red, friable, haemorrhagic lesion with necrosis, simulating a haematoma. Infiltrative, poorly defined growth patterns may be seen. Cortical destruction and soft tissue invasion are common in the bone {38237880; 34742485}.

Histopathology

Angiosarcomas have an infiltrative growth pattern and show variable degrees of cytological atypia and architectural differentiation. Well-differentiated vasoformative areas are composed of atypical endothelial cells lining anastomosing vascular spaces, whereas more solid components show endothelial multilayering and sheets of cellular growth. The cells can range from spindled to large epithelioid with pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and vesicular nuclei. Cytoplasmic vacuoles containing fragmented erythrocytes can be present {39463621}. Epithelioid angiosarcoma shows predominance of epithelioid cells forming nests or sheets. Most tumours show a high-grade morphology, with nuclear atypia, increased mitotic activity (most prominent in solid areas), and coagulative necrosis {38237880; 21323951; 32271190}. There may be associated blood lakes, extensive haemorrhage, and organizing haematoma that obscure the neoplasm {34742485}. Extravasated erythrocytes can be numerous, and haemosiderin deposition may also be present.

Angiosarcoma arising in the head and neck is frequently dermal based, characterized by a dissecting growth pattern within the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, and is often associated with an inflammatory infiltrate. It occurs frequently in sun-damaged skin. Soft tissue angiosarcoma can be extensively haemorrhagic and usually shows high-grade epithelioid to spindle cell morphology. In primary breast angiosarcoma, well-differentiated vascular channels lined by relatively bland endothelial cells may predominate; the diagnosis is mostly based on the infiltrative growth pattern. Angiosarcoma of bone is often epithelioid (> 90% of cases), mimicking metastatic carcinoma, with a variable inflammatory infiltrate including neutrophilic or eosinophilic granulocytes {21323951}.

On immunohistochemistry, angiosarcomas typically show membranous CD31 and nuclear ERG positivity, with variable expression of CD34 {7512718; 21317715; 11474291; 38237880; 21323951; 32271190}. D2-40 can also be expressed. As expression of vascular markers other than ERG and CD31 can be variable, the use of more than one vascular marker is recommended. Epithelioid angiosarcomas tend to express keratins and/or EMA, which increases the risk of misdiagnosis as carcinoma, particularly in multifocal bone cases. Loss of perivascular SMA-positive pericytic cells is common. Irradiation- and lymphoedema-associated angiosarcomas are frequently strongly positive for MYC {21909081}. Angiosarcomas are negative for CAMTA1, TFE3, and FOSB. SMARCB1 (INI1) is retained in nearly all cases.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows scattered tissue fragments showing variably rudimentary vascular architecture and solid regions and dispersed single spindle or epithelioid cells with nuclei, which can be large, with prominent or bar-shaped nucleoli and abundant eccentric cytoplasm including spider-like or tadpole shaped or vacuoles rarely containing red cells. Necrosis and atypical mitoses can be present {12951681; 33964187}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The status of MYC gene amplification can be evaluated either by FISH or the surrogate MYC immunostaining in challenging cases to distinguish from other atypical vascular lesions, including ones occurring in the setting of radiation {20949568; 21909081}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: vasoformative malignant tumour, or poorly differentiated tumour with expression of endothelial markers (CD31, ERG).

Staging

Staging is according to the TNM system (Union for International Cancer Control [UICC] or American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC]), although it is less relevant than in other sarcomas because of the overall poor prognosis.

Prognosis and prediction

Surgery is the treatment of choice for tumours that can be completely resected. Adjuvant chemotherapy seems to improve survival in patients with localized disease who achieved surgical complete remission status, and evidence of tumour shrinkage and/or disease stabilization has been reported {32616718; 38604052}. Radiotherapy has been mainly used with palliative intent for inoperable disease {32616718}.

Angiosarcomas are aggressive tumours with a low 5-year survival {33718199; 40492503}. More than half of patients die of disease within 1 year {9630175}, with a local recurrence rate of 20% and distant metastases in approximately half {9630175}. The most frequent site of distant metastasis is the lungs, followed by lymph nodes, soft tissues, bone, liver, and other sites {9630175; 15166670; 32616718}.

Most angiosarcomas are considered high-grade. Older patient age, retroperitoneal location, and large size are associated with poor outcome {9630175; 17974557; 20485141; 32616718}. Although the value of traditional grading systems remains controversial {15153886; 18813119; 3815265}, histological grading in primary breast angiosarcomas has been shown in some studies to impact outcome {36376999}. Histological prognostic factors (macronucleoli, < 5 eosinophils/2 mm2, ≥ 3 mitoses/2 mm2, p16 expression) have also been identified for bone angiosarcomas {21323951; 23599148}.
