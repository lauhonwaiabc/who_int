2.7.3 Simple bone cyst

Definition

Simple bone cyst (SBC) is an intramedullary, unicystic or multicystic bone neoplasm lined by a variously cellular fibrous membrane and filled with serous or serosanguineous fluid.

ICD-O coding

None (To be added).

ICD-11 coding

FB80.5 Simple bone cyst

Related terminology

Not recommended: solitary bone cyst; unicameral bone cyst.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Most SBCs arise in the proximal humerus (50%) and proximal femur (25%), followed by the calcaneus (6%), other long tubular bones (tibia, fibula, radius, ulna), and the pelvic bones. Rare cases (< 2%) have been described in other locations, including the spine, the small bones of the hands and feet, and the jaws {38181108; 18940504}. In long bones, SBC is located in the metaphysis or metadiaphysis.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients may be asymptomatic or present with pain, swelling, or limitation of motion. Many SBCs are discovered after pathological fracture {24788445}.

Imaging

Roentgenograms show a central lytic lesion, which is well-demarcated, symmetrical, and usually mildly expansile. It initially abuts the growth plate. As the skeleton grows, the cyst appears to migrate away from the growth plate. The cortex may be thinned with endosteal scalloping. A periosteal reaction is absent unless a pathological fracture is present. The largest diameter ranges from < 20 to 160 mm (median: 50–80 mm) {34089379; 32991339; 33316098}. In the presence of a fracture, fragments of cortical bone may float freely in the cyst fluid, a pathognomonic finding referred to as the fallen fragment sign {2781324}. The finding of a gas bubble in the most non-dependent portion of a solitary bone cyst, referred to as the rising bubble sign {19288095; 6494941}, has also been described as pathognomonic. MRI confirms the high water content of the cyst fluid, which shows low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and bright, homogeneous signal intensity on T2-weighted or other fluid-sensitive sequences {10627734}. SBCs usually lack fluid-fluid levels. Fractures, however, may cause trabeculations, which may result in a multiloculated appearance and fluid-fluid levels, mimicking aneurysmal bone cyst {34089379; 32991339}.

Epidemiology

The majority (80%) of SBCs are identified during the first two decades of life, with reported median age at presentation from 11 to 15 years. The estimated annual prevalence is around 0.3 cases per 100 000 individuals {38181108; 34089379; 32991339; 16205152; 22034879}, and the actual incidence is not known as many of these are never discovered {38181108; 28289785}. Male individuals are more commonly affected than female individuals with a reported M:F ratio of 2:1 {38181108; 34089379; 32991339; 16205152; 22034879}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

EWSR1::NFATC2 or FUS::NFATC2 fusions have been identified in approximately 60% of SBCs, usually in a low number of reads {32991339; 33316098; 34081036; 34089379}. These in-frame fusions join exons 5, 6, or 7 of the EWSR1 gene or exon 6 of the FUS gene with exon 3 of the NFATC2 gene. There is no evidence of EWSR1 gene amplification as sometimes seen in NFATC2-rearranged sarcoma {32991339; 34081036}. In a subset of SBCs, NFATC2 fusions may not be detected because of a low number and uneven distribution of lesional cells harbouring the fusion {33316098; 34089379}. No NFATC2 rearrangements have been detected in SBC of the jaws, suggesting they might be molecularly different from SBC in long bones {36939112}.

Macroscopic appearance

SBC is lined by a thin, smooth layer of tan fibrous tissue and contains serous or serosanguineous fluid. Small bony ridges may be present in the cyst. After pathological fracture, the cyst may contain thickened reparative foci or brownish haemorrhagic foci similar to those of an aneurysmal bone cyst.

Histopathology

The wall of SBC is composed of a thin layer of fibrous tissue, which can be hypocellular, or contain foci of nodular fasciitis–like spindle cells. A true lining is often absent; occasionally, inconspicuous spindle cells can be observed on the luminal surface. Foci of chronic inflammation, haemosiderin deposits, foam cells, cholesterol clefts, linear calcifications, and reactive woven bone may be present in the wall. Commonly, fibrin-like collagen deposits are seen on the cyst surface and within the wall. These deposits, which are not pathognomonic of SBC, may become mineralized, resulting in a cementum-like or ossified appearance {22034879; 34089379; 32991339; 31049020; 36555836}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnosis is aneurysmal bone cyst, in particular following a pathological fracture of SBC, presence of fluid-fluid levels on MRI and multinucleated giant cells, and prominent nodular fasciitis–like spindle cell proliferation histologically {34089379}.

Cytology

Aspiration generally yields a colourless to amber fluid with scant cellularity that may include histiocytes, chronic inflammatory cells, and giant cells {15787646}. In the absence of a confirmatory curettage, if the fluid has the above characteristics and the clinical and radiological features are consistent with SBC, cytology may be considered diagnostic. However, the presence of bloody fluid mandates further investigation.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular confirmation is usually not required. EWSR1::NFATC2 or FUS::NFATC2 fusions can be detected by targeted RNA sequencing or other molecular tests (e.g. FISH, RT-PCR).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; variously cellular fibrous lining, focal nodular fasciitis–like areas, fibrin-like collagen deposits.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

SBCs have been treated with a variety of injectables, including steroids and demineralized bone marrow, decompression techniques, and curettage. Treatment success rate depends on the treatment modality and is approximately 80% after curettage {24788445; 38181108}. Local recurrence rates are reported in the range of 10–20% {16205152}. Factors that are associated with increased likelihood of local recurrence may include young patient age, multilocular architecture, large size, and fracture {20808015}. Spontaneous healing after pathological fracture occurs in approximately 10% of patients {30702640}.
