1.7.2 EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour

Definition

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour (EBV-SMT) is a smooth muscle tumour associated with EBV infection, usually in the setting of immunosuppression.

ICD-O coding

8897/1 Smooth muscle tumour of uncertain malignant potential

ICD-11 coding

2F7Y & XH00B4 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of other specified site & Smooth muscle tumour NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: AIDS-associated EBV-positive smooth muscle tumour; EBV-associated post-transplant smooth muscle tumour; smooth muscle tumour in immunocompromised patient; EBV-associated leiomyosarcoma; EBV-associated leiomyoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

EBV-SMTs can occur anywhere in the body, including sites unusual for sporadic leiomyomas and leiomyosarcomas. HIV-associated tumours have a particular predilection for the intra-axial or extra-axial CNS, whereas posttransplant smooth muscle tumours most commonly involve the liver (reported in both the donor and native liver in liver transplant recipients) followed by the lungs, gastrointestinal tract, and spleen {22420456}. Lesions may also affect the head and neck region as well as lymph nodes {36965331; 38067267}. Multicentric tumour distribution is not uncommon, and care should be taken not to misinterpret these tumours as metastatic disease.

Clinical features

The clinical features depend on the location, size, and multifocality of the tumours. Patients may present with an incidental finding or nonspecific pain related to mass effect. More specific features relate to the tumour location, and these include neurological deficits in intra- or extra-axial CNS tumours; obstruction, bleeding, or perforation of the gastrointestinal tract; and cyanosis, fever, and lung infections with endobronchial lesions.

Epidemiology

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour is extremely rare, with an estimated incidence of < 2 cases per 1 million in the general population and 0.7 cases per 1000 solid organ transplant recipients in one case series {16330945}. Occurring over a wide age range (infants to older patients in their sixties) {29535735; 36692065; 34057864} with a slight female predominance, most cases occur in one of three main settings: immunodeficiency due to HIV/AIDS, immunodeficiency after transplantation of a solid organ (kidney, heart, liver), and (least commonly) congenital or primary immunodeficiency. Most patients with primary immunodeficiency disorders such as GATA2 and CARMIL2 deficiency have been children {29535735}, while posttransplant patients and patients with HIV/AIDS are mainly adults. Very rarely, EBV-SMTs can occur in patients who are immunocompetent {36692065; 34057864} and those treated with iatrogenic immunosuppression for autoimmune disease {19079588; 39521699}. Tumours are found several months to years after the onset of the patients’ immunodeficiency syndrome or transplantation.

Etiology

The etiology involves EBV infection in the setting of T-lymphocyte immunosuppression.

Pathogenesis

Knowledge of the pathogenesis of EBV-SMT is limited. Generally, EBV in these tumours achieves a latency type III–like pattern {29535735}. Various theories have been proposed explaining the mode of entry by the virus into precursor tumour cells. The close relationship with blood vessels suggests viral tropism for vascular smooth muscle possibly through the CD21 receptor, which is often expressed in HIV-positive EBV-SMTs. However, CD21 expression is largely absent in posttransplant EBV-SMTs, indicating alternative entry mechanisms. Another possible mechanism involves the fusion of EBV-infected B lymphocytes with smooth muscle cells, a process potentially facilitated by the presence of intratumoural lymphocytes. Concerning tumorigenesis, MYC overexpression and AKT/mTOR pathway activation have been implicated to be main mediators of tumour proliferation {19706821; 23682851}. Importantly, tumour multicentricity is related to multiple infection events, with viral episomal analysis showing independent viral clones rather than dissemination or metastasis of a single clone, which is far less common {16330945}. Furthermore, short tandem repeat (STR) analysis has revealed that posttransplant smooth muscle tumours can be derived from either recipient or donor {22420456}. More recently, a genomic study revealed that EBV-SMTs exhibit a copy-number profile lower than that of leiomyosarcomas clustering more closely to leiomyomas {36965331}. This molecular distinction aligns with the differences in the clinical behaviour observed between EBV-SMTs and leiomyosarcoma. However, there is also recurrent copy-number gain of some oncogenes suggesting malignant potential. Additionally, epigenetic alterations may play an important role in tumorigenesis as recurrent copy-number gains were found in histone deacetylases.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours range from a few millimetres to several centimetres {25572657}, with white to grey cut surfaces, a firm to rubbery consistency, and well-circumscribed to infiltrative borders.

Histopathology

The tumour typically demonstrates identifiable smooth muscle features comprising intersecting fascicles of spindle cells with ample eosinophilic cytoplasm and elongated nuclei, often mimicking a leiomyoma. However, in most cases the tumours also contain a second population of more primitive-appearing round or oval smooth muscle cells. These spindle and round cells may vary in abundance in different tumours and can be intimately admixed within the same tumour. Occasionally the tumour cells may appear epithelioid, or tightly packed, mimicking a low-grade spindle cell neoplasm. A subset of these tumours also exhibit a haemangiopericytoma-like pattern. Cytological atypia is usually mild to moderate but can be marked in patients who are HIV-positive, whose tumours also demonstrate higher mitotic activity {29605279}. Overall, tumours generally have a low mitotic count but can occasionally demonstrate higher mitotic activity (> 10 mitoses/2 mm2 in hotspots) {33891274}. Most of the intratumoural lymphocytes are CD8-positive cytotoxic T cells and negative for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) by in situ hybridization {30120720}. Tumour infiltration is common and usually sparse but can be more pronounced {16330945}. Tumour cells are consistently and diffusely positive for SMA and h-caldesmon. Desmin is sometimes positive often with only focal expression. EBER in situ hybridization is always positive and necessary for the diagnosis.

Cytology

Insufficient data

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology involves demonstration of EBV, especially by in situ hybridization for EBER.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical history of immunosuppression; neoplasm with smooth muscle differentiation; positive expression for EBER transcripts by in situ hybridization.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis is mainly dependent on the condition of the individual patient’s immune system. Histological grading is not a reliable prognostic indicator for biological behaviour or clinical outcome. A comprehensive review of patients with AIDS and EBV-SMTs has revealed more favourable prognosis compared with that of patients with conventional leiomyosarcomas {21437186}. Nevertheless, adverse clinical features, such as the involvement of multiple organs and intracranial regions, have been demonstrated to correlate with decreased overall survival {22420456}. Surgery remains the primary treatment option, especially for tumours that are symptomatic or located in clinically critical sites {33576167}. Several reports advocate for prioritizing immune system restoration as a central therapeutic strategy. These include optimizing antiretroviral therapy in patients with HIV {35141174} and tapering immunosuppressive medications in patients who had transplants to enhance immune function {22420456; 15919482}. In cases related to congenital immunodeficiency, reconstitution by bone marrow transplantation has been reported to be effective {27924436}. Given the possible role of the mTOR pathway in tumorigenesis, there is also interest in using mTOR inhibitors for treating EBV-SMT with some reported success {19706821; 17081922; 30614215}. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are generally considered ineffective in treating these tumours.
