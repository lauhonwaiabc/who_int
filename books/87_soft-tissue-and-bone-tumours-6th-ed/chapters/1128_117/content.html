1.12.8 Ossifying fibromyxoid tumour

Definition

Ossifying fibromyxoid tumour is a mesenchymal tumour of unknown lineage, composed of ovoid or round cells arranged in cords within a fibromyxoid matrix, often with a partial peripheral shell of bone and PHF1 rearrangement, showing variable clinical behaviour.

Malignant ossifying fibromyxoid tumour is defined by high cellularity, high nuclear atypia, and a mitotic rate of > 1 mitosis/6 mm² (> 2 mitoses/50 HPF).

ICD-O coding

8842/0 Ossifying fibromyxoid tumour NOS

ICD-11 coding

2F7C&HX1DA7 Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain behaviour and histogenesis

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The tumours are most often subcutaneous and rarely intramuscular or purely cutaneous. They most often involve extremities, and less often truncal sites and head and neck {2476942; 12657926; 9869828; 18469710; 39809106; 25950586}. Few examples have been reported in genitourinary sites including perirenal soft tissue, ureter, and penis {37026814}.

Clinical features

The tumour typically presents as a painless hard subcutaneous mass.

Epidemiology

This tumour is rare, and only about 300 cases have been reported. It has been described to occur across a wide age range (5–88 years), with a median age of 50 years {2476942; 12657926; 18469710}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

About 80% of cases carry a characteristic fusion often involving the PHF1 gene. The most common fusions are EP400::PHF1 (as many as 40% of cases) and PHF1::TFE3 (10%). PHF1 fusions cause inappropriate chromatin accessibility, resulting in distinct gene expression and methylation profiles {31932680}. Additional fusions involving other genes regulating chromatin accessibility have been described {22796436; 27537276; 30920708; 32352579; 36648026; 37819540}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumour is usually well-circumscribed or encapsulated, and size ranges from 5 to 210 mm with the median size of 40 mm. Bone elements are seen in half of the cases, sometimes encircling the tumour periphery. Cut surface is tan-white, nodular, firm, and rubbery.

Histopathology

Tumours are often surrounded by a fibrous capsule and often organized with a lobular architecture, but in some cases tumour nodules are seen in surrounding fat. Bone elements are often but not always present, sometimes encircling the tumour periphery. The tumour cells are usually uniform, ovoid or epithelioid, and arranged in cords or diffuse sheets in a collagenous or myxoid stroma. Mitotic activity is low in most cases (< 1 mitosis/6 mm² [< 2 mitoses/50 HPF]). Higher mitotic activity is seen in 10% of cases, and these are considered to have malignant potential, as are tumours with high nuclear atypia. Malignant examples can contain osteosarcoma-like bone elements. By immunohistochemistry, tumour cells are variably positive for S100. Desmin and keratin expression is seen in a minority of cases, usually focally, whereas CD34, EMA, SMA, and SOX10 are negative {7661277; 12657926; 18469710; 2476942}.

Cytology

Cytology smears show epithelioid cells with bland round nuclei and eccentric cytoplasm, in a background of abundant eosinophilic matrix, with no necrosis or atypia. Immunohistochemistry and FISH and correlation with imaging are required for the diagnosis {30912617}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detecting a characteristic fusion involving the PHF1 gene can be useful in confirming the diagnosis, especially in cases with unusual histology.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential criteria: ovoid or epithelioid cells forming cords and sheets in a collagenous or myxoid matrix, often with bone elements; immunoreactivity for S100 and often focal desmin; for malignant OFMT: high cellularity; high nuclear atypia; and mitotic rate of > 1 mitosis/6 mm2 (> 2 mitoses/50 HPF).

Desirable: detection of PHF1 rearrangement or uncommon gene fusions in select cases.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Tumours with mitotic activity of > 1 mitosis/6 mm2 (> 2 mitoses/50 HPF) more often recur and may metastasize. However, metastases have also been reported in tumours with lesser mitotic activity. Metastases mostly occur in lungs and soft tissue and can be delayed 20 years after primary surgery.
