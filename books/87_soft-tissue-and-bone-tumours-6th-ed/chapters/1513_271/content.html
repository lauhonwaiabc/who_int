1.5.1.3 Arteriovenous malformation

Definition

Arteriovenous malformation (AVM), formerly known as AVM-type haemangioma, is a fast-flow congenital vascular malformation characterized by direct communication between arteries and veins without an intervening capillary bed. Most AVMs are isolated unifocal lesions, but they may be multifocal or part of complex syndromes.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Sporadic arteriovenous malformation; arteriovenous malformation occurring in Parkes Weber syndrome; arteriovenous malformation occurring in capillary malformation–arteriovenous malformation; arteriovenous malformation occurring in hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia; arteriovenous malformation occurring in Juvenile polyposis/hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia syndrome, arteriovenous malformation occurring in PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome

Localization

Extracranial AVMs including the ones associated with capillary malformation–arteriovenous malformation (CM-AVM) affect predominantly the head and neck, followed by the extremities, trunk, and viscera {18544994}. Parkes Weber syndrome almost always occurs in a lower extremity. Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) has cutaneous and mucosal AVMs manifesting as telangiectasias and occasionally large pulmonary, hepatic, gastrointestinal, and intracranial AVMs {26055853; 32842615}.

Clinical features

AVMs are congenital lesions that may become clinically apparent at different ages, depending on their location, depth, growth rate, and triggering factors. They typically progress over time, especially during hormonal changes (e.g. puberty, pregnancy) or after trauma or surgical intervention. Superficial AVMs present as flat, warm, erythematous lesions, sometimes complicated by pain, ulceration, or bleeding; extensive shunting may lead to high-output cardiac failure {32695695}. Deep AVMs may lack cutaneous signs and are diagnosed later.

Multifocal soft tissue AVMs may occur in patients with CM-AVM and PTEN-hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS). Patients with Parkes Weber syndrome present with a warm overgrown extremity and a variable capillary stain. CM-AVM1 and CM-AVM2 usually present with small and multifocal capillary malformations with or without a pale halo. The most common clinical feature in patients with HHT is spontaneous, recurrent nosebleeds (epistaxis), typically beginning around the age of 12 years. Telangiectasias develop on the lips, tongue, buccal and gastrointestinal mucosa, face, and fingers. Larger AVMs most frequently occur in the lungs, liver, or brain, where bleeding or shunting can lead to sudden and potentially life-threatening complications. Gastrointestinal bleeding occurs in a minority of individuals with HHT {28687708; 29217067; 32842615}. In patients with PHTS, vascular anomalies often coexist with macrocephaly, developmental delay, and autism spectrum disorder {37800459; 25549896}.

Epidemiology

Soft tissue AVMs are rare vascular anomalies, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 per 100 000 individuals – significantly lower than that of venous or lymphatic malformations {26598124}. They are typically diagnosed in childhood or early adulthood and show no sex predilection.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most AVMs are solitary and sporadic, typically harbouring somatic activating variants in MAP2K1; less frequently, variants in KRAS, BRAF, or HRAS are detected {28190454; 32799314; 32799314; 31637524}. Inherited forms arise in rare vascular syndromes, primarily CM-AVM and PHTS. CM-AVM1 is linked to autosomal dominant variants in RASA1 {24038909; 29891884}, CM-AVM2 is caused by autosomal dominant variants in EPHB4 {28687708}, and PHTS is associated with autosomal dominant variants in PTEN {19668082}. HHT is autosomal dominant, genetically heterogeneous, and is classified according to the genes / genomic regions involved: HHT1 (ENG), HHT2 (ACVRL1), HHT3 (5q31), HHT4 (7p14), HHT5 (GDF2), and juvenile polyposis / HHT syndrome (SMAD4) {32842615}. Endothelial MAP2K1 variants in AVMs activate the RAS/MAPK pathway {32703450}, causing altered expression of many genes involved in processes important for vasculogenesis (e.g. cell migration, adhesion, extracellular matrix organization, tube formation, angiogenesis) {37422456; 35972708; 28190454}.

Macroscopic appearance

AVMs are poorly defined lesions that contain variable numbers of small and large blood vessels, admixed with native tissues.

Histopathology

AVMs exhibit variable morphology. The nidus is composed of disproportionately enlarged arteries and veins, along with vessels of indeterminate morphological features. Arteries exhibit tortuosity, irregular calibres, focal fragmentation or duplication of the internal elastic lamina, and focal neointimal hyperplasia. Veins demonstrate intimal hyperplasia, thickened smooth muscle coats, and adventitial fibrosis – changes driven by high-pressure arterial flow. A generalized increase in small vessels resembling small arteries, capillaries, or veins is commonly observed. Many AVMs contain lobular foci of small-calibre vessels, predominantly of capillary type, which may mimic other vascular lesions such as pyogenic granuloma, infantile or congenital haemangioma, or the pseudokaposiform proliferation seen in Stewart–Bluefarb syndrome {27607322; 31637524; 32799314}.

The intervascular stroma is variably cellular and shows oedema, fibrosis, haemosiderin deposition, and occasional fat entrapment. Chronic inflammation, consisting mainly of lymphocytes and macrophages, is frequently present.

Lesions subjected to prior embolization or sclerotherapy often exhibit thrombotic occlusion of vessels, with variable degrees of organization and recanalization; intraluminal or interstitial foreign material; ischaemic-type necrosis; and extensive perivascular and stromal fibrosis.

Cytology

FNABs are not routinely performed.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Sporadic AVMs harbour somatic MAP2K1, KRAS, HRAS, or BRAF variants. Germline variants in RASA1, EPHB4, and PTEN may be demonstrated in patients with CM-AVM1, CM-AVM2, and PHTS.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: vessels of different sizes and types, including both arteries and veins with high-pressure arterial flow features; clinical and radiological evidence of an arteriovenous shunt within a poorly defined vascular lesion.

Desirable: for sporadic AVM: demonstration of somatic MAP2K1 variants (or other variants); for AVM associated with a syndrome: demonstration of germline mutations in any of these genes: RASA1, EPHB4, ENG, ACVRL1, GDF2, or SMAD4.

Staging

The Schobinger classification defines the clinical progression of AVMs in four stages (quiescent, expansion, destruction, and decompensation), on the basis of symptoms and haemodynamic changes {9727427}.

Prognosis and prediction

Most AVMs are progressive and eventually require therapeutic intervention. Because of their infiltrative growth, risk of bleeding, and high rates of recurrence, AVMs remain challenging to manage. Treatment options include embolization, surgical resection, and targeted pharmacological agents – primarily inhibitors of the RAS/MAPK/ERK signalling pathway. AVMs associated with CM-AVM syndromes typically follow a more indolent course. Recurrent growth following incomplete treatment is common. Advances in endovascular techniques and targeted therapies have improved disease control, but long-term follow-up is essential given the persistent risk of progression and relapse {31030813; 32557381}.
