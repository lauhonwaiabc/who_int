1.6.1 Glomus tumour

Definition

Glomus tumour is a mesenchymal neoplasm composed of cells resembling the perivascular modified smooth muscle cells of the normal glomus body.

ICD-O coding

8711/0 Glomus tumour NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Y & XH47J2 Other specified neoplastic haemangioma & Glomus tumour NOS

2E81.0Y & XH21E6 Other specified neoplastic haemangioma & Glomus tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Acceptable: glomangioma; glomangiomyoma.

Subtype(s)

glomangiomatosis; malignant glomus tumour

Localization

The vast majority occur in the distal extremities, particularly the subungual region, the hand, the wrist, and the foot {11145243; 14080348; 20530151; 32604167}. Rare tumours have been reported in almost every location, including gastrointestinal tract {11859201}, genitourinary system {19912496; 10750892; 15974822; 28438616}, mediastinum {8883329}, nerve {8600806}, bone {3036410}, and lung {10935651}. An unusually large number occur in the stomach {11859201}. Fusion-positive benign glomus tumours are primarily seen in male patients, with a predilection for the extremities, while malignant glomus tumours occur mostly in the viscera and soft tissue {18422692; 11145243; 30314644; 32604167}.

Clinical features

Cutaneous glomus tumours are typically small (< 10 mm), reddish-blue nodules often associated with a long history of pain, particularly with exposure to cold or minor tactile stimulation. Deep-seated or visceral glomus tumours present as a nonspecific mass.

Epidemiology

Glomus tumours are rare, accounting for < 2% of soft tissue tumours {14080348}. Multiple lesions may be seen in 10% of patients. They can occur at any age, but most are diagnosed in young adults (20–40 years). Subungual glomus tumours are far more common in women {11677663; 8732413}. Malignant glomus tumours are rare {11145243; 28834810; 30314644; 23999936; 32604167}. NOTCH-rearranged benign glomus tumours of soft tissue are overwhelmingly seen in male patients {32604167}.

Etiology

The syndrome of multiple familial glomus tumours (glomuvenous malformations) shows autosomal dominant inheritance {10393451; 11231328} and is caused by inactivating mutations in GLMN, which is predominantly expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells {10364524; 11845407; 15053987}. Another genetic mechanism, demonstrated in about 70% of familial multiple glomus tumours, is the uniparental disomy, further supporting a somatic second-hit model {23375657}. An association between digital glomus tumours and neurofibromatosis type 1 has been reported, with frequent involvement of multiple digits {19738042; 12161612; 7829715; 22250039}. Biallelic NF1 inactivation underlies the pathogenesis of neurofibromatosis type 1–associated glomus tumours {19738042}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic benign and malignant glomus tumours of soft tissue or visceral origin harbour recurrent NOTCH-family gene rearrangements, with CARMN (MIR143HG)::NOTCH1/NOTCH2/NOTCH3 fusion genes in more than half of cases {23999936; 32604167}. NOTCH-family gene rearrangements have not been detected in subungual glomus tumours or other tumours of pericytic origin {32604167}. In addition, a small subset of sporadic glomus tumours show oncogenic BRAF and KRAS variants {22317887}, and BRAF variants have been associated with malignant histology {28834810; 28438258}.

Macroscopic appearance

Glomus tumours typically form circumscribed, ovoid or round nodules with a solid or cystic and often haemorrhagic cut surface.

Histopathology

Glomus cells are small, uniform, and rounded, with a centrally placed round nucleus, amphophilic to lightly eosinophilic cytoplasm, and sharply defined cell borders. Occasionally, cases show oncocytic or epithelioid change {7672784; 3038728}. Solid glomus tumours account for approximately 75% of cases and are composed of nests of glomus cells surrounding capillary-sized vessels. Glomuvenous malformations (also known as glomangiomas) are most common in patients with multiple or familial lesions, account for approximately 20% of cases, and are characterized by cavernous haemangioma–like vascular structures surrounded by small clusters of glomus cells. Glomangiomyomas show transition from typical glomus cells to elongated cells resembling mature smooth muscle. In some glomus tumours, a branching, haemangiopericytoma-like vasculature is present (glomangiopericytoma) {9591720}. Glomangiomatosis is an extremely rare subtype of glomus tumour with an overall architectural resemblance to a diffuse vascular malformation (so-called angiomatosis) but containing nests of glomus cells investing vessel walls {11145243; 12376927; 15372213; 19267531}. Symplastic glomus tumours show striking nuclear atypia in the absence of any other features indicative of malignancy (e.g. large size, deep location, mitotic activity, necrosis) {19448066; 19155729; 11145243; 19602065}.

The diagnosis of malignant glomus tumour should be reserved for tumours showing either marked nuclear atypia (with any level of mitotic activity) or atypical mitotic figures {11145243}. A component of pre-existing benign-appearing glomus tumour is often present. There are two types of malignant glomus tumour: one resembling a leiomyosarcoma or fibrosarcoma and the other resembling a primitive round cell tumour. Immunohistochemical demonstration of SMA and pericellular collagen IV is required for this diagnosis in the absence of a clear-cut benign precursor. Glomus tumours not fulfilling the criteria for malignancy but having at least one atypical feature other than nuclear pleomorphism are designated as being of uncertain malignant potential. Although glomus tumours of > 20 mm in size and in deep location were previously considered malignant, subsequent experience suggests that these have uncertain malignant potential. An infiltrative growth pattern, high cellularity, and spindled morphology are also features significantly more common in malignant glomus tumours and glomus tumours of uncertain malignant potential {28834810; 32604167}.

Glomus tumours of all types typically express SMA and MSA. Staining for h-caldesmon is also positive, whereas CD34 expression is focal or absent {11145243; 25614464}. BRAF p.(Val600Glu) (V600E) expression is seen in the BRAF-mutated molecular subset. Gastric glomus tumours are commonly synaptophysin positive {38291661}.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows tightly packed, demarcated nests in tissue fragments of small, round to polygonal epithelioid cells with round hyperchromatic nuclei and even fine chromatin, with intercellular magenta (Giemsa) myxoid stroma {23635014}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of NOTCH-family gene rearrangements and/or BRAF variants can be helpful, especially in malignant examples {28834810; 30556047}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: monomorphic round to epithelioid cells with centrally placed, round nuclei and well-defined cell borders, arranged in perivascular nests; malignant glomus tumours may usually be recognized by an adjacent benign component. In malignant examples lacking a benign component, immunohistochemistry and/or molecular diagnosis are required for definitive diagnosis.

Desirable: immunohistochemical expression of SMA, h-caldesmon, and collagen IV; NOTCH-family gene rearrangements or gene fusions.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Typical glomus tumours, glomuvenous malformations, and symplastic glomus tumours are benign. Malignant glomus tumours are aggressive, with metastases and death from disease in as many as 40% of patients. Some large, visceral glomus tumours without other atypical features (uncertain malignant potential) have behaved aggressively. The natural history of histologically malignant glomus tumours in the skin may be favourable, although more data are needed {30314644}.
