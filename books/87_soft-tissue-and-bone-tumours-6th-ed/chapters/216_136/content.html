2.1.6 Osteochondroma

Definition

Osteochondroma is a benign cartilaginous neoplasm consisting of a bony projection covered by a cartilage cap, arising at the external surface of bone and containing a marrow cavity that is continuous with that of the underlying bone.

ICD-O coding

9210/0 Osteochondroma

ICD-11 coding

2E83.Y & XH5Y87 Other specified benign osteogenic tumours & Osteochondroma

Related terminology

Not recommended: (osteo)cartilaginous exostosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Osteochondromas arise in bones that are formed by endochondral ossification during skeletal development, explaining their localization. The most common sites include the metaphysial regions of the distal femur, proximal tibia, proximal humerus, and less frequently flat bones. The craniofacial bones are generally not affected, except for the mandibular condyle.

Clinical features

Many osteochondromas, whether solitary or multiple, are asymptomatic and found incidentally. Osteochondromas develop and increase in size during the first decade of life, and they stop growing around puberty, when the growth plates close. Symptoms are often related to the size and location of the lesion. The most common presentation is that of a hard, longstanding mass. Complications include bursa formation; arthritis; and impingement on adjacent tendons, nerves, vessels, or spinal cord {7702095}. Increasing pain and/or a growing mass in an adult patient may be a manifestation of progression towards a peripheral chondrosarcoma. A sharp change in pain is indicative of fracture of the stalk.

Imaging

Osteochondromas are typically located at the transition of diaphysis to metaphysis of the bone. The radiological hallmark of osteochondroma, best seen on CT and MRI, is the continuity of bone marrow into a pedunculated (slim base) or sessile (broad base) outgrowth combined with flaring of cortical bone into the margins of the outgrowth. Osteochondromas point away from the adjacent joint. Smooth calcifications can be seen. MRI may display the cause of symptoms on the basis of mechanical compromise of neighbouring tendons, muscles, bones, vessels (including pseudoaneurysm), nerves, roots, and spinal cord. A bursa, exhibiting septations, heterogeneity, and early inhomogeneous enhancement on MRI, can develop and must be differentiated from the cartilaginous cap {40011261}. Bone deformity may be caused by the lesion itself, or by metaphysial undertubulation. Fat, haematopoietic marrow, and cartilaginous nests may be seen on MRI in the base of the stalk. Septations in the lobulated lesion may enhance on MRI and/or may show mineralization on CT and MRI. The size of the cartilaginous cap is measured perpendicular to the tidemark (the bone–cartilage interface) and can be measured on fluid-sensitive MRI. In adults, a cap measuring > 20 to 30 mm and/or marked inhomogeneity is suggestive of chondrosarcoma {37778187; 3890184; 33595238; 35193636; 10671608; 20392983}. A bulky benign cartilaginous cap may be present in the paediatric age group, but it should decrease in size with ageing. Only in young children, active endochondral ossification may cause inhomogeneity in the cap. In the paediatric age group osteochondromas may completely regress {36547191}.

Epidemiology

Osteochondroma is one of the most common benign bone tumours, providing in radiological studies and case series an estimated prevalence of 0.4–3% {18853760; 28852349}. The reported incidence of 35% of all benign bone tumours and 8% of all bone tumours may be an underestimate because many osteochondromas are asymptomatic. The average age at diagnosis of solitary osteochondroma is 18 years (median: 15 years; range: 2–77 years), with a male predominance (65% vs 35%) [[Picci P, Manfrini M, Fabbri N, et al., editors. Atlas of musculoskeletal tumors and tumorlike lesions. The Rizzoli Case Archive. Cham (Switzerland): Springer International Publishing; 2014.]]. Approximately 15% of patients with osteochondroma have multiple lesions characteristic of the autosomal dominant hereditary multiple osteochondromas syndrome (see section Multiple osteochondromas (EXT1, EXT2)).

Etiology

Osteochondromas are caused by biallelic inactivation of the EXT1 or EXT2 gene. In patients with multiple osteochondromas, heterozygous germline alterations are found in the EXT1 or EXT2 gene (see section Multiple osteochondromas (EXT1, EXT2)), combined with somatic loss of the remaining wildtype allele within the cartilage cap of the osteochondromas {10441575; 20080592; 11169766; 20813973; 20418910}. In solitary sporadic osteochondromas, homozygous deletions of EXT1 can be found in about 80% of tumours {17341731; 20418910}. Osteochondromas have been reported to occur after total body irradiation in childhood {23965908}.

Pathogenesis

The finding of biallelic inactivation of the EXT1 or EXT2 gene within the cartilage cap supports the neoplastic nature of these tumours. The cell of origin is considered to be either a chondrocyte within the growth plate {20080592} or a mesenchymal progenitor cell from the perichondrium {23458899}. Most of the mutations found in the EXT1 or EXT2 gene are predicted to result in a truncated or non-functional protein {19810120}. The EXT gene products, EXT1 and EXT2, are glycosyltransferases involved in heparan sulfate biosynthesis. The cartilage cap of osteochondroma is composed of a mixture of wildtype (normal heparan sulfate) and mutated (heparan sulfate–deficient) cells {20534475; 20080592; 21506131}. Heparan sulfate proteoglycans serve as key modulators of endochondral ossification by forming an osmotic gradient around chondrocytes and by controlling signal transduction {16072037}. Loss of heparan sulfate may give a proliferative advantage to the mutated chondroprogenitor cells and leads to loss of polarity {18654627; 20421870}. Because defective heparan sulfate mainly affects multiple signalling pathways, including those involving hedgehog {15177029}, BMP {29277722}, FGFR3 {29545125}, and WNT/β-catenin {29545125}, disturbed signalling causes EXT-mutated cells to grow out of the bone and recruit normal cells to form an osteochondroma.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteochondroma may be cauliflower-like, sessile, or pedunculated {24706308}. Within the underlying stalk, a cortex and medullary cavity can be recognized. The size of the overlying glassy bluish-white cartilaginous cap is important and should be well documented. It should be measured perpendicular to the bone–cartilage interface, at its thickest portion. The cartilage cap is usually thin (and thickness decreases with age). A thick and irregular cap (> 2 cm) may be indicative of progression.

Histopathology

The lesion is composed of three distinct layers: perichondrium, cartilage, and bone. The outermost layer is a fibrous perichondrium, continuous with the periosteum of the underlying bone. Beneath this lies a cartilage cap that resembles disorganized growth plate cartilage and undergoes endochondral ossification. Superficial chondrocytes appear clustered, while those near the bone interface are arranged in vague columns and enlarge, resembling hypertrophic chondrocytes of the growth plate.

With age, the cartilage becomes progressively less cellular and may show extensive, coarse, and irregular calcification. Binucleated cells, focal necrosis, calcification, nodularity, and degenerative cystic or mucoid changes may be present but are not indicative of malignancy {22555180}. Nuclear atypia and mitotic figures are absent. At the base of the lesion, osteoclast-mediated resorption of mineralized cartilage occurs, and the residual cartilage matrix serves as a scaffold for subsequent bone deposition.

Differential diagnosis

The histological distinction between osteochondroma and secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma grade 1 is challenging and should be made within a multidisciplinary team context, incorporating radiological findings, particularly the thickness of the cartilage cap, tumour location, and the patient’s age. Histological features such as loss of lobular architecture, nodularity, broad fibrous septa, myxoid change, binucleated chondrocytes, and increased cellularity must be interpreted with caution, as they can appear in benign lesions. Fractures through the stalk may induce a localized fibroblastic response that can mimic malignancy.

Periosteal chondrosarcoma can be distinguished from osteochondroma by the absence of a stalk. Subungual exostosis, although often highly cellular, shows a characteristic fibrillary cartilaginous matrix, not present in osteochondroma. Dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica (Trevor disease), a non-hereditary skeletal dysplasia, presents with cartilaginous overgrowth of the epiphysis and may closely resemble osteochondroma; however, it is characterized by chondrocyte clustering within a more fibrillary chondroid matrix {16622899}.

Parosteal osteosarcoma may also mimic osteochondroma due to the presence of a cartilage cap, but it can be differentiated by the infiltration of atypical spindle cells interspersed within neoplastic bone. Furthermore, parosteal osteosarcoma lacks continuity between the tumour and the medullary cavity on both imaging and histological examination, whereas such continuity is a hallmark of osteochondroma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing is not required for the diagnosis of osteochondroma. Chromosomal aberrations involve 8q22-q24.1, where the EXT1 gene is located {9576285; 7507706; 12393280}, or 11p11.2, where the EXT2 gene is located {21703028}. Variants in IDH1 or IDH2 are absent in osteochondromas {21598255; 22057234}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; cartilaginous cap, with growth plate–like architecture (in children) or extensive calcification (with increasing age); underlying stalk, with medullary and cortical bone that is continuous with that of the underlying bone; the size of the cartilaginous cap should be well documented and should be < 2 cm in the skeletally mature.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Excision of the osteochondroma is usually curative, although recurrence is seen after incomplete removal. Multiple recurrences or recurrence of a well-excised lesion should raise suspicion for progression. The risk of progression to secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma is estimated at about 1% for solitary and as high as 5% for multiple osteochondromas {7702095; 9272707; 8027127}. In very rare instances, osteosarcomas, spindle cell sarcomas, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcomas can develop in osteochondroma {10458834; 11896078; 17473135}.
