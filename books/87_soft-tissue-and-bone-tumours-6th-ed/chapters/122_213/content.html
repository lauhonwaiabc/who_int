1.2.2 Lipomatosis

Definition

Lipomatosis is a diffuse overgrowth of adipose tissue in a varying anatomical distribution.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

EF02.1 Subcutaneous lipomatosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: Madelung disease; Launois–Bensaude syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Diffuse lipomatosis; multiple symmetrical lipomatosis; pelvic lipomatosis; steroid lipomatosis; HIV lipodystrophy

Localization

Diffuse lipomatosis involves the trunk, a large portion of an extremity, head and neck, abdomen, pelvis, or intestines. Macrodactyly or gigantism of a digit may be present {1892721; 5770417}. Symmetrical lipomatosis manifests as symmetrical deposition of fat in the upper body, particularly the neck. In pelvic lipomatosis, there is diffuse overgrowth of fat in the perivesical and perirectal areas. Steroid lipomatosis is characterized by accumulation of fat in the face, sternal region, or upper-middle back (buffalo hump). HIV lipodystrophy shows accumulation of visceral fat, breast adiposity, and cervical fat pads {9675454; 11746223}.

Clinical features

Subclassification is based on clinical findings and anatomical distribution. Patients present with massive accumulation of fat in affected areas that may mimic a neoplasm {38871514}. Patients with symmetrical lipomatosis can have neuropathy and involvement of the CNS {7783758; 3179687}. Accumulation of fat in the lower neck can cause laryngeal obstruction and compression of the vena cava. Pelvic lipomatosis causes urinary frequency, perineal pain, constipation, and abdominal and back pain. Bowel obstruction and hydronephrosis can develop. Imaging in lipomatosis shows the extent of fat accumulation and excludes other processes. Patients with HIV treated with antiretroviral medications may develop hyperlipidaemia, insulin resistance, and fat wasting in the face and limbs {25598476}.

Epidemiology

Lipomatosis is rare. Diffuse lipomatosis usually occurs in children aged < 2 years, but it may affect adults. Pelvic lipomatosis often affects young Black and Asian male individuals {916046; 38152349}; the incidence is 0.6–1.7 cases per 100 000 hospital admissions in the USA {30681625; 38152349}. Multiple symmetrical lipomatosis develops in middle-aged men (M:F ratio: ~19:1), and the mean age is 51 years, mostly in patients of Mediterranean descent. Patients have a history of liver disease or excessive alcohol consumption {39648639}.

Etiology

Multiple symmetrical lipomatosis is associated with liver disease or excessive alcohol consumption. Steroid lipomatosis manifests in patients on hormone therapy or who have increased endogenous production of adrenocortical steroids {31448201}. Patients who are HIV-positive and treated with nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors and protease inhibitors often develop lipodystrophy {25598476; 40724797}.

Pathogenesis

Patients with multiple symmetrical lipomatosis have variants in mitochondrial DNA genes; familial subtypes are recognized {30873109; 30283804}. Upregulation of CK2, AKT, and ERK1/2 has also been described {33114687}. Some examples of facial infiltrating lipomatosis have been shown to have PIK3CA variants {37452404}, and mutation in PALB2 (encoding an intranuclear protein that anchors BRCA2 to nuclear structures) has been described in a family with familial multiple subcutaneous lipomatosis {26845227}. The umbrella term “PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum” encompasses the clinical entities associated with somatic PIK3CA variants including macrodactyly, fibroadipose hyperplasia or overgrowth, hemihyperplasia multiple lipomatosis, congenital lipomatous overgrowth, fibroadipose infiltrating lipomatosis, scoliosis/skeletal and spinal anomalies, vascular malformations, epidermal naevi, and related megalencephaly syndromes {25557259; 32692051; 36729078; 35256403}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of lipomatosis is the same for all subtypes. The lesions are poorly circumscribed aggregates of soft, yellow fat identical in appearance to normal fat. The differences are the site of involvement and the distribution of the fat.

Histopathology

All the different types of lipomatosis have identical morphological features, consisting of lobules and sheets of mature adipocytes that may infiltrate other structures such as skeletal muscle.

Cytology

Where cytological confirmation of a diagnosis is required, aspirated material would show tissue fragments of adipocytes containing a single large, clear cytoplasmic vacuole with a compressed rim of cytoplasm and a small nucleus with an inconspicuous nucleolus.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: diffuse overgrowth of mature adipose tissue in a particular anatomical region.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Idiopathic forms of lipomatosis tend to recur after surgery. Treatment is palliative surgical removal of excess fat. Massive accumulation of fat in the neck region may cause death due to laryngeal obstruction. Fat in steroid lipomatosis regresses after steroid levels are lowered. Patients with HIV treated with antiretroviral medications may experience metabolic disturbances.
