1.8.4 Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma

Definition

Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS) is a malignant neoplasm composed of a monomorphic population of primitive round cells showing skeletal muscle differentiation and is defined by a recurrent gene fusion, usually PAX3::FOXO1 or PAX7::FOXO1.

ICD-O coding

8920/3 Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B55.Z & XH7099 Rhabdomyosarcoma & Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: mixed embryonal and alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma; fusion-negative alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ARMS most commonly arises in the deep soft tissue of the extremities {7882714}. Other sites include the head and neck, genitourinary and gynaecological tracts, paraspinal tissue, and the perineal region {6620082; 11835233; 18973919; 26756883}.

Clinical features

ARMS is a highly aggressive and rapidly growing malignant soft tissue tumour, presenting at diagnosis with distant or locoregional metastases via lymphatic or haematogenous spread in 25–30% of patients {18467730; 22378628; 28521080}. Symptoms are typically related to tumour location and size. Head and neck tumours with meningeal extension often cause cranial nerve deficits {21873599}, paraspinal tumours may result in spinal nerve compression {27932889; 28875443}, and perirectal or pelvic tumours may lead to constipation or symptoms of bowel obstruction {24045440}. Primary disseminated tumours resembling leukaemia may rarely occur {25305220; 29096655}.

Epidemiology

ARMS is the second most common type of rhabdomyosarcoma, constituting about 25% of these tumours in different case series {28521080; 28271871; 22378628; 29956493}. It has an annual incidence of 1–1.2 cases per 1 million population aged 0–20 years {36069287}. Compared with embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS), ARMS occurs in a slightly older population, with a peak incidence among individuals aged 10–25 years (median age: 15 years) and roughly equal incidence in male and female patients {28521080; 28271871; 22378628; 29956493}. A subset of cases have also been observed in adults aged > 40 years {18973919; 22089931}.

Etiology

Compared with ERMS, ARMS rarely harbours germline cancer predisposing variants {38546643; 37638828}.

Pathogenesis

A t(2;13)(q36;q14) translocation is found in the majority of ARMS, and a t(1;13)(p36;q14) is seen in a smaller subset. These translocations juxtapose PAX3 (at 2q36.1) or PAX7 (at 1p36.13) with the FOXO1 gene at 13q14.11, to generate chimeric genes that encode PAX3::FOXO1 and PAX7::FOXO1 fusion proteins {8098985; 8187070; 8275086}. PAX3 and PAX7 represent transcription factors that play essential roles in myogenesis {26424495}. The PAX::FOXO1 fusion proteins function as oncoproteins affecting growth, survival, differentiation, and other pathways through activation of numerous downstream target genes such as MET, ALK, FGFR4, MYCN, IGF1R, and MYOD1 {8643596; 15688035; 20663909; 28446439; 30617281}. Less common fusion gene variants include the fusion of PAX3 to FOXO4, NCOA1, MAML3, or INO80D and the fusion of FOXO1 to FGFR1 {12183429; 19953635; 21666686; 24436047; 39147032}.

Amplification of genomic regions 2p24 (containing the MYCN oncogene) and 12q13-q14 (including CDK4) occur most often in PAX3::FOXO1 ARMSs, and amplification of regions 1p36 (which encompasses the PAX7 locus) and 13q31 (which includes MIR17HG) are associated specifically with PAX7::FOXO1 tumours {9872655; 10534762; 19422036; 21220470; 10825007; 30617281}. Analyses of individual genes in sporadic ARMS have implicated several important oncogenic pathways. Inactivating variants of TP53 and CDKN2A/CDKN2B and activating variants of FGFR4 are present in a small subset {8703847; 15098008; 19809159}. Cytoplasmic or membranous ALK protein overexpression occurs in the majority of ARMSs, coinciding with ALK gene copy-number gain or amplification in a smaller subset; however, targeting this kinase in vivo does not yield therapeutic efficacy {18788887; 22184391; 23307059; 29855693}. Tumours that were previously considered fusion-negative ARMS were shown to be distinct from fusion-positive ARMS at the clinical, genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic levels, being more similar to ERMS {12183429; 16849537; 17471488; 20351326; 26226845; 22419069; 26138366; 29973406; 30373318; 30565669}.

Macroscopic appearance

ARMS is soft, fleshy, and grey, with variable amounts of fibrous tissue, as well as occasional necrotic areas and haemorrhage. Deep-seated tumours involving the musculature commonly infiltrate surrounding tissues.

Histopathology

ARMS is highly cellular and composed of primitive round cells with scant cytoplasm and hyperchromatic nuclei. Tumour cells are arranged in nests separated by fibrovascular septa, which frequently exhibit loss of cellular cohesion in the centre, conferring a pattern of irregular alveolar spaces and a varying degree of cystic change {28521080; 24113309}. The solid subtype of ARMS is composed of sheets of neoplastic cells that have cytomorphological features of ARMS but lack septa or discohesion. Mitotic activity is often brisk. Occasionally, recognizable rhabdomyoblastic differentiation can be discerned. Wreath-like multinucleated tumour giant cells are a common feature of ARMS. Rare cases may show clear cell morphology, with pale-staining and glycogen-containing cytoplasm. Chemotherapy and radiation treatment can induce histological changes such as decreased cellularity, more pronounced rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, and increased stromal fibrosis. Tumours with mixed embryonal and alveolar features were previously considered to be a subtype of ARMS, but most such cases lack PAX::FOXO1 fusions, thus appearing to be clinically and biologically more akin to ERMS. However, fusion genes have been detected in rare cases of mixed ERMS/ARMS, which therefore represent examples of ARMS {23765537; 26756883}.

Rhabdomyoblastic differentiation of ARMS manifests immunohistochemically with positive reactions to desmin, myogenin, and MYOD1. The nuclear expression of myogenin is strong and diffuse, unlike in ERMS and other rhabdomyosarcoma subtypes in which the staining pattern is heterogeneous or focal {10666368; 15536621; 30098515}. MYOD1 expression in ARMS may be limited. Expression of keratin, MUC4, ALK, OLIG2, or neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD56, and INSM1) may also be observed, representing potential pitfalls {18487991; 30975032; 31299267; 31493794; 33368602}.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows high cellularity with uniform round cells seen singly and in loosely cohesive tissue fragments with scant blue (Giemsa) to finely vacuolated cytoplasm, along with rhabdomyoblastic cells with abundant cytoplasm and large round to oval nuclei. Wreath-like multinucleated giant cells are a common feature {17636492; 29627494; 24599626; 24513670; 30098515}. Stripped nuclei and mitoses are present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

PAX3::FOXO1 and PAX7::FOXO1 account for about 70–90% and 10–30% of the fusions, respectively {28521080; 22447499}. Presence of the PAX3::FOXO1 or PAX7::FOXO1 fusion oncoprotein can be assessed immunohistochemically using antibodies targeting the junctional region of the fusion protein or the FOXO1 C-terminus {33299109; 36860202}. Rare cases show alternative novel gene fusions (see Pathogenesis, above), which can be detected by various molecular strategies. Absence of the PAX3::FOXO1 and PAX7::FOXO1 fusion or a novel gene fusion suggests a diagnosis of ERMS with a primitive phenotype (formerly known as fusion-negative ARMS or mixed ERMS-ARMS).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: uniform primitive round cell morphology with rare rhabdomyoblasts; strong, homogeneous nuclear immunoexpression for myogenin; evidence of PAX3::FOXO1, PAX7::FOXO1, or alternative fusion gene.

Staging

Rhabdomyosarcoma may be staged on the basis of site of origin using the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system for soft tissue sarcomas {36631406}. In children, the TNM system is used, but a modified TNM staging system is employed by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and by the College of American Pathologists {40007148} [[International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting [Internet]. ICCR; 2020. Paediatric rhabdomyosarcoma; cited 2025 Jun 10. Available from: https://www.iccr-cancer.org/datasets/published-datasets/paediatrics/paediatric-rhabdomyosarcoma.]]. The Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group (IRSG) classification system is also used for surgicopathological evaluation, which includes assessment of margins and residual disease {15059650}.

Prognosis and prediction

Risk stratification predictive of outcome has been addressed with surgicopathological staging and fusion-based classification (the IRSG grouping system) {24326270; 30617281; 30373318}. The prognosis for patients with fusion-positive ARMS is worse than that for patients with fusion-negative rhabdomyosarcoma and ERMS, and the prognosis for patients with PAX3::FOXO1 ARMS is inferior to that for patients with PAX7::FOXO1 ARMS {23526739; 20351326; 22454413; 22447499; 28035744}. Amplifications of MYCN, CDK4, and MIR17HG, as well as rare pathogenic TP53 alterations, have also been correlated with poor outcomes {11807989; 19422036; 21220470; 34166060}.
