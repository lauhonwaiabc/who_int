1.2.1 Lipoma

Definition

Lipoma is a benign tumour composed of mature adipocytes.

ICD-O coding

8850/0 Lipoma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E80.0Y Lipoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lipomas most commonly occur in the upper back, proximal extremity, and abdominal region, typically presenting as a superficial (subcutaneous) soft tissue mass. A smaller subset of lipomas are deep-seated, arising within the skeletal muscle (intramuscular lipoma), on the surface of bone (parosteal lipoma), or within the tendon sheath, or they may involve the gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, or bronchial tree {3366443; 4815578; 25568733; 8419426; 8160042; 24121172; 29369244; 2122666; 16359147; 29748845; 13376651}. Intramuscular lipomas most frequently involve the thigh (40–50%), followed by the shoulder, chest wall, and upper limb {4815578; 18040635}. Rare examples of retroperitoneal lipomas with cytogenetic and molecular confirmation have also been documented {18551755; 19654500}. Synovial lipomatosis (lipoma arborescens) represents a lipomatous proliferation of the subsynovial connective tissue, often associated with chronic joint disease. Studies have demonstrated that this adipocytic proliferation lacks HMGA2 overexpression, supporting its classification as a non-neoplastic, reactive process rather than as a true neoplasm {25585965; 22219560}. Similarly, lipoma of the spermatic cord is not a true lipoma and represents herniation of preperitoneal or retroperitoneal fat into the inguinal canal {12112356; 32793626}.

Clinical features

Lipomas typically present as painless masses; however, larger tumours may compress adjacent peripheral nerves, resulting in pain or tenderness. Gastrointestinal lipomas may cause abdominal discomfort, melaena, or bowel obstruction {29369244}. Approximately 5% of patients, most often male patients, have multiple tumours in the back / posterior shoulder region {6670522}. Additionally, individuals with PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome, an autosomal dominant syndrome that includes Cowden syndrome and Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome, may also have multiple lipomas {10353779; 20301661}.

Epidemiology

Lipoma is the most common mesenchymal neoplasm in adults, has a slight male predilection, and tends to be associated with obesity {6670522}. In a 10-year case series, these tumours mostly occurred in the fifth to seventh decades of life and were rare in the paediatric population {38132190}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of lipomas is related to reactivated expression of the HMGA2 protein, which plays a role in the development of the mesodermal lineage during embryogenesis {10747931; 15593017; 28139834}.

Aberrant karyotypes, the vast majority of which are pseudodiploid or near-diploid, have been reported in close to 500 lipomas, most of which were deep-seated. Four major cytogenetic subgroups have emerged: (1) structural rearrangements of chromosome bands 12q13-q15 (two thirds of abnormal cases), (2) loss of material from chromosome arm 13q (15%), (3) structural rearrangements of band 6p21 (5%), and (4) supernumerary ring chromosomes (5%); about 5% of the cases display various combinations of these aberrations {1988102; 17370328}. Structural rearrangements involving 12q13-q15 are usually translocations. Chromosomal rearrangements of 12q target the HMGA2 gene, encoding an architectural transcription factor. The most common chimeric transcript is HMGA2::LPP, but seven other fusion partners to HMGA2 have been reported {23508853; 26202160}. The deletions affecting chromosome 13, with a minimal deleted region in 13q14, overlap with those in spindle cell lipoma, but the molecular outcome remains unclear {21563233}. Rearrangements of 6p21 affect HMGA1, a gene closely related to HMGA2 {17370328; 23508853}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lipomas, including intramuscular lipomas, are well-circumscribed tumours with a uniform, glistening, yellow to pale-tan cut surface. Superficial lipomas are typically < 50 mm, whereas those that are deep-seated may reach sizes > 200 mm. Osteolipomas and chondrolipomas harbour areas of grossly identifiable bone and cartilage, respectively. Chalky white foci of fat necrosis and dystrophic calcification, especially in large deep-seated tumours, may be present.

Histopathology

Histological examination reveals a well-differentiated lipomatous proliferation composed of mature adipocytes. Variably sized paucicellular fibrous septa may be present, but cytological atypia (atypical hyperchromatic stromal cells) is absent. Areas of fat necrosis are not uncommon, especially in larger or deep-seated lipomas. Intramuscular lipomas may show infiltration into surrounding skeletal muscle, and this finding has no clinical significance. However, atrophic skeletal muscle cells, a potential mimic of the atypical hyperchromatic stromal cells characteristic of atypical lipomatous tumour / well-differentiated liposarcoma, are often seen in these areas. Lipomas containing nodules of metaplastic bone or cartilage are termed osteolipoma and chondrolipoma, respectively {21822651; 40654063}.

Cytology

Aspiration specimens show tissue fragments of adipocytes containing a single large, clear cytoplasmic vacuole with a compressed rim of cytoplasm and a small nucleus with an inconspicuous nucleolus.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not usually required, except in cases of retroperitoneal tumours and deep-seated fatty tumours > 100 mm, in which the exclusion of MDM2 amplification is desirable {26146760; 20679883; 38135062; 30709444; 38134838}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: circumscribed yellowish-tan mass; uniform proliferation of mature adipose tissue without atypical hyperchromatic stromal cells.

Desirable (in selected cases): absence of giant marker/ring chromosomes / MDM2 amplification.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The recurrence rate of lipomas is < 5%. Deep-seated lipomas are more difficult to completely excise, leading to a higher rate of local recurrence.
