2.1.15 Periosteal chondrosarcoma

Definition

Periosteal chondrosarcoma is a malignant cartilaginous neoplasm that occurs on the surface of bone underneath the periosteum and invades the underlying cortex or is > 5 cm.

ICD-O coding

9221/3 Periosteal chondrosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B50.Z & XH1S32 Chondrosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Periosteal chondrosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: juxtacortical chondrosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Periosteal chondrosarcoma predominantly affects the surface metaphysis of long tubular bones, most commonly the distal femur, followed by the proximal humerus and proximal tibia {24891585; 35690562}. Less than 20% of cases are found at other locations.

Clinical features

Periosteal chondrosarcoma usually presents as a palpable tumour accompanied frequently by pain {24891585}. The tumour is usually > 5 cm in maximum dimension, and size is the most reliable feature to differentiate periosteal chondrosarcomas from periosteal chondromas that are usually < 3 cm.

Imaging

On radiographs, a large lobulated mass (> 5 cm) located on the cortex is seen {19098166; 24891585}. Cortical bone is involved. Thickening, thinning, and saucerization of the cortex are signs seen in isolation or in combination {36318319}. Intralesional calcification may be seen, but is not prominent and is better seen on CT. The outward peripheral margin is seen as a partially calcified shell. Transformation of cortical (low signal) to trabecular bone (high signal) is often seen on T1-weighted MRI {36318319}. Signal intensity is a combination of high, intermediate, and low signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences, reflecting areas of cartilage and calcification. A septal nodular enhancement pattern (correlating with cartilage), often in combination with heterogeneous enhancement, can be seen in gadolinium chelate–enhanced MRI {36318319}. Invasion of bone marrow, seen on MRI, is reported to occur in 8–85% of cases {35407670}. The main differential diagnosis is periosteal chondroma. Invasion of bone marrow and a size larger than 5 cm are signs in favour of periosteal chondrosarcoma. However, periosteal chondrosarcoma of 3 cm or less may occur, and cortical or bone marrow involvement may not be visible radiologically {24891585; 35690562; 35407670}.

Epidemiology

Periosteal chondrosarcoma is very rare, accounting for 0.5–2% of all chondrosarcomas {16826117; 35690562}. Cases report occurrence across a wide age range (9–79 years), peaking during the third decade of life, with a male predominance {16826117; 24891585; 25648524}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

A subset of periosteal chondrosarcomas are associated with IDH1/IDH2 variants, similar to central chondrosarcomas {21598255; 22074484}. The genetic pathway is distinct from secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma, which is associated with EXT1/EXT2 variants.

Macroscopic appearance

Periosteal chondrosarcoma appears as a lobular cartilaginous mass, usually larger than 5 cm, attached to the cortex of the bone. Cortical invasion and adjacent sclerosis are frequently seen. There may be soft tissue extension beyond the periosteum.

Histopathology

Hyaline cartilaginous tumour is composed of atypical chondrocytes. Binucleate figures are usually present. Soft tissue infiltration and infiltration of cortical bone, including infiltration of the Haversian canals, are features of malignancy that are supportive of this diagnosis, particularly in cases that are smaller than 5 cm in maximum dimension {25648524}.

Calcification and endochondral ossification can be present {19098166; 4051099; 25648524}. The outer surface may be covered by a shell of reactive new bone formation.

Grading has been shown to be unrelated to prognosis {25648524}, although most lesions are histologically consistent with grade 1 or grade 2 chondrosarcoma {19098166; 4051099; 25648524}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses include periosteal chondroma, secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma, and periosteal osteosarcoma. Periosteal osteosarcoma, predominantly chondroblastic, has more severe pleomorphism and areas of osteoid deposition.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

IDH1/IDH2 variants are found in a subset of periosteal chondrosarcomas {21598255; 25648524; 22057234}, although variant analysis is rarely used for diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cartilaginous tumour arising on the bone surface, frequently > 5 cm in size, showing cortical / soft tissue invasion.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]] does not recommend that the TNM staging system for bone tumours be applied to periosteal chondrosarcoma. However, the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020.]] can be applied. See also the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

Periosteal chondrosarcomas are treated with complete surgical excision. Marginal excision or curettage is associated with high risk of recurrence and pulmonary metastases {16826117; 39570338}. The overall risk of metastatic disease is low (5–12.2%) {24891585}. Histological grading, in the largest study, does not correlate with outcome {25648524}.
