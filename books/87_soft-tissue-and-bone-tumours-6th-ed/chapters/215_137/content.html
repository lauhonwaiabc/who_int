2.1.5 Enchondroma

Definition

Enchondroma is a benign hyaline cartilaginous neoplasm that arises within the medullary cavity of bone.

ICD-O coding

9220/0 Enchondroma

ICD-11 coding

2E83.Y & XH9SY5 Other specified benign osteogenic tumours & Enchondroma

Related terminology

Not recommended: chondroma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Enchondromas most commonly affect the short tubular bones of the hands [[Picci P, Manfrini M, Fabbri N, et al., editors. Atlas of musculoskeletal tumors and tumorlike lesions. The Rizzoli Case Archive. Cham (Switzerland): Springer International Publishing; 2014.]]. The long tubular bones are less frequently affected, and location in flat bones is very uncommon (< 1%).

Clinical features

Most enchondromas are solitary; occasionally they are multifocal, affecting one or multiple bones, a syndrome referred to as enchondromatosis (see section Enchondromatosis (IDH1, IDH2)). The size of the enchondroma is usually < 4 cm {33169221}. Typically an enchondroma does not cause pain. Enchondromas in the short tubular bones of the hands and feet may cause palpable swellings, pain, and pathological fracture, whereas the long-bone lesions are more often asymptomatic.

Imaging

Enchondromas typically arise centrally or eccentrically in the metaphysis and may extend into the diaphysis and even epiphysis {36140587; 23771600}. They are osteolytic, lobulated, and well defined, especially on MRI. The ring-and-arcs matrix mineralization of cartilage, seen on radiographs and CT, increases with age, and may coalesce into solid areas of calcification. However, calcifications may be absent, especially in young patients and in tumours involving small bones of the hands and feet. Enchondromas clearly visualized on MRI may be invisible on radiographs; therefore the incidence of enchondromas detected increased parallel to the increased use of MRI for unrelated indications {30217294}. Superficial cortical scalloping (especially in phalanges and subcortical locations) is frequently present {36140587; 19277645}. On MRI, fat (high signal on T1-weighted sequences), mucoid (high signal on fluid-sensitive sequences), and calcifications (signal void on all sequences) may be seen {23771600; 36140587}. Periosteal reaction in the absence of a fracture, perilesional oedema, and remodelling are not MRI features of enchondroma {36140587}. Enchondromas are not well vascularized; therefore the septonodular enhancement characteristic of cartilaginous tumours occurs relatively late (> 10 seconds) after contrast agent injection {8430192; 1939751; 23771600; 22179165; 10671608}. Size increase over the years is more common when physes are not yet closed, but enchondromas may increase or decrease in size over the years also in adulthood {29197957}. Radiological differentiation between enchondroma, atypical cartilaginous tumour, and high-grade chondrosarcoma is described in section Central atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1.

Epidemiology

Enchondromas are relatively common, accounting for 10–25% of all surgically removed benign bone tumours. The exact incidence is unknown because many are discovered incidentally on radiographic images taken for other reasons. The prevalence of enchondroma as an incidental finding on knee MRI is approximately 2–3%; 84% measure < 2 cm {25994192}. Enchondromas that come to clinical attention are usually found in the third to fourth decades of life (range: 1–86 years, median: 36 years) [[Picci P, Manfrini M, Fabbri N, et al., editors. Atlas of musculoskeletal tumors and tumorlike lesions. The Rizzoli Case Archive. Cham (Switzerland): Springer International Publishing; 2014.]]. On average, patients with an enchondroma are younger than those with a chondrosarcoma. The sexes are equally affected [[Czerniak, B. Dorfman and Czerniak’s bone tumors. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier Saunders; 2016.]].

Etiology

Enchondromas may be caused by heterozygous somatic mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase genes IDH1 and IDH2. Mutations are found in 52% of sporadic enchondromas and in almost 90% of enchondromas in patients with enchondromatosis (see section Enchondromatosis), indicating that these represent an early driver event for enchondroma formation {21598255; 22057234; 22057236}.

Pathogenesis

Isocitrate dehydrogenases are enzymes involved in the conversion of isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, producing energy for the cell. The gain-of-function variants give the mutant enzyme the activity to convert α-ketoglutarate into the oncometabolite D-2-HG (2-hydroxyglutarate). Indeed, increased levels of D-2-HG can be detected in cartilage tumours with an IDH1 or IDH2 variant {22057236}. This causes epigenetic changes (i.e. altered histone methylation and hypermethylation) {22057234; 23863747}; as a consequence D-2-HG has been shown to promote chondrogenic differentiation and inhibit osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, the presumed precursor cells of enchondroma {26046462; 26161668}.

Macroscopic appearance

If treated, the specimen is usually received in fragments (curettage). These tissue fragments contain bluish-white glistening cartilage tissue admixed with medullary bone. Gritty, yellow foci are seen as a result of calcification, and red areas represent ossification or surrounding bone marrow. Resection specimens are uncommon and demonstrate a well-marginated, often multinodular, whitish-blue glistening tumour in the medullary cavity of the bone, with intact cortex. In the short tubular bones, the lesions are less multinodular and have a more confluent growth pattern.

Histopathology

Histologically, enchondromas are hypocellular, with an abundance of hyaline cartilage matrix. The tumour cells are embedded within sharp-edged lacunar spaces and evenly dispersed. When the cytoplasm is visible, it is usually eosinophilic. The nuclei are typically small and round, with condensed chromatin (lymphocyte-like). Cytological atypia and mitoses are absent. The architecture can be multinodular or confluent. In cases with multinodular growth, the lobules can be separated by delicate fibrous septa with small-calibre vessels. The separate nodules can be surrounded by lamellar bone, which is referred to as encasement and is a sign of slow growth {4064409}. Normal bone marrow can be present between the nodules. Enchondromas do not invade and destroy the cortex, entrap pre-existing lamellar bone, or extend into soft tissue. Degenerative features, such as ischaemic necrosis or calcification, can be prominent.

In the small phalangeal bones, as well as in patients with enchondromatosis, enchondromas can be much more cellular; in such cases, the tumour cells occasionally have more-open chromatin and small nucleoli. Binucleated cells can be seen. The matrix composition can be more myxoid, and then cells become more elongated or stellate in shape. Cortical destruction, soft tissue extension {10547545; 28797501}, and entrapment of host lamellar bone (see section Central atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1) are not allowed even under these circumstances; often a diagnosis cannot be established without proper radiological correlation.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry is usually not helpful in the diagnosis. The tumour cells are positive for S100. Approximately 24% are positive for the p.(Arg132His) (R132H) mutation–specific IDH1 antibody, limiting its usefulness because this represents only a small percentage of the IDH variants that can be found in cartilage tumours {21598255; 22057234; 22057236}.

Differential diagnosis

The distinction between enchondroma and central atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1 (ACT/CS1) can be difficult {9308471; 4064409} and is subject to interobserver variability, even among experts {18852676; 17908885}. The application of a combination of five parameters (high cellularity, presence of host bone entrapment, open chromatin, mucoid matrix quality of ≥ 20%, and patient age of > 45 years) is helpful in differentiating between enchondromas and central ACT/CS1s {18852676}, with mucomyxoid matrix degeneration and entrapment being the most discriminating. In the phalanges, enchondromas are more cellular and can have worrisome histological features that do not predict the clinical behaviour. The main discriminating factors in distinguishing enchondroma from ACT or chondrosarcoma in the phalanges are the presence of mitoses, cortical breakthrough, soft tissue involvement {10547545}, and host lamellar bone entrapment.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology is rarely used for diagnosis. Hotspot mutations are exclusively found at the IDH1 p.Arg132 and the IDH2 p.Arg172 positions. These mutations are specific to enchondromas, periosteal chondromas, and conventional and periosteal chondrosarcomas; they are absent in other mesenchymal tumours of bone. Mutation analysis cannot be used to distinguish enchondroma from ACT/CS1.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; abundance of cartilaginous matrix; absence of cytological atypia, mitoses, cortical invasion, and soft tissue extension; tumours arising in small bones and/or in patients with enchondromatosis may show more cellularity and atypia.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Asymptomatic enchondromas can be followed clinically and do not need to be treated. In case of symptoms, curettage is usually curative. Longstanding enchondromas identified radiologically have been shown to progress. However, malignant transformation of enchondromas is thought to be rare (< 1%) in a non-syndromic setting. Pain without the presence of a fracture and radiological changes should raise the possibility of progression.
