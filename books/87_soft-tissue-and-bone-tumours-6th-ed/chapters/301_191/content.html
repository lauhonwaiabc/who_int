3.0.1 Plasmacytoma

Definition

Plasmacytoma is a solitary neoplasm of clonal plasma cells without evidence of plasma cell (multiple) myeloma or end-organ damage due to plasma cell neoplasia.

ICD-O coding

9731/3 Solitary plasmacytoma of bone

9734/3 Extramedullary plasmacytoma

ICD-11 coding

2A83.2 & XH4BL1 Solitary plasmacytoma & Plasmacytoma NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Solitary plasmacytoma of bone (SPB); extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP)

Localization

The axial skeleton is affected more often than the appendicular skeleton, with the thoracic vertebrae being the most common site for solitary plasmacytoma of bone (SPB) {32934063; 33145987}. Other bones commonly affected include the pelvis, ribs, skull, and long bones {33145987; 33201300}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with localized pain, sometimes accompanied by fracture. Involvement of a vertebral body with extension to surrounding tissue may cause spinal cord compression, a clinical emergency {22752147}. SPB occasionally presents as a soft tissue lesion because of extraosseous extension of tumour. A small serum or urine M component is commonly detectable {22752147; 28393375; 32934063}.

Imaging

SPB manifests as a well-circumscribed lytic bone lesion without identifiable matrix {22549792; 30218303}.

Epidemiology

Plasmacytomas account for 2–5% of all plasma cell neoplasms {33145987; 34190199; 38001246}. The incidence rates of SPB and extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) in the USA are 0.45 cases per 100 000 person-years and 0.09 cases per 100 000 person-years, respectively {33270992}. Men are more commonly affected than women (M:F ratio: ~1.5–2:1 for SPB and ~2–3:1 for EMP) {28056880; 28393375; 31707123; 33270992}. Black people are at higher risk than White people for SPB, whereas Hispanic individuals have an intermediate risk {22654647; 33270992}. Risk for EMP shows less difference among racial groups {33270992}.

SPB accounts for < 5% of all plasma cell neoplasms {28393375; 34190199; 38001246}. In a recent population-based study from the USA spanning the years 2000–2020, the age-standardized incidence rate per 100 000 population was 0.38 for men and 0.20 for women, and the majority of cases were reported in non-Hispanic White people (68.52%) and individuals aged ≥ 55 years (75.22%) {38001246}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

SPB and plasma cell myeloma / multiple myeloma (PCM/MM) share molecular and genetic features and are distinct from EMP. The most common aberrations include t(11;14) (IGH::CCND1), t(4;14) (IGH::NSD2), and del(1p)/gain or amp(1q), with CDKN2C deletion/CKS1B amplification. MYC rearrangement and TP53 deletion can also be detected in SPB. SPB may also harbour variants in genes such as KRAS, NRAS, TP53, and BRAF. IG heavy and light chain genes are clonally rearranged, with somatic hypermutation in the variable region of the IG heavy chain gene, but without ongoing mutations.

Macroscopic appearance

Bone lesions have a gelatinous consistency and are often haemorrhagic.

Histopathology

Neoplastic plasma cells can be small and mature or enlarged and immature. Some neoplastic cells may contain immunoglobulin inclusions, including Russell bodies or Dutcher bodies. Infrequently, the neoplastic cells are large and poorly differentiated, with blastic or anaplastic morphology. Rare cases may be associated with amyloid deposition {25842207} or crystal-storing histiocytosis {25474517}.

Immunophenotyping can be performed by flow cytometry and/or immunohistochemistry. The immunophenotype of the neoplastic cells is usually as follows: CD138+, CD38+, IRF4 (MUM1)+, CD45− or dim, CD19−, CD20− (positive in a small subset of cases), PAX5− or dim, with monotypic cytoplasmic kappa or lambda expression. CD56 is rare or absent in EMP but is usually positive in SPB. Cyclin D1 is expressed in a subset of cases. Other markers that can be positive include EMA, CD30, and CD43. Plasmablastic cases can express cytoplasmic CD3, a potential diagnostic pitfall. Rare cases of EBV+ EMP in immunocompetent patients (EPIC) have been reported {25556356}. EPIC involves the nasal cavity and, less often, other head and neck sites or the gastrointestinal tract {22969303}. The histological and immunophenotypic features are similar to those of other EMPs, except that EPIC often contains many CD8+ T cells {25556356}. Rare EBV+ osseous plasmacytomas have been described {7894217; 25556356}.

SPB may resemble large cell lymphoma, plasmablastic lymphoma, round cell sarcoma, germ cell tumours, or metastatic carcinoma or melanoma {25842207; 22231043}. Occasional SPBs are composed of small lymphocyte-like plasma cells and may mimic a low-grade lymphoma. These SPBs often harbour CCND1::IGH. Solitary plasmacytoma of soft tissue, if not due to extraosseous extension of SPB, should be distinguished from low-grade B-cell lymphoma with marked plasmacytic differentiation. The latter often has a component of clonal B cells retaining variable CD19 and CD45 expression and plasma cells lacking CD56 or KIT (CD117) expression, and usually lacks the genetic changes common in SPB.

Cytology

The cytological features range from small mature forms to large immature or anaplastic forms, with scant to abundant basophilic cytoplasm. Immunoglobulin inclusions may be identified, including Russell bodies or Dutcher bodies. Cases of EMP tend to be more differentiated.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clonal IG gene rearrangement may assist in cases with ambiguous clonality by light chain immunohistochemistry, in situ hybridization, or flow cytometry.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a biopsy-proven clonal plasma cell neoplasm of bone or an extramedullary site; no clonal B cells; no other (plasma cell) lesions on physical examination or radiographic studies; no end-organ damage (hypercalcaemia, renal insufficiency, anaemia, and bone lesions [CRAB]) due to plasma cell neoplasm; < 10% clonal plasma cells on non-targeted bone marrow sampling.

Staging

Patients should undergo thorough evaluation to exclude PCM/MM, including complete blood count; serum creatinine, calcium, and free light chains; serum and urine protein electrophoresis and immunofixation; bone marrow aspiration and biopsy to investigate the presence of clonal plasma cells (including flow cytometry, FISH, and conventional cytogenetics); and highly sensitive imaging with whole-body CT, PET/CT, or MRI. Evidence of disease in additional sites and/or > 10% clonal plasma cells in a random bone marrow biopsy would be diagnostic of PCM/MM. Cases with < 10% of marrow involvement are considered plasmacytoma with minimal bone involvement.

Prognosis and prediction

SPB carries a 56–70% 5-year risk of progression to PCM/MM {21437886; 27605358}. Radiation therapy provides excellent local control {16677383; 22752147; 34190199}. Tumour size of > 50 mm is significantly associated with local recurrence and increased risk of systemic progression {32739971; 32934063}. Surgical resection, particularly for spinal lesions, may improve outcome and prevent instability or cord compression, and has been associated with improved survival {31715413; 33145987}. Patients with SPB with minimal bone marrow involvement are significantly more likely to develop PCM/MM compared with those with negative marrow studies {22549792; 24876564; 24939658; 25439696}; systemic therapy in such patients has been suggested {33549387}.

Standard management of EMP comprises involved-field radiation therapy {29976492}. Local recurrences occur in 5–20% of cases {16229966; 18543343; 21437886}. The risk of progression of EMP to PCM/MM is lower than that for SPB, with 5-year progression-free survival rates of 70–93% and 38–44%, respectively {21437886; 27605358}. Cytogenetic abnormalities in EMP appear to lack the prognostic significance they have in PCM/MM {18326524; 23000906}.
