1.11.7 Ectopic meningioma and meningothelial hamartoma

Definition

Ectopic meningioma is a benign meningothelial neoplasm affecting extracranial and extraspinal locations. Meningothelial hamartoma represents a developmental rest, composed of non-neoplastic arachnoid cells. In both, a CNS connection must be excluded.

ICD-O coding

9530/0 Meningioma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2A01.0Z Meningeal tumours, unspecified

Related terminology

Ectopic meningioma

Acceptable: extradural meningioma; extracranial/extraspinal meningioma; extraneuraxial meningioma; heterotopic meningioma; cutaneous meningioma; calvarial meningioma; intraosseous meningioma.

Meningothelial hamartoma

Acceptable: meningothelial choristoma; rudimentary meningocoele; sequestrated meningocoele; meningothelial rest; meningothelial heterotopia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

More than 90% of ectopic meningiomas present in the head and neck region. Orbital examples have no dural connection. Other locations include the skull, sinonasal tract, oropharynx, lung, mediastinum, middle ear, scalp, parotid gland, and neck. Meningothelial hamartomas most commonly present as solitary scalp masses, usually in the occiput. Rests occur in the lung (meningothelial-like nodules) and lymph nodes, among other sites {29939782; 28315423}.

Clinical features

The presenting signs and symptoms of ectopic meningiomas vary greatly, with the most common pattern being that of a painless, slow-growing mass. Meningothelial hamartoma is typically detected as an incidental scalp mass or dimple, sometimes with alopecia and/or tenderness. It can mimic vascular neoplasms {35220326}, encephalocoele {38904440}, or lipoma {40161141}. It may be associated with lesions such as naevus sebaceus {24329815}. Meningothelial hamartoma has been exceptionally reported as a late postsurgical occurrence, probably forming because of displaced cells {35873844}.

Epidemiology

Ectopic meningiomas are rare. In one review, they accounted for 1–2% of all resected meningiomas and presented at all ages, with bimodal peaks in the second and in the fifth to seventh decades of life. There is a slight female predominance {11117866}. Meningothelial hamartomas are typically found in neonates or infants, with no known sex or racial predisposition {21906138}, although they rarely present later in life {35220326}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Four histogenetic hypotheses have been postulated for ectopic meningiomas: meningothelial cells carried along nerve sheaths exiting the skull or vertebral column, ectopic arachnoid cells, meningothelial cells displaced during trauma, and pluripotent mesenchymal cells capable of undergoing meningothelial differentiation or metaplasia. Meningothelial hamartomas in the scalp are considered pathogenetically related to meningocoeles and meningoencephalocoeles but without an intracranial connection. In other locations, they are often incidental, possibly representing ectopic rests or metaplastic meningothelial-like cells (e.g. pulmonary nodules).

Molecular studies are limited, but one intranodal rest in a patient with Cowden syndrome indirectly implicated the PTEN gene {28315423}. An association with Gorlin syndrome has also been reported. The pathogenesis remains uncertain {35873844; 33723758}.

Macroscopic appearance

Ectopic meningiomas resemble their CNS counterparts but may show greater invasiveness. Colour and consistency vary with cellularity, collagen deposition, and tumour grade, but a rubbery, tan-white mass is most common. Calvarial meningiomas are typically hyperostotic {24997535}. Hamartomas are solitary nodules; plaques; or spongy tan-grey, red, or flesh-coloured lesions in cutaneous and/or subcutaneous scalp.

Histopathology

Ectopic meningiomas feature histological appearances and grades similar to those of intracranial meningiomas. Benign meningothelial tumours predominate. In the scalp, meningothelial hamartomas typically show slit-like spaces resembling lymphatics but lined by meningothelial (rather than endothelial) cells. Well-formed meningothelial clusters and psammoma bodies can also be encountered. Highly sensitive and specific immunohistochemical markers are generally lacking, with coexpression of vimentin, EMA, and PR in most {10800982}. SSTR2A {26195322} can also be expressed in these, including in pulmonary meningothelial-like nodules {29939782}. Expression of p63 may also be seen in meningiomas; therefore, this is not a reliable marker to distinguish them from epithelial neoplasms {23924346; 37114141}.

Cytology

Ectopic meningiomas show cytological features similar to those of their CNS counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: meningothelial rest or neoplasm located entirely outside of and with no connection to the CNS.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

As with other meningiomas, tumour grade and extent of resection play prognostic roles for ectopic meningioma. Intraosseous skull base cases have higher recurrence rates than those of the convexity. Distant metastases have been reported in about 6% of cases, mostly in anaplastic examples {11117866}. Hamartomas are usually cured by surgery alone, with only rare recurrences.
