1.5.1.4 Venous malformations

Definition

Venous malformations (VMs) are slow-flow vascular anomalies arising during early embryonic development, giving rise to masses of abnormal ectatic venous channels of variable calibre.

XXX

ICD-11 coding

XXX

Related terminology

Not recommended: venous haemangioma; venous angioma; cavernous haemangioma; verrucous haemangioma.

Subtype(s)

Common venous malformation (CVM); venous malformation of the blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome (BRBNS); genuine diffuse phlebectasia of Bockenheimer (GDPB); verrucous venous malformation (VVM).

Localization

VMs typically involve the skin, mucosa, and subcutaneous tissue, but may extend into deeper structures such as muscle, bone, and internal organs. The most common sites for common venous malformations (CVMs) are the head and neck, followed by the extremities and trunk. Specific subtypes demonstrate anatomical predilections: VMs associated with blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome (BRBNS) predominantly affect the palms, soles, and trunk; verrucous venous malformations (VVMs) primarily affect the lower extremities {34698142}.

Clinical features

CVMs are mostly sporadic and unifocal, accounting for > 90% of VMs {10709058; 11566267}. CVMs appear as bluish to purple, soft, and compressible nodules, ranging in size from small, localized lesions to extensive and deeply infiltrative ones; sometimes they involve an entire limb in genuine diffuse phlebectasia of Bockenheimer. Spontaneous thrombosis within the lesion can result in swelling and pain. Phleboliths can be noted on palpation. BRBNS manifests as multiple raised blue papules that increase in number over time; rarely, a congenital large mass may also be present. VVMs usually affect the lower extremities and present as single or multiple deep red macular stains progressively becoming hyperkeratotic {34698142; 26055853; 27607317}. A subcutaneous pattern of VVM has been reported with minimal cutaneous stain {33899768}.

Epidemiology

All entities affect both sexes, with an estimated incidence of 1 to 5 cases per 10 000 births {20870869; 10709058}. Lesions are frequently noted at birth or develop early in life {34698142}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most CVMs are sporadic and caused by somatic variants in TEK (60%) or PIK3CA (20%) {29217067; 19079259; 26637981}. Familial cases of multiple cutaneous and mucosal VMs of the common type are caused by germline variants in TEK, followed by a second somatic event {19888299; 26319232}. Dual (cis) TEK variants within the C-terminal domain cause VMs in BRBNS {27519652}. VVM is associated with somatic MAP3K3 variants {25728774}.

variants in TEK or PIK3CA cause upregulation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, essential for angiogenesis and vasculogenesis {27519652; 29217067}. MAP3K3 variants cause upregulation of RAS/MAPK signalling {25728774}.

Macroscopic appearance

On the cut surface, venous malformations appear as unencapsulated, red, spongiform masses intermingled with normal tissue. Calcified phleboliths are occasionally visible macroscopically. Most VVMs exhibit verrucous epidermal changes, which are absent in the subcutaneous pattern, and typically show solid, violaceous lobules within the subcutaneous tissue.

Histopathology

VMs are characterized by haphazardly arranged large, ectatic, irregular, variably sized venous channels in the dermis, subcutis, and deep soft tissues. The vessels are lined by a single layer of flat endothelial cells and display a concentric smooth muscle wall, often discontinuous or thin relative to the luminal diameter. In some areas, the vessel walls show irregular strands or nodules of smooth muscle merging with fibromyxoid stroma, probably representing the sequelae of organized thrombi. Lumina are variably empty or congested, and organizing thrombi, which may evolve into calcified phleboliths, may be seen. Papillary endothelial hyperplasia (Masson phenomenon) is commonly observed. Endothelial cells show diffuse immunoreactivity for CD31, whereas CD34 expression is variable. Negative staining for lymphatic markers such as D2-40 and PROX1 aids in distinguishing VMs from lymphatic malformations {21839353; 27607322; 34698142}. However, lymphatic marker expression has been reported in VMs associated with BRBNs {38512910}. As in other vascular malformations, WT1 is not expressed, and the proliferative index (Ki-67) is extremely low {21697701}. VMs in BRBNS typically show closely apposed, small, abnormal, and often spongiform veins and organizing thrombi. VVM has dispersed or clustered channels with the morphology of enlarged capillaries or venules involving the dermis and subcutis. Rare cases of VVM are predominantly or wholly restricted to the subcutis {33899768}. In VVM, the endothelium is focally or diffusely immunopositive for GLUT1 and focally positive for D2-40 and PROX1 {26055853; 27607322}.

Cytology

FNABs are not routinely performed.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In selected cases, demonstration of pathogenic variants in TEK, PIK3CA, and MAP3K3 may help confirm the type of malformation.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For CVM and BRBNs: dysmorphic venous channels For VVM: capillary or venular-type channels with focal or diffuse endothelial immunopositivity for GLUT1 and focal immunopositivity for D2-40 or PROX1

Desirable:

For all subtypes: compatible imaging For CVM: demonstration of a somatic TEK or PIK3CA variant For BRBNS: demonstration of a somatic TEK variant For VVM: demonstration of a somatic MAP3K3 variant

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

CVMs are lesions that typically follow an indolent clinical course. The most common complications associated with their slow progression are aesthetic disfigurement and chronic, often significant pain. Localized intravascular coagulopathy, characterized by elevated D-dimer levels and normal to low fibrinogen concentrations, is frequently seen {20870869}. Depending on their size and anatomical location, VMs can become life-threatening because of bleeding, progressive expansion, or compression of vital structures {8894808}. Sclerotherapy and/or surgical excision are the main therapeutic options in symptomatic cases. The extent of intestinal involvement and the presence of other organ involvement dictate the prognosis of BRBN. Lesions associated with BRBNS and VVMs may require surgical removal. Treatment response to sirolimus has been reported in BRBNS and other forms of VMs {31879989; 29945674; 30855337}.
