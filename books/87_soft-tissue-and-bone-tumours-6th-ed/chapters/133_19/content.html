1.3.3 Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour of digits

Definition

Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour of digits are self-limited, benign neoplasms composed of spindle cells and osteoblasts associated with bone formation.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

FB31.Y - Myositis ossificans.

FB51.Y - Fibro-osseous pseudotumour of the digit.

Related terminology

Not recommended: pseudomalignant osseous tumour of soft tissue; myositis ossificans circumscripta; myositis ossificans traumatica.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Myositis ossificans can occur throughout the body {13539055; 12894350; 19130083; 1566115; 18384209; 3866484}, with common sites including trauma-prone areas, such as the elbow, thigh, buttock, and shoulder. Although it typically arises within skeletal muscle, similar lesions occur in the subcutis and in the tendons/fascia and are known as panniculitis ossificans and fasciitis ossificans, respectively. Myositis ossificans can also arise on the surface of bone (parosteal myositis ossificans), mimicking a primary bone tumour {38741286}. Fibro-osseous pseudotumour typically arises in the fingers and less frequently the toes {19678826; 18164411}. Myositis ossificans–like lesions have also been reported in the mesentery {16501833}.

Clinical features

The lesions typically grow rapidly, and the clinical and radiological features evolve over time. In the early phase (1–2 weeks), there is swelling and pain. Imaging reveals soft tissue fullness and oedema on radiographs and CT, while MRI shows heterogeneous signal intensity with high signal on T2-weighted images. Flocculent mineralization becomes evident at the periphery of the mass 2–6 weeks after the onset of symptoms, eventually forming an eggshell-like layer of bone, with a radiolucent centre. In contrast, mineralization in fibro-osseous pseudotumour tends to be more randomly distributed. Over time, myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour become hard and well demarcated and pain diminishes {13539055; 12894350; 19130083; 1566115; 18384209; 3866484}.

Epidemiology

Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour can occur from infancy through late adulthood, but they most commonly occur in physically active young adults with a mean age of approximately 32 years {13539055; 12894350; 19130083; 1566115; 18384209; 3866484; 28275867; 26320160}. The M:F ratio varies from 1.1 to 1.9:1 for myositis ossificans {39623749; 38161430}, whereas female individuals are more frequently affected by fibro-osseous pseudotumour in published case series {29787930; 23210090}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most cases of myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour harbour USP6 fusion genes, most commonly the COL1A1::USP6 fusion {18265974; 30946936; 29787930; 29661729; 26142538}, but also ANGPTL2::USP6 {32157177}, MYH9::USP6, and UBE2G1::USP6 {36727055}. However, this is an evolving list of fusion partners that will probably expand over time. Importantly, all described fusions retain the entire protein-coding sequence of USP6, because of a characteristic promoter-swapping mechanism.

Macroscopic appearance

Myositis ossificans is typically well delineated and ovoid, has a glistening soft-tan haemorrhagic centre and a firm greyish-white gritty periphery, and is 20–120 mm (average: 50 mm) in size. In contrast, fibro-osseous pseudotumour is generally smaller, less well demarcated, and appears tan, grey, or pink on gross examination.

Histopathology

Myositis ossificans is characterized by a distinctive zonal architecture. In the centre, the lesion is densely cellular, composed of spindle cells oriented randomly or in short intersecting fascicles. The spindle cells have eosinophilic cytoplasm and plump vesicular nuclei with nucleoli. Normal mitoses are often numerous. The stroma is vascular and myxoid and contains fibrin, extravasated erythrocytes, scattered lymphocytes, and osteoclast-like giant cells. Occasionally, blood-filled cystic spaces may be observed {30946936}. At the periphery, the spindle cells merge with osteoblasts that rim and populate poorly defined trabeculae and sheets of woven bone, which are surrounded by well-formed trabecular and cortical-type bone that eventually remodels into lamellar bone. Occasionally, the matrix contains cellular hyaline cartilage, or the zonal architecture is not well developed {13539055; 12894350; 19130083; 1566115; 18384209; 3866484}. In contrast, fibro-osseous pseudotumour lacks zonal architecture, and its bone is randomly distributed. A key differential diagnosis is extraskeletal osteosarcoma, which is distinguished by the absence of zonation and the presence of malignant cytological features.

Cytology

Cytology aspirate features vary with the stage of the lesion progression; they show a dual cell population of spindle cells and large ganglion-like cells set in a myxoid stroma in tissue fragments, a variable number of osteoblasts associated with osteoid, and a haemorrhagic or myxoid background with debris and stripped nuclei {20890998}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular studies for USP6 rearrangement may be useful in the appropriate clinicopathological context.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: hypercellular fascicles of uniform spindle cells; admixed woven bone with zonation, being most mature at the periphery.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Treatment of myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous pseudotumour is usually simple excision. The prognosis is excellent, and recurrence is uncommon.
