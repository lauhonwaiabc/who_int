1.3.14 Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma

Definition

Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma (CAF) is a benign neoplasm with a propensity to arise in the soft tissues of the hands and feet in children and adolescents, characterized by alternating zones of bland spindle cells and less cellular, partly calcified zones with plump to epithelioid cells.

ICD-O coding

8816/0 Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma

ICD-11 coding

EE6Y & XH8ZE3 Other specified fibromatous disorders of skin and soft tissue & Calcifying aponeurotic fibroma

Related terminology

Acceptable: juvenile aponeurotic fibroma; aponeurotic fibroma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

CAF arises most commonly on the palmar surfaces of the hands and fingers, followed by the plantar surfaces of the feet and toes {5506609}. Wrists and ankles are less commonly involved {9865839}. Unusual locations are proximal extremities, the knee, and the trunk {9865839; 8864926}. Rare examples have been documented in the head and neck region {22033965}. Most lesions arise in the subcutis, often connected to tendons and aponeuroses {5506609; 9865839}.

Clinical features

CAF typically presents as a slow-growing, superficially located painless mass {9865839}. Radiographic studies often demonstrate stippled intralesional calcifications.

Epidemiology

CAF is a rare neoplasm. Its peak incidence is between the ages of 5 and 15 years, although well-documented examples in adults have been reported. Male individuals are more often affected than female individuals {9865839}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

A recurrent FN1::EGF gene fusion has been identified, suggesting that the likely pathogenetic mechanism is increased expression of mature EGF peptide, which stimulates tumour growth in an autocrine/paracrine fashion {26691015}.

Macroscopic appearance

Resected specimens are usually 10–30 mm, appearing as poorly defined, greyish-white to gritty soft tissue masses {9865839}.

Histopathology

CAF typically displays a characteristic morphology of alternating zones of bland, short spindle cells arranged in fascicles, as well as less cellular areas with plump to epithelioid cells and extracellular calcifications {13032926}. The cellular component has a fibromatosis-like appearance with infiltration of adjacent soft tissue components. Entrapment of nerves and vessels may be seen. The cells show no nuclear atypia or mitotic activity. Within the cellular component, there are scattered, sometimes confluent, less cellular areas with larger cells that may appear epithelioid or chondrocyte-like, within a hyalinized, sometimes chondroid matrix with finely granular to coarse calcifications. The cells are often arranged around the calcifications. Osteoclast-type giant cells are frequently seen in these areas, and osseous metaplasia has been described. Lesions typically contain both components to a varying degree, although non-calcified lesions showing only a fibromatosis-like pattern can be seen. There is observational evidence suggesting that lesions may evolve from a spindle cell–predominant morphology to less cellular lesions with more extensive calcifications {5506609; 30310176; 28828138}.

By immunohistochemistry, lesional cells react with SMA, MSA, CD99, and (in the chondroid areas) S100. Focal positivity for ERG around calcified areas has been observed {36371890}. Nuclear staining for β-catenin is not seen {17711447}.

Cytology

Insufficient data

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of the FN1::EGF gene fusion may be helpful in cases of CAF lacking calcification {30310176}, although this is not required for routine diagnosis as most cases display the salient features.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: geographical areas of calcified matrix surrounded by palisading epithelioid fibroblasts; interspersed bland spindle cells in a collagenous stroma.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Largely due to the infiltrative features of CAF, local recurrence, which may occur years after initial excision, is observed in as many as 50% of cases {5506609; 9865839}. The risk of local recurrence seems to be higher in younger children (aged < 5 years). CAF typically has a limited growth potential, and multiple recurrences are rare {8864926; 9865839}. Conservative surgical management, with preservation of function, is typically recommended.
