2.5.2 Giant cell tumour of bone

Definition

Giant cell tumour of bone is a locally aggressive, rarely metastasizing tumour type composed of neoplastic mononuclear cells, macrophages, and osteoclast-like giant cells. A small subset of cases are malignant.

ICD-O coding

9250/1 Giant cell tumour of bone

ICD-11 coding

2F7B & XH4TC2 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of bone or articular cartilage & Giant cell tumour of bone NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: benign fibrous histiocytoma; osteoclastoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Giant cell tumour of bone typically affects the ends of long bones (distal femur, proximal tibia, distal radius, and proximal humerus) in skeletally mature individuals {5479455; 28505000}. Giant cell tumours in the axial skeleton arise most commonly in the sacrum and vertebral body {8421014; 21045974}. Flat bone involvement is rare but pelvic bones are most frequently affected. Small numbers of giant cell tumours occur in the tubular bones of the hands and feet {23723282; 27499898} and, rarely, the gnathic bones {27499898; 28505000}. Malignant giant cell tumours share an anatomical distribution similar to that of conventional tumours {12733152; 22350004; 28505000; 27499898; 31285528; 40077813; 40157506}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Giant cell tumour presents with pain, swelling, and occasionally restricted joint movement. The duration of symptoms is typically weeks to months {3805057}. Tumours may be discovered incidentally and occasionally during pregnancy {3805057}. A pathological fracture occurs in 5–12% of patients {3805057; 16311110}, most commonly in the distal femur {3514036}. Vertebral and sacral lesions often present with insidious onset of pain {16311110} and frequently a neurological deficit {24614982}. Persistent pain or continued tumour growth on denosumab therapy may raise concern for malignant transformation; repeat imaging and tissue sampling should be considered in such cases {32335707; 35814640; 40077813}.

Imaging

When arising in long bones, giant cell tumours are typically lytic and eccentric and extend to the subchondral bone plate {3514036}. Trabeculation is common, ranging from fine to coarse, and represents endosteal ridging {11553835}. Tumours show a narrow zone of transition, with well-defined, either sclerotic or non-sclerotic borders and no matrix mineralization. Periosteal reaction is unusual, and its presence may indicate a fracture {9714386}. Less commonly, tumours are centrally located and show a poorly defined margin. The overlying cortex may be normal, thinned to a variable degree, or breached by tumour that is contained within an attenuated periosteum; a true soft tissue component is uncommon {16311110}. Primary malignant giant cell tumours may show aggressive appearances, but they are usually indistinguishable from conventional tumours; similarly, secondary malignant giant cell tumours are generally indistinguishable from recurrent conventional disease; therefore, the diagnosis of malignancy is made on the basis of histology {12733152; 35814640}. When arising in skeletally immature individuals, they usually involve the metaphysis.

CT without contrast is the imaging of choice for giant cell tumours of the axial skeleton (spine and sacrum) because of their poor radiographic visualization secondary to location {12151896; 11553835}. In the sacrum, tumours are typically located eccentrically within proximal segments, causing lytic destruction {24614982}, frequently involving the sacroiliac joint {24614982; 21045974}. Vertebral lesions cause lytic destruction and may cause kyphosis. 18F-FDG PET/CT shows high 18F-FDG uptake (average maximum standardized uptake value: 11), most likely reflecting high metabolic activity {30231890}.

On MRI, an intermediate to low T2 signal intensity is typical, due to haemosiderin from chronic haemorrhage, but evidence of recent haemorrhage {8571864; 24679193} and fluid-fluid levels {11553835} may also be seen, in keeping with aneurysmal bone cyst-like changes. On T1-weighted MRI, the signal intensity ranges from hypointense to hyperintense (the latter in case of haemorrhagic components). After contrast, tumours show intense enhancement, which can be inhomogeneous with cyst-like areas of non-enhancement. A classic feature is the dynamic enhancement curve demonstrating rapid early enhancement followed by a washout phase (type 4 curve) {23380432}. Dynamic contrast enhancement / MRI perfusion is a useful technique to distinguish recurrent giant cell tumour of bone from postoperative scar tissue or bone grafting material, based on their enhancement type curves, and for assessment of denosumab treatment response {32335707; 37683333}.

Denosumab-treated giant cell tumours show new bone formation, mainly along the periphery of the lesion on radiographs or CT, with well-defined marginal sclerosis and neocortex formation indicative of a positive response {29386457; 29429890; 32335707}. On MRI, signal intensity decreases on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences after denosumab treatment because of dense woven bone formation in the tumour. Furthermore, cystic components decrease in size during treatment, best appreciated on fluid-sensitive images {29386457}. Reduction in 18F-FDG PET avidity can predict a favourable tumour response to denosumab treatment. Given the rarity of non-response, in case of no decrease or even an increase in maximum standardized uptake value during denosumab treatment, malignant progression needs to be excluded histologically {30231890}.

Epidemiology

Giant cell tumours represent 4–5% of all primary bone tumours, with an estimated incidence rate of 1.2–1.7 cases per 1 million person-years {27060625; 30148063; 29987945}. The peak incidence is between the ages of 20 and 45 years, with approximately 10% of cases occurring in the second decade of life {27526293}. Giant cell tumours can rarely arise in skeletally immature individuals {5479455; 28505000; 27526293}. A slight female predominance is reported, with the notable exception of a slight male predominance in a large series of patients from China {26998425; 22398741}.

Malignant giant cell tumours account for < 10% of all giant cell tumours, and the affected patients are generally the same age as those with conventional tumours {19902306; 27060625; 30935298}. Most malignant giant cell tumours of bone represent transformation of a previously diagnosed conventional giant cell tumour of bone, at a median interval of several years {12733152; 40077813}. Only about 2% of giant cell tumours of bone are malignant at presentation {30935298}.

Etiology

The etiology of sporadic giant cell tumours is not known, whereas the phaeochromocytoma-paraganglioma and giant cell tumour syndrome are caused by an early postzygotic H3.3 mutation {26700204; 28476232}. Synchronous or metachronous multicentric giant cell tumours are rare and most commonly involve the extremities in young patients {37126833}. Some patients with multicentric disease have the phaeochromocytoma-paraganglioma and giant cell tumour syndrome {26700204}. Solitary and multicentric giant cell tumours have been reported in the setting of Paget disease of bone {25196811} and Gorlin–Goltz syndrome {23530933; 25804026}, but it remains uncertain whether these tumours are biologically related to conventional giant cell tumour of bone or are on a spectrum with other osteoclast-rich lesions that also occur in these patients {26849110; 37310481}. In a few tested cases, H3-3A (H3F3A) variants were not identified in giant cell tumours arising in Paget disease of bone {28968976}, which instead harbour PFN1 alterations {33599011}. Transformation to malignancy may occur after radiation therapy and denosumab therapy, although the risk of malignant transformation is similar in denosumab-treated and denosumab-naive tumours {31704134; 40077813}.

Pathogenesis

Conventional giant cell tumour

At least 95% of giant cell tumours harbour pathogenic H3-3A (H3F3A) sequence variants, approximately 90% of which are G35W (G34W), with the next most common being G35L (G34L); rarer variants (G35M (G34M), G35R (G34R), and G35V (G34V)) have also been reported {24162739; 28505000; 28545165; 29241742; 38391356}. Rarely, tumours instead harbour H3-3B (H3F3B) variants {26457357; 36800629; 40077813}. Although most tumours are diploid, tetraploidy and aneuploidy may occur and correlate with recurrence risk and malignant transformation {19242928; 24162739; 28545165; 40077813}. H3.3 point substitutions sterically hinder interactions with H3 K36–specific methyltransferase SETD2, leading to loss of di- and tri-methylation of the H3 K36 residue {30181289; 33067396}. Giant cell tumour of bone exhibits a distinct methylation profile including hypermethylation of CDKN1A and CDKN2A {35522566}.

Malignancy in giant cell tumour

The mechanism by which malignant transformation occurs is unknown. In contrast to conventional tumours, malignant giant cell tumours of bone harbour alterations in TP53 in approximately 30% of reported cases {23748877; 34785767; 40077813; 40157506}, loss of H3K27me3 in 25% of reported cases {34785767; 40077813}, and TERT promoter alterations in 10% of cases {32866294; 40157506}. BRAF, HRAS, KRAS, or NRAS variants are present in 20–30% of malignant tumours {11903582; 19396867; 33633024; 33707617; 38476985; 40077813}, although rarely these alterations may be present in conventional tumours {40077813}. Malignant giant cell tumours exhibit complex copy-number alterations in about 90% of reported cases, sometimes relative to a tetraploid baseline {32866294; 40077813; 40157506}, while conventional and metastasizing conventional giant cell tumours of bone show flat copy-number profiles {32866294; 40077813}. Malignant giant cell tumour of bone harbours a methylation profile distinct from that of conventional tumours {32866294; 40157506}, and malignant tumours with osteosarcomatous differentiation exhibit profiles distinct from conventional osteosarcoma {40157506}. About 15% of malignant tumours acquire deletion of the H3-3A mutant allele with malignant transformation {33707617; 34785767; 40077813}.

Macroscopic appearance

Conventional giant cell tumour is well defined and eccentrically located within the end of a long bone, often resulting in asymmetric expansion. The surrounding cortex is frequently thinned, and in some areas it may be effaced. The subchondral bone plate can be focally eroded, but the tumour seldom extends into the joint cavity. Grossly viable tumour is characterized by a soft, friable, tan-red, and haemorrhagic appearance. Cream-yellow and firm white areas corresponding to xanthomatous change and fibrous histiocytoma–like tissue are commonly seen. Areas of haemorrhage and blood-filled cystic spaces corresponding to aneurysmal changes may be seen. In contrast, malignant giant cell tumour is typically firmer and more fleshy.

Histopathology

Giant cell tumour of bone is composed of neoplastic mononuclear cells with numerous admixed non-neoplastic osteoclast-type giant cells, some of which contain many nuclei. The mononuclear cells in giant cell tumour usually appear histiocytoid, with amphophilic cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei, and distinct nucleoli. Some examples, formerly termed “benign fibrous histiocytoma” of bone, are dominated by spindle cell cytomorphology, with mixed fascicles and whorls of neoplastic cells and admixed histiocytes. The presence of atypical mitotic figures should raise suspicion for malignancy.

Some examples can show aneurysmal bone cyst-like changes and necrosis, findings of no prognostic significance in isolation. Bone formation is an uncommon feature, although reactive/metaplastic bone formation can occasionally be striking and can be prominent when associated with a fracture. Exceptionally, an otherwise typical giant cell tumour contains benign-appearing hyaline cartilaginous nodules, raising the differential diagnostic consideration of dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma {28059095; 27526293}.

Occasionally, otherwise conventional giant cell tumours may contain scattered mononuclear cells with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, irregular nuclear contours, smudged nuclear chromatin, and nuclear pseudoinclusions. The significance of such atypia is uncertain and probably represents degenerative change {27020452; 15365783}, and tested examples so far have lacked genetic features of malignancy {40077813}. Nevertheless, atypical cases can be challenging to classify, particularly on a biopsy specimen and in the setting of denosumab therapy.

The cortical bone surrounding the giant cell tumour is often severely eroded by osteoclasts and is commonly replaced by a reactive eggshell-like rim of new bone at the tumour periphery. In areas, the cortical bone can be completely effaced by tumour, which is generally contained by periosteum; it is uncommon to see frank soft tissue invasion. Focal entrapment of host bone can be seen at the leading edge of the tumour. Vascular invasion may be present, especially after previous curettage.

Conventional giant cell tumour may metastasize to the lung, and in these cases the histological appearance is similar to that at the primary site {26730686; 24162739; 3056645}. Peripheral reactive bone formation can also be seen in these tumour nodules.

Malignant giant cell tumour

Malignant transformation occurs in < 5% of conventional giant cell tumours {3745247}, generally in persistent or locally recurrent disease. Malignant tumours can lack an evident conventional component, in which case the history of a conventional tumour at the same site supports the diagnosis. De novo malignancy in a giant cell tumour occurs in < 2% of tumours {30935298}, and it is typically represented by a discrete region of frank sarcoma with an associated conventional component. Secondary malignant transformation is more common than the primary malignancy and occurs after treatment of a conventional giant cell tumour, including with radiotherapy.

The malignant component of a giant cell tumour does not have specific histological features. Some examples show the morphology of a high-grade undifferentiated sarcoma, and most cases show osteosarcomatous and/or chondroblastic differentiation, the latter of which can resemble chondroblastoma {28505000; 3745247; 2554283; 40077813; 40157506}.

Some undifferentiated sarcomas of bone without an apparent conventional giant cell tumour component harbour H3-3A variants characteristic of giant cell tumour. These tumours generally occur in subarticular sites typical of giant cell tumours, and they cluster with malignant giant cell tumours by methylation analysis {28505000; 31285528; 32866294; 34785767; 40077813; 40157506}. Clinically, they also show aggressive behaviour similar to that of malignant giant cell tumour of bone. Given this clinical, genetic, and epigenetic overlap, it has been suggested that de novo bone sarcomas with characteristic H3-3A variants are indeed primary malignant giant cell tumours of bone {28505000}.

Changes in denosumab-treated giant cell tumour

Giant cell tumours treated with denosumab for several months show substantial new bone formation, associated with bland neoplastic mutant spindle cells and a striking depletion of osteoclast-like giant cells. However, variable amounts of conventional giant cell tumour may persist {22711702; 26338802; 29241742; 29206281}. These features may mimic de novo osteosarcoma or secondary malignant giant cell tumour. However, denosumab-treated giant cell tumours generally lack substantial nuclear atypia, high mitotic activity, atypical mitotic figures, extensive necrosis, and infiltration of pre-existing bone {26414220; 28235628}. Nevertheless, there are occasional reports of malignancy arising subsequent to denosumab-treated giant cell tumour {25758379; 26798348; 29048579}. Given that the low incidence of malignant transformation on denosumab therapy is similar to the incidence in denosumab-naive tumours, it is uncertain whether denosumab might play a role in malignant transformation {31704134; 40077813}.

Immunohistochemistry

Histone H3 G34W immunohistochemistry is a reliable surrogate marker for molecular analysis, with 85% sensitivity for giant cell tumour of bone in general and essentially 100% specificity {28505000; 29241742; 29757500}. However, some malignant giant cell tumours of bone acquire deletion of the H3-3A mutant allele, such that the sensitivity is lower in malignant tumours {28545165; 31285528; 33707617; 34785767; 40077813}.

Differential diagnosis

Benign fibrous histiocytomas located in the epiphysis in the mature skeleton are now generally considered to represent giant cell tumour of bone and those in the metaphysis to represent non-ossifying fibroma. Tumours with extensive aneurysmal change can be difficult to distinguish from primary aneurysmal bone cyst. The differential diagnosis of malignant giant cell tumour is undifferentiated sarcoma of bone and giant cell–rich osteosarcoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

H3-3A or, rarely, H3-3B variants are detected in as many as 96% of giant cell tumours {28505000; 24162739; 28545165; 26457357; 28059095; 29241742}; 90% of these are represented by G35W (previously G34W). The H3.3 G35L (G34L) variant is much less common and is mostly found in tumours in the small bones of the hand, patella, and axial skeleton. There have also been occasional reports of H3.3 G35V (G34V), H3.3 G35R (G34R), and H3.3 G35M (G34M) {24162739; 28545165; 27499898}. Detection of the variant does not distinguish between conventional and malignant giant cell tumours, and some malignant giant cell tumours of bone acquire H3-3A gene deletions, such that the mutant allele is lost {28545165; 31285528; 33707617; 34785767; 40077813}. Immunohistochemistry for p53 and/or H3K27me3 is abnormal in about 30–50% of malignant giant cell tumours {34785767; 40077813}. Complex copy-number alterations are present in about 90% of reported malignant giant cell tumours of bone and might be helpful to distinguish them from benign tumours {32866294; 40077813; 40157506}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; conventional tumours show either (1) numerous large osteoclasts together with a mononuclear cell neoplastic component without atypia or (2) fibrous histiocytoma–like morphology in the right imaging context; malignant giant cell tumours of bone show overt features of malignancy, including osteosarcomatous and chondrosarcomatous differentiation, and either (1) an associated conventional component or (2) a prior history of a conventional tumour.

Desirable: H3.3 G35 (G34) alteration.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Between 15% and 50% of conventional giant cell tumours recur locally after curettage and usually within 2 years. Between 3% and 7% of patients develop metastatic lung disease with histological features of conventional giant cell tumour {20012988; 26730686; 28650536}. Predictors of metastatic disease include local recurrence and possibly surgical manipulation. Vascular invasion is commonly seen in cases that metastasize to the lung.

The median interval between metastasis and local recurrence is 15 months and most occur within 36 months, but delayed metastasis (as late as 10 years) has been described {28650536}. Pulmonary metastases are slow-growing and are thought to represent pulmonary implants that result from embolization of intravascular growths of giant cell tumour. Some conventional pulmonary implants can regress spontaneously, while a small proportion exhibit progressive enlargement and lead to the death of the patient as a result of reduced lung volume {26730686; 3056645}. Denosumab has been used with benefit in the treatment of conventional metastasizing disease {28324746; 28573059}.

Denosumab may also be used as the treatment for giant cell tumours that are unresectable or where surgical resection will probably result in excessive morbidity. Neoadjuvant denosumab therapy can be used to facilitate surgical resection of locally advanced tumours. However, there is no consensus about optimal treatment dose, duration, or interval between treatments.

Malignant giant cell tumours behave as high-grade sarcomas in both primary and secondary examples, and about two thirds of patients develop metastatic disease {9549577; 12733152; 19902306; 40077813}. PDL1 expression levels have been shown to correlate with shorter recurrence intervals {32377818; 34285260}, and there are isolated reports of patients responding to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, including a complete response in a patient with metastatic malignant giant cell tumour with high PDL1 expression {30638772; 32853765; 40077813}.
