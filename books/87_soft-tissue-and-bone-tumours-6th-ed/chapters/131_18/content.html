1.3.1 Nodular fasciitis

Definition

Nodular fasciitis (NF) is a benign mesenchymal neoplasm composed of plump, uniform fibroblastic/myofibroblastic cells that display a tissue culture–like pattern.

ICD-O coding

8828/0 Nodular fasciitis

ICD-11 coding

FB51.2 & XH5LM1 Pseudosarcomatous fibromatosis & Nodular fasciitis

Related terminology

Not recommended: pseudosarcomatous fasciitis.

Subtype(s)

Intravascular fasciitis; cranial fasciitis

Localization

NF typically develops on the surface of fascia and extends into the subcutis, although occasional cases are intramuscular {153036}. Dermal localization is less common {20716998; 27686647}. Any anatomical site can be involved, but the upper extremities, trunk, and head and neck are the most frequently affected. Intra-articular involvement has been described {16434899}. Intravascular fasciitis is usually subcutaneous. It occurs in small to medium-sized vessels, predominantly in veins but occasionally involving arteries {7246849}. Cranial fasciitis typically involves the outer table of the skull and contiguous soft tissue of the scalp and may extend downwards through the inner table into the meninges {7351023}.

Clinical features

NF typically grows rapidly, with a preoperative duration in most cases of no more than 2–3 months. Soreness or tenderness may be present. Most tumours measure 2 cm and are almost always < 5 cm {4501523; 6462780}. Intravascular fasciitis may enlarge more slowly but generally does not exceed 2 cm. Cranial fasciitis, like NF, expands quickly and may reach a somewhat larger size than typical examples of the latter. When the skull is involved, radiological imaging often reveals a lytic defect, frequently bordered by a sclerotic rim. In contrast, NF presents as a nonspecific soft tissue mass, often with poorly defined, tentacle-like margins.

Epidemiology

NF is relatively common in different case series {4501523; 6279273; 14450278; 14361319; 6462780; 13915406; 13737962}. It occurs in all age groups but more often in young adults. Intravascular fasciitis {7246849} and cranial fasciitis {7351023} are rare. In different case series, intravascular fasciitis is found mostly in people aged < 30 years {24581345; 7246849}, whereas cranial fasciitis develops predominantly in infants aged < 2 years {30743035; 40136575}. NF and intravascular fasciitis occur equally frequently in male and female individuals {21779880; 24581345}, but cranial fasciitis is more common in boys {30743035}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The identification of recurrent USP6 fusion genes in NF has firmly established its clonal neoplastic nature {7621414; 12550774; 21826056; 16115235; 7923068; 12606136; 9797787; 28752842}. USP6 is a deubiquitinating protease involved in cell trafficking, protein degradation, signalling, inflammation, and repair. Multiple promoter partners have been and continue to be described {21826056; 28752842; 27113271; 38304953; 33161462; 34643284; 35175167; 36828621; 39912885; 38651320; 33558945; 31880592; 31827231; 32170450; 39912885; 3421357; 33404853}; the most frequent partner is MYH9 (22q12.3), which encodes non-muscular MYH9, which is involved in cell-shape maintenance, cell motility, adhesion, differentiation, and development {22202063; 28752842}. The USP6 fusion results in transcriptional upregulation of the entire USP6 coding sequence through a classic promoter-swapping mechanism. Often showing self-limited growth and occasionally showing spontaneous regression, NF, a tumour harbouring a recurrent gene fusion, has been proposed to represent a possible model of transient neoplasia {21826056}. USP6 rearrangements are also found in aneurysmal bone cyst, myositis ossificans, fibro-osseous pseudotumour of digits, and fibroma of tendon sheath; indeed, some examples of the latter are probably NF {23769422; 15509545; 15026324; 29787930; 18265974; 27125357; 32661296; 34088995; 30758758; 31827231; 35686345; 32635781}. These entities share overlapping histological features with NF, supporting the biological relationship with USP6-associated neoplasia {33000481; 32661296}. Recent mechanistic studies have identified the NF-κB and JAK-1/STAT3 pathways as critical mediators of tumorigenesis by USP6 {27440725; 22081069; 34381187}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, NF may appear either circumscribed or infiltrative; however, it notably lacks the thin fibrous peripheral pseudocapsule characteristic of most mesenchymal tumours. The cut surface is generally homogeneous and varies from myxoid to fibrous in appearance, and central cystic change may be occasionally present. Intravascular fasciitis may exhibit either a nodular or a plexiform architecture, with the latter arising from extensive intravascular growth. Cranial fasciitis is typically well-circumscribed and has a rubbery to firm consistency, sometimes displaying focal myxoid or cystic changes centrally.

Histopathology

NF is composed of plump spindle to stellate cells lacking nuclear hyperchromasia or pleomorphism. Mitotic figures may be plentiful, but atypical forms are not observed. Although the tumour may be highly cellular, it typically exhibits cystic discohesion and myxoid stromal changes, imparting a torn, feathery, or tissue culture–like appearance. Hypercellular areas often show S- or C-shaped fascicles, or a storiform growth pattern. Stromal collagen is usually minimal, though keloidal collagen bundles may be present and occasionally prominent. Microcystic stromal changes are also characteristic. Common additional features include extravasated erythrocytes, lymphocytes, and osteoclast-like giant cells. A key low-power diagnostic clue is the absence of a peripheral fibrous pseudocapsule; instead, NF generally infiltrates surrounding tissue (at least focally), often extending into adjacent fat in a tentacle-like pattern, sometimes accompanied by fat necrosis and chronic inflammation at the interface. In intramuscular locations, NF may infiltrate muscle fibres. Numerous small vessels may give NF a granulation tissue-like appearance. Older examples can be diagnostically challenging because of stromal hyalinization and a resulting sparse population of small condensed spindled to stellate cells; however, the tentacle-like peripheral extension with fat necrosis remains a helpful clue. Exceptionally rare cases of NF may show striking pleomorphic features (morphologically malignant) but have behaved in a benign fashion {3421357; 33625261; 33161462; 38651320}. In contrast, rare examples have shown true malignant behaviour (malignant NF), including 1 patient with fatal lung metastases, in an otherwise histologically typical NF {31538390; 39912885; 27113271; 37106302}. Notably, these clinically malignant examples were all large and bulky tumours {39912885; 27113271; 37106302; 31538390}.

Intravascular fasciitis and cranial fasciitis are similar to NF histologically, although intravascular fasciitis often displays a greater number of osteoclast-like giant cells. Intravascular fasciitis ranges from predominantly extravascular, with only a minor intravascular component, to predominantly intravascular. Osseous metaplasia is occasionally seen in NF (fasciitis ossificans) and cranial fasciitis {6814399; 4974865}.

By immunohistochemistry, the neoplastic cells express SMA in a typical (myo)fibroblastic (tram-track) pattern; desmin positivity is occasionally found, usually focally {1928550}. Nuclear β-catenin may be seen in cranial fasciitis {18587328}.

Cytology

Cytology aspirates show loosely cohesive tissue fragments of bland spindle cells with unipolar curved to bipolar processes, round to oval elongated nuclei, and occasional small nucleoli in a myxoid matrix, along with occasional ganglion-like larger cells, mitoses, and a small number of inflammatory cells {30311731; 26981858}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing is usually unnecessary in clinically and histologically typical cases. However, in challenging cases, break-apart USP6 FISH or targeted RNA fusion assays or next-generation sequencing techniques may be used to support the diagnosis. USP6 fusion genes are not identified in all cases of NF, and a negative result does not exclude the diagnosis {28752842; 29623468; 30139549}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bland, myofibroblastic spindle cell proliferation with a feathery, tissue culture–like growth pattern; variably myxoid stroma with microcystic changes; extravasated red blood cells.

Desirable: assessment of USP6 gene rearrangements can be helpful in selected cases.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Recurrence of NF after excision is rare, although occasional cases have been reported, especially if incompletely excised. Very rare examples of clinically malignant behaviour have been reported {27113271; 31538390; 39912885}. In contrast, cases with atypical nuclear features and mitoses (morphologically malignant NF) have not been shown to behave differently from classic examples.
