1.11.4 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a neuroectodermal tumour with Schwannian differentiation, composed of epithelioid to polygonal cells with copious lysosome-rich granular eosinophilic cytoplasm. A small subset is malignant.

ICD-O coding

9580/3 Granular cell tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Not recommended: granular cell schwannoma; granular cell myoblastoma; Abrikossoff tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Most cases affect deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue, particularly of the head and neck, trunk, and proximal extremities {6246310; 26637199}. The single most common site is the tongue (25%), followed by breast (5–15%) {6095165; 15569210}. Visceral involvement of the gastrointestinal tract (oesophagus, large bowel) and respiratory tract (larynx) is also common {20661017; 14502147}. Although most granular cell tumours are solitary, as many as 10% are multifocal, and these can be regional or involve multiple organ sites {10089680; 6246310; 36534754}. Malignant granular cell tumours typically arise in deep soft tissues, predominantly in the trunk and extremities, with the thigh being the most common site. Head and neck and oral locations are less common sites for malignant tumours {9669341}.

Clinical features

Although usually asymptomatic, granular cell tumour can be pruritic to painful in the skin and tongue. Cutaneous lesions are firm, flesh-coloured to reddish-brown, and 0.5–3.0 cm in size. Tumour growth is usually indolent.

Epidemiology

Granular cell tumours constitute 0.5% of all soft tissue neoplasms and typically affect adults aged 40–60 years but can occur at any age {26637199}; they are more prevalent and frequently multiple among African Americans {6246310}. Most studies report a female predominance {28898987}. Malignant granular cell tumour is extremely rare (1–2%), occurs across a wide age range of 3–87 years (median: 53 years), and predominantly affects female individuals (65%), White populations (70%), and the trunk (in up to one third of cases) {28989104}.

Etiology

Most granular tumours are sporadic; however, multicentric cases have been reported, some of which are in association with various syndromes, including neurofibromatosis type 1 {26637199}, Noonan syndrome {18577039; 22915371; 20537083}, and LEOPARD syndrome (multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, retardation of growth, and sensorineural deafness) {19054014}. LEOPARD syndrome is characterized by multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, growth retardation, and sensorineural deafness. These syndromes are typically linked to aberrant signalling within the RAS/MAPK pathway through inactivating variants.

Pathogenesis

Loss-of-function variants affecting the V-ATPase accessory genes ATP6AP1 (61%) and ATP6AP2 (11%) are found in 72% of granular cell tumours collectively {30166553; 30597645}, irrespective of anatomical site and classification as benign or malignant. These genes play crucial roles in endosomal pH regulation and vesicle trafficking {30166553}. Their inactivating variants are not present in other neoplasms and are therefore pathognomonic for granular cell tumours; however, variants affecting other V-ATPase-related genes have been detected in a subset of granular cell tumours with wildtype ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2 {30597645; 33289181}. These variants are oncogenic and can lead to impaired vesicular acidification, altered distribution of endosomes, and massive intracytoplasmic accumulation of early and recycling endosomes {30166553}. V-ATPase dysfunction may impact the nuclear expression and activity of MITF/TFE3 family proteins in granular cell tumours {30597645}. Multifocal benign granular cell tumours are clonally independent in V-ATPase gene aberrations, while paired primary malignant and metastatic lesions share the same driver variants {36534754}.

Macroscopic appearance

Granular cell tumours are uninodular, firm masses involving the skin/subcutis or submucosa, often with an overlying epithelial hyperplasia {26637199}. On sectioning, the tumour has a yellowish, finely granular texture. Malignant cases are usually > 50 mm in size, but they can broadly range from 10 to 180 mm {9669341; 28989104}. Grossly, tumours are pale grey and firm, often deeper (subcutaneous/intramuscular) than their benign counterparts.

Histopathology

Granular cell tumour has a poorly defined, infiltrative growth pattern and is composed of sheets, nests, and trabeculae of large monotonous epithelioid to polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic and distinctively granular cytoplasm. The cytoplasmic granularity reflects a massive accumulation of lysosomes, including larger intracytoplasmic granules encircled by clear haloes and exhibiting PAS-positive, diastase-resistant staining {17448196; 26637199}. Cell borders may be indistinct, imparting a syncytial appearance. Nuclei, usually centrally located, range from uniformly small and mildly hyperchromatic to larger and vesicular, with prominent nucleoli {9669341; 26637199}. Mitoses are variable in number but usually scant, while perineurial and perivascular involvement is not uncommon {26637199}. For cutaneous and mucosa-associated lingual and oesophageal lesions, pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of overlying squamous epithelium can be florid, mimicking squamous cell carcinoma {6246310; 26637199}.

Malignant granular cell tumours are extremely rare and morphologically vary from overtly sarcomatous to deceptively bland on rare occasions. Histological features associated with malignancy include prominent spindling, a high N:C ratio, vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli, marked pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity (> 2 mitoses/2 mm2), and geographical necrosis {9669341}. The presence of three or more of these parameters is helpful to identify tumours with metastatic propensity {9669341; 40095553}; neither mitotic count nor necrosis alone can identify all tumours that metastasize {26637199; 40095553}.

Granular cell tumours are generally immunoreactive for S100, SOX10, α-inhibin, and calretinin {24746209; 26637199}. The tumours are also positive for CD68, CD63 (NKI/C3), and NSE, probably nonspecific because of the cytoplasmic lysosomal content {8878020; 15214825}. The tumours are negative for keratin, CD163, and myogenic markers. Despite the absence of gene rearrangements, MITF and TFE3 show diffuse nuclear reactivity in most cases {26009539; 24746209}. HMB45 is consistently negative, although focal expression of melan-A may be observed in rare instances {17284958}.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows polygonal epithelioid cells, singly and in sheets, showing abundant granular cytoplasm, uniform round central nuclei, and a low N:C ratio with a granular background and stripped nuclei {11348331; 11748580}. The differential diagnosis includes carcinomas (particularly apocrine) and histiocytic processes {25379050}. Nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, coarse chromatin, vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and an increased N:C ratio suggest malignant progression.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nests, trabeculae, and sheets of epithelioid to polygonal cells; abundant, intensely eosinophilic, and granular cytoplasm; diffuse immunoreactivity to S100 or SOX10.

Staging

There is currently no evidence of the prognostic value of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system for malignant granular cell tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Although granular cell tumour is benign in the vast majority of cases, local recurrence sometimes occurs in incompletely excised tumours. Malignant granular cell tumour has a 50% rate of metastasis, including regional nodal metastasis and distant dissemination {9669341; 26637199}. Older patient age, larger tumour size (> 5 cm), local recurrence, and metastasis are adverse prognostic factors for malignant cases {9669341}.
