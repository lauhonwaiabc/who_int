4.0.5 Multiple osteochondromas (EXT1, EXT2)

Definition

Multiple osteochondromas is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in EXT1 or EXT2 and is characterized by the development of multiple benign cartilage-capped neoplasms at the bone surface.

MIM numbering

133700 Exostoses, multiple, type I; EXT1

133701 Exostoses, multiple, type II; EXT2

ICD-11 coding

LD24.20 Multiple osteochondromas

Related terminology

Acceptable: hereditary multiple exostoses; multiple hereditary exostoses.

Subtype(s)

Rarely, multiple osteochondromas is part of a contiguous gene deletion syndrome: Langer–Giedion syndrome (or trichorhinophalangeal syndrome type II) or Potocki–Shaffer syndrome (proximal 11p deletion syndrome, DEFECT11, 11p11.2 contiguous gene deletion syndrome).

Localization

Osteochondromas are located in bones that develop by endochondral ossification, especially the long bones of the extremities, but flat bones like the ribs, the spine and the pelvis may also be affected.

Clinical features

Osteochondromas enlarge during childhood and stop growing when the physes close at the end of puberty. The number of osteochondromas varies significantly within and between families. Most patients present in the first decade of life {10992031} with limb deformity, impingement on adjacent structures, or a painless bony mass, often first noticed in the ribs and proximal tibia {14207550}. Characteristic deformities involve the forearm (shortening of the ulna with resultant bowing of the radius [39–60%], often accompanied by a pseudo–Madelung deformity at the wrist) {8027127}; valgus angulation of the knee (8–33%) and ankle (2–54%) {8027127; 225330}; limb length inequality (10–50%); and short stature (37–44%) {7702095; 9272707}. Joint movement may be restricted {27920806}. Hip subluxation occurs in the presence of multiple femoral neck osteochondromas and secondary acetabular dysplasia {19721143} and may result in premature arthropathy. Impingement on adjacent structures causes bursa formation; tendon, nerve {9765135}, muscle {8915059}, and vessel dysfunction (including popliteal pseudoaneurysm {23748578}); and spinal cord compression {7702095}. Visceral complications include haemothorax, pericardial effusion {16034772}, diaphragmatic rupture {16395607}, gastrointestinal {18462899} and bladder outflow obstruction {20726435}, and haematuria {9168715}. Patients with multiple osteochondromas may also form abnormal scars {17414021} and develop malformed and/or displaced teeth with abnormal enamel {18654627; 21499719}. A reported 84% of patients with multiple osteochondromas report frequent pain {15832158}, which has a profound impact on quality of life {22637207}.

The most important complication of multiple osteochondromas, estimated to occur in as many as 5% of patients with multiple osteochondromas, is the development of secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour (appendicular skeleton) / chondrosarcoma, grade 1 (ACT/CS1) (axial skeleton, including scapula, skull base, and pelvis), or high-grade chondrosarcoma {7702095; 9272707; 8027127}. The age at diagnosis of secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma ranges from 13 to 63 years (median: 34 years), with 75% occurring between the ages of 20 and 40 years {30591865; 38351015}. The site most frequently affected is the pelvis (44%) followed by the lower limbs (33%) (including femur, fibula, or tibia) {38351015; 30591865}. Male individuals seem to be affected more commonly than female individuals {38351015}. Growth after puberty, worsening pain, or a cartilage cap thickness of > 2–3 cm in an adult should raise suspicion of secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma. Occasionally, patients with multiple osteochondromas also develop central ACT/CS1 (~3.6%) {25568391}.

In Langer–Giedion syndrome, craniofacial dysmorphism and intellectual disability are seen in addition to multiple osteochondromas {6496574}. Patients with Potocki–Shaffer syndrome present with enlarged parietal foramina, multiple osteochondromas, and sometimes craniofacial dysostosis and intellectual disability {8644736}.

Imaging

The diagnosis of multiple osteochondromas is confirmed by conventional radiographs. As with isolated osteochondromas, the hallmark is continuity of the cortex and medulla of the osteochondroma and the bone of origin. They may be sessile or pedunculated. Multiple lesions can coalesce and, when arranged circumferentially in the femoral necks and pubic rami, result in characteristic broadening. Typical metaphysial remodelling and deformity (see above) also occur. MRI is the most useful imaging technique to evaluate benign and malignant complications of osteochondromas {9765135}. T2-weighted MRI (and occasionally CT) can be used to assess the cartilage cap thickness; if > 2–3 cm, this is suggestive of chondrosarcoma {20392983}. The role of 18F-FDG PET needs to be further established {16954943}. Whole-body MRI can be used for surveillance of osteochondromas, particularly in clinically inaccessible sites, and can detect asymptomatic malignant change {31273432}. Annual MRI screening of the affected bones, especially between 20 and 40 years of age, seems beneficial to discover potential malignant transformation at an early stage {30591865; 20233460}.

Epidemiology

Around 15% of osteochondromas occur in the context of hereditary multiple exostoses, inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. The prevalence of multiple osteochondromas is about 1 case per 50 000 people within the general population {8027127; 33622860}, although this may be an underestimation. The prevalence may be slightly higher in male individuals than in female individuals {7702095; 9272707}. Approximately 70–75% of patients have a positive family history {19810120}.

Etiology

Multiple osteochondromas, an autosomal dominant condition, is genetically heterogeneous, and two causative genes, EXT1 at 8q24.11 {7550340} and EXT2 at 11p11.2 {8782816; 8894688}, have been isolated. Pathogenic germline inactivating EXT1 or EXT2 variants, gross deletions, or intragenic duplications have been reported in 70–95% of patients with multiple osteochondromas {19810120}. EXT1 or EXT2 variants are detected in about 65% and 35% of cases, respectively {19810120}. Inactivating variants (nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site mutations) represent the majority (77–80%) of mutations causing multiple osteochondromas {19810120}. No hotspot mutations have been identified, and variants are predominantly found in exons; variants in EXT1 are spread over the different exons, whereas variants in EXT2 more frequently affect the N-terminus. In the remaining cases of multiple osteochondromas, other alterations such as deep intronic or promoter mutations, inversions, translocations, or somatic mosaicism may be the underlying causes {19810120; 21280143; 23341036; 14556251}.

Osteochondromas develop upon biallelic inactivation of EXT1 or EXT2 {10441575; 20813973}; thus, a second somatic hit is required to inactivate the other allele in a chondrocyte within the growth plate {20080592} or in a mesenchymal progenitor cell from the perichondrium {23458899}.

Both EXT1 and EXT2 are also involved in a contiguous gene deletion syndrome. In Langer–Giedion syndrome, the 8q24 deletion includes the TRPS1 gene in addition to EXT1 {7550340; 10615131}. In Potocki–Shaffer syndrome, patients carry an 11p12-p11.2 deletion that includes ALX4 in addition to EXT2 {11106354}.

Pathogenesis

EXT1 and EXT2 mRNA is ubiquitously expressed {7550340; 8782816; 8894688}. Loss of heterozygosity, aneuploidy, or other changes can occur in the cartilaginous cap of osteochondroma, inactivating the second allele in at least a subset of the cells at the somatic level {20813973; 10502322; 23821404}. Somatic compound heterozygous mutations in EXT1 or EXT2 have been reported, but other mechanisms have been postulated {20418910}. The gene products, EXT1 and EXT2, are endoplasmic reticulum–localized type II transmembrane glycoproteins that form a Golgi-localized hetero-oligomeric complex that catalyses heparan sulfate polymerization {9756849; 10639137; 10545594}. Heparan sulfate signalling is involved in multiple signalling pathways, and EXT-family variants therefore affect hedgehog {15177029}, BMP {29277722}, FGFR3 {29545125}, and WNT/β-catenin {29545125} pathway signalling. In particular, BMP signalling was shown to be crucial for osteochondroma development in mice; inhibitors of BMP signalling (e.g. palovarotene) were tested in clinical trials with inconclusive results {28768899; 29120519; 28445472}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross specimens of the individual tumours are similar to those of sporadic osteochondromas.

Histopathology

The tumours are histopathologically identical to their sporadic counterparts (see section Osteochondroma). Malignant transformation leads to a secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 in slightly more than 50% of cases, the remainder being grade 2 or 3 {12782876; 35193636; 31453886; 38901841; 38351015}. Osteosarcoma, spindle cell sarcoma, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma are extremely rare {10458834; 11896078; 19734846}. On histology alone, it is hard to distinguish osteochondroma from secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 in the setting of multiple osteochondromas. Some clinical and radiographic features, including cartilaginous cap thickness, need to be evaluated in a multidisciplinary setting {22555180}. Multiple osteochondromas must be distinguished from metachondromatosis and from dysplasia epiphysealis hemimelica (Trevor disease), which are not related to EXT1 and EXT2 variants. Clinical and radiological features and family history allow distinction.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of multiple osteochondromas is made on the basis of clinical and radiological data; molecular analysis for germline variants in EXT1 or EXT2 is not mandatory for the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: radiologically at least two osteochondromas of the juxtaepiphysial region of long bones and/or a proven germline pathogenic variant in EXT1 or EXT2.

Staging

A clinical classification system has been suggested and recently revised; it is based on the presence or absence of deformities and functional limitations {22258776; 23401177; 34477285}.

Prognosis and prediction

Intrafamilial variability in clinical expression of the disease has been described, and at the moment no strong prognostic factor predicting the evolution of the disease (e.g. number of osteochondromas in adult age, deformities) is known. EXT1 variants seem associated with a more severe phenotype and shorter stature than EXT2 variants {15446535; 16879194; 17589361; 17676624; 22258776}, whereas patients without a disease-causing gene variant usually have a mild phenotype. Additional (genetic) factors contribute to the final clinical presentation {22037484}. Male patients seem to be characterized by a more severe clinical presentation than female patients. Because there is no evidence of the presence of any risk or protective factor related to malignant transformation, an adequate follow-up is required for all patients.
