4.0.4 McCune–Albright syndrome (GNAS)

Definition

McCune–Albright syndrome (MAS) is a disorder caused by somatic mosaic activating GNAS variants and characterized by wide clinical variability and a broad spectrum of endocrine and non-endocrine manifestations, including the classic triad of fibrous dysplasia, precocious puberty, and skin hyperpigmentation.

MIM numbering

174800 McCune–Albright syndrome; MAS

ICD-11 coding

FB80.0 Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Related terminology

Not recommended: Albright syndrome; fibrous dysplasia (polyostotic and monostotic); Mazabraud syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Various sites may be affected, determined by the mosaic distribution of the mutation-bearing cells {12199345}. MAS classically involves the skeleton (fibrous dysplasia), skin (pigmented lesions / café-au-lait macules, preferentially called hyperpigmented macules), and endocrine systems. Any combination of features may be present: MAS is defined as the combination of fibrous dysplasia and one or more extraskeletal feature, or the presence of two or more extraskeletal features {31196103}. Fibrous dysplasia is usually polyostotic, commonly in the appendicular skeleton, craniofacial sites, and spine, but may be monostotic {31196103}. Hyperpigmented macules respect the midline, associated with or radiating from it {22640971}, following Blaschko’s lines {8123575}. Endocrine organs that may be affected include the ovaries, testes, thyroid, adrenals, and pituitary; associated hepatobiliary and cardiac dysfunction also occurs {22640971}.

Clinical features

The clinical features are variable and determined by the tissues affected; presentation is usually due to bone disease or precocious puberty {18489744}.

Fibrous dysplasia in the appendicular skeleton may cause bone pain, limping, and fracture. The most common diagnostic circumstance of fibrous dysplasia is bone pain in about 43% of cases, with incidental diagnosis in 18–24% of cases {37739062}. Craniofacial fibrous dysplasia may cause a painless lump or facial asymmetry; visual or hearing impairment may result from encroachment on orbital / skull base foramina. The spine is frequently involved in polyostotic fibrous dysplasia (63% of subjects using isotope bone scan), and scoliosis was reported in 40% {14996879}.

Hyperpigmented macules show irregular borders and develop in the neonatal period but rarely instigate a clinical referral {18489744}. Oral lentigines have also rarely been reported, similar to those seen in Carney complex and Peutz–Jeghers syndrome {24671640}.

Precocious puberty is the commonest endocrinopathy in girls but rare in boys {16789617}. It may present in patients from a few months up to 7 years of age and is due to estrogen-producing ovarian cysts, independent of the central hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis {22640971}. Hyperthyroidism is seen in approximately one third of cases and, in combination with precocious puberty, accelerates bone maturation; osteoporosis contributes to fracture risk {22640971}. Growth hormone excess, usually accompanied by hyperprolactinaemia, increases growth velocity (counteracting accelerated bone maturation) but is associated with increased risk of blindness in craniofacial fibrous dysplasia {17143235}. Hypophosphataemia is due to FGF23 production by fibrous dysplastic cells, causing renal phosphate loss, and is worse with greater disease burden {22640971; 37975816}. Patients experience more severe bone pain and a greater number of fractures, which occur earlier {15005844}. Cushing syndrome is a rare endocrinopathy in MAS, usually occurring in the neonatal period. Patients have an increased mortality due to opportunistic infection and are more likely to have cognitive/developmental sequelae {20157193}.

There is an increased risk of malignancy in MAS. This is most commonly due to sarcoma developing in bone lesions but also carcinomas (breast and thyroid {22640971; 32760762}).

Imaging

Fibrous dysplasia is often polyostotic and shows typical appearances (see section Fibrous dysplasia). Lesions in the appendicular skeleton are well-defined, usually non-aggressive, with a sclerotic, occasionally rind-like margin. Trabeculation, expansile remodelling, and ground-glass density are common {2188311}. Modelling deformity includes the shepherd’s crook appearance of the proximal femur. Malignant change is suggested by new development of pain, aggressive destruction, and an extraosseous mass within a previously stable lesion; it is more frequent in polyostotic fibrous dysplasia {16850130}. Growth hormone excess may increase the risk of malignancy in craniofacial fibrous dysplasia {22640971}. Bone sclerosis and thickening affecting the craniofacial skeleton and skull base may narrow optic/other canals, and there may be associated orbital displacement {2188311} and basilar invagination {29924878}. Spinal lesions show typical imaging features of fibrous dysplasia. Whole-body isotope bone scan, MRI, or low-dose radiography are useful to assess disease burden {31196103}.

In precocious puberty, ultrasound shows a large unilateral ovarian cyst, which may be haemorrhagic and contain cystic and solid elements; male patients show unilateral or bilateral macro-orchidism, a heterogeneous ultrasound echo pattern, and microlithiasis {22640971}. Approximately two thirds of patients with MAS have an abnormal thyroid ultrasound; half of these are hyperthyroid: goitre, cystic lesions, and solid nodules may be identified {2229316}. Growth hormone excess / acromegaly is associated with a pituitary adenoma (usually a macroadenoma) on imaging in > 50% {36877453}, almost always associated with skull base fibrous dysplasia {24517150}. Unilateral or bilateral adrenal hypertrophy is seen in patients with Cushing syndrome {21836496}.

Imaging may also demonstrate associated hepatobiliary and cardiovascular lesions (gastrointestinal polyps {21357941}, which may obstruct; intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms of the pancreas, which may be associated with pancreatitis {30124968}; and other hepatic and biliary neoplasms {24170100}). Patients with MAS may experience tachycardia, aortic root dilatation, and high-output cardiac failure {2188311}.

Epidemiology

MAS is rare, and the prevalence has been estimated to be 1 case per 100 000 to 1 million population {38174586}. All racial and ethnic groups are affected equally. No significant sex differences have been reported {25719192}.

Etiology

MAS / fibrous dysplasia results from postzygotic somatic activating (gain-of-function) mutations in Gsα, one of several transcripts encoded by GNAS at 20q13.32 {1944469}.

Pathogenesis

MAS develops from postzygotic gain-of-function variants in the GNAS gene that encodes the α-subunit of the Gs signalling protein {1944469}. GNAS is a complex locus with an imprinted expression pattern. Multiple gene products, including maternal, paternal, and biallelic-expressed transcripts, are derived from four promoters and 5′ exons that splice onto a common set of downstream exons (exons 2–13) {1944469}. Somatic GNAS variants in MAS / fibrous dysplasia occur at one of two residues: p.Arg201 (accounting for > 95% of cases) {15126527} and p.Gln227 (accounting for < 5% of cases) {17493233}.

The major product of GNAS is Gsα, which is generated by the most downstream promoter (exon 1). Gsα is the ubiquitously expressed heterotrimeric G protein alpha subunit, which couples seven-transmembrane receptors to the cAMP-generating enzyme adenylyl cyclase and is required for the intracellular cAMP response to hormones and other extracellular signals. The GNAS variants at p.Arg201 and p.Gln227 disrupt Gsα’s intrinsic GTPase activity, causing constitutive activation and abnormal cAMP signalling {2549426}.

There are no known genotype–phenotype correlations {33512531}. Clinical presentation and disease severity are probably determined by the degree of mosaicism and the location and extent of affected tissues.

Macroscopic appearance

Solid tumours related to MAS have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

MAS-related bone disease: Fibrous dysplasia lesions are composed of proliferating fibroblast-like bone marrow stromal cells, which replace normal bone and marrow. Osteoid trabeculae are present throughout the lesions and are discontinuous and non-functional in their orientation {10365102}. Trabeculae are frequently composed of poorly mineralized woven bone {10365102}.

MAS predisposes to a wide variety of hyperplastic and neoplastic lesions. Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Documentation of a somatic activating variant in GNAS is useful in the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Desirable: demonstration of GNAS variant in addition to two or more of the three clinical criteria listed in #56880Box 6.03.

Staging

The ninth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification and version 9 of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging system are applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Individuals with MAS have a variable prognosis depending on disease location and severity. Endocrinopathies persist throughout childhood and adulthood, with the exception of FGF23-mediated hypophosphataemia, which may worsen during periods of rapid linear growth and ameliorate in adulthood {23532406}. Also, neonatal hypercortisolism may undergo spontaneous remission among patients who survive the acute phase {31037426}. Endocrinopathies can generally be managed through multimodal therapies {31196103; 25719192}, and optimizing endocrinopathies may improve skeletal outcomes in patients with fibrous dysplasia {15005844; 29669167; 29924878}. Given the heterogeneous nature of MAS-related pituitary disease, total hypophysectomy is required for surgical cure {11081212; 22564667; 28007843}. GNAS variants in MAS are weakly oncogenic, and affected tissues probably carry a small increased risk of malignant transformation. Fibrous dysplasia rarely undergoes sarcomatous transformation {8111708; 13775207}; the risk may be increased by radiotherapy and uncontrolled growth hormone excess {2754454; 31673695}. Women with MAS have been suggested to have an increased risk of breast cancer {28856726}.
