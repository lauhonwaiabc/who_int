1.12.20 Epithelioid sarcoma

Definition

Epithelioid sarcoma (ES) is a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm exhibiting partial or complete epithelioid cytomorphology and an epithelial immunophenotype. Two clinicopathological subtypes are recognized: the classic (also known as conventional or distal) subtype, which typically arises in acral sites and displays a pseudogranulomatous growth pattern, and the proximal subtype, which is generally more aggressive, predominantly involves proximal or truncal regions, and is composed of nests and sheets of large epithelioid cells harbouring distinctive rhabdoid cytology.

Related terminology

Acceptable: for classic epithelioid sarcoma: conventional epithelioid sarcoma; distal subtype epithelioid sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Classic epithelioid sarcoma (also known as conventional or distal-subtype); proximal subtype epithelioid sarcoma.

Localization

The classic subtype most commonly occurs in the skin or superficial soft tissue of the distal upper extremities, with a predilection for the fingers and hand {5476785; 4014539; 26645461}. Tumours arising in the hand and foot frequently affect volar surfaces with involvement of fascia and tendon structures. The proximal subtype typically originates in deep soft tissues, and it most often affects axial location (predominantly pelvis, perineum, and genitalia); less frequently buttock or hip may be involved {9042279; 11454997}. However, both subtypes might develop in either proximal or distal locations {9042279; 26645461}.

Clinical features

Superficially located examples of conventional ES present as solitary or multiple, slow-growing, usually painless, firm nodules. Lesions often result in non-healing skin ulcers that can clinically mimic other ulcerated dermal processes. In comparison, conventional and proximal-type tumours located in deep soft tissue are usually larger in size and more infiltrative {9042279; 11454997}. This is one of the few sarcomas that regularly metastasizes to lymph nodes. As many as 50% of patients develop distant metastases, most often to the lungs, bones, and brain {4014539}.

Epidemiology

ES is an ultrarare soft tissue sarcoma representing < 1% of all adult soft tissue sarcomas {19224301} and between 4% and 8% of childhood non-rhabdomyosarcomatous soft tissue sarcomas {16353216}. However, it is one of the most common soft tissue sarcomas of the hand {16516741}. The crude incidence rate in the USA ranged from 0.03 to 0.05 cases per 100 000 person-years {19224301}. The classic subtype is reported nearly twice as often as the proximal subtype {19141382; 18607629}. The M:F ratio is 2:1 for the classic subtype {26645461} and 1.6:1 for the proximal subtype {9042279; 11454997}. Both tumours affect patients over a wide age range. The classic subtype presents mainly in adolescents and young adults, whereas the proximal subtype tends to affect a somewhat older population – young to middle-aged adults {26645461}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown.

Pathogenesis

Both distal and proximal subtypes are associated with SMARCB1 loss-of-function alterations, resulting in SMARCB1 (INI1) loss of nuclear protein expression. The SMARCB1 gene (also known as BAF47, INI1, or SNF5), located at 22q11.23, encodes a subunit of two of the three SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complexes (canonical BAF and pBAF). These complexes use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to mobilize nucleosomes, thereby exposing DNA to transcription factors. SWI/SNF complexes are ubiquitously expressed in the nuclei of all normal cells and play a crucial role in regulating the transcription of a large class of genes involved in stem cell biology and differentiation {28945250}. Loss-of-function SMARCB1 (INI1) alterations may be seen in a variety of tumour types {24122795; 28109176; 30709441; 30382607}. Most ES harbour homozygous deletions of SMARCB1, and less frequently, monoallelic deletions, intragenic deletions, or point mutations {23060122; 24585572; 27223121}. In cases associated with heterozygous SMARCB1 loss-of-function, inactivation of the second allele may occur through epigenetic mechanism such as promoter hypermethylation or miRNAs-mediated deregulation {24327545; 24287458; 27223121}. An extreme minority of ES retain SMARCB1 (INI1) protein expression, instead exhibiting alterations in other SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex members, such as SMARCA4 (BRG1), SMARCC1 (BAF155), or SMARCC2 (BAF170); such cases have been correlated with rhabdoid morphology and with adverse prognosis {29309303}. Proximal and distal ES subtypes show different transcriptional and genome-wide methylation profiles, clustering separately regardless of tumour location. Proximal ES show hyperactivation of GATA3 and MYC pathways {37916971; 39491746; 26365879}, while the classic subtype is characterized by endothelial-like molecular profile with elevated proangiogenic SOX17 signalling {39491746}. Regardless of the subtype, no specific secondary genetic alterations have been consistently found {35839307}. Genomic analysis by next-generation sequencing has revealed complex copy-number aberrations (with 22q11.2 and 12p13 being the most frequent losses) {26365879}.

Macroscopic appearance

The classic subtype usually presents as one or more indurated, poorly defined, dermal or subcutaneous nodules measuring a few millimetres to 50 mm. Deep-seated tumours are multinodular masses that extend along nerves and fascial planes. The cut surface is glistening, with a white-tan colour, with foci of necrosis and haemorrhage. The proximal subtype presents as solitary or multiple whitish nodules ranging from 10 to 200 mm, with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis {9042279; 11454997}.

Histopathology

Conventional ES consists of cellular nodules of epithelioid and spindled tumour cells with central haemorrhage and/or necrosis – a growth pattern that imparts a vaguely granulomatous appearance {3717528}. Fusion of necrotizing nodules results in a serpiginous mass with central geographical necrosis. Deep-seated lesions spread along the fascia as undulating bands of cells punctuated by foci of necrosis. Perineural and intraneural invasion is also frequently observed. Both subtypes typically have infiltrative margins, and skip lesions are common in the classic subtype. Tumour nodules are composed of ovoid, polygonal epithelioid cells or plump spindle-shaped cells with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm and mildly atypical nuclei possessing vesicular chromatin and small nucleoli. The epithelioid cells, which are generally concentrated towards the centre of the nodule, gradually transition with the spindled element. Epithelioid cells may display cytoplasmic vacuoles, and the tumour can exhibit haemorrhagic pseudoangiomatous features, mimicking vascular neoplasms. Rarely, large cells and severe cytological atypia with rhabdoid features may be observed in conventional ES, features similar to those of the proximal subtype. Some cases have predominantly spindled morphology. Mitotic activity is variable, usually low. Giant cells, dystrophic calcification, and metaplastic bone formation are detected in as many as 20% of cases {4014539}, and aggregates of chronic inflammatory cells are usually present at the periphery of the tumour nodules.

The proximal subtype is characterized by a multinodular and sheet-like growth of large and sometimes pleomorphic epithelioid (carcinoma-like) cells with enlarged vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli {9042279; 11454997}. Abundant coagulative tumour necrosis is frequently encountered, but this feature does not generally result in a pseudogranulomatous pattern. Cells with rhabdoid features are more frequent in the proximal subtype, in which differentiation from extrarenal rhabdoid tumour becomes challenging when the rhabdoid cell predominates {9042279}. Occasional cases may have a prominent myxoid stroma {21083605}.

Both distal and proximal subtypes show immunoreactivity for epithelial markers, including low- and high-molecular-weight keratins and EMA {10452506}. More specifically, most cases express CK8 and CK19 {10452506} but are typically negative or only focally positive for CK5/6 {12946229}. Unlike in carcinoma, ES shows CD34 positivity in > 50% of cases and are claudin-4 negative {28084340}. SMARCB1 (INI) loss of nuclear expression is present in the vast majority of cases of both types {19033866; 18973917; 21357725}. ERG expression is observed in 40–67% of ES, predominantly in classic subtype cases, potentially resulting in confusion with endothelial tumours {23774169; 24072183; 25479928}. Proximal-subtype ES may express GATA3 {39491746}.

Cytology

Aspirates show high cellularity with loosely cohesive tissue fragments and single polygonal, epithelioid, and spindle cells with round to pleomorphic nuclei, vesicular chromatin, and distinct nucleoli, and abundant, well-defined cytoplasm containing perinuclear inclusions. Multinucleated giant cells and stroma are variably present. Immunohistochemistry and next-generation sequencing are required for diagnosis {7145123; 30291790}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For the classic subtype: epithelioid and/or spindle cell morphology often with a pseudogranulomatous pattern; loss of SMARCB1 and expression of epithelial markers (EMA and keratins) For the proximal subtype: epithelioid/rhabdoid morphology with marked nuclear atypia; loss of SMARCB1 and expression of epithelial markers (EMA or keratins)

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) or American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system may be used {36073670}.

Prognosis and prediction

Clinical series of patients with ES have reported 5-year and 10-year overall survival rates of 45–70% and 45–66%, respectively {24046109; 25663591; 28756018; 28799063}. Overall survival rates drop to 24% in patients with metastatic ES, whereas patients with localized ES fare better, with an overall survival rate of 62–88% when patients are treated with R0 surgery {11454997; 24046109; 28756018}. On multivariate analysis, deep location correlates with lower overall survival, accounting for the poorer survival associated with proximal-subtype ES {24046109; 28756018; 28799063}. Conversely, location in the hand correlates with good outcome in patients with localized ES treated with R0 surgery {28756018}. The rates of local recurrence, lymph node dissemination, and metastasis for localized ES are 14–25%, 34–52%, and 33%, respectively {24046109; 28756018; 28799063}. A reported 22–30% of cases are metastatic at the time of diagnosis {24046109; 25663591; 28756018}. Multimodal management of localized ES is said to be associated with better local control rates when surgery is combined with isolated limb perfusion chemotherapy {24046109} or neoadjuvant radiotherapy {28756018}. Other adverse prognostic factors that have been reported include older age, high grade / high mitotic activity {16557275; 19141382; 20932739; 24046109; 36073670; 32307188}, nodal involvement {25663591; 28799063; 27785659}, tumour size > 50 mm {19141382; 18607629; 16557275; 36073670}, and proximal-subtype histology with the presence of rhabdoid cells {20932739; 18607629}.
