2.4.1 Haemangioma of bone

Definition

Haemangioma of bone is a benign vascular tumour composed of vascular channels of small or large calibre. When haemangiomas involve a large, localized region, or are widespread throughout the skeleton, the term “angiomatosis” can be used. Some lesions are probably malformations rather than true neoplasms.

ICD-O coding

9120/0 Haemangioma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Y & XH5AW4 & XA5GG8 Benign vascular neoplasms & Haemangioma NOS & Bones

Related terminology

Acceptable: cystic angiomatosis.

Not recommended: massive osteolysis.

Subtype(s)

Angiomatosis

Localization

Vertebral bodies are the most commonly affected site (30–50%), followed by the calvarium (20%), and long bones, where haemangiomas tend to involve the metaphysis or diaphysis {38237880}. Medullary origin is frequent, but 45% of cases are either periosteal (33%) or intracortical (12%) {11000295}. Multifocal occurrence is frequent.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

The majority of haemangiomas in the bone, especially those arising in the spine, are asymptomatic incidental radiographic findings. However, large vertebral tumours may cause cord compression, pain, and neurological symptoms. Symptomatic tumours occurring elsewhere are painful and may cause a pathological fracture.

Imaging

Haemangioma is a common incidental finding in the axial skeleton {31498790}. Imaging features depend, especially in the axial skeleton, on the balance between fat and vascular tissue {25348558}. The more common first variety has low fat density on CT and high signal intensity on T1-weighted MRI. If the vascular component dominates, MRI signal intensity is high on fluid-sensitive and gadolinium chelate–enhanced sequences; the high T1-weighted signal of classic haemangioma may be small or even absent {3763864; 33635741}. Soft tissue extension causing symptoms may be present {3763864}. Typically, haemangiomas in the axial skeleton show thickening of primary vertical (spine) or radiating spoke wheel (skull and pelvis) trabeculae, known as polka-dot sign, on radiographs, CT, and MRI. Although fat and thickened trabeculation occurs also in the appendicular skeleton, imaging features are more varied. Lesions may be (partly) cystic, sclerotic, and may also show cortical involvement and periosteal reaction {37052653; 33407312; 32594199}. The more atypical haemangiomas in any location may have increased uptake on 18F-FDG PET/CT (and other PET/CT exams with specific tracers like PSMA), which may be mistaken for metastases, especially when there are multiple, or growing, haemangiomas {37052653; 25348558; 32353715}.

Epidemiology

Haemangiomas are common lesions; autopsy studies have identified them in the vertebrae of approximately 10% of the adult population. However, clinically significant symptomatic tumours are uncommon and account for < 1% of primary bone tumours. Haemangiomas can occur at any age (mean: 32 years) and show a slight preference for women (60%) {38237880; 10741493}.

Etiology

Some lesions are true neoplasms, whereas others are probably developmental anomalies/malformations.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis is unknown for most cases. A small number of osseous haemangiomas have been reported to harbour fusions of the EWSR1 and NFATC2, or less often NFATC1, genes {23480895; 34081036}.

Macroscopic appearance

Haemangioma manifests as a soft, well-demarcated, dark-red mass. It may also have a honeycomb appearance, with intralesional sclerotic bone trabeculae and scattered blood-filled cavities.

Histopathology

Haemangiomas have variable histological features and comprise different morphologies such as cavernous (gaping vascular spaces), capillary (lobulated small vessels), mixed, venous, and arteriovenous patterns. In general, the vascular spaces are lined by a single layer of flat and cytologically bland endothelial cells. Secondary changes include reactive sclerosis of involved bone, new bone formation, calcification, and thrombosis {34742485}. EWSR1::NFATC1/2 fusion is present in a small subset of histologically typical haemangiomas of bone {23480895; 34081036}, as well as in other osseous vascular tumours displaying epithelioid morphology and nuclear atypia {34310785; 38189436}.

The tumour cells stain for endothelial markers, such as CD31, ERG, FLI1, and CD34. The network of preserved pericytes can be highlighted by immunohistochemistry for SMA.

Cytology

FNABs are not routinely performed.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; thin-walled blood vessels lined by a single layer of non-atypical endothelial cells.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Haemangiomas have an excellent prognosis and a low rate of local recurrence. Treatment is not required in most cases. Symptomatic patients may benefit from intralesional curettage, arterial embolization, or other conservative measures.
