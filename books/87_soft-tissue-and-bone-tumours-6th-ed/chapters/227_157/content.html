2.2.7 Periosteal osteosarcoma

Definition

Periosteal osteosarcoma is a malignant, predominantly chondroblastic, intermediate-grade bone-forming sarcoma arising on the surface of the bone, underneath the periosteum.

ICD-O coding

9193/3 Periosteal osteosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH48A9 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Periosteal osteosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: juxtacortical chondroblastic osteosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Periosteal osteosarcoma usually occurs in the diaphysis and metadiaphysis of the femur and tibia {21472720; 30363676; 16290134; 15333772; 7096408}. Other long bones and flat bones are involved uncommonly, with rare examples reported in craniofacial and acral bones {15333772; 30363676; 16290134}. Bilateral tumours have been rarely documented {22349598}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients present with swelling and/or pain of short duration (weeks to months) {7096408; 15333772}.

Imaging

Imaging of periosteal osteosarcoma typically reveals a broad-based, fusiform, lucent soft tissue mass attached to the cortex, accompanied by cortical thickening and/or scalloping-erosion. Despite cortical erosion by the mass, the endosteal cortex usually remains intact. The tumour exhibits varying degrees of mineralization, appearing as focal calcified clumps. Aggressive periosteal reactions, such as perpendicular (hair-on-end), spiculated, lamellated, and/or Codman triangle {15333772; 21071381}, may be present. The lesion’s density diminishes from the cortical base towards the surface. CT and MRI often indicate a significant chondroblastic component, characterized by low CT attenuation and hyperintensity on fluid-sensitive MRI sequences, with typical chondroid (septonodular) enhancement. Low-signal-intensity foci in all sequences may be observed, representing calcified tissue and/or periosteal reaction. Marrow infiltration is uncommon, and abnormal marrow signal with focal adjacent marrow replacement on MRI usually signifies reactive changes {15333772}. However, medullary invasion has been reported {21472720; 18521348}. Axial images demonstrate that the tumour encircles a maximum of approximately 50% of the bone circumference {15333772}.

Epidemiology

Periosteal osteosarcoma is a rare tumour that accounts for 1–6% of all osteosarcomas {39675926; 21472720; 30363676; 19197972} and has an annual incidence of approximately 3–4 cases per 1 million population. Peak incidence was observed for both sexes in the second decade of life (range: first to eighth decade of life) {21472720; 30363676; 16290134; 24932034; 24957555}. Sex distribution is inconsistent among studies, but the largest cohort showed a mild female predominance, and other studies reported an M:F ratio of 1:4 {16290134; 24957555}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

A small number of cases that were genetically studied demonstrated no consistent chromosomal abnormalities {12402305; 9140456}. Complex chromosomal alterations and TP53 mutations have been recorded in some cases {7513549; 9824113}. Bilateral and familial occurrence has been reported in a setting of Li–Fraumeni syndrome {22349598}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumour typically manifests as a well-demarcated, lobulated, and broad-based fusiform mass that is attached to the surface of the cortex underneath the periosteum. Cortical thickening and scalloping may be conspicuous. The cut surface reveals pale glistening cartilaginous tissue. Reactive bone spicules emanating from the cortex may be visible, and the tumour is usually delimited by the periosteum on its surface {1063059; 7096408}.

Histopathology

The tumour comprises poorly defined lobules of cytologically atypical cartilage. The tumour cells often condense at the lobular periphery, merging with hypercellular tissue with primitive spindle cells. Tumour bone or osteoid is often produced in the interlobular septa or at the lobular periphery, but the transition to osteoid matrix also occurs within the cartilage lobule {1063059; 7096408}. Tumour osteoid may be inconspicuous or may not be sampled in biopsy. Fibroblastic areas or myxoid changes may be present. The nuclear atypia is usually intermediate. Elevated periosteum usually covers the tumour surface. Perpendicular periosteal reaction coursing through the tumour tissue is characteristic, corresponding to the hair-on-end pattern on imaging. Medullary invasion is typically absent; however, focal invasion into the medullary cavity may occur.

Differential diagnosis

Periosteal chondrosarcoma predominantly affects the metaphysis of long tubular bones in older individuals, has coarse calcification, and consists of large lobules of well-differentiated hyaline cartilage without interlobular atypical spindle cells or tumour osteoid formation. IDH1 and/or IDH2 variant analysis may be helpful in ambiguous cases {35690562; 21598255}. Parosteal osteosarcoma exhibits a well-circumscribed surface mass overlying the periosteum. It is predominantly fibroblastic with more mature-appearing bone formation, and MDM2 amplification is present in the majority of the cases {21336260; 30001240}. High-grade surface osteosarcoma has anaplastic high-grade features throughout, is not necessarily chondroblastic, and is usually osteoblastic or fibroblastic {10091787}. Medullary invasion in periosteal osteosarcoma should be at most focal, if present, to reliably distinguish it from conventional chondroblastic osteosarcoma with extracortical invasion.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

MDM2/CDK4 amplification (as in parosteal osteosarcoma) and IDH1 and/or IDH2 mutations (as in periosteal chondrosarcoma) are absent in periosteal osteosarcoma {25680902; 21598255}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a bone tumour with compatible imaging; surface location under the periosteum; intermediate-grade, largely chondroblastic osteosarcoma.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]] does not recommend applying the TNM staging system for bone tumours to surface/juxtacortical osteosarcoma or juxtacortical chondrosarcoma. However, other staging systems, such as the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020.]], do include these tumours in the bone staging system. See the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

Periosteal osteosarcoma has a better prognosis than conventional and high-grade surface osteosarcoma, with disease-free survival rates of 89% at 5 years and 77–86% at 10 years. Marrow involvement, originally thought to preclude the diagnosis {1063059}, has been reported and may predict more aggressive behaviour; cases without marrow involvement show a trend towards a better outcome at 5 years {21472720; 30363676; 24932034}. Wide excision is the treatment of choice. Local recurrence, metastasis, and death usually occur within 3 years of diagnosis {16906092}. Chemotherapy has not proven to improve survival, although this remains controversial {35485208; 39675926; 21472720; 30363676}. Assessment of postchemotherapy necrosis is not predictive of outcome {16290134}.
