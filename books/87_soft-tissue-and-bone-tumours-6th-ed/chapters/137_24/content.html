1.3.7 Fibromatosis colli

Definition

Fibromatosis colli is a benign, self-limited fibrous proliferation occurring in the sternocleidomastoid muscle of infants. Some cases progress to shortening of the muscle, resulting in torticollis.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

FB51.Y Other specified fibroblastic disorders

Related terminology

Not recommended: sternocleidomastoid tumour of infancy; pseudotumour of infancy; congenital muscular torticollis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Fibromatosis colli affects the sternocleidomastoid muscle, with a predilection for the right over the left. Most commonly, the middle or distal third of the muscle body is involved; however, about 15% of cases involve the entire muscle {10356139; 7814599}.

Clinical features

Fibromatosis colli presents as a mass within the sternocleidomastoid muscle, most often congenitally or within the first 2–4 weeks of life. Continued growth may occur over several weeks, followed by stabilization and then spontaneous resolution in most cases {7484683; 7814599; 10356139}. Approximately 10–20% of patients develop congenital muscular torticollis {7484683; 7814599}. These patients demonstrate head tilt towards the involved sternocleidomastoid muscle, with the chin directed towards the contralateral shoulder. Plagiocephaly may also occur. Fibromatosis colli is associated with other musculoskeletal congenital anomalies, including hip dysplasia, metatarsus adductus, talipes equinovarus, and prominent ears {7484683; 7814599; 10356139}. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging method, demonstrating an isoechoic to hypoechoic solid, well-defined ovoid mass in continuity with the sternocleidomastoid muscle {9719983; 9545475}. If MRI is performed, fibromatosis colli is typically T1-isointense to muscle and T2-hyperintense or heterogeneously hyperintense {38737379}.

Epidemiology

Fibromatosis colli is uncommon; however, it is the most common cause of a neck mass in the perinatal period. The reported incidence in a case series was 0.3–2.0% of live births {11379737}. There is a slight male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.5:1) {7814599; 20091898}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is uncertain; however, a scar-like/reactive process due to injury of the sternocleidomastoid muscle is most likely, acquired either in utero or at the time of delivery {24205484; 8459000; 20091898}. Possible mechanisms include fetal malposition/crowding, birth trauma, vascular compromise, and infection {20091898; 8459000; 7484683}.

Macroscopic appearance

If fibromatosis colli is excised, the gross appearance is that of a firm to hard, tan-white mass with associated skeletal muscle.

Histopathology

Surgical specimens are infrequent, obtained only from the minority of patients requiring tenotomy for persistent torticollis. When histology is obtained, sections demonstrate a paucicellular, scar-like fibroblastic proliferation entrapping skeletal muscle {20091898}.

Cytology

Cytology aspirates can be diagnostic {2762289; 20091898; 11074630; 25584233}, showing typically scant to mild cellularity, spindle to plump fibroblasts singly or in loosely cohesive tissue fragments with collagen fibres and atrophic/degenerating skeletal muscle. The fibroblastic cells have bland, oval to elongated nuclei and unipolar or bipolar tails of cytoplasm; bare nuclei may also be present. Atrophic, multinucleated myocytes are often admixed {2762289; 20091898; 11074630; 25584233}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: fusiform mass in the sternocleidomastoid muscle of infants; bland fibroblastic proliferation with associated atrophic skeletal muscle.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of fibromatosis colli lesions are self-limited, with resolution within the first 6 months of life; however, a subset of cases progress with continued mass-like growth and/or the development of torticollis. If untreated, torticollis will worsen with patient growth and development {7484683; 11379737}. Within the first year of life, passive manual stretching/manipulations or physiotherapy is effective in about 95% of cases. However, in those cases that do not resolve by stretching/physiotherapy (< 10% of cases), operative management may be required {11379737; 10356139; 20859860}. Patients who present at an age > 1 year are more likely to require surgical intervention {7814599; 20859860; 11379737}.
