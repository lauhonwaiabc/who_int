2.6.1 Benign notochordal cell tumour

Definition

Benign notochordal cell tumour (BNCT) is a benign tumour showing notochordal differentiation and almost exclusively developing in axial skeleton.

ICD-O coding

9370/0 Benign notochordal tumour

ICD-11 coding

2E89.Z & XH7MT7 Benign mesenchymal tumours of uncertain differentiation, unspecified & Benign notochordal tumour

Related terminology

Not recommended: notochordal hamartoma; giant notochordal rest.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

BNCTs occur in the skull base, vertebral bodies, and sacrococcygeal spine. Occasionally, they can be multiple and may be associated with chordoma {15803192; 17895760}. Small numbers of extraskeletal BNCTs have been reported, including in the lung {21716081}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

BNCTs are usually found in adults but have been reported in children. Most BNCTs are found incidentally on imaging, although they may be associated with pain.

Imaging

Radiographs may show mild sclerosis, no abnormalities, or extensive sclerosis resembling an ivory vertebra {21847746; 18188556}. CT may reveal an intraosseous sclerotic lesion (typically < 35 mm) without bone destruction (osteolysis) or extraosseous extension {36063190}, or it may fail to demonstrate a lesion. Small intralesional fat components may appear on CT because of trapped bone marrow {36063190}. MRI shows hypointense to isointense signal on T1-weighted images, high signal on T2-weighted images, and no enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images {36063190} without extraosseous extension, unlike chordoma. Occasionally, T1-hyperintense foci corresponding to entrapped fatty bone marrow are seen within the lesion {22415576}. BNCTs show low activity on technetium bone scans and 18F-FDG PET/CT studies {39001221}. Radiological differentials include intraosseous hibernoma and haemangioma, which (unlike BNCT) show contrast enhancement and/or increased uptake on 18F-FDG PET/CT (especially hibernoma).

Epidemiology

These are rare tumours and the specific epidemiology of BNCT is uncertain, but data from an autopsy study describe an occurrence of 20% of microscopic BNCT among Japanese individuals aged 7–82 years {15186275}. The prevalence of BNCT detected during imaging was reported to be 0.76% in another study {23464492}.

Etiology

An association between germline rs2305089 SNP in TBXT and the development of BNCT and chordoma has recently been reported {40323130}.

Pathogenesis

The embryonic protein brachyury (encoded by TBXT) is expressed in BNCTs.

Macroscopic appearance

BNCTs are located within bone. The cut surfaces reveal a firm, well-demarcated but unencapsulated bright-tan tumour lacking any lobular configuration.

Histopathology

BNCT consists of solid sheets of adipocyte-like vacuolated tumour cells and eosinophilic, less-vacuolated tumour cells. The nuclei are usually bland, and mitotic figures are absent. Some cystic spaces filled with colloid-like eosinophilic material may be present. The tumour often contains isolated islands of bone marrow. The involved bone trabeculae are usually viable and often show reactive appositional bone formation. Unlike chordoma, BNCT lacks a lobular architecture, fibrous septa and capsule, extracellular myxoid matrix, and tumour vasculature.

Immunohistochemistry

BNCT is immunoreactive for brachyury, cytokeratin, EMA, and (variably) S100.

Differential diagnosis

Distinction between BNCT and chordoma can be difficult, particularly on a biopsy: the juxtaposition of a chordoma with BNCT confounds the challenge {28797506}.

Ecchordosis physaliphora spheno-occipitalis (EPS) can be confused with BNCT. EPS represents extraosseous intradural residual embryonic notochordal tissues, typically located in the dorsal region of the clivus. EPS is often incidental and found in 0.6–2.0% of autopsy cases and in 1.7% of patients on MRI {15569763}. Microscopically, unlike BNCT, EPS resembles chordoma including showing basophilic myxoid matrix.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; notochordal differentiation without atypia; positive for brachyury.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis is excellent, and BNCTs do not require any surgical procedure. Malignant transformation to chordoma is rare, but the incidence is unknown. There is no consensus in terms of clinical surveillance {25609192}.
