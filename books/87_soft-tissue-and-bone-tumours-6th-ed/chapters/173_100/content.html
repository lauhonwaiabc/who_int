1.7.3 Leiomyosarcoma

Definition

Leiomyosarcoma (LMS) is a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm with smooth muscle differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8890/3

ICD-11 coding

XXX

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Primary soft tissue LMS most frequently arises in the extremities and retroperitoneal/intra-abdominal cavities, with the latter commonly originating in large vessels, particularly the inferior vena cava and its tributaries {22517390; 23354568; 32891345}. Most tumours are deep, arising from small to medium-sized vessels; however, a minority of tumours may have their origins from other smooth muscle tissue sources (e.g. external genitalia) {11756765; 1879812}. A venous origin is approximately five times more frequent than arterial {4687797}.

Visceral LMS can arise in smooth muscle–rich organs (e.g. uterus [myometrium], gastrointestinal tract [muscularis], dermis [arrector pili]) and, given its distinct biology, should be considered separately {20167595; 11756765}.

Clinical features

Presentation is largely predicated on the site of origin. Patients most commonly present with local mass effect and/or pain. Tumours arising from the inferior vena cava may present with Budd–Chiari syndrome, leg oedema, and/or deep vein thrombosis {24093170}.

Epidemiology

LMS accounts for approximately 10% of soft tissue sarcomas {17013893; 32138705; 35796499}. The incidence increases with age; most cases occur in the fifth to seventh decades of life {35796499}. LMSs are exceptionally rare in children, and it is likely that many previously reported paediatric cases represent instead SRF-rearranged myoid neoplasms {39864664; 10403297}. Retroperitoneal tumours are significantly more common in women, whereas a slight reversal of this trend occurs in the extremities {1606532; 39328327}.

Etiology

Most cases are considered sporadic. A minority are associated with genetic predisposition syndromes (e.g. Li–Fraumeni syndrome, retinoblastoma, genomic instability syndromes) {5360287; 8200898; 39150540; 40291347} and/or environmental risk factors (e.g. radiation exposure, cyclophosphamide) {3978554; 35695212}.

Pathogenesis

LMS is a molecularly complex and heterogeneous tumour, lacking a consistent disease-defining genetic alteration. Karyotypic analyses typically demonstrate combined numerical and structural changes {3180008}. Comparative genomic hybridization frequently reveals widespread chromosomal gains (e.g. 1q, 16p, 17p, 20q), losses (e.g. 10q, 11q, 13q, 16q, 17p), and amplifications in DNA copy number, as well as chromothripsis and whole-genome duplication {9736047; 11232643; 11284036; 23329812; 23825676; 26541895; 29321523}.

Regions of frequent loss correspond to known tumour suppressor genes, including PTEN (10q23), RB1 (13q14), and TP53 (17p13) {20884963; 26541895}. Sequencing studies additionally reveal that biallelic inactivations in TP53 and RB1 are frequent and represent truncal alterations; these arise from SNVs, in addition to other mechanisms such as copy-number variations, chromosomal rearrangements, and indels {2216456; 8198970; 8644845; 26541895; 29321523}. These tumour suppressors are inactivated in > 90% of LMSs, underscoring their central role in tumour development {29321523}.

Various additional alterations contribute to LMS. Some pertain to pathways shared with TP53 and RB1, such as CDKN2A {8644845; 9269824; 14595762; 29321523; 33788262}. Other mechanisms include the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, such as frequent PTEN deletions and occasional PI3K mutations {17496901; 21837670; 28002802; 29100075}; genes maintaining telomeres, such as ATRX {25229770; 26692951; 29321523}; genetic signatures of homologous recombination (DNA repair) deficiency (HRD), such as ATM and BRCA2 alterations and HRD mutation signatures {29321523; 34301934; 37773632}; cellular differentiation, such as MYOCD amplification {19276386; 36672483}; tumour suppression, such as MAP2K4 {19901961; 31164371}; epigenetic regulation {29100075; 29321523}; and many others.

Gene expression profiling can distinguish LMS from other types of soft tissue sarcoma {11965276; 16230383; 17359542}; moreover, it has contributed to the identification of at least three molecular subtypes of LMS. While not purely multiclass, possibly owing to differences in study design, these include: type I, which is enriched for genes encoding proteins involved in smooth muscle differentiation, and which may be associated with a better prognosis {16230383; 17359542; 19901961; 21412072; 23329812; 25896974; 27607470; 29100075; 29321523; 32518213}; type II, which appears associated with less differentiated, potentially more immunogenic, tumours, and which has been associated with a less favourable outcome {11965276; 12592363; 19290004; 21125665; 29100075; 32518213; 34301934; 37386008}; and type III, which is enriched for visceral tumours, especially uterine LMS {25896974; 29100075; 32518213; 40100098}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours range from < 10 mm to > 200 mm. They may be well circumscribed and/or infiltrative {11756765; 10403297}. Cut surfaces are typically firm, fleshy, whorled, and white-tan-grey {14486179; 10403297; 11756765}. Necrosis, cystic degeneration, and/or intralesional haemorrhage may be present, particularly in larger tumours.

Histopathology

LMS is generally composed of intersecting fascicles of spindle cells; focal storiform and microcystic patterns are occasionally observed, and (rarely) the cells may be epithelioid {14486179; 7272926; 10403297; 11756765}. The cytoplasm is typically eosinophilic, sometimes fibrillar, and occasionally contains a perinuclear vacuole {7272926; 11756765}. The nuclei are ovoid and elongated with blunt ends, small nucleoli, and variable pleomorphism. In most LMSs, mitotic activity is conspicuous with atypical figures, but any mitoses in a smooth muscle tumour in an adult patient portends a risk of malignancy {13561254; 7880253; 14486179; 7272926; 11756765}. Osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells are rarely interspersed {8116793; 11756765}. Occasionally, tumours contain focal stroma that may be myxoid, fibrous, or hyalinized; dystrophic calcification is rare {7272926; 10403297; 11756765}. Necrosis may be identified {7272926; 10403297; 11756765}. Tumours occasionally contain a brisk chronic inflammatory infiltrate {10403297}. Lymphovascular invasion may be present, while lymph node metastasis is uncommon.

While a spectrum of differentiation may be observed, a subset of poorly differentiated tumours may resemble an undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma; in such cases, an area of typical morphology, or a preceding history of LMS, is necessary before raising the possibility of LMS. Morphologically, the cells in poorly differentiated / dedifferentiated LMS may be spindled, polygonal, epithelioid, or rhabdoid; bizarre multinucleated tumour cells are common, and mitotic activity is conspicuous {20004935; 22175893}. Rarely, this may be associated with heterologous differentiation.

Tumours are typically positive for desmin, SMA, smooth muscle myosin, and h-caldesmon. None of these markers are entirely lineage specific – although h-caldesmon is the most specific – and must be interpreted in the appropriate context; nevertheless, the presence of immunoreactivity for multiple antigens may offer diagnostic reassurance {10208459; 11068549; 11759055}. Staining decreases in poorly differentiated tumours and may be altogether lost in areas of dedifferentiation {20004935; 22175893; 24889065}. Immunoreactivity for various other markers – including CD34, KIT (CD117), DOG1, keratins, and epithelial membrane antigen – may occur, representing a potential diagnostic pitfall, but is typically focal and/or weak {8924229; 10692172; 12610358; 18223323}.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows high cellularity with tissue fragments showing a fascicular arrangement of spindle cells with elongated, blunt-ended, and variably pleomorphic nuclei, and a variable number of pleomorphic large cells with binucleation or multinucleation and bizarre nuclei, in a variable magenta (Giemsa) matrix. Single dispersed similar cells are in the background. Cell block immunohistochemistry can confirm the diagnosis and exclude the differential diagnoses {12619091; 16900474}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a neoplasm with morphological and/or immunohistochemical evidence of smooth muscle differentiation, with variable nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic activity.

Staging

Tumours are staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]].

Prognosis and prediction

LMS is aggressive, with a significant risk of local recurrence, metastasis, and disease-related mortality.

Multivariate analysis has identified the following as associated with a significant risk of metastasis: patient age (> 60–64 years) {1606532; 11756765; 22517390}; anatomical location, with the retroperitoneum being worse {22517390}; tumour size, > 4–5 cm {11346874; 17154171}; bone or neurovascular involvement {11346874}; prior disruptive surgical intervention {11756765}; mitotic activity {22517390}; French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) grade {11346874; 11756765; 17154171; 39155701}; and intratumoural vascular invasion {1606532}.

Multivariate analysis has identified the following as associated with disease-specific survival: patient age {31119101}; metastases on presentation {22517390}; American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage {11943020}; tumour size > 50 mm {11943020; 31119101}; FNCLCC grade {17154171; 22517390; 28116485; 31119101; 39155701}; and presence of RB1 and ATRX alterations {38488807}.
