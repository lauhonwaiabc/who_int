2.1.12 Secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1

Definition

Secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1 (ACT/CS1) is a locally aggressive, hyaline cartilage–producing neoplasm that arises within the cartilaginous cap of a pre-existing osteochondroma. Tumours located in the appendicular skeleton are referred to as peripheral ACTs, while those arising in the axial skeleton (including the pelvis and scapula) are referred to as CS1s.

ICD-O coding

9222/1 Atypical cartilaginous tumour

9222/3 Chondrosarcoma, grade 1

ICD-11 coding

2B50.Z & XH0FY0 Chondrosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Atypical cartilaginous tumour/chondrosarcoma, grade 1

Related terminology

Acceptable: low-grade peripheral chondrosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None.

Localization

In the axial skeleton (peripheral CS1), the most commonly affected bones include the ilium (19%), followed by the scapula (15%), pubic bone (10%), and ribs (10%). In the appendicular skeleton (peripheral ACT), the tibia (12%) and femur (11%) are the most frequently involved sites.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with a longstanding mass that has recently increased in size or become painful {12782876}. Tumour growth after puberty or the onset of pain should raise suspicion for malignant progression.

Imaging

The imaging features of secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 largely overlap with those of osteochondroma. MRI is the best-validated method to detect ACT/CS1 arising in an osteochondroma {20392983}. In adults, a cartilage cap measuring > 2 to 3 cm and marked inhomogeneity are suggestive of malignant transformation, with cap thickness cut-off values of 2–3 cm achieving sensitivities of 96–100% and specificities of 98–100% {20392983; 38901841}. In addition, pelvic location is associated with higher rates of malignant transformation {20392983}. Secondary peripheral ACT/CS1s show similar imaging features as chondrosarcomas grade 2 or higher, although the latter have more often a diffuse pattern of cap thickening (> 75% of the circumference) and a larger cap (median thickness of 39 mm in ACT/CS1 vs 56 mm in grade 2 or higher) {34714386}.

Epidemiology

Progression to secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 has been predominantly described in patients aged 20–40 years, accounting for approximately 75.2% of cases. The pelvis and proximal femur are the most commonly affected sites, representing 56.2% of reported cases {30591865}. Thus, patients with secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma are 1–2 decades younger than those with primary central chondrosarcoma {12782876}. Following malignant progression of osteochondroma, slightly more than 50% of cases are ACT/CS1 {12782876; 35193636; 31453886; 38901841; 38351015}. In case series reported, the median age for sporadic and multiple osteochondromas–related peripheral chondrosarcoma is around 38 and 34 years, respectively, and men seem to be affected more commonly than women {35193636; 38351015}.

Etiology

Patients with multiple osteochondromas, carrying germline mutations in EXT1 or EXT2, are at increased risk of developing ACT/CS1 within the cartilaginous cap of an osteochondroma (see section Multiple osteochondromas (EXT1, EXT2)). The risk is estimated to be as high as 5% for patients with multiple osteochondromas, compared with about 1% for those with solitary lesions {7702095; 9272707; 8027127; 30591865}.

Pathogenesis

Unlike in osteochondromas (the precursors of secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma) in which EXT1 or EXT2 is biallelically inactivated in at least a subset of tumour cells {10441575; 20080592; 11169766; 20813973; 20418910; 17341731}, the cartilaginous cap of secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma becomes progressively populated by cells retaining at least one functional copy of EXT1 or EXT2 upon malignant transformation. In secondary peripheral ACT/CS1, approximately 40% of EXT alleles are mutated, indicating a coexistence of EXT-mutant and EXT-wildtype cells within the tumour {25024164}. This suggests that wildtype cells within osteochondromas are susceptible to malignant progression and that additional (genetic) alterations beyond EXT1 or EXT2 inactivation contribute to tumorigenesis. These probably include mutations in cell cycle regulatory genes such as CDKN2A {25644707}.

Macroscopic appearance

Secondary peripheral ACT/CS1s are characterized by a thick (> 2 cm), lobulated cartilaginous cap with a mean thickness of 3.9 cm {12782876}. Careful macroscopic documentation of the thickness of the cartilaginous cap is crucial, and the cap should be measured perpendicular to the bone–cartilage interface, at its thickest portion, as the thickness is one of the most important determinants for progression in an osteochondroma {22555180}.

Histopathology

There are no universally accepted histological criteria that indicate progression from osteochondroma to secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 {22555180}. Coarse and irregular calcifications are usually prominent, and evidence of a pre-existing osteochondroma can often be seen. A lobular pattern is common, and sometimes nodules are separated from the main tumour mass in the surrounding soft tissue {12782876}. Binucleated cells, cystic changes (i.e. mucoid-filled cystic spaces), and necrosis can be seen within the cartilaginous cap and should not be interpreted as evidence of progression, because these features can also be seen in benign osteochondromas {22555180}. Increased vascularization can also be seen {22116208}. Correlation between clinical and radiological features combined with the thickness of the cartilaginous cap is crucial to establish a diagnosis of secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 {22555180}. Mitoses and nuclear pleomorphism are absent. Invasion in the stalk is rare but diagnostic of progression.

Differential diagnosis

There is no role for biopsy in distinguishing secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 from osteochondroma or from secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma grade 2/3 {38901841}. The distinction between osteochondroma and secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 solely on the basis of morphology is not possible in the absence of an infiltration into the stalk. It has been shown that binucleation, cystic degeneration, nodularity, or necrosis does not indicate progression {22555180}. The distinction should be made in a multidisciplinary setting in which the thickness of the cartilaginous cap is the most important parameter. In grade 2 and 3 secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma, mitoses and nuclear pleomorphism are seen, both of which are absent in secondary peripheral ACT/CS1.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis is not needed for diagnosis. Variants in IDH1 and IDH2 are absent {21598255; 22057234}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: surface bone tumour with compatible imaging; presence of a pre-existing osteochondroma; thickness of the cartilaginous cap exceeding 2 cm, measured perpendicular to the bone–cartilage interface; absence of nuclear pleomorphism and mitoses.

Staging

Local staging is only indicated for CS1.

Prognosis and prediction

The clinical course can be variable and is dependent on localization and operability. The 5-year and 10-year local recurrence rates for secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma are 15.9% and 17.5%, respectively, and the 5-year and 10-year mortality rates are 1–3% and 4–5% {12782876; 35193636}. Local recurrences can occur and are usually related to incomplete excision {35193636}. Increased histological grade in the recurrence specimen is seen in approximately 10% of sporadic secondary peripheral chondrosarcomas {35193636}. In the pelvis, peripheral CS1 can become very large and thereby difficult to completely excise, eventually becoming inoperable and fatal in some patients {18378543; 12782876}.
