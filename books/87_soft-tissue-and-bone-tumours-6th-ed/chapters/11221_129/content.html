1.12.21 Alveolar soft part sarcoma

Definition

Alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) is a malignant neoplasm of uncertain lineage predominantly affecting the deep soft tissues of the extremities, featuring variably dyscohesive eosinophilic polygonal cells arranged in nests, resulting in a distinct alveolar appearance, and characterized by ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion.

Localization

In adults, ASPS most commonly arises within the deep soft tissue of the thigh or buttock. Tumours are usually large, most often in the range of 50 to 100 mm (median: 65 mm) {26804150; 11169942}. Presentations within visceral organs (such as the stomach, female genital tract, lung/mediastinum, or bladder) are infrequent {2006720; 8598329; 2479947}. In children, ASPS has a predilection for the head and neck region, particularly the orbit and tongue {29350467; 16410131; 2759779}.

Clinical features

Tumours usually present as slowly growing, painless masses. Half of the patients present with distant metastases {26804150; 12837977}, mostly in the lung, bone, and brain. Lymph node metastases are distinctly uncommon. Lung metastases typically form multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules. Radiological studies often demonstrate signs of rich vascularization, which can be a clue to the diagnosis {37987424; 30560359; 26231949; 20593242}. Although ASPSs are isodense or hypodense on CT, they enhance with contrast and frequently demonstrate intratumoural/peritumoural feeder vessels. High vascularity is also apparent on MRI where flow voids are seen, which may lead to confusion with a benign vascular neoplasm or malformation {30560359}.

Epidemiology

ASPS are rare tumours, comprising < 1% of all sarcomas. The tumour most commonly occurs in young adults aged between 15 and 35 years. In the SEER Program cohort, ages ranged from 1 to 78 years, with 72% of patients being under the age of 30 years {26804150}. There is a slight female predominance described in reported cases, though the predominance is less marked in patients aged > 30 years and in children {8531857; 15500657; 11169942; 12837977}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

ASPS is characterized by a specific unbalanced translocation, der(17)t(X;17)(p11.2;q25), which results in the fusion of ASPSCR1 (formerly ASPL) to the transcription factor gene TFE3 {11244503}. The translocation fuses the N-terminal portion of ASPSCR1 with either the third or fourth exon of TFE3, resulting in one of two respective fusion variants, type 1 or type 2. Both fusion proteins retain the TFE3 basic helix-loop-helix DNA-binding domain, leucine zipper dimerization domain, and C-terminal activation domain. They each localize to the nucleus and act as aberrant transcription factors. The type 1 fusion retains the N-terminal TFE3 activation domain, but the type 2 fusion does not. Although the two fusion variants may have different transcriptional activities, they do not convey different prognoses. The translocation is thought to occur in the G2 phase of the cell cycle, with potential selective advantage of ASPS cells for gain of Xp11.2, which may contribute to the slight female predominance {15952162; 15693033}. Occasional ASPS cases harbour variant fusions including HNRNPH3::TFE3, DVL2::TFE3, and PRCC::TFE3 {31433528}.

Macroscopic appearance

ASPSs are usually well-circumscribed but unencapsulated. The neoplasm is most frequently pale grey to yellow. Soft fleshy nodules may be separated by fibrous bands.

Histopathology

Tumours are composed of uniform nests of polygonal cells separated by thin fibrovascular septa. The nests may be solid but usually demonstrate areas of discohesion centrally, giving rise to the characteristic pseudoalveolar pattern. The septal vessels are usually thin and sinusoidal. The neoplastic cells are uniform, large, and epithelioid, having round nuclei with vesicular chromatin and one or two prominent nucleoli, and abundant well-delineated granular eosinophilic cytoplasm. Prominent cytoplasmic glycogen can create a clear cell appearance, and rarely tumour cells may have a xanthomatous appearance that mimics histiocytes {15735860}. Mitotic activity is typically low, and geographical necrosis is uncommon except in larger lesions. Most tumours demonstrate vascular invasion at their periphery {2438029}. Tumours of the head and neck area in children tend to be composed of smaller cells in tighter nests, leading to confusion with granular cell tumour and paraganglioma.

Approximately 80% of ASPS contain characteristic PAS-positive, diastase-resistant crystalline inclusions. These may be extremely prominent but more often are very scant. The crystals are composed of the monocarboxylate transporter protein MCT1 and its cellular chaperone CD147 {11943706}. Mitochondria are often numerous, contributing to the granular eosinophilic cytoplasm.

By immunohistochemistry, tumour cells consistently demonstrate nuclear labelling using an antibody for the C-terminal portion of TFE3 that is retained in the fusion protein. Nuclear labelling for TFE3 reflects overexpression of the fusion protein relative to the low levels of native TFE3 protein, which is ubiquitously expressed {12766578; 22173238}. The downstream markers cathepsin K and GPNMB are consistently expressed {21602817; 23355199; 38219952}. ASPSs are negative for epithelial, neuroendocrine, and melanocytic markers. Approximately 50% of tumours will demonstrate focal immunoreactivity for desmin; however, myogenin and MYOD1 expression are negative.

Cytology

Cytology aspirates show high cellularity with large polygonal cells with round nuclei, vesicular chromatin, and prominent nucleoli; and abundant, often well-defined, granular to finely vacuolated concentric or eccentric cytoplasm, seen mainly singly or in cohesive alveolar tissue fragments {19787801; 1879268; 32853443}. Binucleated or multinucleated giant cells and intracytoplasmic rod-like crystals are present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of the ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion can be helpful for diagnosis in select cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: eosinophilic polygonal cells with nested/alveolar pattern, prominent sinusoidal capillaries.

Desirable: immunopositivity for TFE3; demonstration of ASPSCR1::TFE3 fusion in select cases.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) or American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system can be used.

Prognosis and prediction

Adverse prognostic factors include presence of distant metastasis at diagnosis, older age, tumour size of > 10 cm, and truncal location {26804150}. ASPS in children, typically presenting with smaller masses in the head and neck, are associated with a better prognosis. Overall survival is 82% at 2 years and 56% at 5 years, with long-term follow-up showing survival rates progressively declining to 38% at 10 years and 15% at 20 years {5953380}. Surgical excision of metastases has been shown to improve survival, as ASPS is typically slow growing and resistant to conventional chemotherapy {5953380}. Novel therapeutics have shifted treatment paradigms and improved outcomes {36692840; 30347044}, with a recent trial showing sustained responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as the anti-PDL1 agent (atezolizumab), in one third of patients with advanced ASPS {37672694}.
