1.12.12 Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour

Definition

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour (PMT) is a uniform spindle cell neoplasm with matrix deposition, grungy calcification, and a rich vascular network, usually with FN1 gene rearrangements, causing tumour-induced osteomalacia (TIO) in most affected patients, usually through production of FGF23.

Malignant PMT shows frankly sarcomatous morphology.

Related terminology

Acceptable: phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, mixed connective tissue type.

Subtype(s)

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, malignant

Localization

PMTs may occur in virtually any soft tissue or osseous location, most commonly in the extremities and the head and neck region {14707860;19609206; 27443518; 30206408; 31301876}. PMTs are extremely rare in the retroperitoneum, viscera, and mediastinum {10846577; 19125258}. Multifocal PMTs are extremely rare {31301876}.

Clinical features

PMTs typically present with bone pain and muscle weakness with hypophosphataemia, elevated serum levels of alkaline phosphatase and FGF23, and inappropriately normal or low 1,25D3. PMTs are responsible for almost all cases of mesenchymal tumour–associated TIO, despite often being reported with other diagnoses {14707860; 31301876}. The mean/median duration from the onset of symptoms to the clinical diagnosis of TIO was about 3 years {28450684; 35857061}. Non-phosphaturic PMTs (tumours showing morphological features of PMTs without TIO) in most instances may represent small, superficial PMTs identified before the onset of TIO {19609206}.

Most PMTs harbour small, inapparent, and isolated lesions that may require very careful clinical examination and nuclear imaging (preferably 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT) for localization {23295468; 27533306; 30206408}.

Epidemiology

PMTs are exceptionally rare, probably accounting for < 0.01% of all soft tissue tumours {19609206; 14707860; 3545439; 27443518; 28614212}. They have been most reported in middle-aged adults of either sex (with a slightly male predominance in reported cases), but they can also occur in paediatric or elderly patients {28450684; 30206408; 35857061}. A more significant male predominance (M:F ratio: 2.67:1) has been reported in PMTs with admixed epithelial elements in the jaws {30206408}.

Etiology

The etiology of PMT is unknown. In a unique case, a germline chromosomal rearrangement causing α-Klotho upregulation may have predisposed the patient to later develop multiple PMTs {27443518; 18308935}.

Pathogenesis

Nearly half of all PMTs contain either FN1::FGFR1 or, less commonly, FN1::FGF1 fusion {27443518; 25319834}. Other fusions (FN1::FGFR2, NIPBL::BEND2, KIAA1549::BRAF, et al.) are rarely identified in a small subset of FN1::FGFR1 fusion-negative cases {37391169}. A considerable proportion of FN1::FGFR1 and FN1::FGF1 fusion-negative PMTs have genomic rearrangements upstream of KL gene and overexpress α-klotho. α-Klotho is a transmembrane enzyme and acts as an obligatory coreceptor for FGF23-FGFR1 binding, resulting in an FGF23-FGFR1 autocrine loop that drives tumorigenesis through activated FGFR pathways {37742927; 31792355; 29342138}. Receptor tyrosine kinase FGFR1 signalling is upregulated, and FGFR1 expression is common in PMTs regardless of the fusion status {37742927; 26464698; 25319834}. These findings suggest that different driver molecular events could converge in the activation of FGFR1 signalling, leading to PMT tumorigenesis and upregulation of FGF23. Excess production of FGF23, a phosphaturic hormone that acts to inhibit renal proximal tubule phosphate reuptake, is responsible for TIO in most instances {11344269; 11409890; 12187320}. However, low-level expression of FGF23 can also occasionally be identified in non-PMT tumours, including occasional cases of fibrous dysplasia {12952917}, aneurysmal bone cyst, and chondromyxoid fibroma of bone {22933546; 38303543}. Rare cases of PMT with known TIO are FGF23-negative, presumably reflecting production of other phosphaturic hormones.

Macroscopic appearance

Most PMTs present as nonspecific, isolated soft tissue or bone masses and may contain calcified or haemorrhagic areas {31301876}.

Histopathology

PMTs of soft tissue tend to focally infiltrate surrounding tissues. PMTs are usually hypocellular to moderately cellular and composed of bland, spindle to stellate cells with a rich vasculature. A very well developed capillary network is typically present, with some cases also showing larger vessels arranged in a staghorn pericytoma-like pattern, or in a pattern resembling cavernous haemangioma. The matrix of PMT typically calcifies in a characteristic grungy (or flocculent) fashion, sometimes forming flower-like slate-grey crystals, and in some instances, it may contain foci closely resembling primitive cartilage or osteoid {19609206; 14707860; 3545439; 31301876}. Osteoclast-like giant cells (usually in areas of haemorrhage), fibrohistiocytic spindled cells, mature adipose tissue, microcystic change, and a peripheral shell of woven bone may be present. Mitotic activity and necrosis are usually absent. PMTs with KL upstream rearrangement / α-Klotho overexpression tend to show a predilection for skeletal/sinonasal locations and cellular solitary fibrous tumour–like morphology {37742927}.

A small number of PMTs are histologically malignant, show frankly sarcomatous features (high cellularity, high nuclear grade, marked nuclear atypia, elevated mitotic activity, and necrosis), and resemble fibrosarcoma or undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma. Malignant PMT typically develops in lesions that have recurred locally, often more than once {31301876; 14707860; 11310207; 18329207; 30206408}.

Immunohistochemistry plays a limited role in diagnosis. Expression of FGF23 protein has been documented in PMTs, although commercially available antibodies to FGF23 have questionable specificity {14707860; 19609206}. Chromogenic in situ hybridization for FGF23 is the preferred method for demonstrating expression.

Cytology

Aspirates show bland spindle cells with finely granular chromatin, indistinct nucleoli, and scant delicate cytoplasm; scattered osteoclasts associated with stromal matrix can also be found {18181193; 22927293}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of a fusion gene (especially FN1::FGFR1 or FN1::FGF1) is not required for routine diagnosis, but it can be of value in the diagnosis of morphologically ambiguous or non-phosphaturic PMT {29514106}. Chromogenic in situ hybridization is the preferred method for confirming FGF23 mRNA expression {31301876}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a bland spindle cell tumour with matrix deposition, grungy calcification, and/or a rich vascular network; clinical evidence of hypophosphataemia and/or osteomalacia; malignant examples show frankly sarcomatous morphology.

Desirable: FGF23 overproduction in the serum or tumour (in the absence of TIO).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The overwhelming majority of PMTs are histologically and clinically benign, with complete excision resulting in the resolution of phosphate wasting and osteomalacia {14707860; 12187320; 19841566; 12054166}. Local recurrence is uncommon, and malignant transformation is rare. Malignant tumours may metastasize and cause death.
