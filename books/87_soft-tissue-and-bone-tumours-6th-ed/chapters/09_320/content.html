Index > Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours (6th ed.)

0.9 Foreword

Each edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours (also known as the WHO Blue Books) has incorporated sequential advances in knowledge, transitioning from little more than a simple list of diagnostic terms to a comprehensive, evidence-based, hierarchical tumour classification, incorporating multiple tumour characteristics and providing International Classification of Diseases codes (ICD-O, ICD-11). This sixth edition builds upon the strong foundation established in previous editions, especially the fifth. The content from the fifth edition has been reviewed and updated or rewritten as necessary, on the basis of new research evidence and feedback from end-user surveys. We have maintained the classification structure (site, category, family, type, and subtype) and the process (led by the WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board) introduced in the fifth edition, while embarking on several important new initiatives.

With the aim of enhancing the quality and worldwide applicability of the classification, six subcommittees have been formed to guide the editorial board in specific areas identified as requiring refinement. We consider this input especially relevant in the current era of rapid advancement in molecular genetics and computational pathology, which has raised justifiable concerns about how such developments may impact regions or settings lacking these facilities. In addition, the editorial board now includes a special representative from a resource-challenged setting, as well as representatives from the fields of molecular genetics, radiology, and oncology, each contributing their perspective from their setting or specialty. Coauthors from high-incidence countries for important cancer types have also been included wherever possible, bringing their practical experience at the bench to the teams of authors. Thanks to their input, we have adopted a more layered approach to defining the essential and desirable diagnostic criteria for many tumours in this edition, balancing scientific advances with global relevance. The threshold for defining a new tumour on the basis of published evidence has now been accepted as a minimum of two case series with at least 15 cases each, from two independent research groups. The definitions of a tumour subtype and a pattern have also been refined, with a subtype defined as “a tumour that is recognized as being different from the main tumour type in at least one dimension (clinical, histological, or genetic), preferably with a different treatment or outcome” and a pattern as “a tumour that differs from the main tumour type in morphological aspects only, but not in its underlying biological or molecular features, treatment, or outcome”.

All the research evidence referenced in the fifth edition has been systematically captured and synthesized into citation maps, now accessible via the open-access WHO Classification of Tumours (WCT) Evidence Explorer (https://wct-evidence-explorer.iarc.who.int/). These citation maps enable expert editors and authors to identify areas where the previously cited evidence was absent and/or of low level, prompting them to modify the content and bridge these evidence gaps in the sixth edition.

New images have been provided wherever possible, with at least one gold-standard image for each tumour type, while figures from the fifth edition remain available on WHO Classification of Tumours Online (https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int/). The website also now hosts whole slide images for almost all tumours, with deep zoom functionality enabling users to explore these images as if viewing slides under a microscope.

ICD-O-4 codes have been developed for this edition (an important update from the ICD-O-3.2 codes used in the fifth edition), in response to the need for a more granular coding system capable of accommodating all separate tumour types and subtypes. The update uses five-digit morphology codes (versus four-digit codes in ICD-O-3.2), with alphanumeric values that provide more leverage to accommodate all tumour types and subtypes.

Continuing our efforts to improve consistency and standards within the WHO Blue Books, cytogenetic descriptions follow the guidelines in ISCN 2024 [[Hastings RJ, Moore S, Chia N, editors. ISCN 2024: An International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (2024). Basel (Switzerland): Karger; 2024.]], nucleic acid and protein sequence variants are annotated using the MANE Select v1.4 transcript set {35388217}, and chromosome bands and copy-number coordinates are based on GRCh38/hg38 in the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) {39460617}, unless stated otherwise. Like in the previous edition, where possible, mitotic counts have been converted from the traditional denominator of 10 HPF to a defined area expressed in mm2 {34079071}.

Another improvement in this edition is the updated approach to referencing. The substantial reference lists for these books are now published online only, allowing our readers to retrieve published literature more efficiently, in alignment with recent ICMJE recommendations  [[International Committee of Medical Journal Editors [Internet]. International Committee of Medical Journal Editors; 2026. Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals. Updated 2026 Jan [cited 2026 Jun 1]. Available from: http://www.ICMJE.org.]]. The references for a given section in the volume, with direct links to PubMed abstracts, are accessible via a QR code in each section. A full reference list for the volume is also available to download from https://publications.iarc.who.int/670.

A point of discussion for this edition has been whether to use the term “germline” or “constitutional” to describe pathogenic sequence variants that occur in every cell of the body (i.e. not somatic). A constitutional pathogenic variant is defined as genetic variant present in all cells of the body (including germ cells), whereas a germline pathogenic variant technically is a genetic variant occurring only in germ cells (i.e. known familial transmission). However, as these terms are still often used interchangeably, we have chosen to include the clarification “germline (constitutional) pathogenic variant” at the beginning of each applicable section and use “germline pathogenic variant” thereafter.

Like in all IARC publications and other WHO information products, English usage in the WHO Blue Books follows WHO house style. This means that spellings typically follow UK conventions (e.g. ageing, anaemia, artefact, centre, gynaecology, haematology, oedema, oesophagus, programme but computer program, tumour), with some exceptions (e.g. estrogen, etiology, fetus, leukocyte). The general rule is to follow the first spelling listed in the latest edition of the Oxford English Dictionary (e.g. organization and categorize rather than organisation and categorise); however, in some cases, a different spelling has become established usage within IARC/WHO, and the recommendations of international nomenclature-setting bodies take precedence where applicable.

This sixth-edition volume, compiled by a panel of international experts from across the globe, provides a tumour classification based on the best evidence currently available. This volume is intended to support pathologists and non-pathologists alike, not only in routine clinical work but also in conducting research and collecting cancer registry data impacting epidemiological information for the prevention and early detection of cancer.

We have continued to prioritize the needs of WHO Blue Books users worldwide while incorporating new knowledge. In this context, tests using immunohistochemical and molecular markers are listed within desirable diagnostic criteria for most tumours; however, in settings with facilities for these tests, it is prudent to perform the tests to establish or confirm the diagnosis. We also recommend that, to the extent possible, pathologists in all settings embark on acquiring these facilities to empower themselves, as keeping abreast of the latest scientific advances is imperative for the benefit of our patients.
