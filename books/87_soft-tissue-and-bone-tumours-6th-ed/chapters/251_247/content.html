2.5.1 Aneurysmal bone cyst

Definition

Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) is a benign expansile neoplasm of bone characterized by a variable mixture of multiloculated blood-filled cystic spaces and solid areas.

ICD-O coding

9260/0 Aneurysmal bone cyst

ICD-11 coding

FB80.6 & XH23E0 Aneurysmal bone cyst & Aneurysmal bone cyst

Related terminology

Not recommended: giant cell reparative granuloma of small bone; giant cell lesion of small bones.

Subtype(s)

None.

Localization

ABC can affect any bone, but most often arises in the metaphysis of long bones, especially the femur, tibia, and humerus (50%); the posterior elements of vertebral bodies (25%) and pelvis (10%); as well as the small tubular bones. Rare examples occur in the soft tissues {1626294; 11756770; 30603771}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

The most common signs and symptoms are pain and swelling, which are rarely due to fracture. In the vertebrae, ABC can compress nerves or the spinal cord and cause neurological symptoms.

Imaging

On radiographs and CT, ABC presents as a lytic, expansile lesion with well-defined margins. Most tumours contain a thin shell of subperiosteal reactive bone. On MRI, characteristic fluid-fluid levels created by the different signal intensities of the cyst contents are present. Signal intensity can be variable on T1- and T2-weighted images, depending on the age of the blood products. Contrast administration can show septal enhancement and can be helpful in detecting solid components, which suggest ABC-like changes {7863874; 1591685}. On bone scan, ABCs show increased peripheral radiotracer uptake, with a central photopenic area.

Epidemiology

An annual prevalence of 0.32 per 100 000 individuals (range: 0–1.238) has been reported for ABCs, with an M:F ratio of 1.8:1 among detected cases and a median age of 11.1 years (range: 1–19.7 years) {10379320}. ABC affects individuals of all age groups but is most commonly seen during the first two decades of life (80%). The estimated annual incidence is 0.15 cases per 1 million population {10379320; 36950810}.

Etiology

ABC is considered a clonal neoplastic process of uncertain etiology.

Pathogenesis

ABC contains cytogenetic rearrangements of the USP6 gene, which is located at chromosome band 17p13.2. The most common translocation, t(16;17)(q22;p13) {10825009; 15026324; 10502326; 1777412; 11106078}, results in the fusion of CDH11 to USP6, leading to upregulation of USP6 transcription through a promoter-swapping mechanism {15026324; 15735689}. No fusion protein is produced, and the entire USP6 protein sequence is preserved. Cellular and animal models of ABC and nodular fasciitis have demonstrated that overexpression of USP6 in putative cells of origin is sufficient to reproduce the molecular and histological features of these tumours, while also revealing key roles for NF-κB, JAK1/STAT3, and WNT/β-catenin signalling in tumorigenesis {27440725; 27162353}.

USP6 fusion genes are found in approximately 70% of primary ABCs but are absent in ABC-like changes that occur in association with other tumours {15509545}. The most frequent USP6 fusion partner is CDH11, but several others have been and continue to be identified {15735689; 28449806; 30553465; 27910166; 30767316; 36950810; 32011035; 34369017; 35717456; 36205240; 32859633; 32635781; 36727055; 38429095; 33608306; 33558945; 37328256; 32157177; 33000481; 33147323; 34089379; 35175167; 32583473}.

The clonal neoplastic component of ABC harbouring USP6 fusion genes consists of a bland spindle cell population that is morphologically indistinguishable from normal fibroblasts and myofibroblasts {15509545}. Other cells commonly seen in ABC, such as chronic inflammatory cells, endothelial cells, metaplastic bone-associated osteoblasts, and multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells, do not harbour a USP6 rearrangement and are reactive in nature {15509545}.

USP6 gene rearrangements are found in both cystic and solid ABCs of the small bones of the hands and feet, but not in the so-called giant cell reparative granulomas of gnathic locations {24742829}.

Other tumours also show USP6 fusion genes, including nodular fasciitis, myositis ossificans, fibro-osseous tumour of the digits, and a subset of fibromas of tendon sheath. These tumours are currently believed to represent a biological spectrum known as USP6-associated neoplasms {36950810; 36205240; 32635781; 33608306; 22202063; 23769422}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, ABC is well-defined, multiloculated, and composed of blood-filled, cystic spaces separated by tan-white, gritty septa (sponge-like). More solid areas can be present, representing a solid portion of the ABC. The peripheral soft tissue component may be surrounded by a thin shell of reactive bone formation.

Histopathology

ABC is well circumscribed and contains blood-filled cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa. These septa are composed of a moderately dense, cellular proliferation of bland fibroblasts, with scattered osteoclast-type multinucleated giant cells and reactive woven bone rimmed by osteoblasts. The woven bone often follows the contours of the fibrous septa. In approximately one third of cases, basophilic bone (heavily mineralized woven bone), referred to as blue bone, is observed, although this feature is not pathognomonic. Mitoses are commonly present and may be numerous; however, atypical mitotic figures are absent. Necrosis is rare and typically occurs only in the setting of a pathological fracture. The solid subtype of ABC contains the same histological components. Lesions previously classified as giant cell lesions of small bones exhibit identical morphology to the solid subtype of ABC {8431852; 1423194; 6850506}.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemically, the lack of expression of mutant histones H3.3 G34W (encoded by H3-3A) and K36M (encoded by H3-3B) could help distinguish ABCs from giant cell tumours of bone and chondroblastomas with ABC-like changes, respectively.

Differential diagnosis

ABC-like areas can be seen in other benign and malignant bone tumours that have undergone haemorrhagic cystic change. The term “secondary aneurysmal bone cyst” was previously used for such areas but is no longer acceptable. The majority of ABC-like changes develop in association with benign neoplasms, most commonly giant cell tumour of bone, osteoblastoma, chondroblastoma, and fibrous dysplasia {7863874; 2835141; 1591685}. However, ABC-like changes may also complicate sarcomas, especially osteosarcoma. The most important differential diagnosis is telangiectatic osteosarcoma because both may have similar imaging characteristics and gross appearance. In telangiectatic osteosarcoma, the fibrous septa contain obvious hyperchromatic, pleomorphic neoplastic cells, often displaying atypical mitotic figures, features not seen in ABC.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

USP6 locus rearrangement or USP6 fusion genes can be detected by molecular cytogenetic analysis (FISH) or next-generation sequencing, respectively.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; haemorrhagic cystic or solid lesion composed of fibroblasts, osteoclast-like giant cells, haemosiderin pigment, and new bone formation.

Desirable: USP6 rearrangement.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

ABC is a potentially locally recurrent neoplasm, with reported recurrence rates after curettage ranging from 20% to 70%. However, more recent studies have documented lower recurrence rates, averaging around 30% {37638388}. Spontaneous regression following incomplete removal has been reported, although this is uncommon. The presence and type of USP6 fusion genes have not been correlated with specific clinical or pathological features.
