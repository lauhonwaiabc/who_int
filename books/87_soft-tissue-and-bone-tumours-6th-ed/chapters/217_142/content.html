2.1.7 Chondroblastoma

Definition

Chondroblastoma is a benign neoplasm of bone that predominantly arises in epiphysial or apophysial regions. It is composed of mononuclear chondroblastic stromal cells, osteoclast-like giant cells, and areas of eosinophilic chondroid matrix. A very small subset is malignant.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Approximately 75% of chondroblastomas involve the epiphysial (subchondral) region of long bones, most commonly the femur, followed by the proximal tibia and proximal humerus {2793161; 4023729}. Other sites include the talus, calcaneus, patella, and pelvic bones (especially the acetabulum). Less frequently, the ribs, vertebrae, and small bones of the hands and feet are affected. Involvement of craniofacial bones is exceptional. The anatomical distribution in adults is more variable {36604179; 31429985}.

Malignant chondroblastoma tends to arise in older individuals and has a unique propensity for flat bones, with the majority of cases arising in the rib, scapula, and pelvis {32601382; 40348060}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

The clinical presentation varies depending on the location of the tumour. The most common symptom is pain. Local tenderness, joint effusion, impaired movement, and muscle atrophy may also be observed.

Imaging

Chondroblastoma typically presents as a well-defined, often sclerotically marginated, translucent lesion eccentrically located in a long bone adjacent to a growth plate, usually in the epiphysis or in the epiphysis and metaphysis, and rarely in the metaphysis or apophysis only {4023729}. This relationship with the physis, in combination with marked reactive changes (seen on MRI) present in > 90% of patients, typically allows a confident radiological diagnosis {39170650}. The poorly defined reactive inflammatory changes in bone, soft tissues, and synovium exhibit high signal intensity on fluid-sensitive and gadolinium chelate–enhanced images outside the well-defined tumour margin {39170650}. Periosteal reaction is common and can be seen at some distance, for instance in the diaphysis {4023729}. The tumour is inhomogeneous and typically has large areas of low MRI signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences due to mineralization and trabeculation, which are also well seen on CT {39170650; 6470260; 4023729}. Aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes can be seen as fluid-fluid levels on MRI in areas of marked expansion {19936740; 10524855}. Although diagnosis is normally straightforward, a chondroblastoma extending towards the subchondral bone may look like a giant cell tumour (no reactive changes) or clear cell chondrosarcoma (older age group and no reactive inflammatory changes). Chondroblastoma can occur in any bone, and even in soft tissue {39691756}. Periosteal location is rare {37733062}. Patients with chondroblastoma in atypical locations (20%) such as flat bones, hand, foot, and spine tend to be older (third to fifth decade of life) {4023729; 37344889; 39170650; 37075609}. Although rare (< 1%), metastases of benign chondroblastoma can be detected in lungs, or less frequently in bone, soft tissue, or liver {38927987; 32594198}. Malignancy should be suspected when there is marked destruction of cortical bone with soft tissue extension.

Epidemiology

Chondroblastoma accounts for < 1% of all bone tumours. The incidence rate of chondroblastoma fluctuates across populations and geographical areas. Most occur in skeletally immature patients in the second to early third decades of life (10–25 years of age), although it may present at any age {4023729; 28634924; 2793161}. There is a male predilection, with a reported M:F ratio of 1.56–3.48:1{31428555; 36604179}.

Malignant chondroblastoma is extremely rare and arises in older skeletally mature patients with a median age of 40 years {32601382; 40178617; 40348060}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Whole-genome sequencing of chondroblastomas reveals H3.3 K37M (K36M) as the only recurrent mutation; K37M (K36M) substitutions in chondroblastoma are more frequent in the H3-3B gene on chromosome 17 {24162739}. The K37M (K36M) alteration inhibits the histone methyltransferases NSD2 (MMSET) and SETD2, which results in reduced global H3.3 K36 methylation and aberrant H3.3 K27 methylation {27229140; 27474439; 27174990}. These epigenetic pathway alterations probably block mesenchymal differentiation and influence tumorigenesis in chondroblastoma {30098026; 27174990}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, chondroblastomas are typically well-defined eccentric lesions with a thin sclerotic rim. The cut surface varies from soft to rubbery with gritty and haemorrhagic areas. Chondroid matrix may be prominent. Blood-filled spaces may be seen in some cases with aneurysmal bone cyst change {5051664; 5445403}.

Histopathology

Histologically, chondroblastomas are composed of sheets of neoplastic mononuclear cells interspersed with osteoclast-like giant cells and areas of eosinophilic chondroid matrix. The mononuclear cells are ovoid to polygonal cells with small, singular, grooved nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm {14880556; 5051664}; their cytoplasmic borders are usually distinct. Epithelioid cells may be present. Basophilic hyaline cartilage is uncommon. Pericellular lace-like or chicken-wire calcification is characteristic and typically observed around degenerative cells. Nuclear atypia and/or mitotic figures may be found. Aneurysmal bone cyst–like change is common. Mitoses are found in most cases (0–4 mitoses/2 mm2), and no atypical mitoses are observed. Necrosis is present in some cases, which is often composed of ghost cells lacking an inflammatory background; necrosis is usually observed in cells with pericellular calcification.

Malignant chondroblastomas are exceedingly rare. The few reported cases demonstrate a permeative and sheet-like growth composed of discohesive chondroblastoma-like cells within a myxoid stroma, with variable but often inconspicuous osteoclast-type giant cells. These tumours often show cortical destruction, soft tissue infiltration, and lymphovascular invasion {32601382; 40348060}. Extremely rare cases of chondroblastoma and malignant chondroblastoma have shown transformation into high-grade sarcoma, such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma {36604179; 30532312; 8951497; 40178617; 40348060}.

Immunohistochemistry

Histone H3.3 K36M immunostaining shows nuclear expression of the mononuclear stromal cells and is helpful in small biopsies {26844533; 36604179}. Although not specific, SOX9, S100, cytokeratin, and DOG1 may be focally positive {19801163; 2579018; 1692005; 26402312}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes primary aneurysmal bone cyst, giant cell tumour of bone, clear cell chondrosarcoma, chondromyxoid fibroma, and an extremely rare subtype of osteosarcoma: chondroblastoma-like osteosarcoma {10192265}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Virtually all chondroblastomas harbour a K37M (K36M) substitution in one of the genes that encode H3-3: H3-3B (> 95%) on chromosome 17 or (less frequently) H3-3A on chromosome 1 {24162739; 26844533; 36604179}. Chondroblastoma and malignant chondroblastoma show unique methylation profiles. Copy-number variations appear specific but not sensitive to the malignant subtype {40348060}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; sheets of mononuclear stromal cells; islands of eosinophilic chondroid matrix and osteoclast-like giant cells.

Desirable: epiphysial/apophysial location; a fine network of pericellular chicken-wire calcification; detection of the K37M (K36M) variant in the H3-3A/H3-3B genes by immunohistochemistry/sequencing.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Chondroblastomas are benign tumours that are successfully treated in > 80% of patients with surgical curettage with or without adjuvant treatment of the surgical bed {30418383; 15972914}. Radiofrequency ablation may be used {19304917}. The recurrence rates vary with site of disease, being lower in long bones and higher in flat bones and craniofacial bones, ranging from 10% to 18% {30418383; 15972914; 3604981; 8253461}. There are no reliable histological parameters predicting local recurrence {8253461; 12913842; 28971570}. In exceptional cases, so-called benign pulmonary metastasis may develop {16007463}. The recurrence rates of malignant chondroblastoma reach 50%, with approximately one third of patients developing metastasis and rare patients dying of disease {32601382; 40178617; 40348060}. Extremely rare cases of chondroblastoma may undergo malignant transformation to high-grade sarcoma {36604179; 30532312; 8951497; 40178617; 40348060}.
