2.2.8 High-grade surface osteosarcoma

Definition

High-grade surface osteosarcoma is a high-grade malignant bone-forming neoplasm arising on the surface of the bone.

ICD-O coding

9194/3 High-grade surface osteosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH6TL0 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & High-grade surface osteosarcoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Tumours usually involve the long bones. The femur is the most common site, followed by the tibia. The humerus and fibula are less frequently involved {18300258;10091787; 32953434}. Other rarely affected sites include the radius, the short tubular bones, and the pelvis {17410355; 10091787}. In the long bones, high-grade surface osteosarcomas are most frequently diaphysial or diametaphysial.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Swelling and pain of short duration are usual.

Imaging

Radiographs often show a large, broad-based radiodense mass attached to the diaphysis of a long-bone cortex, extending into soft tissues with variable, often less, mineralization than in typical parosteal osteosarcoma {18300258}. High-grade surface osteosarcoma is frequently > 10 cm at presentation {32681279}. Periosteal reaction is less common and sparser than in periosteal osteosarcoma {11810163}, although some reports note similar aggressive radiating bone spicules {32681279; 39407770}. Purely radiolucent lesions are rare {7889716; 10091787}. Cortical erosion and medullary involvement, rare in periosteal osteosarcoma, occur in about 50% of cases and are best seen with cross-sectional imaging {18300258}. A radiolucent cleavage plane from the periosteum is unusual {11810163}. Circumferential involvement typically exceeds 50%, sometimes 100%, more than in other surface osteosarcomas {21071381}. MRI usually reveals a large, heterogeneous mass with low T1- and T2-weighted signal (depending on mineralization), cortical erosion, and medullary involvement. Marked heterogeneous enhancement postcontrast is typical, with necrotic areas showing no enhancement. Distinguishing high-grade surface osteosarcoma with extensive intramedullary components from conventional osteosarcoma with a large extraosseous mass can be difficult, but a bulk external to the bone suggests high-grade surface osteosarcoma {32681279}.

Epidemiology

High-grade surface osteosarcomas account for < 1% of osteosarcomas. The published studies describe an average age at diagnosis of 41 years (range: 10–90 years), and reported incidence shows a male predominance with a peak incidence in the second to third decade of life {10091787; 11810163}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenetic mechanisms of high-grade surface osteosarcoma are unknown but probably identical to those of conventional osteosarcoma. High-grade osteosarcomas arising on the surface of bone with MDM2 and CDK4 amplification more probably represent high-grade transformation of parosteal osteosarcoma.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumour is usually well circumscribed externally and situated on the fibrous periosteal surface. It often extends through the periosteum to attach to or even erode the underlying cortical surface. Endosteal extension may be present, but the bulk of the tumour is outside the bone. Its cut surface is variable because its appearance depends on the predominant type of extracellular matrix produced, as well as on the cellularity of the tumour stroma.

Histopathology

These tumours are histologically identical to high-grade conventional osteosarcoma, with the same degree of matrix variability. This includes cases with prominent chondroblastic differentiation, which show greater cytological atypia than periosteal osteosarcoma. No areas of low-grade osteosarcoma should be demonstrable {10091787}. The distinction from conventional high-grade intramedullary osteosarcoma with large soft tissue extension can be difficult, as the histological features are identical. If the bulk of the mass is centred outside the medullary space, a diagnosis of high-grade surface osteosarcoma should be considered; however, this distinction is unlikely to affect overall tumour management, and in rare cases the distinction is essentially arbitrary. Immunohistochemistry is not relevant to making the diagnosis.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; histologically high-grade osteosarcoma; arising on the surface of the bone.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]] does not recommend applying the TNM staging system for bone tumours to surface/juxtacortical osteosarcoma or juxtacortical chondrosarcoma. However, other staging systems, such as the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020.]], do include these tumours in the bone staging system. See the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

The overall 5-year survival rate of patients with high-grade surface osteosarcoma, compiled from the two studies reporting follow-up, is 62% {10091787; 18300258}. Factors that favour survival include good response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy {10091787} and localized disease. Poor response to chemotherapy, metastatic disease at the time of presentation, and local recurrence are adverse factors {10091787; 18300258; 32953434}. The presence of medullary involvement does not seem to be an independent prognostic factor {10091787; 32953434}.
