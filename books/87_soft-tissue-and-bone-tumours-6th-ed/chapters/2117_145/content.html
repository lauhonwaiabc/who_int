2.1.17 Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma

Definition

Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma (MCS) is an aggressive sarcoma composed of primitive round to ovoid cells admixed with a well-differentiated hyaline cartilage component and characterized by a distinctive HEY1::NCOA2 fusion.

ICD-O coding

9240/3 Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B50.Z & XH8X47 Chondrosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Mesenchymal chondrosarcoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Most tumours arise in bone, somatic soft tissue, or intracranial sites {14416919; 20123451; 29044531; 25529371; 3697943}. Intraosseous tumours frequently involve the craniofacial skeleton – particularly the jaws, which account for approximately 50% of craniofacial cases {9635527; 18038872} – as well as the ribs or chest wall, ilium, vertebrae, and lower extremities, with a predilection for the femur {14416919; 20123451; 29044531; 25529371; 3697943}. Within bone, MCS typically presents within the medullary cavity; surface-based tumours are also observed {28352960}. Approximately 50% of cases arise in somatic soft tissue {4694076; 3697943; 26975384; 32734374}, including primary intravascular locations {34264900; 25457141; 26133997; 12514602; 28917072}. Among extraskeletal sites, the meninges are particularly common {3697943; 20123451; 38762839; 24124145; 365318; 35672279}. Visceral involvement is rare but has been reported in the kidney, lung, and intra-abdominal organs {20138330; 22208489; 26975384; 25529371; 12078803; 37760551}.

Clinical features

The cardinal symptoms are pain, swelling, or both, particularly when the tumour involves or compresses adjacent neural structures {29044531; 3697943}. Approximately 15% of patients present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis {25529371}.

Imaging

Skeletal MCS typically appears as aggressive lytic and destructive masses, with poorly defined margins {12975513; 14416919}. However, a subset may exhibit well-defined borders with a sclerotic rim {14416919}. Osseous expansile remodelling is common, and cortical destruction with extraosseous soft tissue extension is frequently observed {14416919}. Additional features may include bone sclerosis, cortical thickening, and superficial bone surface involvement. In both intraosseous and extraskeletal MCS, radiography and CT often reveal chondroid-type calcifications. On MRI, tumours are usually isointense to muscle on T1-weighted sequences, while fluid-sensitive sequences demonstrate heterogeneous intermediate signal intensity, unlike the high signal intensity typically seen in conventional chondrosarcoma. Postcontrast MRI reveals avid diffuse heterogeneous enhancement in the solid non-necrotic areas, reflecting the tumour’s prominent vascularity, and is quite distinct from the peripheral and septal enhancement observed in conventional chondrosarcoma {12975513; 34264900; 32734374}.

Epidemiology

MCS is rare and accounts for 2–4% of all chondrosarcomas. The peak incidence is reported in published cases in the second and third decades of life, with a wide age range and a median age of 30 years {25529371}. There is a mild male predilection in reported cases (M:F ratio: 1.3:1) {25849226; 25529371; 26975384}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

MCS harbours a recurrent in-frame gene fusion between HEY1 (exon 4) and NCOA2 (exon 13) {22034177}. This fusion is detected in nearly all well-characterized MCS cases and is absent in other tumour types, establishing it as a defining molecular event {29582189}. HEY1 and NCOA2 are approximately 10 Mb apart on chromosome 8, mapping to 8q21.13 and 8q13.3, respectively, and share the same transcriptional orientation. As such, the fusion probably arises through a small interstitial deletion, del(8)(q13.3q21.1), which is typically undetectable by conventional cytogenetic methods. The HEY1::NCOA2 fusion is believed to drive tumorigenesis by promoting the proliferation of primitive, undifferentiated mesenchymal cells, a subset of which subsequently differentiates into well-formed hyaline cartilage. A single case with an alternative IRF2BP2::CDX1 fusion has been reported {23185413}. Secondary genetic alterations include gain of chromosome 12, loss of CDKN2A affecting the RB1 pathway, and loss of TP53 {22674453}. In contrast to conventional chondrosarcoma, variants in IDH1/IDH2 are absent {21598255; 22674453}. MCS has a distinct DNA methylation profile {29572501; 37225644}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours range from grey-white to tan-pink and vary from firm to soft. They typically present as circumscribed masses, measuring 9–300 mm. A biphasic appearance is apparent in most tumours, with often centrally located mineralized deposits that range from small to prominent. Either the cartilaginous component or the fleshy, haemorrhagic primitive component may predominate {20123451; 20138330; 6825046; 13883114}.

Histopathology

MCS is a biphasic malignancy. The primitive component is characterized by sheets of small, primitive round or ovoid cells with fine chromatin and a high N:C ratio. Spindle cell morphology may be seen, especially in intracranial locations, and may grow in vague fascicles. A hallmark of the primitive component is its elaborate vascular architecture, which includes prominent staghorn (haemangiopericytoma-like) vessels as well as delicate branching capillaries. The second component, interspersed among primitive cells, is varying amounts of well-differentiated hyaline cartilage, ranging from large areas to discrete minute foci. The well-organized hyaline cartilage may show central coarse calcification and endochondral ossification {20138330; 20123451}. In some areas, the matrix may mimic osteoid deposition. Tumour necrosis may occur.

Immunohistochemistry

The primitive round cell component is positive for SOX9 and CD99, and focal S100 expression may be seen {20138330; 8958297}. The cartilaginous component is positive for S100 and SOX9. Expression of NKX3-1 {31972596; 32779239; 33302222; 33443863; 33640159} and NKX2-2 {26847175; 22446943; 25031013} has been documented in most reports. Positive expression of SOX9 and NKX3-1 may be helpful when the fusion testing is not available {33302222; 33640159}. Aberrant expression of EMA, desmin, myogenin, and MYOD1 may be observed {29559236}. SMARCB1 (INI1) expression is retained {20123451}. PAX7, FLI1, SMA, GFAP, and keratins are typically negative {29920735; 20123451; 21084965}.

Differential diagnosis

When only the primitive component is biopsied, there may be confusion with other small round cell neoplasms. The round cells in MCS may demonstrate partly overlapping immunophenotypes with those of Ewing sarcoma or NFATC2-rearranged sarcomas.

Cytology

There is a biphasic appearance of medium-sized primitive cells with round to ovoid nuclei and fine chromatin, high N:C ratio, and basophilic extracellular cartilage matrix suspended by a delicate capillary vasculature stroma {12925982}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of HEY1::NCOA2 fusion can be helpful.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: primitive round to ovoid cells with fine chromatin; a high N:C ratio; islands of well-differentiated cartilage; elaborate vascular architecture.

Desirable: HEY1::NCOA2 fusion.

Staging

Staging is according to bone sarcoma protocols (see <>TNM staging of tumours of bone). See also the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

MCS is an aggressive sarcoma, with estimated overall 5-year and 10-year survival rates as high as 60% and 40%, respectively, although these figures vary among studies. Distant metastases to soft tissue, bone, lung, pancreas, and other sites {37760551} can occur late in the disease course, sometimes > 20 years after initial diagnosis. The clinical trajectory is often protracted and relentless, necessitating long-term surveillance. There are no histological features that reliably correlate with prognosis. Prognostic factors influencing survival include the presence of metastatic disease, tumour size, and (though to a lesser extent) the primary tumour site {25529371; 26975384; 25849226; 29044531; 29582189}. Children, adolescents, and young adults may have slightly better outcomes {18438777}. While some studies find no difference in behaviour based on site {25529371}, others report that tumours arising in the craniofacial skeleton tend to follow an improved course compared with axial tumours that are associated with poorer outcomes. Complete surgical resection combined with chemotherapy is considered the standard of care, with additional agents that may improve outcome {25529371; 26975384; 25849226; 29044531; 29582189; 27418251}.
