1.5.3 Epithelioid haemangioma

Definition

Epithelioid haemangioma (EH) is a vascular tumour composed of well-formed blood vessels lined by epithelioid endothelial cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The tumour is benign in soft tissue and locally aggressive in bone.

ICD-O coding

9125/0 Epithelioid haemangioma

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Y & XH10T4 Other specified neoplastic haemangioma & Epithelioid haemangioma

Related terminology

Not recommended: angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia; haemorrhagic epithelioid and spindle cell haemangioma; histiocytoid haemangioma.

Subtype(s)

Conventional epithelioid haemangioma; cellular (atypical) epithelioid haemangioma

Localization

The most common location is in the skeleton, most commonly involving long tubular bones (~40%), followed by short tubular bones of the distal lower extremities, flat bones, vertebrae, and small bones of the hands {18852673; 38237880}. The tumours can involve any location in long tubular bones, but the epiphysis is a more common location in adolescence {28721475}.

Cutaneous and soft tissue lesions are most commonly located in the head and neck region, especially the forehead, preauricular region, and scalp, followed by the distal extremities and trunk {4008683; 26135557}. The penis can be involved, particularly by the cellular (atypical) subtype {15087672; 25043949}. Some EHs arise within large vessels.

Approximately 10–25% of patients present with multiple/multifocal lesions, including different bones, usually confined to the same anatomical region {29266025; 18852673; 21948309}.

Clinical features

Patients present with cutaneous erythematous-violaceous papules or angiomatoid nodules. Deep-seated lesions manifest as pain localized to the involved anatomical site.

Imaging

On imaging, skeletal tumours present as well-defined lytic, sometimes expansile, septated masses that may erode the cortex, may develop regular periosteal reaction, and may extend into soft tissue, mimicking malignancy {38237880}. Like other vascular tumours, it may be multifocal in a regional distribution {38237880}. MRI signal intensities are not specific; however, surrounding inflammatory change, depicted as high signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences, is a typical feature {28721475}.

Epidemiology

EH occurs across a wide age range, with a peak incidence in the fourth decade of life and no sex predilection {18852673; 21948309; 26135557}. For skeletal lesions, an M:F ratio of approximately 1.4:1 has been reported {18852673; 33034932}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Rearrangements involving FOS or FOSB are identified in approximately half of all EH cases {26135557; 37553246; 34225547}. Fusion partners for FOS include LMNA, MBNL1, VIM, and lincRNAs {26173738; 26135557; 33034932}. For FOSB, the most common fusion partner is ZFP36, with rare fusions involving WWTR1 or ACTB {25043949; 26135557; 26173738}. FOSB rearrangements are more frequently observed in EHs of the extremities, trunk, and penis {25043949}. The cellular (atypical) subtype more often harbours FOSB fusions. FOS rearrangements are more common in cellular EHs of bone and soft tissue, but are rare in head and neck lesions {26135557; 26173738; 33034932}. Identical breakpoints involving the FOS gene were identified in multifocal disease, supporting the notion that they represent a single neoplastic clonal spread, instead of multiple independent tumours {26173738; 37519041}.

These fusion events cause dysregulation of the FOS family of transcription factors, which encode leucine zipper proteins that can dimerize with proteins of the JUN family, thereby forming the transcription factor complex AP-1. The AP-1 transcription factor binds to TPA-responsive elements (TREs; 5′-TGAC/GTCA-3′) of the promoter and enhancer regions of target genes, thereby regulating a wide variety of physiological and tumorigenic processes, including cell proliferation, tumour invasion, distant metastasis, and angiogenesis. In the FOS fusions, the various fusion partners are at the C-terminal end of the protein and cause loss of the transactivating domain {26173738}, thereby preventing the normal rapid protein degradation of FOS, resulting in prolonged FOS activation and overexpression {29150442; 34225547}. The FOSB fusions occur at the N-terminal part of the protein and are most probably activating promoter-swap events, causing upregulation of FOSB {28797505; 34225547; 37553246}.

GATA6::FOXO1 fusions have been identified in a subset of EH with a predilection for the skin in the head and neck {33318580; 38457669}. In addition, TPM3/4::ALK fusions are described in the cutaneous haemangioma with epithelioid features, which exhibits close morphological resemblance to cellular EH {40070162; 37787425}.

Gene fusions are absent in cases described as angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia, possibly indicating a different pathogenesis {26135557; 37553246}. Cutaneous epithelioid angiomatous nodules may express FOSB or FOS (c-FOS), but it remains unclear whether they fall within the spectrum of EH or represent a separate entity {14726818; 35490255}.

Macroscopic appearance

In the bone, EHs are typically < 70 mm and characterized by well-defined, nodular, soft, solid, red, and haemorrhagic lesions. They may expand the bone, erode the cortex, and extend into the soft tissue. Superficial EHs are usually 5–20 mm in size, only rarely exceeding 50 mm {4008683}. Most exhibit a nonspecific nodular appearance.

Histopathology

Conventional EH is typically well demarcated and has a distinctive lobular architecture at low power. The centres of the lobules are the most cellular with solid growth, while an increased maturation of the vascular lumina may be seen at the periphery, surrounded by an intact pericytic layer {18852673; 21948309; 21904630}. The tumour vessels are lined by a single layer of large and polyhedral epithelioid endothelial cells, with occasional nuclear hobnailing and protrusion into the lumen, resembling a tombstone. The lesional cells have oval or kidney-shaped nuclei with vesicular or fine chromatin, which tend to be hyperlobated or cleaved, and abundant, deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm, with variable intracytoplasmic vacuoles (blister cells). Cytological atypia is often mild, and mitotic activity is generally low. Marked lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, prominent lymphoid follicles, and abundant eosinophils are not uncommon, particularly in head and neck lesions. A subset of EH may display intravascular growth, with centrifugal extension into adjacent soft tissues {32094426}.

The cellular (atypical) subtype exhibits more solid and infiltrative growth and exuberant and compact proliferation, with increased cellularity. It may display mild to moderate nuclear pleomorphism, sheet-like appearance with inconspicuous or slit-like spaces, and little intervening stroma, as well as foci of necrosis {25043949}.

In the bone, tumours replace the marrow cavity, and often infiltrate pre-existing bony trabeculae. Cortical destruction and extension to the soft tissue can be seen. The periphery of the lobules may contain small arteriole-like vessels lined by flat endothelial cells. Osteoclast-type giant cells, reactive bone formation, and large dilated vessels lined by tumour cells may be present. Intralesional haemorrhage and areas containing fascicles of cytologically banal, sometimes mitotically active spindle cells can be seen, especially in the short tubular bones of the extremities {36206448; 10223237}.

In contrast to conventional EH, angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia shows a vascular blow-out pattern with centrifugal growth from a muscular vessel, and variable degrees of inflammation {26135557; 28009608; 29527734; 26173738; 26135557}. Despite reports of FOSB immunopositivity, angiolymphoid hyperplasia with eosinophilia typically lacks FOS and FOSB fusions, and the relationship with conventional EH is debated.

Tumour cells consistently express endothelial markers including CD31, FLI1, and ERG, with a more variable and often diminished expression of CD34. Some cases also show immunoreactivity for keratins and EMA {8333560; 21904630; 21317715}. SMA highlights the pericytic layer, accentuating the vasoformative architecture. FOS and FOSB are valuable ancillary markers in diagnosing EH; however, their interpretation requires caution, as expression can be observed in other vascular neoplasms {35635207}. Expression of FOS can be decreased because of decalcification {31768625}.

Cytology

FNABs are not routinely performed.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FOS/FOSB rearrangements, as well as less common alternative fusions, can be demonstrated to support the diagnosis {28009608; 29527734; 26135557; 26173738; 33318580; 38457669; 40070162; 37787425}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: well-formed vessels with a lobular architecture, lined by epithelioid endothelial cells; variable eosinophilic infiltrate.

Desirable: expression of endothelial markers; FOS or FOSB expression; or demonstration of the underlying gene fusion in selected cases.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

EH in soft tissue is benign, whereas EH of the bone is considered as a locally aggressive lesion. Soft tissue lesions are often excised, and treatment of skeletal lesions consists of curettage and, less frequently, marginal en bloc excision. Isolated cases with limited lymph node involvement have been reported {5062527; 18852673}, probably representing intranodal manifestations of multifocal disease {1373579}. Local recurrence rates vary depending on the anatomical site, with higher rates for osseous, multifocal lesions, ranging from 10% to 62% {16501828; 21948309; 21904630; 27660230; 32271190; 33034932}. Most recurrences follow an indolent course and can be managed with re-excision; however, rare cases may display more aggressive behaviour.
