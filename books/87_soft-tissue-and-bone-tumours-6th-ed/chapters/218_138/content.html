2.1.8 Chondromyxoid fibroma

Definition

Chondromyxoid fibroma is a benign cartilaginous neoplasm with a lobulated and zonal architecture composed of chondroid, myxoid, and myofibroblastic areas.

ICD-O coding

9241/0 Chondromyxoid fibroma

ICD-11 coding

2E82.Z & XH89S0 Benign chondrogenic tumours, site unspecified & Chondromyxoid fibroma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Chondromyxoid fibroma can occur at almost any osseous site. It is most frequent in the metaphysis of long bones, most often the proximal tibia and distal femur. Approximately 25% of cases occur in the flat bones, mainly the ilium. When arising in bones of the feet, chondromyxoid fibroma often involves metatarsals. Other sites of involvement include the ribs, vertebrae, skull and facial bones, and tubular bones of the hand.

Clinical features

Pain is the most common symptom, usually mild and sometimes present for several years {9596266}. Swelling is infrequent and most commonly observed in the bones of the hands and feet.

Imaging

Radiographically, chondromyxoid fibroma in a long bone is typically a well-demarcated, oval-shaped, lobulated, osteolytic, eccentric, metaphysial lesion, with scalloping and expansion of the cortex {9596266}. Periosteal reaction is commonly seen on MRI and underreported in radiographs {21835889}. In one third of the patients, the epiphysis is involved {21835889}. Juxtacortical or surface chondromyxoid fibroma is rare {32803376}. In the small bones, fusiform expansion of the entire bone is typical. On fluid-sensitive MRI sequences, chondromyxoid fibroma typically has an intermediate signal in the periphery, corresponding to fibrous tissue, and a high signal intensity centrally, corresponding to myxoid and cartilaginous tissue {21835889}. On gadolinium chelate–enhanced MRI, (septal)-nodular enhancement is commonly seen. Reactive high signal intensity changes in surrounding soft tissue and/or bone marrow are visible in > 50% of patients on fluid-sensitive sequences {21835889}. When located in a long bone, radiological differential diagnosis includes aneurysmal bone cyst, non-ossifying fibroma, giant cell tumour, and chondrosarcoma. The differential diagnosis for juxtacortical tumours includes benign parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation and even metastasis {32803376}.

Epidemiology

Chondromyxoid fibroma is a rare tumour {19700940; 18157043}. Although published cases show that it may affect a wide age range (first to seventh decades of life), it is described to occur most often in the second and third decades of life and more frequently in male individuals {6337162; 9596266}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Chondromyxoid fibroma is driven by recurrent rearrangements of the GRM1 gene, a glutamate receptor typically expressed in neurons of the CNS. These alterations involve complex genomic events, primarily chromoplexy and less commonly chromothripsis, that result in the fusion of GRM1 with various 5′ partner genes through promoter swapping or gene fusion mechanisms {24658000; 30166462}. This repositioning places the entire coding region of GRM1 under the control of highly active promoters such as COL12A1, BCLAF1, or MEF2A, leading to a 1400-fold increase in its expression {30166462}. This overexpression is observed in approximately 90% of chondromyxoid fibroma cases, while GRM1 expression remains absent or minimal in a large cohort of non-chondromyxoid fibroma mesenchymal and cartilaginous tumours, highlighting its high specificity as a molecular marker for chondromyxoid fibroma. However, about 10% of chondromyxoid fibromas do not exhibit GRM1 upregulation, suggesting that alternative genetic mechanisms may underlie tumour development in this subset {24658000}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross features of chondromyxoid fibroma include well-defined margins, grey or white tumour, lack of obvious necrosis, cystic change, and liquefaction. The tumour is multilobulated and well-demarcated from the surrounding bone. Typically, there are scalloped margins.

Histopathology

Typically, chondromyxoid fibroma is sharply demarcated from the surrounding bone. Lobules of the tumour may be separate from the main lesion. A lobular pattern with stellate or spindle-shaped cells in a myxoid background is evident {9596266}. Lobules demonstrate hypocellular centres with hypercellular peripheries. The centre of the lobules shows morphological features more similar to those of hyaline cartilage, in terms of both extracellular matrix and cell composition {15880456; 11488075}. Rarely, the transition from myxoid to eosinophilic matrix may be seen. Microscopic cystic or liquefactive change is uncommon and usually focal when present. Hyaline cartilage is present in 19% of cases {9596266}. Calcification, when present, is usually coarse, occurring more frequently in older patients and in flat-bone tumours {9840392}. Individual cells within lobules have oval to spindled nuclei and indistinct to densely eosinophilic cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic extensions, often bipolar or multipolar, are frequent. Enlarged, hyperchromatic, and pleomorphic nuclei are noted in 20–30% of cases. Mitoses are uncommon, and atypical mitoses are not identified {9596266}. Entrapment of surrounding bone trabeculae by tumour is very rare. There may also be haemosiderin deposition and inflammatory cells, usually lymphocytes. Aneurysmal bone cyst–like areas are noted in approximately 10% of long- and flat-bone lesions {9596266}.

Immunohistochemistry

GRM1 immunohistochemistry has emerged as a useful surrogate marker for GRM1 rearrangement, showing moderate to strong cytoplasmic staining in 97–100% of cases {35650682; 38890779}. Although acid decalcification can modestly reduce immunoreactivity, most decalcified specimens still demonstrate at least moderate staining in > 50% of tumour cells. The cartilaginous nature of this tumour is reflected by immunopositivity for S100 {2263591; 21078438; 19801163; 2793160}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes conventional chondrosarcoma with myxoid matrix, enchondroma, chondroblastoma, myoepithelial tumour, phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, chondromyxoid fibroma-like osteosarcoma, and acral fibrochondromyxoid tumour {39181449}. Chondrosarcomas more often affect older patients and the diaphysis of long bones, with more frank atypia and mitoses. Moreover, osteoclast-like giant cells are often present at the periphery of the lobules. Enchondroma is composed of nodules of mature hyaline cartilage, which undergoes endochondral ossification, not seen in chondromyxoid fibroma and lacks osteoclast-type giant cells. Chondroblastoma occurs in the epiphysis of long bones and is composed of sheets of round to polygonal cells with scattered osteoclast-type giant cells, does not exhibit a lobular architecture, and is characterized by the histone H3 K36M mutation, which can be detected by immunohistochemistry. GRM1 immunohistochemistry is useful in distinguishing chondromyxoid fibroma from its histological mimics.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis is generally not needed for diagnosis. In selected cases, identification of GRM1 rearrangement or overexpression can be done by FISH in about 75% of cases or by whole-genome mate-pair sequencing and RNA sequencing {38890779; 24658000}. The gene fusion may not be detected by targeted RNA sequencing, because of the complexity of the fusion mechanism {38890779}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; lobulated lesion with zonal architecture; spindle and stellate cells embedded in chondroid and/or myxoid matrix in the centre of the lobules; spindle-shaped myofibroblast-like cells at the periphery of the lobules admixed with osteoclast-like giant cells.

Desirable: detection of GRM1 overexpression by immunohistochemistry in selected cases.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is excellent, even for recurrent tumours. Recurrence has been reported in approximately 9–15% of cases treated locally {25217119}.
