1.5.4 Lymphatic malformation

Definition

Lymphatic malformations (LMs), formerly known as lymphangioma and lymphangiomatosis, are slow-flow vascular anomalies arising during early embryonic development that may manifest as localized lesions (termed common lymphatic malformations [CLMs]) or as widespread, often multifocal, infiltrative processes classified as complex lymphatic anomalies.

Related terminology

Not recommended: cystic hygroma; lymphangioma circumscriptum; lymphangiomatosis; intra-abdominal cystic lymphangioma; haemangiolymphangioma.

Subtype(s)

Common lymphatic malformation (CLM)

Complex Lymphatic anomalies (CLA): generalized lymphatic anomaly (GLA); kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA); Gorham–Stout disease (GSD), central conduction lymphatic anomaly (CCLA), also known as channel-type lymphatic malformation

Localization

CLMs may be superficial, deep, or involve both superficial and deep tissue planes. Most microcystic LMs arise in the skin of the proximal extremities and limb girdles. Macrocystic LMs commonly affect the soft tissues of the head and neck, but can also present in the extremities, axilla, groin, mediastinum, gastrointestinal tract, mesentery, retroperitoneum, and anogenital region. In generalized lymphatic anomaly (GLA), LMs are multifocal and typically affect the spleen, skeletal system, and soft tissues, often accompanied by serous cavity effusions. Gorham–Stout disease (GSD) commonly involves bones and adjacent soft tissues at the shoulder girdle, pelvis, mandible, ribs, or spine. In contrast to GLA, GSD tends to show less frequent visceral involvement and macrocystic lymphatic malformations {26806875}. In kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA), the mediastinal soft tissues, lungs, bones, and spleen are the most frequently involved sites {26055853}.

Clinical features

CLMs typically present as localized or regional swelling. In superficial microcystic forms, translucent or haemorrhagic cutaneous vesicles may be observed. Deep lesions can displace adjacent structures, potentially leading to compression or obstruction of thoracic or abdominal organs. By involving multiple localizations, CLAs disrupt lymphatic fluid homeostasis, leading to impaired lymph drainage and leakage of lymph into body cavities. CLAs can be associated with intestinal lymphangiectasias, causing protein-losing enteropathy, anaemia, and sometimes lymphocytopenia {26806875}. Patients with GLA present with multiple cystic lesions in soft tissue and viscera, frequently associated with serous-cavity effusions. Bone lesions may be seen, but cortical destruction is not a feature. When massive osteolysis is observed, GSD should be considered. GSD presents insidiously with pain, swelling, or pathological fractures {35385405}. Long-bone involvement may result in limb-length discrepancy or functional impairment, while vertebral lesions can cause neurological compromise from collapse or instability. KLA is characterized by serous cavity effusions, consumptive coagulopathy, and progressive respiratory and multiorgan failure. The spleen and bone are frequently affected {26055853; 31030813}. Lymphatic malformations may occur as part of a combined vascular anomaly or within the context of a syndromic disorder {34166072}.

Epidemiology

LMs are common paediatric lesions, typically presenting at birth or during early childhood {10442614; 31864533}. Congenital cases can be detected prenatally by ultrasound or fetal MRI. Delayed presentations, especially in mesenteric locations, have been reported {4051731; 10936792; 22696733}. No consistent sex predilection has been observed {10442614; 31864533}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Somatic PIK3CA mutations are commonly identified in cases of CLM and GLA {25681199; 29217067}, while somatic BRAF mutations have been occasionally reported in CLM {35373151}. KLA is most often associated with somatic NRAS mutations and rarely with CBL {32894644; 39644020; 30542204}, HRAS {35202617}, or PIK3CA variants {37194624}. A somatic activating KRAS variant was identified in a patient with GSD {32591603}. An EML4::ALK fusion has been recently reported in a patient with GSD and another with a large genitourinary LM {39529238}. Patients with Noonan and Turner syndromes have a higher incidence of LMs {35221870; 37705207}. LMs are common manifestations in syndromes characterized by tissue overgrowth, such as the PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum that includes Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome, CLOVES (congenital lipomatous overgrowth, vascular malformations, epidermal naevi, and scoliosis/skeletal/spinal anomalies), and capillary malformation of the lower lip, LM of the face and neck, asymmetry of the face and limbs, and partial/generalized overgrowth (CLAPO), as well as Proteus syndrome {34166072}. An EPHB4 variant has been reported in central conduction lymphatic anomaly {29905864}.

RAS/MAPK signalling, acting in concert with PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, is essential for vascular development. Constitutive activation of RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling causes abnormal endothelial differentiation and disordered vascular development {28687708}. Aberrant proliferation of lymphatic vessels within bone leads to excessive osteoclast activation and bone matrix resorption in GSD.

Macroscopic appearance

LMs may be macrocystic (cysts > 10 mm), microcystic, or mixed. The macrocystic forms may be unilocular or multilocular and usually have thick walls. The microcystic form is spongiform with thin walls. The cystic content may be clear, haemorrhagic, or chylous. Spongiform and macrocystic forms can also be observed in GLA and KLA.

Histopathology

Macrocystic LM consists of one or more dilated, relatively well-circumscribed cystic spaces, whereas microcystic LM consists of small, anastomosing lymphatic vessels with an infiltrative growth pattern. Many cases show mixed macrocystic and microcystic morphology {26055853}. The cystic spaces typically contain proteinaceous fluid, sometimes with lymphocytes and histiocytes. In cases with intralesional haemorrhage, vessels are filled with blood, and abundant interstitial haemosiderin is often present. Reactive lymphoid tissue is frequently observed between vessels and may form follicles. Intravascular papillary projections resembling valves may be seen. The endothelium is flat or mildly swollen, but lacks atypia. The muscular wall varies from nearly absent to thickened and is composed of disorganized smooth muscle fibres {21095470}. Macrocystic lesions exhibit one or more large cystic cavities with thick fibrous walls and occasional smooth muscle strands. Cutaneous lesions are typically composed of small-calibre channels with little or no smooth muscle wall. Superficial vessels are often dilated, and the overlying epidermis may show hyperplasia with papillomatosis, raising differential diagnosis of verrucous venous malformation {30216519}. KLA is characterized by dilated, redundant, and abnormally configured lymphatic channels, typically accompanied by dispersed clusters of variably canalized, and often haemosiderotic, spindled lymphatic endothelial cells. Cellular pattern of KLA mimicking that of kaposiform haemangioendothelioma has been reported {35202617}. In GSD, bone trabeculae show progressive resorption with replacement by thin-walled, dilated lymphatic and capillary channels, and prominent osteoclast activity with variable reactive osteoblastic bone formation.

D2-40, PROX1, and VEGFR3 are useful for diagnosing macrocystic patterns. In large lymphatic vessels, D2-40 and LYVE1 may be negative or only focally positive, whereas PROX1 and VEGFR3 are usually positive. Lymphatic malformations also show immunoreactivity for ERG and CD31 and are negative or only focally positive for CD34 {26055853; 21095470}. Spindle cells in KLA show a lymphatic endothelial immunophenotype {35385405}.

Cytology

FNABs are not usually performed but can show plentiful lymphocytes and macrophages.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of variants, most commonly in PIK3CA or NRAS, supports the diagnosis and helps identify targeted treatment in selected cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malformed and usually excessive lymphatic channels; kaposiform clusters of lymphatic endothelial cells in KLA; compatible clinical and imaging features.

Desirable: immunopositivity for lymphatic endothelial markers; supporting molecular findings.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

LMs are benign but often challenging lesions to manage. In CLM, sclerotherapy or surgical excision are standard therapeutic options. More complex entities such as GLA, GSD, and KLA require a multimodal treatment approach, including agents targeting the PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR and RAS/MAPK/MEK pathways. KLA remains associated with significant mortality related to respiratory compromise and bleeding {35385405; 31030813}. The prognosis of GSD is variable; while some patients achieve spontaneous stabilization, others exhibit relentless disease progression. Morbidity in GSD is largely site dependent, with spinal involvement conferring the highest risk of mortality. Targeted therapies, such as trametinib, sirolimus, or alpelisib, may be effective in patients with CLAs. Recent evidence supports using ALK inhibitors in LMs harbouring ALK fusions {39529238}.
