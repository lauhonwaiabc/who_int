2.7.5 Fibrous dysplasia

Definition

Fibrous dysplasia is a benign, medullary, fibro-osseous neoplasm that can be multifocal and is characterized by distorted, poorly organized, and inadequately mineralized bone and intervening fibrous tissue.

ICD-O coding

8818/0 Fibrous dysplasia

ICD-11 coding

FB80.0 Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Related terminology

Not recommended: liposclerosing myxofibrous tumour; fibrocartilaginous dysplasia; ossifying fibroma of long bones; Kempson–Campanacci lesion.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Fibrous dysplasia can affect any bone but most commonly involves the calvarium, jaw, femur, and rib, in both monostotic and polyostotic forms {12703030; 32619461}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Fibrous dysplasia presents as an osseous deformity, pain, or pathological fracture involving a single (monostotic form) or multiple (polyostotic form) bone(s). The polyostotic form typically presents in childhood, while the monostotic form, which is 6–10 times more common, may be undetected until adulthood. In Mazabraud syndrome, fibrous dysplasia is associated with soft tissue myxoma. In McCune–Albright syndrome, fibrous dysplasia is associated with endocrinopathies and abnormalities of cutaneous pigmentation {14691924; 3720010}. Rarely, fibrous dysplasia produces tumour-induced osteomalacia through secretion of excess FGF23 {7848576; 12952917}.

Imaging

Radiographs and CT typically show a well-defined or partially poorly defined, non-aggressive, bubbly lesion with a ground-glass matrix, reflecting the unevenly mineralized microtrabecular osteoid matrix. In addition, a mixture of osteolytic and more sclerotic components is present. Soft tissue extension and periosteal reaction are absent when there are no fractures. Especially in small bones like ribs and craniofacial bones, the lesion may be expansile and covered by a neocortex. Radiological features change over time, as deformity, cyst formation, and fracture (healing) occur {33052697}. The lesions are often large and cause inappropriate stress response causing deformities {39902226}. Bowing deformity of the proximal femur affected by fibrous dysplasia (shepherd’s crook deformity) is a specific feature.

MRI signal intensities reflect the histological components and are thus heterogeneous, predominantly low on T1, and heterogeneously high on fluid-sensitive sequences. On gadolinium chelate MRI, a milk-cloud enhancement pattern is seen, which partly corresponds to the ground-glass areas, but is seen even more frequently {31062134; 32040603}. Sometimes cartilaginous components and fat can be identified {40075784}. Accompanying muscular myxoma (Mazabraud syndrome) may be seen as a high signal–intensity tumour on fluid-sensitive MRI {39536670}. MRI, CT, bone scintigraphy, and PET/CT can delineate the extent of polyostotic disease {20680622}. The latter two are also used to quantify metabolic activity.

Epidemiology

Fibrous dysplasia is rare, and epidemiological data are scarce. A cohort study based on the nationwide Danish National Patient Registry reported a prevalence of 66% in the general population, occurring in children and adults and comparable between sexes. An incidence rate of 3.6 cases per 1 million person-years (95% CI: 2.9–4.5) was reported for 2015–2018 {38174586}. The monostotic form accounts for 80–85% of all cases and shows no sex predilection. Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia has a female predilection {14697371}.

Etiology

Fibrous dysplasia is caused by postzygotic activating missense mutations in the GNAS (20q13.32) gene, which encodes the alpha subunit of the stimulatory G protein (Gsα); p.(Arg201His) and p.(Arg201Cys) are the most common variants {22245114; 26574629}.

Pathogenesis

GNAS is a complex locus with an imprinted expression pattern, but the major protein product is Gsα. The mutant Gsα in fibrous dysplasia causes constitutive activation and increased cAMP signalling, which affects the WNT/β-catenin and hedgehog pathways {29158412; 2549426}. In bone, Gsα promotes mesenchymal osteoprogenitor cells but prevents terminal differentiation {37920253}. Studies of knock-in mice constitutively expressing Gsα p.(Arg201Cys) replicate the human disease {24764158}. Developmental timing of the GNAS mutation determines the extent of clinical manifestations ranging from monostotic fibrous dysplasia to McCune–Albright syndrome {3720010}. In longstanding cases, the lesional cells harbouring the GNAS variant diminish due to apoptosis. This can make it challenging to identify the variant, depending also on the detection limits of the method used {18597624}.

Macroscopic appearance

Fibrous dysplasia is a tan-grey lesion that expands the host bone. Cystic degeneration is common; grossly identifiable translucent blue-grey cartilage nodules are rare.

Histopathology

Fibrous dysplasia is composed of delicate, irregular, curvilinear trabeculae of woven or (rarely) lamellar bone without conspicuous osteoblastic rimming in a background of fibroblast-like spindle cells and a collagenous stroma. The interface between bone and stroma is reminiscent of periosteum, with Sharpey-like fibres anchoring bone to the surrounding collagen. Significant cytological atypia or pleomorphism is absent. The unevenly mineralized microtrabecular osteoid matrix correlates with the ground-glass opacity on radiographs. Nodules of benign hyaline cartilage are rarely present. Over time, cystic change, foam cells, adipose tissue, fibromyxoid stroma, and dystrophic calcifications can be increasingly seen. The term “liposclerosing myxofibrous tumour” was previously applied to such lesions, often affecting the intertrochanteric region of the femur of adults. However, this term is no longer used since it has become evident that these lesions represent late-stage or degenerated fibrous dysplasia {23576919; 40075784}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The reported GNAS mutation frequency is 50–70%; however, the variant discovery rate is dependent on the technology used and is adversely affected by sample decalcification. Somatic mosaicism may also account for the reported variability in mutation detection rates in the literature {26574629}. Molecular assays are diagnostically useful, because occasionally fibrous dysplasia can be confused histologically with low-grade central osteosarcoma {26248895}. The only recurrent clonal chromosome aberrations described to date include trisomy 2 and structural aberrations of 12p13 {11104029}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; a fibro-osseous lesion consisting of irregular curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone without prominent osteoblastic rimming embedded within a collagenous stroma with bland fibroblast-like cells.

Desirable: activating missense mutations in GNAS.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Monostotic fibrous dysplasia is a benign lesion that may result in skeletal deformity, whereas polyostotic fibrous dysplasia may be crippling. Hypophosphataemia may be used to measure the activity and extent of skeletal lesions {31196103}. Exceptional cases of malignant transformation have been described {10525796; 30809751}.
