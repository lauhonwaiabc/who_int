2.1.1 Subungual exostosis

Definition

Subungual exostosis is a benign osteocartilaginous lesion arising on the surface of the distal phalanx beneath the nail bed.

ICD-O coding

9213/0 Subungual exostosis

ICD-11 coding

EE13.Y & XH1XL9 Other specified nail disorder & Subungual exostosis

2E83.5 & XH1XL9 Osseous and chondromatous neoplasm, benign & Subungual exostosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: Dupuytren exostosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Nearly 80% of cases involve the dorsal or medial surface of the distal phalanx of the big toe. The remaining cases involve distal phalanges of other toes or fingers.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with painful swelling of the nail bed leading to elevation and deformation of the nail plate. Ulceration and infection may occur. The preoperative period ranges from months to years.

Imaging

A radiograph shows a trabeculated osseous protuberance with an uninterrupted underlying cortex, typically on the dorsomedial aspect of the distal first toe, allowing for a confident diagnosis of subungual exostosis {33946096}. A fibrocartilaginous cap of variable thickness may be present. On ultrasound, the lesion is hyperechoic with a hypoechoic fibrocartilaginous cap. MRI signal characteristics are not specific. The signal intensity of the fibrocartilaginous cap can be somewhat inhomogeneous, but on fluid-sensitive sequences the signal intensity is lower than that of the hyaline cartilaginous cap of an osteochondroma {30777226}. The main differential diagnosis is a subungual osteochondroma that shows, in contrast to the subungual exostosis, cortico-medullary continuity between the lesion and the underlying bone. When the lesion is in an atypical location (~20%), such as phalanges of the fingers, ossified lesions that are continuous with the cortex should be included in the differential diagnosis. These include florid reactive periostitis, bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation, fibro-osseous pseudotumour of digits, periosteal chondroma, and myositis ossificans {33946096}.

Epidemiology

Subungual exostosis occurs relatively frequently, but despite many documented cases, its epidemiology remains unstudied. It most commonly affects young individuals, with > 50% of reported cases being in those aged under 18 years (median: 25.7 years). There is no sex predilection reported {24146360; 30156888}.

Etiology

Preceding trauma and infection have been reported in 29% and 14% of cases, respectively {24146360}, although their causal relationship to the tumour is unknown. The clonal cytogenetic findings suggest a true neoplastic process.

Pathogenesis

Cytogenetic analyses of subungual exostosis consistently demonstrate t(X;6)(q24-q26;q15-q25), often as the sole change {9885985; 15252309; 16284948}. The breakpoints are located in the COL12A1 gene on chromosome 6 and near the IRS4 gene, encoding an insulin receptor substrate, on the X chromosome. The translocation does not result in any fusion transcript; instead, it leads to increased expression of IRS4 at the mRNA and protein levels {20340132}. This translocation is different from that of bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP) {36853784}, supporting the distinctness of the disease.

Macroscopic appearance

The lesion consists of small bony and cartilaginous fragments.

Histopathology

Subungual exostosis is composed of peripheral fibrocartilaginous tissue and a stalk of bone. The cartilage can look atypical with hypercellularity and nuclear enlargement, and it can blend with a spindle cell proliferation. Mitoses can be seen. The cartilaginous cap can be tenuous or even absent. Bone forms through enchondral ossification or directly from spindle cells, and intertrabecular tissue is hypervascular and fibrous. Subungual exostosis should be distinguished from osteochondroma, which is rare in the small bones of the hands and feet. Unlike osteochondroma, a fibrillary cartilaginous matrix with marked proliferative activity and an irregular bone–cartilage interface, as well as enlarged, bizarre, and binucleated chondrocytes may be seen in subungual exostosis; continuity with the underlying medullary cavity is lacking. Subungual exostosis is also different from BPOP because it affects the distal phalanx by definition, whereas BPOP has a wider anatomical distribution, often involving more-proximal bones. BPOP is characterized by at least focal presence of distinct blue bone, which is generally absent in subungual exostosis. Subungual exostosis should also be distinguished from fibro-osseous pseudotumour of digits. It typically involves the soft tissue of the proximal phalanx of the fingers, shows disorganized bone and a fasciitis-like background, and harbours USP6 fusions {29787930; 30946936}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a small osteocartilaginous lesion on the distal phalangeal bone in a subungual location; no continuity between the lesion and the medullary cavity of native bone.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Subungual exostosis is benign and is adequately treated with simple excision. Local rapid recurrence has been reported {3284396}. Rare recurrences can be managed by re-excision.
