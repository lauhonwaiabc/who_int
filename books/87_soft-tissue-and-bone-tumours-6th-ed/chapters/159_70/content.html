1.5.9 Kaposi sarcoma

Definition

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a locally aggressive KSHV/HHV8-induced endothelial proliferation.

ICD-O coding

9140/3 Kaposi sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B57.Z & XH36A5 Kaposi sarcoma of unspecified primary site & Kaposi sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: idiopathic multiple pigmented sarcoma of skin; angiosarcoma multiplex; granuloma multiplex haemorrhagicum.

Subtype(s)

Classic indolent Kaposi sarcoma; endemic African Kaposi sarcoma; AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma; iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma; non-epidemic Kaposi sarcoma in men who have sex with HIV-negative men {18525262; 40079529}.

Localization

The most common site of involvement is the skin of the distal extremities. Mucosal membranes, lymph nodes, and visceral organs can be affected, sometimes without skin involvement. Brain and bone involvement is rare, even in disseminated disease.

Clinical features

Five clinical and epidemiological forms are recognized {30521260; 34830846}. Classic KS is characterized by reddish-purple/dark-brown macules, plaques, and nodules that can ulcerate; it is particularly frequent in distal extremities in the setting of immunosenescence. It is usually indolent, with the majority of (elderly) patients exhibiting localized disease, with nodal and visceral involvement occurring rarely; lymphoedema is a frequent accompanying condition {38398082; 40229708}. Among the known risk factors for developing classic KS are previous KSHV/HHV8 infection, age ≥ 50 years, contact with siliceous volcanic soil, and bloodsucking insect bites {39415564}.

Endemic KS may be localized to skin and has a protracted course; a lymphadenopathic form in children is rapidly progressive and highly lethal {30521260; 34830846}. Skin involvement is not typical in children. Visceral involvement may occur in some adult patients. Untreated AIDS-associated KS is the most aggressive type. In skin, lesions are most common on the face, genitals, and lower extremities; oral mucosa, lymph nodes, gastrointestinal tract (the most common extracutaneous site), and lungs are frequently involved. Nodal and visceral disease can occur without mucocutaneous lesions. KS can develop at any stage of HIV infection, but is more frequent in advanced immunosuppression {11423260; 25709361; 26261737}. Iatrogenic KS is relatively uncommon, developing months to years after solid-organ transplantation or immunosuppressive treatment, notably corticosteroids {8348508; 9520025; 34830846}; it may resolve upon immunosuppressant withdrawal, although its course is unpredictable. It typically affects the lower limbs. Mucosal and visceral involvement is relatively common. Whereas skin lesions and lymphadenopathy are obvious disease signs, visceral organ involvement may be silent or symptomatic, depending on lesion site and extent. The non-epidemic KS subtype occurring in HIV-negative men who have sex with men presents with disease on the lower extremities, with one fifth having lymphoedema {40079529}. Most of these patients have indolent disease and favourable outcomes.

Epidemiology

Classic indolent KS predominantly occurs in non–HIV-infected elderly men of Mediterranean, eastern European, or Ashkenazi Jewish descent {38398082; 40229708}. Endemic African KS occurs in middle-aged adults and children without HIV in equatorial, sub-Saharan Africa {40079529}. AIDS-associated KS is found in individuals infected with HIV-1, most frequently in men who have sex with men; its incidence has been reduced with the advent of HAART {10950365; 11346932}. A very rare non-epidemic KS subtype is described in a younger population (compared with the classic subtype) of HIV-negative men who have sex with men {18525262; 40079529}.

Etiology

KSHV/HHV8 (KS-associated herpesvirus), found in KS endothelial cells, is a double-stranded linear DNA gamma-herpesvirus encoding a latency-associated nuclear antigen (LANA), the product of the viral gene ORF73 {7997879; 7700310; 35454820}. Sequencing shows linkage of KSHV/HHV8 genetic variants with specific populations {20079510}. KSHV/HHV8 is mainly sexually transmitted. KSHV/HHV8 DNA is detected in all KS forms (> 95% of AIDS-related and non-related KS) {7723505; 12244338; 10749966}. Antibodies against KSHV/HHV8 nuclear antigens appear before clinical KS {8657239} and can be detected in peripheral blood. Not all KSHV/HHV8 infections lead to pathological manifestations, and most primary infections are asymptomatic {27584730}.

Pathogenesis

There is serological correlation between KSHV/HHV8 infection and KS. Not all seropositive individuals have KS; infection is required but not sufficient for disease induction, which results from a complex interplay of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors {11423257; 11536246}. The genomic landscape of KS is still poorly understood, and it is unclear whether KS is a consistently monoclonal disease entity, with evidence suggesting that KS can be either polyclonal or monoclonal {9301550; 9077377; 9815980}. KSHV/HHV8 interferes with RB and p53 tumour suppressor signalling, increasing expression of antiapoptotic factors, such as BCL2, and preventing inactivation of the MYC oncogene {30212382}. Comparative genomic hybridization analyses show recurrent 11q13 involvement, including that of FGF4 and FGF3 target genes. Y chromosome loss appears recurrent in early disease, whereas additional recurrent changes of chromosomes 16, 17, 21, X, and Y appear during tumour growth {10786811; 16954721}. SNP-based chromosomal microarray analysis has shown the anaplastic KS cohort to demonstrate highly recurrent whole chromosome gains of chromosomes 7, 11, 19, and 21, with losses of chromosome 6q and the Y chromosome. Compared with conventional KS, anaplastic KS cases showed significantly more total copy-number alterations and more frequent gains of chromosome 7 and chromosome 11q13.1 {37080393}. KRAS and TP53 alterations are reported {8074494; 8030271; 24469037} in addition to aberrant expression of genes related to neoangiogenesis and proliferation that may impact endothelial cell transformation by KSHV/HHV8 (KS-associated herpesvirus) {12538164; 15958552; 30699189; 35454820}. Both viral and cellular DNA-encoded microRNAs have been shown to play important roles in pathogenesis {19013245; 17500590}.

Macroscopic appearance

Skin lesions (patches, plaques, nodules) range from small to several centimetres. Involvement of mucosa, soft tissues, lymph nodes, and visceral organs presents as variably sized haemorrhagic nodules that can coalesce.

Histopathology

All five clinical and epidemiological KS subtypes show identical histological features. Early skin lesions show subtle vascular proliferation {3691903}. In the patch stage, vascular spaces are increased in number, dissecting collagen fibres in the upper reticular dermis. Lining endothelial cells are flattened or oval, with no or minimal atypia. Pre-existing vessels may protrude into lumina of new vessels. Ovoid/spindle endothelial cell proliferations surround pre-existing vessels, and admixed lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, extravasated erythrocytes, and haemosiderin deposits are common. Rarely, lesions resemble lymphangioma or haemangioma, causing diagnostic confusion {15769278; 24067801; 9056649}. The papillary dermis is uninvolved in early stages. In the plaque stage, all patch-stage characteristics are exaggerated, and angioproliferation is extensive, with jagged vascular spaces. The inflammatory infiltrate is denser, with extravascular erythrocytes and siderophages, as well as frequent intracellular or extracellular hyaline globules. The nodular stage comprises circumscribed, cellular nodules of spindle cell fascicles with minimal atypia, hyaline globules, and numerous slit-like spaces containing erythrocytes, with peripheral ectatic vessels. Rarely, KS shows marked nuclear atypia or pleomorphism, known as anaplastic KS, which is clinically aggressive with increased metastatic potential {21845069; 19638066; 37080393; 37403378; 37040901; 37028800}. Anaplastic KS shows angiosarcoma-like or pleomorphic spindle cell sarcoma morphology; plasma cell–rich chronic inflammation and haemosiderin deposition are commonly present in this pattern {37080393}. Other rare histological patterns include intravascular KS {17944727}; an angiosarcoma-like KS reminiscent of well-differentiated angiosarcoma (angiosarcoma-like KS) {35948518; 38846740}; and an epithelioid and clear cell appearance {34773293; 29660143; 33464749}.

In nodal disease, KS may be unifocal or multifocal and may entirely efface nodes. Early lesions may be subtle, showing only increased numbers of vascular channels with sinusoidal plasma cell infiltrates {4938888}. In viscera, lesions tend to respect organ architecture and spread along native structures, such as pre-existing vessels, bronchi, and liver portal areas, before involving surrounding parenchyma {3626432}.

Both lining endothelial cells of vessels and spindled tumour cells express endothelial markers, including CD31, CD34, and ERG, and lymphatic markers such as podoplanin (D2-40); they show almost invariable nuclear expression of KSHV/HHV8, although this may be very focal in some cases.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows variable cellularity, with irregular tissue fragments of haphazardly arranged or streaming spindle cells with elongate, blunt or spindle, mildly pleomorphic hyperchromatic nuclei with coarse chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli, and spindle cytoplasm within stroma (Giemsa) or as wispy, thin, bipolar cytoplasm of dispersed cells {25547261}. Some cases have vasiform tissue fragments and haemosiderin.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

PCR or in situ hybridization may be required to demonstrate KSHV/HHV8 in rare cases in which immunohistochemistry is negative {7489408}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: proliferation of small slit-like vessels lined by minimally to mildly atypical cells and surrounded by bland spindle cells; extravasated erythrocytes and patchy lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in the early stage.

Desirable: nuclear KSHV/HHV8 expression by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Disease evolution depends on the epidemiological–clinical type and extent. Degree of immunosuppression at diagnosis is the most important survival determinant, but this is modified by treatment (including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunomodulatory agents, antiangiogenic therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors) {38270968; 37940724; 34107450; 35339649; 35454820; 40119751}. Immunosuppression withdrawal can sometimes cause disease resolution. Combined HAART and systemic chemotherapy improve morbidity and mortality {15280789}. Patients with widespread visceral involvement are commonly poorly responsive to treatment.
