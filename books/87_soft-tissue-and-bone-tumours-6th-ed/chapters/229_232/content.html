2.2.9 Secondary osteosarcoma

Definition

Secondary osteosarcoma is an osteosarcoma arising in abnormal bone.

ICD-O coding

9184/3 Secondary osteosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH06W9 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone (encompasses secondary osteosarcoma)

Related terminology

Acceptable: Paget sarcoma; osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone; radiation-associated osteosarcoma.

Not recommended: postirradiation sarcoma; radiation-induced sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone; radiation-associated osteosarcoma; infarct-related osteosarcoma; osteosarcoma due to chronic osteomyelitis; implant-related osteosarcoma; osteosarcoma secondary to fibrous dysplasia

Localization

Paget sarcoma most commonly affects the femur (34%), pelvis (24%), and humerus (24%) {15018182}, reflecting the distribution of uncomplicated Paget disease of bone (PDB), except for a disproportionately high and low incidence in the humerus (24%) and the spine (2%) {15018182}, respectively. Multifocal synchronous tumours may occur (2–17%) {10929418}. Long-bone lesions tend to be metaphysial {6235535}, and tumours may occur at the site of a previous fracture {2028867}. Radiation-associated osteosarcoma most frequently occurs after radiation of the flat bones of the chest wall and pelvis {10561354}. Bone infarct-related osteosarcoma usually arises in the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal femur, while it is rarely seen in the humerus {1423172}. Implant-associated osteosarcoma mainly occurs in the femur, tibia, humerus, and pelvis {11598166}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

New-onset pain and an enlarging mass in a patient with a history of predisposing bone condition suggest the diagnosis of secondary osteosarcoma. Occasionally, the underlying predisposing condition is unknown. Paget sarcoma typically develops in a background of polyostotic symptomatic PDB {2028867}. Pathological fracture is common {15018182}.

Imaging

Radiological features resemble those of primary osteosarcomas, with a poorly defined lytic lesion, cortical destruction, and (usually) soft tissue extension. The tumour is occasionally mineralized {2028867; 11162693}, and features of the underlying bone condition are also visible. Tumours can be multifocal {10929418}.

Unlike conventional osteosarcoma, a marked periosteal reaction is uncommon in the more aggressive Paget sarcoma. The extraosseous mass in Paget sarcoma is usually non-mineralized, but a minority show matrix mineralization {33386903; 15018182}. Radiation-associated osteosarcoma usually presents several years after treatment, and the sarcoma must be within the radiation field, superimposed on postradiotherapy bone changes {11162693; 30844346}. Infarct-related osteosarcoma develops at the margin of an osteonecrosis area and may obscure the infarction {26397350; 30112253}. Its eccentric location often involves the cortex, increasing pathological fracture risk. Secondary osteosarcoma in the context of fibrous dysplasia and chronic osteomyelitis can show residual evidence of the primary bone abnormality but otherwise appears similar to conventional cases {18560851; 12623051}. Bone dysplasia can be seen in some osteosarcoma predisposing syndromes (e.g. Rothmund–Thomson) {22550413; 29668499}.

Epidemiology

Osteosarcoma is the most common sarcoma associated with PDB {17229010}. PDB develops predominantly in populations of northern Europe {37180975}: 50% of bone sarcomas in patients aged > 50 years are secondary to PDB in endemic areas {15018182}. The incidence of Paget sarcoma in PDB is < 1% {15018182}, but as the incidence of PDB is declining, Paget sarcoma also appears to be less frequent {19064007} and is occurring at older ages {19064007}. Most patients are in the sixth to seventh decades of life (range: 32–86 years) {15018182}.

Radiation-associated osteosarcoma most commonly arises in bone after treatment of tumours with long survival (breast and cervical carcinomas, Hodgkin lymphoma, retinoblastoma) {12057168}. Radiation-associated sarcoma is estimated to occur in 0.03% of patients receiving radiotherapy {12057168}, with osteosarcoma accounting for 21–77% {11162693}. The median latency period after radiotherapy is 10 years {12057168} (range: 3–55 years) {6939953}. Most patients are in the fifth decade of life, with an earlier peak in the second decade of life (children treated for hereditary retinoblastoma and other malignancies) {12057168}. Bone infarct-related osteosarcomas and osteosarcomas arising in longstanding fibrous dysplasia and chronic osteomyelitis are rare {30112253; 18560851}.

Etiology

Ionizing irradiation is the strongest risk factor for developing secondary osteosarcoma. Development of a radiation-associated osteosarcoma appears to be dose dependent, with lower, potentially mutagenic doses at the edge of the radiation field {11162693}. Most patients who develop radiation-associated osteosarcoma have received a median of 50 Gy {12057168}. Bone infarction is an acknowledged cause of undifferentiated sarcoma, and an association between caisson disease, sickle cell disease, other biological insults, and osteosarcoma is now recognized {13009398; 13849880; 1423172}. Sarcomas, including osteosarcoma, infrequently develop adjacent to orthopaedic implants {26931145}. A causal link to the constituents of implanted material, which may be carcinogenic in vitro, has been suggested but not proven {11598166}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of secondary osteosarcoma is likely to be variable and determined by its etiology. Comparison of transcriptome identified a signature of 135 genes discriminating radiation-associated sarcomas from sporadic sarcomas; the functions of the signature genes suggest that radiation-associated osteosarcomas were subject to chronic oxidative stress, probably as a result of mitochondrial dysfunction {21470956}. Two mutation signatures of ionizing radiation – an excess of small deletions and balanced inversions (both aberration types generate driver mutations) – have been identified by paired tumour-normal tissue whole-genome sequencing in radiation-associated malignancies, including radiation-associated osteosarcoma {27615322}. Radiation-associated osteosarcomas feature complex karyotypes {10640139; 28643781; 27615322}. Unlike in primary osteosarcoma, losses are more frequent than gains; in particular, loss at 3p is more common (66% vs 3%) {10640139}. By next-generation sequencing analysis, MYC and FGFR1 gene amplifications and PTPRT missense mutations were more frequent in radiation-associated osteosarcoma than in sporadic osteosarcoma {37350195}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic features of secondary osteosarcoma are not significantly different from those of primary osteosarcoma. Juxtacortical locations have rarely been described {10561354; 24426761}. Macroscopic changes of an underlying disease can be present in the adjacent bone.

Histopathology

The histological features of secondary osteosarcoma are not distinguishable from those of primary osteosarcoma. Osteoblastic and chondroblastic high-grade osteosarcomas are the most common subtypes {25970361}. The bone within or around the tumour can show histological features of an underlying disease.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; known history of previous radiation therapy or the presence of an underlying bone abnormality; osteosarcoma histology.

Desirable: histological evidence of underlying abnormal bone.

Staging

Staging is according to bone sarcoma protocols (see <>TNM staging of tumours of bone). See also the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for Paget sarcoma and radiation-associated osteosarcoma, which are typically high-grade tumours unresponsive to chemotherapy, is worse than that of conventional osteosarcoma. In Paget sarcoma, patient age contributes to poor prognosis: the 2-year survival rate is 25%; 29% of patients have pulmonary metastases at presentation {15018182}.

The 5-year survival rate in radiation-associated osteosarcoma is 10–32% {11162693}; 87% of tumours are high-grade {1365680}, and many patients die from metastatic disease within a few months {3280809}. Prognosis is better in operable extremity lesions {11025461}. The use of alkylating agents {3475572} and younger age at treatment {10561354} increase the risk of radiation-associated osteosarcoma and may reduce the latency period {11162693}.
