2.1.11 Central atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1

Definition

Central atypical cartilaginous tumour/chondrosarcoma, grade 1 (ACT/CS1) is a locally aggressive, hyaline cartilage–producing neoplasm arising in the medulla of bone. Tumours located in the appendicular skeleton (long and short tubular bones) are referred to as ACTs, while those in the axial skeleton (flat bones, including the vertebral column, ribs, sternum, pelvis, scapula, and skull base) are classified as CS1.

Primary conventional central ACT/CS1s arise centrally within the bone without a benign precursor. In contrast, secondary conventional central ACT/CS1s are tumours arising centrally in bone in association with a pre-existing enchondroma.

ICD-O coding

9222/1 Atypical cartilaginous tumour.

9222/3 Chondrosarcoma, grade 1.

ICD-11 coding

2B50.Z & XH0FY0 Chondrosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1.

Related terminology

Acceptable: low-grade central chondrosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None.

Localization

Central ACT/CS1s arise in bones formed by endochondral ossification. The most common locations are the femur (31% of cases overall: 17% proximal, 2.5% midshaft, and 11% distal), the pelvic bones (22% overall, 18% in the ilium), the humerus (11%), the tibia (8%), and the ribs (6%). Involvement of the short tubular bones of the hands and feet is rare. Central ACT/CS1 is also uncommon in the spine and the base of the skull.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients with central ACT/CS1 are generally asymptomatic or may present with pain. Central ACT/CS1s are commonly discovered incidentally during imaging for unrelated reasons. Tumours in the skull base can cause neurological symptoms such as headaches, vision changes, hearing loss, facial numbness, or difficulty in swallowing.

Imaging

In the appendicular skeleton, central ACT shows similar imaging features as enchondroma. On radiographs, ACT is an osteolytic lesion with geographical destruction and often contains popcorn calcifications {26220916; 9308471}. On MRI, ACT is multilobulated and hyperintense/hypointense on T2-/T1-weighted sequences, respectively, with septonodular or rings-and-arcs contrast enhancement {36980590}. ACT can also have entrapped fatty marrow {19547983}. Size, cortical scalloping, and perfusion MRI can help differentiate ACT from enchondroma. Indeed, although no cut-off point exists, ACTs are usually larger (5.5 cm on average) than enchondromas (< 5 cm typically and < 2 cm in 80% of cases) {24337911; 25994192}. Deep endosteal scalloping (> 2/3 of cortical thickness) and fast contrast enhancement on perfusion MRI (within 10 seconds of the start of arterial enhancement) support the diagnosis of ACT over enchondroma {36140587; 28695356; 33641451; 8430192; 10671608; 23771600}. The distinction between central ACT and chondrosarcoma grades 2–3 of long bones is clinically more relevant and more reliable on imaging. The presence of entrapped fatty marrow on MRI and the absence of aggressive features of high-grade chondrosarcoma (detailed in section Central chondrosarcoma, grades 2 and 3) are in keeping with ACT {19547983; 24337911; 32429792}. Diffusion-weighted MRI is of no value in cartilage tumours {25744812}. Finally, as maximum standardized uptake values often overlap, PET/CT has limited ability to differentiate ACT from enchondroma but is more accurate in identifying chondrosarcoma grade 2 and higher {28608242; 32571371}.

In the axial skeleton, enchondromas are rare and cartilaginous tumours are most likely chondrosarcomas {9308471}. Additionally, because of similar treatment strategies (wide resection), an accurate distinction between axial grade 1 and higher-grade chondrosarcomas on preoperative imaging is clinically less relevant than in long bones.

Epidemiology

The estimated population prevalence of enchondroma and ACT/CS1 is 2.8% {25994192}. The incidence varies among studies {30632866; 30217294; 39893587}. In the Netherlands Cancer Registry, it has risen significantly, from 1.2 cases per 1 million person-years in 1989–1996 to 6.63 cases per 1 million person-years in 2005–2013, and this increase may be attributed to an ageing population and increased use of diagnostic imaging {30217294}. Conventional central ACT/CS1 is seen mainly in adults in the third to sixth decades of life, with equal sex distribution. Of the central cartilage tumours, 75% are found in patients aged 21–75 years (median: 49 years). Patients with tumours arising in enchondromatosis are generally younger than patients with primary tumours. Central ACT/CS1s (85–90%) are much more common than peripheral ACT/CS1s (10–15%).

Etiology

Patients with enchondromatosis (see section Enchondromatosis (IDH1, IDH2)), carrying a somatic mosaic mutation in IDH1 or IDH2 {22057236; 22057234}, have an elevated risk of progression to central ACT/CS1, depending on tumour location. The overall risk is approximately 40%, but it is significantly lower (15%) in patients with lesions confined to the hands and feet. In contrast, those with involvement of both small bones and long or flat bones face a 46% risk {22147000}. The risk is particularly high when enchondromas are located in the pelvis {22147000}.

Pathogenesis

Similar to enchondromas, about 50% of primary central ACT/CS1s and as many as 78% of secondary central ACT/CS1s (in enchondromatosis) harbour somatic mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase genes IDH1 and IDH2 (see section Enchondroma) {21598255; 22057234; 22057236}. Partial haploidization with or without subsequent genome doubling is more frequently found in the IDH wildtype subgroup {36042521; 19336518; 21949816}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tissue fragments obtained by curettage typically consist of translucent, bluish-grey or greyish-white material, often admixed with red bone marrow. Yellowish-white, chalky foci of calcification are frequently present. In resection specimens, the tumour is usually sharply demarcated and may exhibit cortical erosion, correlating with the cortical scalloping observed on imaging. Soft tissue extension is very rare in central ACT/CS1 and probably indicates a higher-grade tumour. Extensive sampling is essential to exclude areas of higher histological grade or dedifferentiation.

Histopathology

Central ACT/CS1 is characterized by an abundant hyaline cartilage matrix and a lobulated growth pattern, with lobules that are often irregular in shape and variable in size. These lobules may be separated by fibrous bands containing small vessels. The lobular architecture can lead to cortical thinning, which manifests radiographically as cortical scalloping. Unlike in slow-growing enchondromas, which often exhibit an encasement pattern (deposition of lamellar bone around tumour lobules) {4064409}, this feature is uncommon in central ACT/CS1. Instead, the tumour typically shows permeation and entrapment of pre-existing lamellar bone trabeculae (a hallmark of more rapid growth), although this can be challenging to identify in fragmented curettage specimens {4064409; 18852676}. Entrapment is defined as tumour entirely surrounding a normal medullary bone trabecula. Invasion into the Haversian system may be seen.

Cellularity in central ACT/CS1 is low but slightly higher than that in enchondroma. Tumour cell nuclei may appear small and hyperchromatic (lymphocyte-like) or show more open chromatin with visible nucleoli; nuclear size is generally uniform. Binucleation is common, whereas mitotic figures are absent. The surrounding matrix is predominantly hyaline, occasionally displaying mucoid or myxoid change. Necrosis may be present. Residual areas of pre-existing enchondroma with extensive calcification may be recognized in central ACT/CS1.

Immunohistochemistry

The tumour cells show strong expression of S100 and are negative for brachyury. The p.(Arg132His) (R132H) variant–specific IDH1 antibody only recognizes a small percentage of the IDH variants in central ACT/CS1, limiting its diagnostic utility {21598255; 22057234; 22057236}.

Differential diagnosis

There is an overlap between the histological features of enchondroma and central ACT/CS1. The distinction is made by the higher cellularity, irregular cell distribution, and the presence of binucleated cells in central ACT/CS1. A very important criterion is the growth pattern, in which the presence of entrapment (of pre-existing lamellar bone) supports a diagnosis of central ACT/CS1 {4064409; 18852676}. Additionally, the presence of ≥ 20% myxoid changes in the matrix favours the diagnosis of ACT/CS1 over enchondroma {18852676}. This distinction can be challenging on small biopsy specimens, making clinicoradiological correlation essential. Both the anatomical site and the patient's age are important diagnostic considerations {28797501}. For example, if the tumour is located in the medullary cavity of a long tubular bone, features such as increased cellularity, irregular cell distribution, more open chromatin with visible nucleoli, and binucleation are indicative of central ACT/CS1. However, in phalangeal bones, these same features may be seen in benign enchondromas and are not indicative of aggressive behaviour {10547545}. Nevertheless, even in these sites, mitotic activity, cortical destruction, and soft tissue extension are incompatible with enchondroma, although these tumours (ACTs and chondrosarcomas) in the phalanges (but not the metacarpal bones) behave locally aggressively with minimal metastatic potential irrespective of histological grade {10547545; 14599838} and may be managed with less invasive surgery. Similarly, in patients with enchondromatosis or in tumours arising in patients before puberty (before growth plate fusion), increased cellularity, more-open chromatin, and the presence of binucleated cells do not exclude the diagnosis of enchondroma {22555180}. Thus, a multidisciplinary approach with clinicoradiological correlation is often indispensable.

Grade 2/3 conventional central chondrosarcoma is distinguished from ACT/CS1 on the basis of its increased cellularity, the presence of mitoses, myxoid matrix degeneration, spindle cell changes at the periphery of the lobules, increased vascularity, and increased nuclear pleomorphism.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Hotspot mutations are exclusively found at the IDH1 p.Arg132 and the IDH2 p.Arg172 positions. The IDH1 p.(Arg132Cys) (R132C) mutation is the most frequently found mutation. About 50% of primary central ACT/CS1s and as many as 78% of central ACT/CS1s occurring in enchondromatosis have IDH1 or IDH2 mutations {21598255; 22057234; 22057236}. Mutations at the IDH1 p.Arg140 position are extremely rare {29581779}. In the rare occurrence in the skull base, the mutation frequency is very high (85.7%), whereas mutations are rare to absent in the laryngeal and tracheal tumours (11.8%) {30296521}. Tumours in the flat bones are more often IDH-wildtype. IDH-wildtype tumours also tend to be diagnosed in a younger population than IDH-mutant tumours.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: location in the medulla of bone; abundance of cartilaginous matrix, often hyaline, sometimes with myxoid changes; lobular growth pattern with entrapment of pre-existing lamellar bone trabeculae; increased cellularity, with small, condensed nuclei; binucleated cells are common; absence of mitoses and severe nuclear atypia.

Staging

Local staging is only indicated for CS1.

Prognosis and prediction

Central ACT/CS1 exhibits locally aggressive behaviour. Residual disease or local recurrence occurs in 7.5–11% of cases, in contrast to 0% in enchondroma {28501961; 25086127; 22649023}. A recent study has shown that curettage for ACT does not appear to be associated with mortality or malignant progression {39713913}. Reported 5-year overall survival rates for ACT/CS1 are 87–99%, and 10-year overall survival rates are 88–95% {24706044; 22649023; 19411454; 30143018; 890662; 30217294}. The metastatic potential is nearly non-existent {37835390}.

In the long bones, central ACT is treated with curettage and local adjuvants, resulting in favourable outcomes {18378543}. In some patients, active surveillance for ACT may be considered {34439246; 38160684}. In contrast, tumours located in the axial skeleton are associated with a worse prognosis {25687868; 30143018; 30217294}. Therefore, CS1s of the pelvis and spine are treated with surgical excision and negative margins. For skull base CS1, the mortality rate is 5%. A large proportion of the mortality of CS1 is attributed to intracranial or axial tumours that are difficult to treat because of their location. Therefore, the location determines whether to diagnose ACT (in the appendicular skeleton) or CS1 (in the axial skeleton and pelvis), to better reflect clinical behaviour and prognosis {28797501}. Currently, the prognostic significance of IDH mutations remains uncertain {21598255; 29581779; 22057234; 28484589}.
