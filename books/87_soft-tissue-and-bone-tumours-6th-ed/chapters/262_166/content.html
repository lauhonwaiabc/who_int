2.6.2 Conventional chordoma

Definition

Conventional chordoma is a malignant tumour demonstrating notochordal differentiation that almost exclusively arises in bones of the axial skeleton.

ICD-O coding

9370/3 Conventional chordoma

ICD-11 coding

2B5K & XH9GH0 Unspecified malignant soft tissue tumours or sarcomas of bone or articular cartilage of other or unspecified sites & Chordoma NOS

2B5K & XH17D8 Unspecified malignant soft tissue tumours or sarcomas of bone or articular cartilage of other or unspecified sites & Chondroid chordoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Chordomas primarily arise in the axial skeleton, involving bones from the base of the skull to the coccyx. The most common site is the sacrum, followed by the skull base, the craniovertebral junction, and in the mobile spine. Rarely, extra-axial {18301055; 23588366; 29560513}, extraskeletal, and axial multicentric chordomas {21712742} have been reported. Tumours in children and young adults show a predilection for the skull base and cervical vertebrae. Chondroid chordoma predominantly occurs at the skull base.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Chordomas most commonly present with pain and site-related neurological symptoms.

Imaging

Chordoma typically presents as a midline osteolytic lesion with soft tissue extension. Skull base chordoma is midline (85%) and located in the upper clivus, often extending to the lower clivus, posterior clinoid process, cavernous sinus, and occipital condyle. The thumb sign (thumbing of the pons) is a relatively specific radiological sign {29629241}. Sacrococcygeal chordoma commonly involves the lower sacrum (fourth and fifth segments) and coccyx. CT shows a destructive lytic bone lesion with soft tissue extension and sometimes scattered intratumoural calcifications (sequestra of normal bone) {38967687; 36128361}. MRI shows a lobulated mass with low-isointense signal intensity on T1-weighted MRI, frequently with internal hyperintense foci due to intratumoural haemorrhage and/or increased proteinaceous contents {38967687; 36128361}. T2-weighted MRI shows marked hyperintensity (abundant extracellular myxoid matrix) with hypointense internal septations. Postgadolinium enhancement is heterogeneous, moderate to marked in a variable honeycomb pattern (linear enhancement with poorly enhancing myxoid areas) {33806339}. However, chordomas can show mild or no enhancement {35278932}. The degree of contrast enhancement may correlate with aggressive tumour behaviour and poorer prognosis {33806339}. Chondrosarcoma has a higher mean apparent diffusion coefficient value (2051 ± 261 × 10−6 mm2/s) than chordoma (1474 ± 117 × 10−6 mm2/s or lower) {23124635; 35953276; 38967687}. Chordoma shows low activity on 18F-FDG PET {33521875; 28184416}.

Epidemiology

Chordomas are rare tumours and account for only 3% of all bone tumours. Their incidence is estimated at 0.8–1.1 cases per 1 million inhabitants per year, with published cases describing a male predominance {30220042; 38277716}. Although chordoma can occur at any age, it is most commonly reported in patients between the fifth and seventh decades of life.

Etiology

In rare cases, chordoma is associated with a germline tandem duplication of the TBXT gene {24990759}, and rare cases of childhood chordoma have been reported in the setting of tuberous sclerosis, which results from germline loss-of-function variants in the tumour suppressor gene TSC1 or TSC2 {15236319; 21266383}. Some chordomas appear to arise in association with a benign notochordal cell tumour {40323130; 17895760}.

Pathogenesis

The hallmark of chordoma is the expression of brachyury (encoded by TBXT) {16538613; 18301055}: in 27% of cases, this is associated with copy-number gain of TBXT, a transcription factor required for notochordal development {21171078; 29026114}. This recapitulates the tandem duplication of TBXT that underlies familial chordoma {29026114; 19801981}. The strong association of rs2305089 in TBXT in patients with chordoma and benign notochordal cell tumour makes a strong case that this SNP contributes substantially to the development of chordoma {23064415; 40323130}. Brachyury has also been shown to act as a master regulator of an elaborate oncogenic transcriptional network encompassing diverse signalling pathways, including components of the cell cycle and extracellular matrix {22847733}. Finally, growth arrest and senescence upon silencing of TBXT in chordoma cell lines add to the critical role of TBXT in chordoma {21171078}.

The tumours typically exhibit low mutation burden and large copy-number losses, particularly involving chromosomes 1p, 3, 9, 10, 13, 14, and 18 and gains of chromosomes 1q, 7p, and 7q {21602918; 24983247; 29026114}. In addition, PI3K signalling variants have been reported in 16% of cases, and mutations (always inactivating) of LYST have been described in 10% of cases {29026114}. Phosphorylated and total EGFR (HER1) appears to play an important role in the disease because it is expressed in 47% and 67% of chordomas, respectively, and EGFR (HER1) inhibitors reduce cell survival {21171079; 27102572}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, chordoma is a lobular solid mass with a gelatinous appearance. It destroys bone and extends into the surrounding soft tissue. Sacrococcygeal tumours tend to be larger than those at other sites, probably because of longer symptom-free interval before diagnosis.

Histopathology

Conventional chordoma is composed of large epithelioid cells with clear to light eosinophilic cytoplasm, arranged in lobules separated by fibrous septa frequently containing chronic inflammatory cells. The tumour cells may have bubbly cytoplasm (physaliphorous cells). They are arranged as cords and nests embedded within an abundant extracellular myxoid matrix or as more densely arranged epithelioid packets. Chordomas often show a substantial degree of intratumoural cytological heterogeneity, with features including nuclear atypia and pleomorphism ranging from minimal (usually associated with a low mitotic activity) to (less commonly) severe, in which bizarre nuclei or cell spindling can be seen. In the latter, mitotic figures can be detected, and large areas of tumour necrosis may be present. The term “chondroid chordoma” refers to a conventional chordoma in which areas of the matrix mimic a hyaline cartilaginous tumour {7506477; 16819322}.

Immunohistochemistry

The tumour is immunoreactive for brachyury, cytokeratin, and EMA and shows variable S100 positivity. Brachyury staining is nuclear and typically diffuse. While brachyury is a highly specific marker for notochordal differentiation, rare expression has been reported in other tumour types {39929118}. The vast majority show normal expression of SMARCB1 (INI1).

Differential diagnosis

The diagnostic hallmark of chordoma is the nuclear expression of brachyury, which helps distinguish it from other tumours such as metastatic carcinoma, chondrosarcoma, chordoid meningioma, and myoepithelial tumour of bone.

Cytology

Conventional chordoma smears show polygonal/epithelioid cells arranged in clusters within an abundant myxoid extracellular matrix, which is granular and metachromatic (Giemsa stain), forming discohesive tissue fragments. Tumour cells are round and occasionally binucleated, with varying granular or vacuolated cytoplasm. Atypia and pleomorphism can be prominent, especially in poorly differentiated chordoma {12685190; 30218622; 39735361; 40879100}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; presence of notochordal differentiation (nuclear brachyury expression); cells are usually embedded in abundant extracellular matrix.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) or American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system is applicable.

Prognosis and prediction

Conventional chordoma is usually treated with surgery and/or radiation therapy, with medical therapy reserved for patients with metastatic disease {30497218; 30445184; 40204245}. Skull base lesions are treated with intralesional resection, whereas mobile spine and sacral chordomas should be resected with negative margins {38691860}. The overall median survival time is 7 years. Chordomas have high rates of local recurrence and are locally aggressive, but metastases are relatively uncommon at presentation. Approximately 10–20% of chordomas metastasize, more commonly for tumours arising in the sacrum and the mobile spine than for skull base tumours. Lung is the favoured site for metastasis (> 50%), followed by bone, and metastatic disease most commonly occurs in the youngest patients (< 25 years old). It is more frequent among patients after local recurrence {26843835}. Because of this, chest radiograph or chest CT are usually the only examinations carried out in the workup of conventional chordomas {32801018}.
