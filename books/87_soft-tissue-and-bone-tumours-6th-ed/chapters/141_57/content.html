1.4.1 Tenosynovial giant cell tumour

Definition

Tenosynovial giant cell tumour is a benign neoplasm with potential for recurrence, most often arising from the synovium of joints, bursae, and tendon sheaths, and is composed of a variable admixture of mononuclear cells (small histiocyte-like cells and larger epithelioid cells showing synovial differentiation), multinucleated giant cells, foamy macrophages, and inflammatory cells, with haemosiderin deposition and stromal collagenization.

Malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumour is defined by the coexistence of a benign tenosynovial giant cell tumour with overtly malignant areas or by recurrence of a conventional tenosynovial giant cell tumour as a sarcoma.

Related terminology

Acceptable: giant cell tumour of tendon sheath.

Not recommended: pigmented villonodular synovitis.

Subtype(s)

Localized; diffuse.

Localization

Localized tenosynovial giant cell tumours occur predominantly in the hand. Approximately 85% of the tumours occur in the fingers, in proximity to the synovium of the tendon sheath or interphalangeal joint. The lesions may erode bone or involve the skin. Other sites include the wrist, ankle, foot, knee, and rarely the elbow and hip. Localized lesions may seldom be found in large joints {11328844; 28787222; 3943019}. Intra-articular diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours most commonly affect the knee (75% of cases), followed by the hip, ankle, elbow, and shoulder {2791393; 20884045; 25799575; 28787222}, and rarely the temporomandibular and spinal facet joints. Extra-articular tumours most commonly involve the knee, thigh, and foot, and less commonly the finger, wrist, groin, elbow, and toe. Although most extra-articular tumours are located in periarticular soft tissues, they can be purely intramuscular or subcutaneous {10757395}. Most malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumours involve the lower limbs, with a strong predilection for the knee. Other locations include the hip, ankle, fingers, wrist, and pelvic area {30206409; 30594158; 9042281; 18301053}.

Clinical features

These tumours can be divided on the basis of their site (intra- or extra-articular) and growth pattern (localized or diffuse) into two main subtypes, which differ in their clinical features and biological behaviour but appear to share a common pathogenesis. Localized tenosynovial giant cell tumours present as a painless mass that develops gradually over a long period. Diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours are associated with pain, tenderness, swelling, or limitation of motion. Haemorrhagic joint effusions are common. The symptoms are usually of relatively long duration (often several years) {10757395; 28442184; 20884045}. Repeated local recurrences can be destructive, leading to major functional loss {26052431}. Radiographically, most tumours present as poorly defined periarticular masses, frequently associated with degenerative joint disease and cystic lesions in the adjacent bone (often on both sides of the joint). On MRI, tenosynovial giant cell tumours show decreased signal intensity in both T1- and T2-weighted images, with artefacts from haemosiderin deposition {18794322; 26440548}. Malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumours can arise de novo or occur after multiple recurrences of a conventional tenosynovial giant cell tumour {31029509; 30206409; 30594158; 9042281; 18301053}.

Epidemiology

The estimated incidence rates for tumours of the digits, localized tumours of the extremities, and diffuse tumours are 29, 10, and 4 cases per 1 million person-years, respectively {28787222}. The localized form is more common than the diffuse form. Although tumours occur at any age, the localized form usually presents in patients aged 30–50 years, with a female predominance (M:F ratio: 0.5:1) {23185646; 28787222; 3943019}. The diffuse subtype usually affects young adults (< 40 years of age), with a slight female predominance {10757395; 20884045; 7412554}. Malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumour is exceedingly uncommon, with only 50 reported cases; most patients are adults aged 50–60 years {30206409; 30594158; 9042281; 18301053}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Cytogenetic and molecular studies have demonstrated relatively simple structural rearrangements involving CSF1, which encodes colony stimulating factor 1, in approximately 75% of cases; CSF1 fusions involve diverse partner genes with deletion of the 3′ untranslated region, leading to increased CSF1 protein synthesis {16407111; 17527089; 31469468; 36504457}. Only a small subset of cells in tenosynovial giant cell tumours are neoplastic and harbour the CSF1 translocation, while the majority of cells consist of inflammatory cells including macrophages that are recruited as a result of the high CSF1 levels {17527089; 16407111; 30152874; 36007098}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, most tenosynovial giant cell tumours of localized subtype are small (5–40 mm), although lesions of greater size may be found in large joints. Tumours are typically circumscribed, lobulated, and white to grey with yellowish and brown areas. Diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours are usually large (often > 50 mm), firm, or sponge-like. The villous pattern is usually present in intra-articular tumours, whereas extra-articular tumours appear multinodular with variegated colours, with alternation of white, yellowish, and brownish areas {18794322}. Malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumours are generally large, fleshy, and poorly marginated, with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis {18301053; 30206409}.

Histopathology

The microscopic appearance of tenosynovial giant cell tumours is variable, depending on the proportions of mononuclear cells, multinucleated giant cells, foamy macrophages, inflammatory cells, and haemosiderin, as well as the degree of stromal collagenization. Two principal cell types are identified within the mononuclear component: small histiocyte-like cells, with pale cytoplasm and round or reniform nuclei, and larger epithelioid cells, with amphophilic cytoplasm, rounded vesicular nuclei, and frequently a peripheral rim of haemosiderin granules (ladybird cells) {36007098}. While most tumours contain a majority of small histiocyte-like cells, larger cells may predominate. In both types, mitotic activity may be brisk and necrosis can be present.

Localized tenosynovial giant cell tumours are lobulated and circumscribed. Osteoclast-like giant cells are typically apparent but can be inconspicuous in some tumours. Xanthoma cells are frequent, tend to aggregate locally near the periphery of nodules, and may be associated with cholesterol clefts. Haemosiderin deposits are virtually always identified. The stroma shows variable degrees of hyalinization {11328844; 4303016; 3943019}.

Diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours are infiltrative and grow as expansile sheets. Osteoclast-like giant cells are less common in the diffuse form than in the localized form, and they may be rare to absent in as many as 20% of cases. Cleft-like spaces are common and appear either as artefactual tears or as synovial-lined spaces. Blood-filled pseudoalveolar spaces are seen in approximately 10% of cases {10757395; 3943019; 18794322}.

Most malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumours are composed of sheets and nodules of enlarged mononuclear cells and exhibit significantly increased mitotic count (including atypical mitoses), necrosis, cytological atypia including nuclear enlargement and nucleoli, spindling of mononucleated cells, and myxoid changes. These tumours can display areas resembling undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma or myxofibrosarcoma {30206409; 30594158; 9042281; 18301053}.

By immunohistochemistry, the larger mononuclear cells express clusterin, and in 45–80% of cases a small subset of these cells stain for desmin, which highlights their dendritic processes. The smaller histiocyte-like cells are positive for CD68, CD163, and CD45 {19542874; 7628850}.

Cytology

Cytology aspirates can be diagnostic and show mainly single cells with occasional mix of polygonal large mononuclear cells with eccentric cytoplasm and bland nuclei, osteoclast-type giant cells, foamy histiocytes, lymphocytes, and haemosiderin-laden macrophages. Focal necrosis and plentiful mitoses (but no atypical mitoses) are present {28324880; 12889051}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: varying proportions of small histiocytic cells, larger epithelioid synoviocytic cells, foam cells, and multinucleated giant cells; for malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumour: coexistence of a benign tenosynovial giant cell tumour with overtly malignant areas or recurrence of a conventional tenosynovial giant cell tumour as a sarcoma

Desirable: expression of clusterin in select cases.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumour of localized subtype is benign with a capacity for local recurrence. Although 4–30% of cases recur, these recurrences are usually non-destructive and are controlled by surgical re-excision {28787222; 3943019; 11328844; 4303016}. Recurrences are more common in diffuse-subtype tumours, and repeated recurrences may severely compromise joint function. The recurrence rate has been estimated at 40–60% {31029509}. In addition, rare cases with conventional histology may develop metastatic disease (in the lungs or lymph nodes) {28787222; 10757395; 20884045; 28442184}. Malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumour is aggressive with substantial mortality (roughly one third of cases). Approximately 50% of cases metastasize to lymph nodes (inguinal and pelvic) and the lungs {30206409; 30594158; 9042281; 18301053}. Surgery is the mainstay of treatment of tenosynovial giant cell tumours. Targeted therapy directed against the CSF1/CSF1R pathway has shown promising results for unresectable or metastasizing tumours {21823110; 26179200; 26222558; 38843860}.
