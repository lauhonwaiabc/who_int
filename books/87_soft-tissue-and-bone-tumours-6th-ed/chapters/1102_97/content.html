1.10.2 Extraskeletal osteosarcoma

Definition

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma is a malignant neoplasm characterized by the production of bone matrix by neoplastic cells, without connection to the skeletal system and without origin from an underlying neoplasm.

ICD-O coding

9180/3 Osteosarcoma, extraskeletal

ICD-11 coding

2B5F.2 & XH2CD6 Sarcoma, not elsewhere classified of other specified sites & Osteosarcoma, extraskeletal

Related terminology

Acceptable: extraosseous osteosarcoma; soft tissue osteosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The majority of extraskeletal osteosarcomas present as deep-seated soft tissue masses, but occasionally they originate in the dermis or subcutis. They most commonly arise in the limbs, with the lower extremity being the most common location (27–52%); other frequent sites include the buttock, shoulder, trunk, and retroperitoneum {3475157; 2160317; 8635029; 9591729; 29502131}.

Clinical features

Most patients present with a progressively enlarging mass that may be painful {3475157; 2160317; 8635029; 9591729; 29502131}. Some present with lung metastases. Plain radiographs, CT, and MRI usually reveal a large deep-seated soft tissue mass with variable mineralization {22194512; 27528844}. The tumours heterogeneously enhance on postcontrast imaging. By definition, these tumours do not arise or metastasize from bone, but they may secondarily involve the skeleton.

Epidemiology

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma accounts for < 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas and approximately 5% of all osteosarcomas {6571801; 18521290; 24382730; 4507677; 346199}. It typically arises during midlife and late adulthood, between 50 and 60 years, but with a wide age range (12–90 years); occurrence in children is uncommon. There is no clear sex predilection (M:F ratio: 0.8–1.9:1) {3475157; 8635029; 28167373; 25294959; 39762371}.

Etiology

Most cases develop de novo, but approximately 5–10% of cases are associated with previous radiation {3475157; 6571801; 8635029; 24382730}.

Pathogenesis

High-grade extraskeletal osteosarcoma has shown clonal highly complex aberrations by conventional karyotyping {9591630; 9352795}. SNP array and targeted next-generation sequencing has identified recurrent losses in tumour suppressor genes (CDKN2A, TP53, RB1, PTEN, NF1, LSAMP); gains/amplifications in oncogenes (RUNX2, CDC5L, COPS3, EGFR, MDM2, CDK4, PMP22); and mutations involving tumour suppressor genes (TP53, PTEN, RB1, NF1, SMARCA4), chromatin-remodelling genes (ATRX, DAXX, BRCA1, DAXX2, CHEK2, ARID5B), histone methylation and demethylation genes (BCOR, DNMT3A, MAP2K3), and WNT/β-catenin and sonic hedgehog pathway genes (AMER1, AXIN2, GSK3B, GLI1, NOTCH3) {27499911}. TERT promoter and PIK3CA mutations have also been reported {27499911}. The genomic profiles of extraskeletal osteosarcoma show features overlapping those of conventional skeletal osteosarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma. Chromothripsis and kataegis are observed less frequently than in conventional osteosarcoma {27499911; 33949149}. MDM2 amplification has been rarely reported in low-grade extraskeletal osteosarcoma, but this has not been a consistent finding {29489027; 28235629}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours range in size from 10 to 500 mm (mean: 80–100 mm) and vary considerably in gross appearance; many tumours are circumscribed, tan-white, haemorrhagic, and necrotic {3475157; 2160317}. They may show gritty areas of mineralization to heavily mineralized bone formation. Extensive haemorrhagic cystic change is present in a minority of cases.

Histopathology

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma shows a broad spectrum of morphology. All histological patterns of conventional osteosarcoma in bone have been described. The osteoblastic pattern is the most common {3475157; 2160317; 8635029; 9591729; 27499911}, whereas chondroblastic and fibroblastic patterns can also be observed. In general, the tumours are composed of spindle, polygonal/epithelioid/plasmacytoid, or anaplastic cells. Mitotic activity is typically brisk and atypical mitotic figures may be identified. Tumour necrosis is also a common feature.

Identification of the neoplastic bone intimately associated with tumour cells is necessary for diagnosis. Bone is typically deposited in lace-like, ribbon-like, trabecular, or sheet-like patterns. It is sometimes more prominent in the centre of the tumour, with the more densely cellular areas located in the periphery, a pattern that is the reverse of myositis ossificans. Neoplastic bone/osteoid must be distinguished from fibrillary collagen deposition (pseudo-osteoid) {6277450; 3475157}. The amount and distribution of the neoplastic bone vary, and there is no minimum amount of neoplastic bone that is required for diagnosis.

SATB2 immunoreactivity could aid in detecting osteoblastic differentiation when bone is difficult to distinguish from collagenous stroma {23701429; 28235629}, but the staining is not specific {26644288}.

The rare well-differentiated examples contain abundant bone deposited in well-formed trabeculae, surrounded by a minimally atypical spindle cell component similar to parosteal or low-grade central osteosarcoma; high-grade transformation or dedifferentiation has been rarely reported {1929787; 15892008}. Similar to parosteal and low-grade central osteosarcoma, they may express MDM2 {26009571; 28235629; 29489027}.

Cytology

Cytology aspirates show high cellularity with variably spindle, epithelioid, and atypical osteoblasts with eccentric cytoplasm {9678733; 12203867; 17427216}. There may be an intermixed population of osteoclast-type giant cells. Eosinophilic (Pap) or orange (Giemsa) osteoid is present with variable bone matrix.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant cells in association with neoplastic bone; no connection to the skeletal system; exclusion of heterologous osteoblastic differentiation of other tumour types.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging can be applied.

Prognosis and prediction

Studies have shown, with complete resection, an overall survival of 37–66% at 5 years in the non-metastatic setting and a 5-year disease-free survival of 43–82% {8635029; 11786582; 17909926; 18521290; 24382730; 28167373}. Multivariate analysis showed that patient age, tumour size, positive surgical margin, axial tumour site, metastasis, and treatment with chemotherapy are significant prognostic factors for overall survival {24401667; 25294959; 27499911; 28167373}. Tumours harbouring simultaneous TP53 and RB1 copy-number losses or loss of CDKN2A were associated with worse overall survival and/or local recurrence {27499911}.
