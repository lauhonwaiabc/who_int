1.6.2 Myopericytoma, including myofibroma

Definition

Myopericytoma is a distinctive perivascular myoid neoplasm that forms a morphological spectrum with myofibroma. Rarely, myopericytomas may show atypical or malignant features.

ICD-O coding

8824/0 Myopericytoma

8824/1 Myofibromatosis

ICD-11 coding

XH2HE9 & XH0953 Myopericytoma & Myofibroma

Related terminology

Not recommended: infantile haemangiopericytoma.

Subtype(s)

Myopericytomatosis; myofibromatosis; infantile myofibromatosis

Localization

Myopericytoma generally arises in the dermis or subcutis, and it only rarely involves deep soft tissue. The distal extremities are most commonly involved, followed by the proximal extremities, neck, trunk, and oral cavity {17252599; 18154577}. Very rarely, these neoplasms arise in visceral locations and intracranial sites {20655090; 16281913}. The majority of solitary myofibromas arise in the skin of the extremities, head and neck, and trunk {8514276; 7284977}. Myofibromatosis in infancy may involve viscera, including the liver, heart, gastrointestinal tract, brain, and bone {7284977}.

Clinical features

Myopericytoma usually presents as a painless, slow-growing, superficially located nodule, which can be present for years. Most cases arise as a solitary lesion, but multiple lesions involving a particular anatomical region or different regions are sometimes seen {16330949; 9591720; 20123399}. Myofibromas may present as solitary or multicentric lesions (myofibromatosis). Lesions arising congenitally or in the first years of life may involve viscera or bone {16330949}.

Epidemiology

Myopericytoma may occur at any age; however, most cases are seen in adults {16330949}. Myofibroma is rare and may be present at birth, appear in the first 2 years of life, or arise in adults with a male predominance {10630943; 23387453; 30871614}.

Etiology

A subset of solitary and multiple (non-visceral) forms of infantile myofibromatosis are familial, following an autosomal dominant mode of inheritance with variable penetrance and expressivity {12894106; 3236168; 21834820}. However, some reports have suggested instead an autosomal recessive inheritance {11260217}. Germline variants in the PDGFRB gene have been shown to underlie familial, autosomal dominant infantile myofibromatosis {23731537; 23731542}. Germline mutation in NOTCH3, a gene implicated in the pathogenesis of some glomus tumours {23999936}, has also been reported in a familial myofibromatosis kindred {23731542}. An association between myopericytoma and EBV has been reported in patients with AIDS {19675451}.

Pathogenesis

Variants in the PDGFRB gene appear to represent a common pathogenesis for myopericytoma, myopericytomatosis, and myofibroma {28505006; 27776010}. Variants in the NOTCH3 gene may also be associated with myopericytoma, myopericytomatosis, and myofibroma {31566039; 36788105}.

Macroscopic appearance

In superficial locations, myopericytoma tends to be well circumscribed, with nodules measuring < 20 mm in diameter; larger neoplasms may be seen in deep soft tissues {16330949}. Myofibromas typically present as well-circumscribed nodular lesions, ranging from 3 to 105 mm in greatest diameter, with a median size of approximately 30 mm {22486319}. The lesions tend to be larger in younger patients.

Histopathology

Myopericytomas are unencapsulated, usually well-circumscribed, nodular or lobular lesions composed of cytologically bland, oval to spindle-shaped, myoid tumour cells with characteristic multilayered, concentric growth around numerous small vessels. The tumours show variable cellularity, ranging from cellular and solid-appearing to hypocellular and collagenous/myxoid. Lesional blood vessels tend to be numerous and variable in size; branching, haemangiopericytoma-like vessels may be present. Blood vessels outside of the tumour mass may show a similar concentric perivascular proliferation of myoid, spindled cells {16330949}. In some cases, a more prominent fascicular or whorled arrangement of neoplastic cells resembling myofibroma or angioleiomyoma is present {16394283; 17270242}. Intravascular myopericytomas have been reported {16330949; 12358814}. Some myopericytomas have glomus-like features or combined morphology with angioleiomyoma {9591720; 36788105}. Rarely, myopericytomas may show degenerative features, including symplastic nuclear atypia, stromal hyalinization, and cystic change {16330949}. A very unusual process of diffuse dermal and subcutaneous involvement by numerous microscopic myopericytomatous nodules has been reported under the term “myopericytomatosis” {28505006}. Rare malignant myopericytomas occur mainly in the deep soft tissues, as infiltrating neoplasms showing marked nuclear atypia and a high mitotic count, in addition to features more typical of myopericytoma {12405913}. By immunohistochemistry, myopericytomas express SMA, h-caldesmon, and collagen IV, and they are at most only focally positive for desmin and/or CD34.

Solitary myofibromas or lesions in a multicentric form (myofibromatosis) are nodular, well-circumscribed neoplasms characterized by a distinctive biphasic growth pattern. Classically, the centre of the lesion is composed of immature-appearing, plump, spindled tumour cells associated with a haemangiopericytoma-like branching vasculature, whereas the periphery of the lesion consists of nodules and fascicles of variably hyalinized, myoid, or even chondroid-appearing cells. This pattern of zonation may be more haphazard or even reversed in older children and adults {8514276}. Mitotic figures are variable in number and, especially in the immature-appearing tumour areas, may be numerous. The cellular zones of myofibroma may undergo necrosis and calcification. Particularly in infants, myofibromas may be composed almost entirely of these more primitive, cellular zones; such cases were historically labelled infantile haemangiopericytoma {8067513}. The morphological features of the cellular zones of myofibroma share some features with myopericytoma, suggesting that these are related entities. Intravascular extension is commonly seen and does not imply malignancy. Immunohistochemically, both components of myofibroma express SMAs and collagen IV and are usually negative for desmin and h-caldesmon.

Rare myofibromas/myopericytomas show atypical features, in particular high cellularity, a predominantly fascicular pattern with inapparent myoid nodules, infiltrative growth, and perineurial invasion {24921644}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for myopericytoma: bland, myoid-appearing spindled cells growing in a concentric pattern around numerous small vessels; for myofibroma: biphasic growth pattern, with primitive cellular zones often showing necrosis, calcification, and mitotic activity, surrounded by hyalinized nodules of myoid spindled cells

Desirable: immunohistochemistry for SMA.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Most myopericytomas do not recur even if marginally or incompletely excised. Local recurrences probably represent persistent rather than truly recurrent growth. Extremely rare malignant myopericytomas have shown aggressive growth and distant spread, with poor clinical outcome {12405913}. The overall prognosis for solitary myofibromas is excellent, and these tumours do not recur locally or metastasize. Infants with multicentric or generalized myofibromatosis involving viscera and brain have a worse outcome {7284977}. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as imatinib, may be effective in cases of paediatric myofibroma and myofibromatosis carrying PDGFRB variants {31017643}.
