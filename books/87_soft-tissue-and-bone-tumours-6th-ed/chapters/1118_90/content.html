1.11.8 Benign triton tumour / neuromuscular choristoma

Definition

Neuromuscular choristoma (NMC) is a fusiform expansion of peripheral nerve containing heterotopic mature skeletal muscle cells intermixed with native Schwann cells, axons, and perineurium.

ICD-O coding

XXX

ICD-11 coding

2F38 & XH98Z3 Benign neoplasm of other or unspecified sites & Schwannoma (neurilemmoma)

Related terminology

Acceptable: neuromuscular hamartoma; nerve rhabdomyoma; intraneural rhabdomyoma/choristoma.

Not recommended: ectomesenchymal hamartoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Classic cases involve proximal large nerves or plexuses, most commonly the sciatic nerve and the brachial plexus, although cranial nerve and intraorbital examples have also been reported {28168739; 35972536}.

Clinical features

Patients present with either progressive pain or classic features of peripheral neuropathy. Most cases involve a proximal large nerve or plexus, often associated with limb atrophy/undergrowth. On MRI, NMC features signal intensities similar to those of adjacent skeletal muscle and, in contrast to lipomatosis of the nerve, has limited (< 50%) interfascicular fat; it also has higher signal than normal nerve on postcontrast MRI {40310544}. Desmoid fibromatosis develops in roughly 80% of patients and is contrast-enhancing, with hypointense T1/T2 signals compared with muscle {37382327}. Intraoperative nerve stimulation induces abnormal contraction of the nerve in addition to the innervated muscle {29800277}. A radiological review suggested that approximately 5% of sporadic limb-associated desmoid fibromatosis cases show evidence of an occult NMC precursor, suggesting that the NMC–desmoid fibromatosis combination may be currently underdiagnosed {30168738}.

Epidemiology

NMC is extremely rare, with < 100 cases reported {27259010; 28168739; 30708195}. It presents mostly in infancy or childhood, although adult onset also occurs {38285975}. The sexes are roughly equally represented {38285975; 27259010; 28168739; 30708195}.

Etiology

Published etiological hypotheses have included hamartomatous overgrowth of muscle spindles, a developmental lesion with intraneural entrapment of skeletal muscle during embryogenesis, myogenic metaplasia of nerve sheath elements, and a benign intraneural neoplasm (rhabdomyoma), the latter possibly supported further by its clonal CTNNB1 alteration.

Pathogenesis

Although studies have been limited to date, most cases of NMC and both components of NMC–desmoid fibromatosis show CTNNB1 exon 3 mutations similar to those of sporadic desmoid fibromatosis, with the p.(Ser45Phe) variant accounting for the vast majority, possibly reflecting somatic mosaicism in the patient {27259010; 34718655; 35190814}.

Macroscopic appearance

Fusiform proximal nerve enlargement with multifascicular involvement is typical. On cut surface, a subset of nerve fascicles appear beefy red, like skeletal muscle. When present, foci of desmoid fibromatosis are grossly identical to their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Nerve fascicles contain haphazardly arranged bundles of mature skeletal muscle with cross-striations intercalated between intraneural elements. Some cranial nerve examples have reportedly contained adipose tissue as well, although these may represent a different entity. In terms of immunostains, the myogenic component expresses markers of mature skeletal muscle, including desmin. Residual nerve includes neurofilament-positive axons surrounded by S100- and SOX10-positive Schwann cells. EMA highlights perineurium surrounding individual fascicles. Nuclear β-catenin may be seen in a subset of both the neural and myogenic cells within the NMC, as well as in adjacent desmoid fibromatosis components when present.

Cytology

There are no reports of this rare neoplasm in the cytopathology literature.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for NMC: presence of mature skeletal muscle within nerve fascicles; for NMC–desmoid fibromatosis: associated invasive fibroblastic/myofibroblastic proliferation with sweeping fascicles.

Desirable: immunohistochemical validation of both endoneurial myogenic and neural elements; intact EMA-positive perineurium surrounding individual fascicles; nuclear β-catenin immunoreactivity in pure NMC or NMC–desmoid fibromatosis.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Because these lesions typically involve large functional nerves, accurate intraoperative diagnosis is critical to prevent an overly aggressive approach. Although NMC is considered benign, there is frequent development of desmoid fibromatosis (mostly postsurgical), which is otherwise identical to sporadic desmoid fibromatosis {27259010; 37382327}. It is hypothesized that trauma/surgery stimulates CTNNB1-mutant fibroblasts/myofibroblasts to transform into desmoid fibromatosis akin to counterparts forming after abdominal surgery in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome. 18F-FDG PET uptake hotspots may identify early foci of desmoid fibromatosis transformation {40310544}. More advanced desmoid fibromatosis often necessitates radical resection or limb amputation {21819193}. Given the risk of desmoid fibromatosis transformation after biopsy, a no-touch approach has been advocated for patients with classic clinical and radiological features.
