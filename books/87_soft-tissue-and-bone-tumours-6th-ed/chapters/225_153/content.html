2.2.5 Osteosarcoma

Definition

Osteosarcoma is an intramedullary high-grade sarcoma in which the tumour cells produce bone.

ICD-O coding

9180/3 Osteosarcoma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH1XF3 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Osteosarcoma NOS

2B51.Z & XH5CL5 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Telangiectatic osteosarcoma

2B51.Z & XH4EZ4 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Small cell osteosarcoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Conventional osteosarcoma; telangiectatic osteosarcoma; small cell osteosarcoma

Localization

Conventional osteosarcomas can arise in any bone, but the vast majority originate in the long bones of the extremities, most commonly in the distal femur (30%), followed by the proximal tibia (15%) and the proximal humerus (15%), which are sites of the most proliferative growth plates. In long bones, the tumour is usually metaphysial (90%) and rarely diaphysial (9%) or epiphysial. The jaws are the fourth most common site of origin {39913876; 39984867; 21437228; 24246156}. Involvement of the small bones of the extremities {36716613} and multicentric osteosarcoma, either synchronous or metachronous, are rare {16877608; 36497311}. Recently, 11 sarcomas with EWSR1::SSX fusions were described {41139024}, including 7 osteosarcomas, 3 of which were multicentric. The distribution of telangiectatic osteosarcomas is similar to that of conventional osteosarcoma {27469493; 36815074}. Small cell osteosarcoma is very rare and more commonly develops in the diaphysis of long bones (10–15%) {2804905; 25723116}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

In patients with paediatric conventional osteosarcoma, the most common signs/symptoms are pain (74%), swelling/palpable mass (39–48%), pathological fracture (6–15%), and limping (6%). Pain is generally intermittent and often related to strain. The skin overlying the tumour may be warm and erythematous, and a history of trauma is present in 9–47% of cases {3492261; 39909718; 10819277; 16627266}. The clinical presentation of telangiectatic osteosarcoma and small cell osteosarcoma is similar to that of conventional osteosarcoma; however, in telangiectatic osteosarcoma, a pathological fracture is more commonly observed (seen in ~30% of cases) {1884532; 9179055; 27469493}.

Imaging

Radiographs of conventional osteosarcomas typically show an intramedullary osteolytic lesion without sclerotic margins and poorly defined cloud-like matrix mineralization, producing a mixed lytic/sclerotic pattern. Cortical destruction and periosteal reactions (perpendicular, parallel/onion-skin, or divergent/sunburst) are common, sometimes forming Codman triangles {39407770; 35594197}. Extraosseous extension is frequent, large, eccentric, and often mineralized.

The osteoblastic subtype may appear purely sclerotic, mimicking a giant bone island, or resemble fibrous dysplasia because of lytic, sclerotic, and ground-glass areas {39407770}. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma shows aggressive bone destruction, expansile remodelling, large extraosseous components, and a higher rate of pathological fractures {21071381; 39407770}. Small cell osteosarcoma may mimic Ewing sarcoma when predominantly lytic and non-mineralized, especially in long-bone diaphysis {21071381; 39407770}.

CT is recommended when radiographs are inconclusive, particularly in complex anatomical areas (spine, pelvis), as it provides better visualization of tumour mineralization and periosteal reactions in poorly mineralized tumours.

MRI is the preferred method to assess tumour extension and muscular, joint, and neurovascular involvement. It typically shows an intramedullary metadiaphysial tumour with soft tissue extension, inhomogeneous low T1 signal, inhomogeneous high T2 signal, and intense postgadolinium enhancement. Necrotic areas demonstrate heterogeneous T1 signal (sometimes high after fat suppression due to haemorrhage), high T2 signal, and no enhancement. In osteosarcomas with extensive matrix mineralization (e.g. conventional osteosarcoma osteoblastic) or fibrous tissue (e.g. conventional osteosarcoma fibroblastic), MRI usually shows short T1 and T2 relaxation times and extensive low-signal areas on all sequences. The chondroid component, typical of chondroblastic osteosarcoma, appears as low signal on T1-sweighted imaging, very high homogeneous signal on T2-weighted imaging, and peripheral lobulated (septonodular-like) enhancement {39407770}. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma presents as a multicystic lesion with blood-filled spaces and fluid-fluid levels, mimicking aneurysmal bone cyst but is usually more aggressive, with thickened septa and nodular/solid components {30725994; 22540307}. Quantitative and functional MRI techniques, such as dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI and diffusion-weighted imaging with apparent diffusion coefficient maps, have been explored as biomarkers of neoadjuvant treatment response {37464020; 33146792}.

Metastatic patterns of osteosarcoma

The lungs are the most common metastatic site in osteosarcoma (80%), followed by bones (30–35%) and lymph nodes (2%). Therefore, whole-body imaging is recommended. Chest CT, whole-body bone scintigraphy, and/or PET are used to detect additional disease sites {35594197}, although 18F-FDG PET/CT appears more efficient for diagnosing metastases {37201958}. Skip bone metastases are separate foci of osteosarcoma within the same bone or across an adjacent joint (transarticular). Their reported prevalence ranges from 1% to 25%, depending on detection methods (imaging vs histopathology) {39407770}. In multifocal (multicentric) osteosarcomas, the bone lesions are almost constantly completely densely sclerotic {28166155}.

Epidemiology

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumour in children and young adults (35–50% of cases). It has a bimodal age distribution, with most cases developing between 15 and 25 years of age (55% of cases); 30% of cases occur in individuals aged > 40 years, and 10% in individuals aged > 60 years {40203873; 39984867; 39966776; 39966776}. The incidence is 3 cases per 1 million person-years and is higher in adolescents (8–11 cases per 1 million person-years at age 15–19 years). Worldwide, the incidence rates per 1 million person-years vary from 3 to 5 cases in the male population and 2 to 4 cases in the female population {32400205}. The M:F ratio is 1.4:1 {39941258}, and the incidence is higher in Black populations and in some Asian countries than in Western European countries {39984867; 35226758}; indigenous African and African American male individuals are disproportionately more commonly affected {32400205}. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma is a rare subtype, accounting for 2–12% of all osteosarcomas. Its distribution is similar to that of conventional osteosarcoma {30725994}. Small cell osteosarcoma accounts for about 1.5% of all osteosarcomas [[Nowlen F, Tade Y, Puvvadi S, et al. Small cell osteosarcoma: An NCDB analysis of demographic and socioeconomic factors of diagnosed patients. J Clin Oncol. 2024;42(16_suppl):e23504(2024). doi:10.1200/JCO.2024.42.16_suppl.e23504.]].

Etiology

Although the etiology is unknown, there is an increased incidence of primary osteosarcoma associated with several genetic syndromes. Inactivation of the TP53 gene due to mutations or loss of heterozygosity / deletions occurs in individuals with Li–Fraumeni syndrome, who have an increased incidence of osteosarcoma {1978757; 21837677}. Patients with hereditary retinoblastoma also have a high risk of developing osteosarcoma {3718823}, in particular after receiving ionizing radiation therapy. The genes causing these syndromes are also the most commonly mutated genes in sporadic osteosarcoma (TP53 in > 90% and RB1 in as many as 56% of cases) {24703847; 25512523; 26632267}. Germline variants in various RECQ helicases underlie another group of rare syndromes associated with conventional osteosarcoma, including Bloom syndrome (BLM [RECQL3]), Werner syndrome (WRN [RECQL2]), and Rothmund–Thomson syndrome (RECQL4) {19657341}. Syndrome-related conventional osteosarcomas have been recognized for a long time; but the increasing use of DNA sequencing for genotyping neoplasms and also the germline of individuals has identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variants in as many as 28% of patients with conventional osteosarcoma. Genes involved in DNA repair pathways are most commonly affected {32191290}.

Pathogenesis

The precise pathogenesis and cell of origin of conventional osteosarcoma are unknown, but animal models and single cell RNA sequencing studies point to mesenchymal stem cells as underlying the disease {19718709; 38775859}. A hallmark of conventional osteosarcoma is chromosomal instability as a consequence of chromothripsis/chromoplexy. Although deep sequencing revealed a multitude of potential driver genes that seem to occur early during tumour development, the actual trigger of chromoanagenesis (chromosome shattering and reassembly) remains unknown {28643781}.

Somatic genetics

Conventional osteosarcoma is characterized by highly complex chromosomal aneuploidy and both intertumoural and intratumoural heterogeneity because of chromosomal instability. Somatic alterations involve various numerical and structural alterations, whereas specific point mutations are rather infrequent {28643781}. Specific translocations have not been identified so far.

Single-nucleotide variants

Given the high amount of genetic instability, conventional osteosarcoma is characterized by a remarkably low mutation burden (0.3–1.4/Mb) {25512523; 36418292}. Recurrent SNVs often inactivate tumour suppressor genes, especially TP53, RB1, NF2, PTEN, and ATRX, which are neither specific to conventional osteosarcoma nor targetable {28643781; 37812025}. Variants of IDH1 and IDH2 have not been detected in conventional osteosarcomas and can be helpful in distinguishing between chondroblastic osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma {21598255}. Kataegis, a phenomenon of localized hypermutations, has been demonstrated in 50–74% of conventional osteosarcomas {24703847; 39814020}.

Copy-number variations

In contrast to the low number of SNVs, conventional osteosarcomas have one of the highest rates of copy-number variations (CNVs) among all paediatric cancers, contributing to > 90% of all functional genetic alterations {22641210}. A major mutational process driving chromosomal complexity and instability in conventional osteosarcoma is chromothripsis, resulting from chromosomal shattering in micronuclei or through fragmentation of dicentric chromosomes {30805029; 39814020}. In conventional osteosarcomas, chromothripsis often involves > 10 or even > 15 chromosomes, can be accompanied by whole-genome doubling, and is found in > 90% of cases {26632267; 28643781}. Additionally, specific patterns of chromothripsis and segmental amplifications have been observed, which can result in cancer driver events including amplification of CDK4/MDM2 {28643781}. These mechanisms are mostly early events, but clonal diversification can also be promoted by subclonal chromothripsis, which has been shown to occur in 74% of conventional osteosarcomas and subclonal CNV caused by alternative mechanisms {37812025; 39023390; 39814020}.

Despite intertumoural heterogeneity, recurring amplifications, such as gains of chromosome arms 6p (22–50% of cases, harbouring RUNX2, VEGFA, E2F3, CCND3, and CDC5L), 8q (16–55% of cases, harbouring MYC), and 17p, have been identified in conventional osteosarcomas {14506695; 14603437; 15298715; 20610556; 24703847; 25512523; 26632267; 28643781}. Additional driver genes affected by copy-number gains include CCNE1, RAD21, IGF1R, AKT family, and MCL1 {36418292; 39663392; 37812025}. Amplification of MYC is associated with resistance to chemotherapy and adverse outcome, particularly in homologous recombination (HR)–proficient tumours {20610556; 36418292}. Treatment-resistant subclones commonly presenting with oncogene amplifications are generally already present at diagnosis, emerge after treatment, and persist as the predominant clone at subsequent recurrences {37812025}.

Tumour suppressor or oncogenic pathway alterations can be caused by SNVs or CNVs. Inactivation of TP53 is the most common mutation in conventional osteosarcomas (> 90%) and can occur through chromosomal deletion, point variants, and structural rearrangements, commonly affecting the first intron of the gene, with the latter mechanism appearing to be relatively specific to conventional osteosarcoma {24703847; 25762628; 38010733}. Single double-strand breaks affecting intron 1 of TP53 can generate unprotected terminal ends of 17p, which can translocate to another chromosome or its sister chromatid to form dicentric chromosomes during mitosis. This can trigger break-fusion-bridge cycles and eventually segmental amplifications and was termed loss-translocation-amplification chromothripsis. The mechanism also seems to be relatively specific to conventional osteosarcoma and an alternative way to inactivate TP53 while simultaneously activating oncogenic signalling cascades. It has been reported to occur in 48% of osteosarcomas {39814020}.

The TP53 antagonist MDM2 is amplified in about 10% of cases, suggesting a pre-existing central low-grade osteosarcoma that underwent transformation in at least a subset of cases {20196171; 26248895}. FGFR1 amplifications have been demonstrated in 18.5% of cases and associated with a poor response to chemotherapy {24861215}; alterations in the IGF1R signalling pathway were observed in 14% of conventional osteosarcomas {28643781; 35887382}. Homozygous loss of CDKN2A occurs in as many as 25% of conventional osteosarcomas, is associated with an adverse outcome, and has been implicated in osteosarcoma development from a mesenchymal progenitor {19718709; 36418292; 39814020}. RB1 is deleted in up to 50% of osteosarcomas {25512523}. Other recurrently deleted genes include PTEN, LSAMP, DLG2, ATRX, and WWOX {24703847; 28494505; 36418292}. The extent of genome-wide loss of heterozygosity correlates with progressive disease {39814020}. A smaller subset of conventional osteosarcomas (< 10%) do not harbour alterations in the TP53 pathway (including MDM2) and generally show less complex genomic alterations {39814020}.

Distinct patterns of large-scale transitions and loss of heterozygosity reminiscent of that seen in BRCA1 and/or BRCA2–deficient tumours have been identified in conventional osteosarcomas, suggesting a deficiency in homologous recombination repair (HRD, or BRCAness) {26632267}. Indeed, when compared with pan-cancer data published in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort, the HRD score of conventional osteosarcomas ranked second only to that of ovarian cancer {29617664; 36418292}. The mutation spectrum of conventional osteosarcoma has been demonstrated to be predominantly caused by defective DNA repair {36418292}. The HRD-related single-base substitution (SBS3) mutation signature is conserved in > 90% of conventional osteosarcomas, and its prevalence is comparable with BRCA1-deficient cancers. Furthermore, the mutations attributed to HRD-related SBS3 appear to increase over time, indicating that HRD is a continuing and active mutational process during the course of the disease {29489755; 37812025}. These findings point to a potential sensitivity of conventional osteosarcoma to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors {27447864; 33963544}. Additionally, activation of the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres pathway has been detected in 53% of conventional osteosarcomas {39814020}.

Macroscopic appearance

Conventional osteosarcoma usually presents as a large (50–100 mm) intramedullary mass centred in the metaphysial region, with variable extension into the adjacent diaphysis and epiphysis. The cut surface is heterogeneous, depending on the type and degree of mineralization of the predominant matrix. Heavily mineralized tumours are tan-white/yellow and densely solid (resembling cortical bone), whereas non-mineralized cartilaginous components are either grey and rubbery (if hyaline in nature) or mucoid (if the matrix has undergone myxoid degeneration). Areas of haemorrhage, tumour necrosis, and cystic change are common. When extraosseous infiltration occurs, the tumour usually forms an eccentric or circumferential soft tissue mass that displaces the periosteum peripherally. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma shows a haemorrhagic multicystic lesion filled with blood clots. The gross features of small cell osteosarcoma are indistinguishable from those of conventional osteosarcoma.

Histopathology

Conventional osteosarcoma shows a broad histomorphological spectrum; essential to the diagnosis is the identification of neoplastic bone formation by malignant mesenchymal cells. Osteosarcoma grows in an infiltrating and permeating pattern, replacing the marrow space, encasing and eroding the pre-existing bony trabeculae, and infiltrating into the Haversian system. It reaches the subperiosteal space, often infiltrating the surrounding soft tissues. Neoplastic cells generally show highly atypical and pleomorphic nuclei with hyperchromasia and nuclear membranous irregularity; the cytoplasm is generally densely eosinophilic, with eccentrically placed nuclei (resembling osteoblasts). Tumour cells can also be fusiform, plasmacytoid, epithelioid, or become small and normalized in appearance (mimicking benign osteocytes). Mitotic activity is generally brisk, including atypical forms {34742481}. No minimum quantity of bone formation is required, and any amount is sufficient to render the diagnosis. Characteristically, the bone matrix is intimately associated with the tumour cells. It varies in quantity and is woven in architecture, deposited as primitive, disorganized trabeculae that may produce fine (filigree) or coarse lace-like patterns, or broad, large sheets of compact bone formed by coalescing trabeculae. This lesional bone is eosinophilic on H&E-stained sections if unmineralized and basophilic/purple if mineralized, and it may have a pagetoid appearance caused by haphazardly deposited cement lines. The neoplastic bone can be deposited on the surface of pre-existing trabeculae in an infiltrative manner. Distinguishing unmineralized osteoid matrix from other eosinophilic extracellular materials such as collagen or compacted fibrin matrices may be difficult and subjective. Collagen tends to be less glassy and more fibrillar, and it is frequently deposited in broad aggregates or elongated fibrils compressed between lesional cells {34742481}.

High-grade central conventional osteosarcoma has been classically divided into osteoblastic (75%), chondroblastic (15%), and fibroblastic (10%) patterns, depending on the predominant pattern present within the entire tumour {34742481}. Osteoblastic osteosarcoma consists of osteoblast-like cells producing bone or osteoid. Chondroblastic osteosarcoma is predominantly composed of hyaline cartilage showing severe cytological atypia, with a minor component of neoplastic bone. The chondroid matrix may appear myxoid with single cells or delicate cords of cells displaying more-subtle atypia. Neoplastic cartilage usually merges with areas containing neoplastic bone, often with condensation and spindling of tumour cells at the periphery of the chondroid nodules {34742481}. It usually shows a poorer morphological response to chemotherapy than other patterns, although the survival is similar {12044509}. In fibroblastic osteosarcoma, the malignant cells are usually spindled, often, but not always, demonstrating severe cytological atypia. The neoplastic cells are often arranged in a fascicular to storiform pattern and are associated with extracellular collagen, with inconspicuous neoplastic bone formation.

Other uncommon histological patterns have been described. Osteoblastoma-like osteosarcoma is composed of uniform osteoblastic tumour cells rimming the surface of the neoplastic bony trabeculae, often in single layer, in association with an oedematous, richly vascularized stroma (as typically observed in osteoblastoma). The tumour infiltrates and encases pre-existing bone as a sign of osteodestructive growth {8302813; 31753571}. The presence of areas of conventional (high-grade) osteosarcoma seems to predict a more aggressive behaviour {30152881}. Chondroblastoma-like osteosarcoma is composed of small uniform round cells with nuclear grooves, growing in sheets, occasionally associated with islands of pink matrix, resembling chondroblastoma. Different clinicoradiological and histological features (such as significant cytological atypia, infiltrative growth, osteoid production, tumour necrosis, and increased numbers of atypical mitoses) have been reported to distinguish chondroblastoma-like osteosarcoma from chondroblastoma. The absence of H3.3 K37M (K36M) mutation distinguishes chondroblastoma-like osteosarcoma from (malignant) chondroblastoma {10192265; 34742481; 35791778; 40348060}. Sclerosing osteosarcoma is characterized by abundant eosinophilic osteoid, with scattered embedded tumour cells, often showing only mild nuclear atypia (referred to as normalization) {34742481}. Large polyhedral tumour cells mimicking metastatic carcinoma characterize the d epithelioid osteosarcoma {7683966; 34742481}. Non-neoplastic, osteoclast-type giant cells scattered throughout the tumour are the hallmark of giant cell–rich osteosarcoma {12597223; 37292033}. Lack of the H3.3 G35W (G34W) mutation helps in distinguishing a malignant transformation of giant cell tumour of bone {28505000; 40077813}. Chondromyxoid fibroma-like osteosarcoma is a hypocellular tumour with chondromyxoid stroma and inconspicuous osteoid, mimicking chondromyxoid fibroma. The clue for the diagnosis is neoplastic bone formation, in addition to cytological atypia, necrosis, atypical mitoses, and soft tissue or bone invasion {34820622; 31996205}. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma-like osteosarcoma is characterized by a fascicular and storiform growth of highly pleomorphic cells, with a minimal amount of tumour bone formation {34742481}.

Telangiectatic osteosarcoma is composed of multiple blood-filled or empty cystic spaces, simulating an aneurysmal bone cyst; however, the septa are populated by pleomorphic cells showing substantial nuclear hyperchromasia; atypical mitoses are easily identified. Osteoid formation is usually focal and confluent and may be absent in biopsies. The septa also contain osteoclast-type giant cells. At the periphery of the lesion, tumour permeation into pre-existing bone trabeculae is often observed. The most rigid definition of telangiectatic osteosarcoma requires a radiographically purely lytic lesion {34742481}. USP6 rearrangements are typically absent in telangiectatic osteosarcoma {31611201}.

Small cell osteosarcoma is composed of relatively uniform small cells with scant cytoplasm, mimicking Ewing sarcoma, associated with lace-like osteoid production. Nuclei are round to oval, and the chromatin may be fine to coarse. Mitoses can easily be found. Small cell osteosarcoma is a diagnosis of exclusion, and underlying fusion transcripts pointing to other undifferentiated round cell sarcomas should be ruled out {25723116; 38332052}.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunophenotype of osteosarcoma is variable and in the vast majority of cases not required for the diagnosis. Osteosarcoma commonly expresses SATB2 {26644288}, which, however, is not {35016891; 36732203}. Keratin and EMA (particularly when showing epithelioid morphology) are occasionally positive, making it particularly difficult to distinguish metastatic carcinoma with reactive osteoid formation {32089927}. MUC4 negativity can help rule out sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma in which the typical sclerotic and collagenous stroma can mimic neoplastic bone formation. FOS expression, typically seen in osteoid osteoma and osteoblastoma, is typically negative or only focally positive in osteosarcoma {31490237; 31768625}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No specific diagnostic molecular pathology tests are available.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; bone formation by tumour cells; permeative and destructive growth pattern; telangiectatic osteosarcoma: blood-filled or empty cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa; small cell osteosarcoma: small blue round cell morphology with focal neoplastic bone formation.

Desirable: tumour cells with high-grade atypia; atypical mitotic figures frequently present.

Staging

The ninth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours stages osteosarcoma of the appendicular skeleton, trunk, skull, and facial bones as follows: T1, ≤ 8 cm (greatest tumour dimension); T2, > 8 cm; T3, discontinuous involvement of the primary bone site. Staging of spinal and pelvic tumours is based on anatomical intraosseous and extraosseous extension and size [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]].

Version 9 of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging manual stages osteosarcoma arising in the appendicular, truncal, and craniofacial bones on the basis of the presence or absence of distant metastasis, histological grade, and greatest tumour dimension (≤ 8 cm vs > 8 cm) [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020.]]. However, dichotomization of tumour size fails to contribute prognostically significant information in this staging system {29200101; 29718558}. Tumours of the pelvic or spinal skeleton are now substaged on the basis of the anatomical extent of intraosseous invasion or presence of extraosseous invasion; prognostic stage groupings for these anatomical sites have not yet been developed.

The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) staging system is arguably the more clinically useful staging system for planning optimal surgical therapy and is based on the presence of metastatic disease, histological grade, and local anatomical extent (defined by the presence of extraosseous extension). However, breaking through the cortex with periosteal elevation (and intact periosteum) does not appear to be associated with worse outcome per se. Instead, tumour extension beyond the periosteum has been shown to significantly correlate with an inferior prognosis {2355025; 28195881; 29718558}.

Prognosis and prediction

Aggressive local growth and rapid haematogenous systemic dissemination characterize the clinical course of conventional osteosarcoma. The use of multiagent chemotherapy led to a dramatic impact on outcome in some studies {21703851; 22055968}, and high-grade osteosarcoma is therefore generally treated with preoperative and postoperative chemotherapy. Local control is achieved via surgical resection with wide margins, often using limb-salvage techniques {21342040}. Radiation can be used for unresectable tumours {20943636}. In the pre-chemotherapy era, more than 80% of patients treated with surgery alone died of disease, whereas 70% of patients with localized osteosarcoma of the extremities are currently long-term survivors {37034214; 20943636; 21703851; 22055968; 39941258}. However, the survival rate of patients with osteosarcoma has not significantly improved over the past 30 years; moreover, patients presenting with metastatic (usually to the lungs) or recurrent disease have a survival rate of < 30% {12743156; 18754698}, and surgery and chemotherapy have limited therapeutic efficacy in these cases {37034214}. Approximately 10–20% of patients present with metastatic disease at diagnosis. Low number of metastases at diagnosis and complete surgical resection of all clinically detected tumour sites are associated with a better outcome {40203873}. Older age, male sex, large tumour size, non-extremity / proximal location, metastatic disease, advanced stage, absence of surgical treatment, inadequate surgical resection margins, and amputation surgery are adverse prognostic factors {37034214; 39941258; 32140401; 11821461; 39966776}. The prognostic impact of skip metastasis (defined as synchronous foci of bone metastases anatomically separate from the primary tumour in the same bone or transarticular if in the bone opposite the joint, in the absence of distant skeletal metastases) is still unclear {38408127}. The histological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy remains one of the most important prognostic factors of overall and disease-free survival {21703851; 22055968}. A good response is usually defined as ≥ 90% necrosis {6577010; 29200101}. No differences in prognosis seem to be related to the different subhistotypes {12044509; 39423470; 34268409}. Multicentric osteosarcoma is associated with an extremely poor prognosis, usually lacking effective treatment options {36497311}. Several studies have tried to identify molecular or immunohistochemical (such as Ki-67, p16, p21, or PDL1) prognostic markers {38753746; 33648449; 38186021}; however, none is currently used in clinical practice.
