1.3.28 Solitary fibrous tumour

Definition

Solitary fibrous tumour (SFT) is an intermediate, rarely metastasizing fibroblastic neoplasm characterized by bland spindle cell morphology; a patternless growth pattern; a prominent branching, thin-walled, dilated (staghorn) vasculature; and NAB2::STAT6 gene fusion.

ICD-O coding

8815/0 Solitary fibrous tumour, benign

8815/1 Solitary fibrous tumour NOS

8815/3 Solitary fibrous tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2F7C & XH7E62 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of connective or other soft tissue & Solitary fibrous tumour NOS

2B5Y & XH1HP3 Other specified malignant mesenchymal neoplasms & Solitary fibrous tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Not recommended: haemangiopericytoma; giant cell angiofibroma; benign solitary fibrous tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

SFTs may occur at any anatomical site, including superficial and deep soft tissues and within visceral organs and bone. About 30–40% of extrapleural, extrameningeal SFTs arise in the extremities; 30–40% arise in deep soft tissues, the abdominal cavity, the pelvis, or the retroperitoneum; 10–15% arise in the head and neck; and 10–15% arise in the trunk {29039030; 26924782; 28838212; 22575866; 28731041; 30206022}. Deep tumours are more common than superficial tumours, accounting for 70–90% of cases {29039030; 26924782}. In the head and neck, the sinonasal tract and orbit are the most common sites, followed by the oral cavity and salivary glands {28877055}.

Clinical features

Most tumours present as slow-growing, painless masses. Abdominopelvic tumours may present with distension, constipation, urinary retention, or early satiety, whereas head and neck SFTs may present with nasal obstruction, voice changes, or bleeding {29772933; 27893469}. Large SFTs may cause paraneoplastic syndromes such as Doege–Potter syndrome, with the induction of severe hypoglycaemia or (more rarely) acromegaloid changes due to tumour production of IGF2 {30788455; 30278559}. The radiographic features of SFT are largely nonspecific. CT demonstrates a well-defined, frequently lobulated mass that is isodense to skeletal muscle, with heterogeneous contrast enhancement due to the extensive tumour vasculature {30225611; 27153789}. MRI shows slight hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and variable hypointensity to hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, corresponding to fibrous and cellular or myxoid areas, respectively. Intralesional and peripheral flow voids are common {29682137; 27153789; 29028514; 34430995}. Larger or aggressive cases may display increased heterogeneity because of fibrosis, haemorrhage, necrosis, myxoid and cystic degeneration, or calcifications {27153789}.

Epidemiology

SFTs are rare and affect men and women equally and are most common in adults, with a peak incidence between 40 and 70 years {28838212; 29039030; 22575866; 28731041; 30206022; 24360568}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The genetic hallmark of SFT is a paracentric inversion involving chromosome 12q, resulting in the fusion of the NAB2 and STAT6 genes {23313954; 23761323; 23313952}. NAB2 encodes for a transcriptional repressor of EGR1, while STAT6 encodes for a transcription factor. Multiple distinct NAB2::STAT6 fusion variants have been reported according to distinct exonic breakpoints, with NAB2 exon 4 – STAT6 exon 2 including full-length STAT6 protein and NAB2 exon 6 – STAT6 exon 16/17 including only the C-terminal STAT6 transcriptional activation domain representing the most frequent variants. There is a correlation between NAB2::STAT6 fusion variant and anatomical site, histological features {24513261; 25582503; 30584643}, gene expression, and global DNA methylation levels {33497701; 33745702; 38523251; 39357799}, with the NAB2::STAT6 full-length STAT6 variant occurring more frequently in classic fibrous SFTs. However, the role of NAB2::STAT6 fusion variant in clinical behaviour of SFTs remains unclear. The NAB2::STAT6 fusion is thought to convert wildtype NAB2 from a transcriptional repressor of EGR1-mediated signalling into a transcriptional activator via replacement of the C-terminal repression domain by the transcriptional activation domain of STAT6, thereby resulting in a feedforward loop of constitutive EGR1-mediated transactivation of proliferation and survival-associated growth factors, including IGF2 and FGFR1 {23313952; 32216968; 38659891}. Secondary oncogenic mutations associated with aggressive behaviour and dedifferentiation include TERT promoter variants {27562490; 29985536} and TP53 alterations {26022454; 25582503; 37980418; 40420616}. Rare cases of dedifferentiated SFT may also show loss of RB1, and/or PTEN variants {40420616}.

Macroscopic appearance

SFTs are well-circumscribed masses that typically measure 50–100 mm, although some lesions may exceed 250 mm in greatest dimension. The cut surface is nodular and tan to reddish-brown, and it occasionally shows haemorrhage, myxoid change, or cystic degeneration {9160308; 10667425}.

Histopathology

SFTs are composed of haphazardly arranged spindled to ovoid cells with indistinct small basophilic nucleoli and pale eosinophilic to lightly amphophilic cytoplasm, embedded within a variably collagenized and vascularized stroma. A prominent branching, frequently dilated vascular network is often present (termed staghorn or haemangiopericytomatous). Scattered smaller or medium-sized hyalinized blood vessels may also be identified. There is a wide morphological spectrum of growth patterns, ranging from paucicellular lesions with abundant stromal keloidal-type collagen to highly cellular tumours consisting of closely spaced cells with little or no intervening stroma. Morphological patterns correlate with the NAB2::STAT6 fusion variant {24513261; 25582503; 30584643}.

SFTs most often have low mitotic counts, without substantial nuclear pleomorphism or necrosis. Tumours demonstrating a high mitotic count with or without increased cellularity, atypia, necrosis, and infiltrative growth have traditionally been termed “malignant”, but risk stratification models more accurately predict prognosis, and the use of the term “malignant SFT” is discouraged. By immunohistochemistry, SFT typically shows strong and diffuse expression of CD34 and nuclear STAT6 {23575898; 24030747; 25873501}. STAT6 is a sensitive and almost entirely specific marker for SFTs. However, weak expression of STAT6 in as many as 4% of non-SFT mesenchymal neoplasms has been reported {24030747; 25873501; 25901508}. Notably, a subset of dedifferentiated liposarcomas and GLI1-amplified mesenchymal neoplasms harbour STAT6 coamplification and may therefore express nuclear and cytoplasmic STAT6 detectable by immunohistochemistry {24457460; 25873501; 36316249; 36316249}.

Unusual morphological variations may be present in SFTs, including storiform, herringbone, neural-like, and fascicular growth patterns. Myxoid stromal changes may be present and can be focal or extensive {10349983}. Cytologically, the tumour cells may show plasmacytoid features, clear cell features, and epithelioid or round cell features {36253890}. Fat-forming (lipomatous) SFTs harbour a component of mature adipose tissue, which can be single fat cells, focal clusters of fat cells, or a diffuse fatty component {10524520; 11014579; 7793472; 33887215}; this pattern is characterized by upregulated expression of the nuclear receptor PPARG regulating adipocyte differentiation {33887215}. Giant cell–rich SFTs, formerly known as giant cell angiofibroma, show features of conventional SFT with an admixed population of multinucleated giant cells within the stroma and lining pseudovascular spaces {7573691; 10895819}. Rare cases (< 1% of all SFTs) show transitions to high-grade sarcoma with or without heterologous elements such as rhabdomyosarcoma or osteosarcoma and are termed “dedifferentiated (anaplastic) SFTs” {40420616; 19133003; 24427361; 22026427; 25582503; 23040384; 19718788; 22613995; 28968297}. Of note, STAT6 expression may be lost in dedifferentiated SFT {40420616; 22613995; 19718788; 26022454; 27189111}. Extremely rare cases of head and neck SFTs with epithelial transdifferentiation and cytokeratin positivity have been described as well {35195577}.

Cytology

Cytology aspirate shows uniform spindle cells with minimally pleomorphic nuclei and wispy thin cytoplasmic processes seen singly and in tissue fragments, along with dense fibrotic collagenous tissue fragments including wispy or ropy collagen, in an often haemorrhagic background. Specific diagnosis of SFT requires STAT6 immunohistochemistry {28914981; 34049841}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

NAB2::STAT6 gene fusions are pathognomonic of SFT, and STAT6 immunohistochemistry is a sensitive and specific surrogate for all fusion types {24030747; 24702701; 23575898}. Because of the close proximity of NAB2 and STAT6 on chromosomal region 12q13, detection of genomic rearrangements is difficult by conventional cytogenetic methods including in situ hybridization. There is a wide diversity of breakpoints across exonic and intronic regions of both genes, which makes next-generation sequencing–based detection of NAB2::STAT6 fusions on the RNA level the method of choice.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: spindled to ovoid cells in a patternless architecture with prominent branching and hyalinized vasculature; variable stromal collagen deposition.

Desirable: STAT6 expression by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Risk stratification models are preferred over anatomical staging.

Prognosis and prediction

Recurrence (distant or local) occurs in 10–30% of SFTs {22575866; 29039030; 28838212; 26924782; 30206022}, with 10–40% of recurrences reported after 5 years {1203874; 9850176} and rare recurrences seen after 15 years {29039030}. TERT promoter mutations are more common in tumours with aggressive features, but they may also be seen in low-risk SFT {29985536; 27562490; 25582503}. TP53 variants are also associated with dedifferentiation and poor outcomes. Elevated proliferation (> 2 mitoses/mm2, equating to > 4 mitoses per 10 HPF of 0.5 mm diameter and 0.2 mm2 area) {22575866; 24052313; 26924782; 28838212; 32315454; 31991494}, tumour size {24052313; 22575866; 29039030; 32315454}, necrosis {22575866; 26924782; 31991494}, and patient age {22575866; 28838212} are among the most frequent factors found to associate with poor outcomes.

The development of multivariate risk models has resulted in improved prognostication over the traditional benign/malignant distinction, and the term “benign” should not be applied to SFT. The three best-studied prognostic models include the Demicco model (Demicco score), the G-score, and the Salas OS score. In retrospective studies, all have shown significant differences in outcomes between risk groups, although performance may vary according to cohort and outcome evaluated (metastasis-free interval or recurrence-free interval).

The Demicco model is designed to predict metastatic risk (#54990Table 1.01). The current, modified, version (mDemicco score) incorporates mitotic count (≥ 2 mitoses/mm2), patient age (≥ 55 years), tumour size stratified by 5 cm tiers, and presence of ≥ 10% necrosis to classify tumours into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups {22575866; 28731041; 29935303}. This model has been validated for both thoracic and extrathoracic SFTs across a variety of anatomical sites {22575866; 28731041; 30206022; 29935303; 32315454; 34739418; 37899729}. The Demicco score has been shown to be most effective at predicting the small subset of tumours at high risk for early metastasis (within 5 years of diagnosis); the presence of TERT promoter mutations can further stratify intermediate-risk tumours into the high-risk category {29985536}. In comparative studies, the Demicco score consistently demonstrates concordance statistics of > 0.75 for prediction of metastases, with only slightly worse performance when utilized to predict recurrence-free interval. The Salas OS model was developed as part of a set of risk calculators proposed by the French Sarcoma Group (FSG) to variously predict overall survival, local recurrence, and distant metastatic risk {28838212}. It is similar to the Demicco score in that it incorporates mitotic counts (> 2 mitoses/mm2) and age cut-off points (≥ 60 years) {28838212}. It has been shown to be effective at predicting tumours at high risk of recurrence {31206727; 36030294; 31991494}, although the low- and intermediate-risk categories behave very similarly in some studies.

The G-score was developed to predict recurrence-free interval (local or distant), with an emphasis on long-term follow-up. This model incorporates mitotic count (> 2 mitoses/mm2, equating to > 4 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm diameter and 0.2 mm2 area), sex, and extent of tumour necrosis to classify tumours at low, intermediate, and high risk for recurrence (#54991Table 1.02) {31991494}. While it has been internally and independently validated {36030294; 36613891} and performs well at identifying a subset of low-risk SFTs (80% of patients with 10-year recurrence-free interval), the G-score is noted to assign a high proportion (40–45%) of SFTs to the intermediate-risk category. It may therefore be helpful in some circumstances to apply multiple risk models to tumours for improved prognostication.
