4.0.3 Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53)

Definition

Li–Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic sequence variants in the TP53 gene and characterized by multiple primary neoplasms in children and adults, with a predominance of soft tissue sarcomas, osteosarcoma, breast cancer, brain tumours, and adrenocortical carcinoma.

MIM numbering

151623 Li–Fraumeni syndrome

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Not recommended: sarcoma family syndrome of Li and Fraumeni.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

LFS is associated with cancers of soft tissues, bone, brain, breast, adrenal glands, and other organs.

Clinical features

LFS is characterized by the early onset of a broad spectrum of cancers and a high lifetime cancer risk. The five core LFS-related cancers are adrenocortical carcinomas, breast cancer, CNS tumours, osteosarcomas, and soft tissue sarcomas, which constitute about 70% of LFS-related neoplasms {29076966}. In addition, a broad range of other tumours, including lymphomas, gastrointestinal malignancies (gastric and colorectal cancers), melanoma, and lung cancer, have been described in LFS families {19204208; 20301488; 24706533; 26101242}. More recent studies have also reported cancers of the female reproductive organs (ovarian and endometrial carcinomas), the urogenital tract (carcinomas of the kidney and the prostate), and the eyes {14583457; 28509937}.

The classic definition of LFS required sarcoma in a patient before age 45 years, a first-degree relative with any cancer before age 45 years, and a first- or second-degree relative with any cancer before the age of 45 years or sarcoma at any age {29039119; 3409256}. This clinical definition has since been expanded to allow a broader recognition of individuals and families with LFS (see #56076Box 6.01).

Patients and families without the full spectrum of classic criteria have been designated as having Li–Fraumeni–like syndrome (LFL), which has been further defined clinically by two sets of criteria proposed by Birch and Eeles, respectively {29039119; 8118819; 8718514}. The Birch criteria are close to those of classic LFS but do not require a sarcoma, whereas the Eeles definition of LFL includes two first- or second-degree relatives with LFS component tumours at any age, rather than the three required by the classic criteria {29039119; 8718514}. The investigation of other cancer-predisposition genes and non-coding regions of the TP53 gene may explain the occurrence of LFL {30086788; 37377903}.

Sarcomas represent 17.4% of all cancers in the IARC TP53 Database and 36.8% of all cancers in patients aged < 20 years. The most common types are osteosarcoma (40.4%), sarcoma NOS (17%), rhabdomyosarcoma (16.5%), leiomyosarcoma (9.1%), and liposarcoma (4.9%) {21837677; 27328919; 24382691; 32658383; 25896519}.

The incidence of osteosarcoma among female TP53 variant carriers is slightly increased compared with a SEER Program dataset of sporadic cases, but for other sarcomas there are no substantive sex ratio differences. About 67% of sarcomas in carriers of a germline (constitutional) TP53 variant occur before the age of 19 years. Whereas rhabdomyosarcoma and osteosarcoma are almost exclusively observed before the age of 19 years, liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma arise predominantly in adults. With respect to genotype–phenotype correlations, rhabdomyosarcoma and osteosarcoma show a higher-than-expected proportion of missense mutations in the TP53 DNA-binding domain, whereas liposarcomas and leiomyosarcomas are more frequently associated with frameshift, splice-site, and nonsense mutations {21837677}. Compared with other rhabdomyosarcoma histotypes, the anaplastic pattern is overrepresented in TP53 variant carriers and is more frequently associated with non-missense mutations {24382691}.

Epidemiology

More than 1500 germline variants associated with LFS, LFL, and probands with a family history or fulfilling the Chompret criteria are included in version R21 (January 2025) of the TP53 Database from 1990 onwards [[The TP53 Database [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 [cited 2025 Feb 21]. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov.]] {35352025}. The incidence of TP53 germline variants in Europe and North America seems to be 1 case per 5000–20 000 individuals {19204208; 12672316}. In southern Brazil, the founder mutation c.1010G>A p.(Arg337His) occurs with an incidence of 1 case per 300 individuals and is present in 0.3% of the general population {16494995; 24936644}. Within this patient population, the p.(Arg337His) mutation detected by tumour genomic profiling is frequently germline in origin {34270331}. Other putative founder mutations associated with later onset disease have been reported from Sweden, p.(Arg181His) {39757328}, and the Middle East, p.(Arg181Cys) {39673796}. There is evidence of the occurrence of de novo TP53 variants in about 10–20% of individuals with LFS; about 20% of these seem to occur during embryogenesis {29070607}.

Etiology

The genetic basis of autosomal dominant LFS is the presence of a germline variant in the TP53 tumour suppressor gene {1978757; 2259385}. A broad spectrum of TP53 variants involving the coding regions of the gene, typically resulting in a dominant negative or loss-of-function effect, has been found in LFS families, but 20–40% of individuals who fit the clinical definition of LFS and the majority of LFL families may lack detectable variants {14583457; 12619118}. The lack of 100% concordance between TP53 variants and the classic LFS phenotype may be explained by posttranslational alterations, complete deletion, or the effects of modifier genes on the phenotype generated by the presence of specific germline alterations {37377903}. The most frequent alterations are missense variants at specific hotspot codons, mainly clustering in the DNA-binding domain and interfering with DNA binding or disrupting the binding surface structure necessary to modulate target gene transcription {20182602}. Other alteration types include nonsense, splice site, frameshift, large deletions, insertions, and complex rearrangements, with some variants occurring outside of the DNA-binding domain {27984644; 26014290}.

In an open-resource TP53 database, more than 1500 different germline variants are listed, with only four patterns accounting for about 80% of the variants: G:C>A:T at CpG islands in almost 50%, followed by A:T>G:C, G:C>A:T, and deletions (in ~10% each), predominantly involving 11 codons within the coding regions of exons 5–10, most commonly (> 3%) codons 175, 213, 245, 248, 273, 282, and 337 [[The TP53 Database [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 [cited 2025 Feb 21]. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov.]] {35352025}. Lesions within introns or the regulatory regions of the gene have been identified, although their functional significance is unclear, and there are open questions about the significance of some TP53 variants occurring in cancer {28254861}. There is some evidence that genes of the Fanconi pathway, RECQ family genes, and other genes mutated in breast cancer are associated with LFL cancers {30086788}. Widespread use of next-generation and other sequencing technologies is uncovering higher numbers of variants in TP53, as well as in other genes in LFS and LFL {32966936}. This is leading to changes in the classification of Li–Fraumeni spectrum to include phenotypic Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria in the absence of a pathogenic TP53 variant; Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria with a pathogenic TP53 variant; attenuated Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals with a pathogenic TP53 variant who do not meet LFS testing criteria; and incidental Li–Fraumeni syndrome for those individuals who carry a pathogenic variant without a family history of cancer {34709361}.

Pathogenesis

p53 (encoded by TP53) is one of the most prominent tumour suppressors, whose activation as a transcription factor stimulates downstream pathways leading to protective cellular processes including cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence to prevent the propagation of genetically altered cells {11099028}. For tumour development suppression, the importance of these cellular responses has been challenged by findings in mouse models {22682240}. p53 also functions to regulate metabolism and the oxidation-reduction balance to maintain intracellular homeostasis, which may mediate some p53 tumour suppressor activity by protecting cells from oxidative stress {24383451}. Under normal conditions, p53 is maintained at low levels because of rapid turnover mediated by MDM2, its main negative regulator {28254861}.

Cells from LFS individuals exhibit genomic instability, telomere dysfunction, and spontaneous immortalization {23587008}. Alterations in TP53 resulting in these cellular phenotypes of LFS differ from each other in the extent to which they have lost suppressor function and in their capacity to inhibit wildtype p53 in a dominant negative manner {12826609; 17311302}. In LFS-associated tumours, TP53 is usually inactivated by a second hit involving the wildtype allele.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours in patients with LFS have no macroscopic features distinct from those of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Most tumours in patients with LFS have no histopathological features distinct from those of their sporadic counterparts. Exceptions are rhabdomyosarcoma and Wilms tumour, which in LFS more frequently have anaplastic histology, and there are higher rates of breast ductal carcinoma in situ, hormone receptor–positive breast cancers, and ERBB2-positive breast cancers, with a worse outcome in the hormone receptor–positive / ERBB2-negative luminal tumours {38569880}. p53 immunostaining is more often abnormal by either strong diffuse or completely negative nuclear reactivity, but this does not prove the presence of a germline variant, which must be confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Candidates for TP53 germline testing are identified on the basis of the 2015 Chompret criteria (see #56076Box 6.01) {26014290} and the recommendations of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN; see #56077Box 6.02) {30333936}. In the era of testing by next-generation sequencing, it may be necessary to further broaden the clinical criteria to find a greater number of TP53 germline variants {28509937}. TP53 and other high-risk genes are now routinely included in most germline (constitutional) testing panels {30086788} as well as in somatic tumour-only assays. Increased germline and somatic tumour testing have led to challenges in some cases in distinguishing among pathogenic germline variants, known polymorphisms, variants of unknown significance, somatic variants, and clonal haematopoiesis {31533767}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of a germline pathogenic variant in TP53 and/or appropriate family history.

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

To date, studies have not been able to definitively distinguish overall outcome differences between TP53 variant carriers and patients with the same tumour occurring sporadically. There are isolated case reports suggesting poorer response to therapy or accelerated occurrence of radiation-induced second malignancies, but these findings have not been validated in large prospective cohorts. Similarly, there are currently no validated predictive markers of outcome, age of onset, or tumour type that are being applied in individual patients, although extensive research is being done in this field. Surveillance strategies for early tumour detection in TP53 variant carriers have been reported and are now widely used, resulting in reduced mortality and treatment-related morbidity in patients who undergo surveillance (see #61916Table 6.06) {27501770; 28572266; 28772291}. More recent advances using multiomics circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) platforms and introduction of clinical trials to incorporate these modalities may lead to improved prediction of tumour onset and site {37874259}.
