4.0.6 Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)

Definition

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a pathogenic variant in NF1 and characterized by a constellation of clinical criteria.

MIM numbering

162200 Neurofibromatosis type 1; NF1

601321 Neurofibromatosis-Noonan syndrome; NFNS

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.10 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Related terminology

Not recommended: von Recklinghausen disease.

Subtype(s)

spinal neurofibromatosis; mosaic neurofibromatosis type 1 including segmental neurofibromatosis; 17q11.2-microdeletion syndrome; Neurofibromatosis-Noonan syndrome.

Localization

NF1-related features usually develop in neural crest–derived cells such as Schwann cells, melanocytes, glial cells, adrenal cells, and interstitial cells of Cajal. Osteocytes are also involved.

Clinical features

Typical manifestations include multiple café-au-lait macules, axillary/inguinal freckling, Lisch nodules, and choroidal abnormalities imaged by optical coherence tomography scanning {28230061}. Café-au-lait macules, the hallmark of NF1, may be present at birth and develop further during the first few years of life. Lisch nodules are asymptomatic iris hamartomas easily detected by slit-lamp examination. Bone abnormalities can cause serious complications in individuals with NF1, such as severe scoliosis, sphenoid wing dysplasia, non-ossifying fibromas, and congenital tibial bowing that may result in fracture and pseudarthrosis {18248783}. Other clinical signs are short stature, macrocephaly, and deficits in attention and cognitive function. Less common complications include epilepsy and cardiovascular problems.

Neoplastic disease spectrum

Children (aged < 5 years) with NF1 have a higher risk of developing juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia, although the annual incidence is low (1 case per 2000–5000 people) {9180088}. Individuals with NF1 are at increased risk of developing benign and malignant solid tumours of both nervous and non-nervous systems {19410195}. In children with café-au-lait macules and malignant tumours, constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome should be considered in the differential diagnosis {30415209; 32773772}.

Non–nervous system tumours

An association between digital glomus tumours and NF1 has been described {20530151}. Children with NF1 may develop non-ossifying fibromas of the long bones, sometimes reported as Jaffe–Campanacci syndrome {24232412}. Individuals with NF1 have a lifetime risk as high as 6% of developing gastrointestinal stromal tumours {26511941}, which mostly affect the small intestine and are frequently multifocal {16330947; 40043354}. Systematic screening in a cohort of adults with NF1 detects phaeochromocytoma in > 5% {27460956; 27450695}. Women with NF1 have an increased risk of developing breast cancer before the age of 50 years, and systematic screening starting from the age of 30 years is part of international recommendations {28860858; 26926675}. Somatostatinomas (duodenal carcinoids) are rarely detected and are often metastatic when diagnosed {20300877}. Rhabdomyosarcoma in NF1 has a predilection for the genitourinary tract in young children and comprises about 1% of paediatric rhabdomyosarcomas {15127010; 17330850; 33734413}. Other non-nervous system bone and soft tissue tumours that have been reported in NF1 include undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma {33734413}, juvenile xanthogranuloma {25037786}, and inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumour and its malignant progression {37871654}. Non-nervous system tumours may be sentinel lesions for unrecognized NF1 {27390349; 36625737}.

Peripheral nervous system tumours

Multiple cutaneous neurofibromas are benign peripheral nerve sheath tumours that typically develop starting at puberty and are present in > 90% of adults with NF1 {28230061}. In contrast, studies suggest that plexiform neurofibromas are of congenital origin and occur in 30–50% of individuals with NF1 {28230061}. Cutaneous neurofibromas are typically benign, whereas plexiform neurofibromas have a risk of becoming malignant. Individuals with NF1 have an 11.7–15.8% lifetime risk of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) development {26926675; 23036231}. Individuals with a germline NF1 microdeletion have an at least two times increased risk of developing MPNSTs {12660952}.

CNS tumours

Tumours of the optic pathway occur in 15% of children with NF1 {28806346}. Only one third of children with optic pathway gliomas develop symptoms such as visual impairment. Most NF1-associated brain tumours in children are classified as pilocytic astrocytoma WHO grade 1. Brain tumours in adults with NF1 are less common and have a higher malignant potential {30531922; 36840626}.

Epidemiology

NF1 is an autosomal dominant disorder with a birth incidence of 1 case per 2000–3500 people {25354145; 28230061}. In large genetic databases, mosaicism for an NF1 pathogenic variant in DNA from peripheral blood seems to be as frequent as the clinical form of NF1 {40169570}. These mosaic cases frequently have no or only very few NF1 features.

Etiology

NF1 is caused by heterozygous inactivating mutations in the NF1 gene, mapping to chromosome band 17q11.2, encoding neurofibromin {2134734; 2114220; 1694727}. Neurofibromin is expressed widely during embryonic development, most abundantly in the CNS, occurring in Schwann cells, melanocytes, neurons and oligodendrocytes, leukocytes, adrenal medulla, and many other cell types. Half of the subjects with NF1 have a de novo mutation in the NF1 gene.

Pathogenesis

Neurofibromin acts as a tumour suppressor {8499945; 25877329}. Different interaction partners of neurofibromin have been described, but the most important functional domain is the RAS-GAP–related domain. Neurofibromin is a negative regulator of the RAS/MAPK pathway and stimulates the intrinsic GTPase activity of RAS, resulting in the conversion of RAS-GTP into inactive RAS-GDP {25877329}. SPRED1 (the protein implicated in Legius syndrome) binds to neurofibromin on both sides of the GAP-related domain and recruits neurofibromin to the membrane where it can downregulate RAS-GTP {22751498; 26635368; 27313208}. Neurofibromin functions as a dimer and can be present in an open or closed conformation, binding to RAS in the open conformation only {34707296}.

A second hit in NF1 has been reported in associated tumours such as MPNSTs, neurofibromas, neuroblastomas, astrocytomas, gastrointestinal stromal tumours, optic pathway glioma, phaeochromocytomas, glomus tumours, juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia, and breast cancer {19410195; 29926297}. A second hit has also been reported in non-tumoural manifestations such as café-au-lait macules {17914445} and pseudarthrosis tissue {16773574; 25612910}. The heterozygous NF1 background in non-tumoural cells such as mast cells plays a role in oncogenesis in mouse models of NF1 {18984156}.

A subset of neurofibromas progresses into atypical neurofibroma / atypical neurofibromatous neoplasm of uncertain biological potential (ANNUBP), which has an increased risk of transformation to MPNST. ANNUBP harbours additional genetic alterations, including CDKN2A/CDKN2B deletion {21987445; 30722027; 39500722}. Further accumulation of aberrations in PRC2 genes and, less commonly, in TP53 characterizes transformation to MPNST {25119042; 25240281; 25305755}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross appearance of the tumours in NF1 is overall not different from that in sporadic settings. Plexiform neurofibroma is highly characteristic of NF1, and it assumes multinodular expansion of nerve fascicles and resembles braided strands when affecting large nerves or a plexus. Massive soft tissue neurofibroma presents as large plaque or pendulous lesions. Malignant transformation in neurofibroma corresponds to a fleshy mass often with necrosis.

Histopathology

The tumours are histopathologically almost identical to their sporadic counterparts. MPNSTs typically lose Schwann cell markers (S100 and SOX10) and H3K27me3; however, such loss may be less common than in sporadic MPNSTs {26645727; 27580378}. In NF1, MPNST most commonly arises as a transformation of neurofibroma, and it can be histologically abrupt or through progression of ANNUBP (see section Neurofibroma for the precise definition of ANNUBP) {39500722}. Epithelioid MPNST is not associated with NF1. H3K27me3 loss in sarcomas with skeletal muscle differentiation suggests MPNST with heterologous differentiation (malignant triton tumour) in the NF1 setting {31107719; 38603649}. True rhabdomyosarcoma in NF1 is usually of embryonal type in children {15127010; 17330850; 33734413}, whereas adult rhabdomyosarcoma has rarely been reported {37871654}. NF1-associated gastrointestinal stromal tumours are almost always wildtype for KIT, PDGFRA, BRAF, and genes encoding SDH subunits; and they predominantly consist of spindle cells, often with skeinoid fibres {15897742; 16330947; 40043354}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A pathogenic NF1 variant can be detected in as many as 95% of individuals with a clinical diagnosis of NF1 {10862084}. In 5% of cases, an NF1 microdeletion has been identified {28213670}. Individuals with an NF1 microdeletion including the SUZ12 gene in the deletion have an increased risk of developing MPNST {12660952}. SUZ12 codes for a component of PRC2, which has been implicated in MPNST development {25119042; 25240281; 25305755}. A few other genotype–phenotype correlations have been reported. Individuals with NF1 with a specific 3 bp deletion (c.2970_2972del) or a missense mutation affecting p.Arg1809 do not show neurofibromas or other NF1-associated tumours, and the disorder cannot be differentiated clinically from Legius syndrome {30190611; 26178382; 25370043; 17160901}. Compared with the general NF1 population, individuals with NF1 missense mutations affecting codons 844–848 have a higher incidence of externally visible plexiform neurofibromas, symptomatic spinal neurofibromas, optic pathway glioma, and MPNST {29290338}. Missense variants at amino acids p.Met1149, p.Arg1276, and p.Lys1423 are associated with a specific phenotype {31595648}.

In cases of mosaicism for the NF1 variant, it is frequently not possible to detect the responsible postzygotic NF1 variant in peripheral white blood cells, and the NF1 variant can be identified only in purified cells from affected tissues {17668375}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Diagnostic criteria for NF1 were established during a consensus conference of the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) {3128965}. These criteria were later revised (see #56424Box 6.04), and criteria for mosaic NF1 (see #56425Box 6.05) were established {34012067}.

A summary of the main features of NF1 is presented in #55943Table 6.09.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Individuals with NF1 have an increased standardized mortality ratio (SMR) of 3.22 (2.81–3.68) in a Finnish population study, mainly resulting from malignancy and cardiovascular problems {25354145}. Increased death due to malignancy and cardiovascular problems was also present in a study based on US death certificates {11283797}. In children, the Finnish study showed an SMR of 73.1 {30724342}. A French study showed an SMR of 4.14 {37257636} for the whole NF1 population. A study in north-west England showed an averaged reduced lifespan of 8 years for individuals with NF1 {21694737}. MEK inhibitors are emerging therapeutic opportunities for patients with inoperable plexiform neurofibroma {32187457; 39514826; 39762421}.

Surveillance guidelines for the increased tumour risk have been published {36684394; 30006586}.

The diagnostic criteria for NF1 are met in an individuala with two or more of the following features:

 

Six or more café-au-lait macules > 5 mm in greatest diameter in prepubertal individuals and > 15 mm in greatest diameter in postpubertal individualsb

 

Freckling in the axillary or inguinal regionb

 

Two or more neurofibromas of any type or one plexiform neurofibroma

 

Optic pathway glioma

 

Two or more iris Lisch nodules identified by slit lamp examination or two or more choroidal abnormalities, defined as bright, patchy nodules, imaged by optical coherence tomography / near-infrared reflectance imaging

 

A distinctive osseous lesion such as sphenoid dysplasiac, anterolateral bowing of the tibia, or pseudarthrosis of a long bone

 

A heterozygous pathogenic NF1 variant with a variant allele fraction of 50% in apparently normal tissue such as white blood cells

aApplies to individuals without a parent diagnosed with NF1. For a child of a parent with NF1, only one of the criteria is required for diagnosis. bIf only café-au-lait macules and freckling are present, the diagnosis is most likely NF1 but exceptionally the person might have another diagnosis, such as Legius syndrome. At least one of the two pigmentary findings (café-au-lait macules or freckling) should be bilateral. cSphenoid wing dysplasia is not a separate criterion in case of an ipsilateral orbital plexiform neurofibroma.

The diagnostic criteria for mosaic NF1 are met in an individual with any of the following features:

 

A pathogenic heterozygous NF1 variant with a variant allele fraction of significantly less than 50% in apparently normal tissue such as white blood cells AND one other NF1 diagnostic criterion (except a parent fulfilling diagnostic criteria for NF1)

 

An identical pathogenic heterozygous NF1 variant in two anatomically independent affected tissues (in the absence of a pathogenic NF1 variant in unaffected tissue)

 

A clearly segmental distribution of café-au-lait macules or cutaneous neurofibromas AND another NF1 diagnostic criterion (except a parent fulfilling diagnostic criteria for NF1) or a child fulfilling diagnostic criteria for NF1

 

Only one NF1 diagnostic criterion from the following list: freckling in the axillary and inguinal region, optic pathway glioma, two or more Lisch nodules or two or more choroidal abnormalities, distinctive osseous lesion typical for NF1, two or more neurofibromas or one plexiform neurofibroma AND a child fulfilling the criteria for NF1
