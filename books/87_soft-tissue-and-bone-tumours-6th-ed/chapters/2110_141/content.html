2.1.10 Synovial chondromatosis

Definition

Synovial chondromatosis (SC) is a locally aggressive neoplasm consisting of multiple hyaline cartilaginous nodules growing in a distinctive clustered arrangement involving the joint space, subsynovial tissue, or tenosynovium. A small subset of cases are malignant (synovial chondrosarcoma).

Related terminology

Not recommended: synovial osteochondromatosis; synovial chondrosis; Reichel syndrome; synovial chondrometaplasia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

SC usually affects a single large appendicular joint, most often the knee (50–65% of cases); it may also involve the hip, elbow, shoulder, ankle, metacarpophalangeal {17848703}, and temporomandibular {37562984} joints. Bilateral involvement is rare {32534572}. It can occur in the spine, most often arising from cervical facet joints {26363786}.

Tenosynovial chondromatosis, or chondromatosis in tendon sheaths, most frequently affects the flexor compartment of the hands and wrists as well as the plantar tendon sheaths of the feet {20711779}. Bursae, usually those in described anatomical sites, may also develop chondromatosis (bursal chondromatosis {20711779}). In some cases, extension of SC from the hip into the iliopsoas and obturator externus bursae may also occur {17848703}.

Chondrosarcoma secondary to SC occurs most commonly in the knee {32506447} but has also been reported in the spine {30719535} and in multiple other joints.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Common presenting features include joint pain, swelling, restricted movement, crepitus, and locking {17848703}.

Imaging

Radiographs reveal intra-articular calcification in 75–95% of cases {17848703}, showing either chondroid mineralization {12136332} or ossified nodules. The latter are usually of uniform size and shape {17848703}, but occasionally there is a single, conglomerate mass {8160032}. Joint space is usually maintained, but pre-existent or resultant osteoarthritis may be present {17848703}. Radiographic evidence of pressure erosion occurs where joint capacity is restricted {3786727}. CT is useful for showing subtle calcification that can be missed on radiographs within a low-density, lobular mass {17848703}. MRI features include a lobular T2-hyperintense intra-articular mass with focal areas of low signal intensity (calcification and ossification), fat (marrow within ossified bodies), and typical cartilaginous enhancement {17848703}. Spinal chondromatosis often erodes bony structures adjacent to the joint and may cause neural compression; calcification is often not prominent {26363786}. In tenosynovial chondromatosis, there is an elongated mineralized mass within a tendon sheath or multiple cartilaginous nodules surrounded by tendon sheath fluid {26008872}. In bursal chondromatosis, the mass is round or ovoid within a known bursa {20711779}. Severe degenerative joint disease may mimic synovial chondromatosis, but the bodies typically vary in shape and size.

Chondrosarcoma arising from synovial chondromatosis should be suspected if there is early local recurrence after complete resection {32506447} or rapid worsening of pain {19057985}. Pressure erosion, chronicity, frequent local recurrence, and extra-articular extension are nonspecific {1985712}. Suggestive imaging features include cortical destruction and marrow infiltration {26919862}.

Epidemiology

SC is a rare neoplasm, with an estimated incidence of about 1.8 cases per 1 million person-years {27490219}. It typically occurs in the third to fifth decades of life and is twice as common in men as in women {9670824; 27490219}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

FN1::ACVR2A and ACVR2A::FN1 fusions, as assessed by FISH and/or targeted RNA fusion assays, are found in at least 50% of benign SC cases, as well as in cases with malignant transformation (synovial chondrosarcoma) {25024164; 31273315}. It is not clear whether it is the FN1- or ACVR2A-altered gene that drives tumour development, because both FN1::ACVR2A and ACVR2A::FN1 are predicted to be in-frame. Both genes have been implicated in the development of other neoplasms {27469327; 26691015; 29910124}.

Macroscopic appearance

SC appears as multiple greyish-white, smooth or irregular, uniform or variably sized nodules, usually in the form of intra-articular loose bodies but sometimes embedded within or attached to synovium.

Histopathology

SC is characterized by variably cellular nodules of hyaline cartilage, in which chondrocytes display a distinctive clustered arrangement and typically lack a stromal vascular network. The nodules are often surrounded by a rim of residual synovial tissue. Longstanding lesions may show calcification and/or endochondral ossification {9670824}. SC may exhibit focal cytological atypia, making the diagnosis of secondary chondrosarcoma challenging. This distinction often requires a combination of histological features, including unequivocal nuclear enlargement, pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, and loss of the characteristic chondrocyte clustering {1985712}. Additional findings that support malignancy include permeative infiltration of adjacent cortical or cancellous bone, conspicuous myxoid change, atypical mitotic figures, and transition into high-grade chondrosarcoma {9670824; 12136332; 28694891}.

Differential diagnosis

Primary synovial chondromatosis must be distinguished from multiple osteochondral loose bodies within the synovium seen in severe degenerative joint disease. While both entities lack a well-developed stromal vasculature, concentric layers (rings) of cartilage with a more orderly distribution of chondrocytes would suggest osteochondral loose bodies. Finally, a variety of other benign chondroid neoplasms, including soft tissue chondromas and those with grungy and/or chondroblastoma-like matrix calcifications {31273315}, may mimic SC, a subset of which also harbour FN1 fusions; however, SC is unique in its distinctive clustered chondrocyte architecture, and an ACVR2A fusion appears to be specific to SC among other FN1-rearranged benign chondroid mimics {31273315; 37102574; 38278609; 39181450; 39016330; 11342780}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis is usually unnecessary in clinically and histologically typical examples. However, in challenging cases, targeted RNA fusion assays can be used to detect FN1::ACVR2A rearrangements, but they will not distinguish benign from malignant lesions {30582658}. FN1 FISH can also help support the diagnosis of SC; however, FISH will not distinguish SC from other FN1-rearranged benign chondroid neoplasms. IDH1 and IDH2 variants are absent {21598255; 22074484}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nodular cartilaginous tumour within synovium or loose in joint space; clustering of chondrocytes; minimal atypia and increased cellularity; malignancy is defined by a combination of histological findings, including unequivocal nuclear atypia, loss of chondrocyte clustering, bone invasion, conspicuous myxoid change, and atypical mitotic figures, or by merging with a high-grade chondrosarcoma.

Staging

Malignant examples will be staged according to bone sarcoma protocols (see <>TNM staging of tumours of bone).

Prognosis and prediction

The disease recurs in 15–20% of patients, with higher rates reported for tenosynovial cases {27490219; 12960811}. Malignant transformation to a secondary chondrosarcoma is uncommon, occurring in 5–10% of cases, usually in large tumours and in longstanding cases after multiple local recurrences over several years {9670824; 24737946}. However, malignant cases may present de novo. Early and rapid growth of a local recurrence after treatment should raise suspicion for malignancy {28694891}.
