1.1 Soft tissue tumours: Introduction

Epidemiology
Incidence

Benign soft tissue tumours are much more common than sarcomas, by as much as two orders of magnitude. The annual clinical incidence of new patients consulting a doctor for benign tumours of soft tissue may reach 3000 cases per 1 million population {7282321}. In contrast, the annual incidence of soft tissue sarcoma is about 50 cases per 1 million population {22033323; 8042499; 28687376}. This represents 1–2% of malignant tumours overall, but more than 10% of malignant tumours in children. Rates appear stable over time, with no significant geographical variation. Cancer registries record only malignant tumours and thus do not capture the many intermediate lesions. A French registry that included all soft tissue tumours reported an incidence of about 95 cases per 1 million population when intermediate malignancies were counted {33630918}.

Age and anatomical site distribution

Benign soft tissue tumours show characteristic age and site patterns. Lipomas account for about 30% of cases, fibrohistiocytic and fibrous tumours for another 30%, vascular tumours for 10%, and nerve sheath tumours for 5%. Half of all vascular tumours present in patients aged < 20 years [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Skin tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]]. Nearly all benign tumours are superficial (99%) and small (< 5 cm in 95%) {6581679}.

Soft tissue sarcomas can occur anywhere. About half arise in the extremities, most commonly in the thigh; about 15% occur in the trunk wall; and about 10% in the retroperitoneum. Other sites include the gastrointestinal tract (~8%), head and neck (~6%), and uterus (~5%) {31081028}. Extremity and trunk wall tumours are superficial in about 30% of cases (median: 5 cm) and deep in 60% (median: 9 cm) {8042499}. Retroperitoneal tumours are typically much larger at diagnosis {26727100}. A slight male predominance is observed.

At diagnosis, about 10% of patients with sarcoma have detectable metastases, usually in the lungs. One third of patients die of tumour-related disease, most commonly from pulmonary metastases. Six histological types – undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) – account for about two thirds of all sarcomas, and most carry substantial metastatic risk (see Grading and staging of sarcomas, below) {29211310; 33630918}. Histological type distribution varies over time and across series, reflecting changes in diagnostic definitions (e.g. “malignant fibrous histiocytoma” in the 1990s). Roughly 20% of sarcomas are ultrarare, with an incidence of < 1 case per 1 million person-years {33910263}.

Age influences incidence. Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma occurs almost exclusively in children. Synovial sarcoma is most common in young adults. UPS, liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, and myxofibrosarcoma dominate in older adults. Like that of most cancers, sarcoma incidence rises with age; the median age at diagnosis is about 65 years.

Etiology

The cause of most soft tissue tumours is unknown. In < 10% of cases, genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, prior irradiation, viral infection, or immunodeficiency contribute. Rare associations include development of sarcomas in scar tissue, at fracture sites, or adjacent to surgical implants {10751369}. Some angiosarcomas arise in chronic lymphoedema. The majority of sarcomas, however, appear to arise de novo.

Chemical carcinogens

Epidemiological studies have produced conflicting evidence on herbicide and dioxin exposure. Some Swedish studies linked phenoxy herbicides, chlorophenols, and dioxins to higher sarcoma risk {2313720; 3390800}, while others found no association. Differences may reflect varying dioxin contamination levels in herbicides {9599727; 9344316}.

Radiation

Radiation accounts for about 5% of soft tissue sarcomas {30709440}. Most cases occur after high-dose exposure (≥ 50 Gy), with a typical latency of about 10 years, although shorter intervals have been reported. More than half are classified as UPS, usually aggressive. Angiosarcoma is the most frequent radiation-associated tumour of the skin. Patients with germline mutations in RB1 (retinoblastoma), TP53 (Li–Fraumeni syndrome), or NF1 especially are at high risk {15800318; 21059199; 16735710}.

Viral infection and immunodeficiency

Kaposi sarcoma is driven by KSHV/HHV8 infection {10643499}, and its course depends on host immune status {9300629}. EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours occur in immunodeficient patients {16330945}.

Genetic susceptibility

Several familial syndromes predispose to soft tissue tumours.

Hereditary multiple lipomas or angiolipomas are well described {17511936; 17674589}.

Desmoid fibromatosis arises in familial adenomatous polyposis {8150351}. (“Gardner syndrome” is now considered an obsolete term used to describe extracolonic features that are now known to be present in most patients with familial adenomatous polyposis.)

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) predisposes to benign and malignant nerve sheath tumours; 5–10% of patients with NF1 develop MPNSTs {19508838}, and 5–7% develop gastrointestinal stromal tumours.

NF2-related schwannomatosis (neurofibromatosis type 2) is associated with benign nerve sheath tumours.

Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53 variants) confers very high lifetime sarcoma risk; by the age of 30 years, half of patients develop cancer, and > 30% of those cancers are sarcomas {9554443; 19204208}.

Lynch syndrome (mismatch repair deficiency) has been linked to sarcomas with microsatellite instability, particularly UPS and pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma {39742560; 40190387}.

Retinoblastoma syndrome (RB1 variants) increases the risk of sarcoma, especially after radiation {15800318}.

Large genomic studies suggest that as many as half of patients with sarcoma may carry potentially pathogenic germline variants {27498913}, and defects in telomere or mitotic regulation may also predispose {36656928}. The clinical implications of these findings remain to be clarified.

Clinical features

Clinical findings alone rarely distinguish benign from malignant soft tissue tumours. Most sarcomas of the extremities and trunk wall present as a large, painless mass discovered incidentally. Patients sometimes associate the lesion with minor trauma. Some tumours, however, grow rapidly and cause pain, prompting earlier evaluation.

Intra-abdominal and retroperitoneal sarcomas usually present as asymptomatic abdominal masses detected on imaging. Nonspecific abdominal pain may occur, and less common manifestations include gastrointestinal bleeding, partial obstruction, neurological symptoms, systemic features (fever, fatigue, anaemia), or altered liver function tests.

Because of their rarity and often indolent presentation, sarcomas are frequently mistaken for benign conditions. All superficial lesions > 5 cm and all deep-seated lesions carry a significant risk of malignancy. Such patients should be referred to a specialist centre for biopsy, expert pathology review, and multidisciplinary care {34303806; 35830886}.

Imaging of tumours of soft tissue

Diagnosis of soft tissue tumours usually requires biopsy, but in some cases imaging findings are highly characteristic. For example, a mass arising from the inferior vena cava is almost invariably a leiomyosarcoma, whereas a retroperitoneal lipomatous mass outside the solid abdominal viscera is nearly always a liposarcoma. A rectus abdominis mass in a young woman strongly suggests desmoid fibromatosis, and alternating bands of fat and soft tissue beneath the scapula in older patients are typical of elastofibroma. Although most tumours are accessible for percutaneous biopsy, imaging can guide differential diagnosis, triage, and the choice of diagnostic pathway, particularly in patients with significant comorbidities.

Ultrasound is widely available, inexpensive, and non-invasive, and serves as an effective first-line investigation for suspicious soft tissue masses {19414084} [[National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Suspected cancer: recognition and referral (NG12). London (UK): NICE; 2015 Jun [updated 2017 Jul]. Available from: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng12.]]. Plain radiographs are rarely used, but they may be helpful to assess calcification or bone involvement in very firm or fixed masses. MRI provides the best soft tissue contrast and is the modality of choice for tumours of the limbs, pelvis, and superficial trunk. MRI is valuable for diagnosis, surgical planning, and response assessment, and it also assists radiotherapy planning by delineating tumour extent and peritumoural oedema. For extremity tumours, standard protocols include axial T2-weighted, T1-weighted, and fat-suppressed sequences, with coronal short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) and T1-weighted sequences to define margins, relationship to neurovascular structures, tissue character, bone invasion, and treatment response {26802529; 26696086}. Contrast-enhanced CT is most useful for tumours of the thorax and abdomen and provides excellent definition of tumour–organ and tumour–vascular relationships.

18F-FDG PET/CT has limited value for diagnosis, because it cannot reliably separate low-grade sarcomas from benign lesions {11337565}. It may, however, help direct biopsy to the most metabolically active region in heterogeneous tumours, as glucose uptake correlates with tumour grade {16506050}. PET/CT is also useful in identifying malignant transformation in patients with NF1, in whom increased uptake suggests sarcomatous change of a neurofibroma to MPNST {24516522}. PET/CT is being studied as a tool to predict response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, with promising early results {40422516}. Whole-body MRI is gaining interest as a staging method for bone metastases and for myxoid liposarcoma and is recommended in the European Society for Medical Oncology–European Reference Network for Rare Adult Solid Cancers–European Reference Network for Genetic Tumour Risk Syndromes (ESMO–EURACAN–GENTURIS) guidelines {40378980; 34303806}.

Integration of imaging with histopathology offers opportunities to improve diagnostic accuracy. Percutaneous biopsy provides limited sampling and is prone to grading error, whereas imaging assesses the whole tumour. Studies show that imaging estimation of necrosis can improve biopsy-based grading {31653457}.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging as a diagnostic adjunct. Early studies suggest that AI can help distinguish lipomas from liposarcomas {39351025} and may nearly double the accuracy of preoperative grading in retroperitoneal sarcomas {37922931}. The concept of a virtual biopsy based on imaging features is under active investigation {35847882}. Delayed sarcoma diagnosis remains a major problem, and AI systems embedded into imaging workflows could support earlier triage and referral. Although still developing, AI offers substantial potential to improve outcomes for patients with rare cancers and  is likely to play an increasing role in multimodal diagnostics {39690222}.

Biopsy

Biopsy is mandatory to establish malignancy and determine histological type, subtype, and grade. Clinical guidelines recommend biopsy for all deep-seated tumours > 5 cm. An exception may be radiologically classic well-differentiated fatty tumours in the limbs or trunk, where resection without biopsy may be considered in expert centres.

Techniques

Core needle biopsy is the standard method for extremity and trunk wall tumours. Multiple cores should be obtained through a single tract that can later be excised en bloc. With 2–3 adequate cores, accuracy of histological diagnosis, grading, and prognostication is very high {20872955}.

Image guidance (ultrasound or CT) improves sampling accuracy, particularly for deep or heterogeneous lesions {32514582; 34303806}. For retroperitoneal or intrathoracic masses, a percutaneous coaxial core needle biopsy is recommended {27859013; 32367501}.

Incisional biopsy is an option when core biopsy fails. The incision should be minimal and oriented so the tract can be resected at definitive surgery.

Excisional biopsy should generally be avoided for lesions > 2 cm. It contaminates surrounding tissue planes and complicates subsequent resection, especially in deep or intra-abdominal sites.

Frozen section and FNA are not generally recommended. In select centres with cytological expertise, or where other approaches are not available, these may be of use. FNA with cell block preparation may support immunohistochemistry and/or molecular testing.

Clinical relevance

Biopsy results guide treatment strategy. Sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy varies by histological type and grade. Surgical planning and selection of systemic therapy may be influenced by biopsy findings. Concordance between biopsy and resection is high for type (~90%) but less so for grade, especially in retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma, where grade is underestimated in as many as 40% of cases {30675910; 25354575}.

In experienced centres, biopsy may be omitted if the diagnosis is radiologically certain (e.g. classic liposarcoma or leiomyosarcoma of the inferior vena cava), no neoadjuvant therapy is planned, and complete resection with minimal morbidity is feasible.

Management

Once histological diagnosis, size, grade, and metastatic status are established, treatment should be planned by a multidisciplinary team. The goal is to balance local control and survival with preservation of function and quality of life. Surgery remains the primary treatment, while radiotherapy and chemotherapy are tailored to individual risk and tumour biology.

Surgery

For extremity and trunk wall sarcomas, appropriate surgery is wide en bloc excision with a cuff of healthy tissue. Microscopically positive margins increase the risk of recurrence, metastasis, and death {15625364; 20130465; 11882765; 12733153}. Margins may be deliberately close in order to preserve critical structures; in such cases, preoperative radiotherapy or combined chemoradiotherapy can mitigate the risk {29346043; 23110811}.

The extent of resection depends on tumour size, relationship to vital structures, and expected morbidity. Histological type should also guide surgical planning {39174839}. Amputation is rarely required and is reserved for unresectable tumours in patients without metastases and with potential for rehabilitation.

Low-grade sarcomas of the extremities and trunk wall can often be treated with surgery alone if negative margins are achieved. High-grade or large (> 5 cm) sarcomas usually require surgery combined with radiotherapy. Randomized trials show that radiotherapy reduces local recurrence risk {9440743}. For small (< 5 cm) high-grade sarcomas or any size low-grade sarcomas, surgery alone is acceptable if wide (1–2 cm) margins are feasible. In many cases, radiotherapy is used more often than strictly necessary. A subset of sarcomas can be controlled by surgery alone, with recurrence rates of 5–10% {17893504; 10506627; 29470819}.

Retroperitoneal sarcomas present late and are often large, with frequent organ involvement. Complete en bloc resection at primary presentation offers the best chance for cure. Prognosis depends mainly on completeness of resection, histological grade, and type {26727100; 25915910; 16926564; 37753660}. Survival is inferior compared with extremity soft tissue sarcomas. For well-differentiated and dedifferentiated liposarcomas, removal of all ipsilateral retroperitoneal fat is recommended, as tumour and normal fat are difficult to distinguish. Organ preservation (kidney, pancreas, bladder, etc.) should be individualized and requires specific expertise. Neurovascular sacrifice and en bloc resection of organs such as liver or pancreas must weigh the likelihood of local control against surgical morbidity {33852100; 35623985}. Desmoid fibromatosis is now managed conservatively. Most cases are observed, as many stabilize or regress spontaneously {19568815; 39620931}. MRI T2 signal intensity may help identify lesions more likely to progress {30418203}. Surgery is reserved for selected symptomatic or progressive cases {40710218}.

Radiotherapy

The role of radiotherapy in extremity sarcoma was established in randomized trials comparing amputation with limb-sparing surgery plus radiation {7114936}, and later trials confirmed the benefit of adding radiotherapy to surgery {9440743; 8622034}. Adjuvant radiation is not generally required for low-grade sarcomas unless tumours are large, margins are positive, or recurrence has occurred.

Radiation can be given preoperatively or postoperatively. Both approaches achieve similar control rates {12103287}. Preoperative treatment allows smaller fields and lower doses (50 Gy vs 60–66 Gy) but carries a higher risk of wound complication (35% vs 17%) {23242820}. Postoperative treatment avoids wound issues but exposes larger tissue volumes and increases late fibrosis {15948265}. Intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) provides more conformal coverage and may reduce morbidity {25185087}.

Selected cases of high-grade sarcoma can be managed without radiation. Studies show > 85% local control after surgery alone in carefully chosen cases {17893504; 10506627; 1919628}. Candidates include those with wide (> 1 cm) negative margins or intact fascia.

In retroperitoneal sarcoma, the STRASS and STREXIT studies support preoperative radiotherapy for well-differentiated and grade 2 dedifferentiated liposarcomas, which often recur locally {32941794; 35833413}. Leiomyosarcoma shows little benefit, as failure is mainly metastatic. Data for rarer histological types are limited. Radiotherapy is sometimes considered for solitary fibrous tumour, which appears radiosensitive.

Adjuvant and neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Chemotherapy has the greatest impact in specific histological types. Ewing sarcoma and alveolar and embryonal rhabdomyosarcomas require systemic therapy because of high metastatic risk and chemosensitivity.

For other high-grade sarcomas, benefit depends on size, grade, histology, and patient factors. Nomograms can help estimate risk {27068860}. Randomized trials and meta-analyses show that chemotherapy improves disease-free survival and local control {2674336; 8201375; 9400508}, with overall survival benefit demonstrated in selected trials and meta-analyses {11230464; 18521899; 30690293}. However, many trials have not shown survival improvement, leaving the role of chemotherapy debated.

Preoperative anthracycline plus ifosfamide can be considered for large, high-grade sarcomas, especially histological types most likely to respond (synovial sarcoma, myxoid/round cell liposarcoma, pleomorphic liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, MPNST, UPS). Three cycles are equivalent to five, with less toxicity {27733375}.

Subtype-specific activity has been demonstrated for several agents. Gemcitabine combined with docetaxel is active in undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma and leiomyosarcoma {17602081}. Trabectedin has shown efficacy in myxoid or round cell liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma {17586092}. Gemcitabine combined with taxanes is effective in angiosarcoma {18809609; 21464156}, while ifosfamide shows particular benefit in myxoid or round cell liposarcoma and synovial sarcoma {15383796; 19088035; 17592298}. Notably, in high-grade myxoid liposarcoma, trabectedin achieves outcomes equivalent to anthracycline/ifosfamide but with a more favourable toxicity profile {38232337}.

Multimodal treatment

Most sarcomas are treated with surgery combined with radiotherapy, while chemotherapy is added for patients at high risk of distant relapse. Candidates are usually young, fit patients with large, grade 3 tumours. Several sequencing strategies are in use. One approach is neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery and postoperative radiotherapy. Another is concurrent chemotherapy with preoperative radiotherapy followed by surgery, with adjuvant chemotherapy considered in selected cases. A third option is sequential neoadjuvant chemotherapy and preoperative radiotherapy before surgery, sometimes followed by postoperative chemotherapy. The advantage of preoperative systemic therapy is that chemosensitivity can be assessed early, allowing ineffective regimens to be discontinued. Emerging strategies include the combination of preoperative radiotherapy with immunotherapy. A recent trial demonstrated improved survival when pembrolizumab was added to radiotherapy for grade 2–3 undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, and dedifferentiated or pleomorphic liposarcoma of the extremities, although further validation is needed {39547252}.

Terminology used to reflect biological potential

Soft tissue tumours are divided into four biological categories: benign, intermediate (locally aggressive), intermediate (rarely metastasizing), and malignant. These definitions were introduced in the third (2002) edition of this volume and remain in use.

Benign: Most benign soft tissue tumours do not recur, and those that do recur usually do so in a non-destructive fashion. Complete excision is almost always curative. Exceptionally, a morphologically benign lesion may metastasize, but this phenomenon is exceedingly rare – probably less than 1 in 50 000 cases. The best-documented example is cutaneous benign fibrous histiocytoma.

Intermediate (locally aggressive): Tumours in this group frequently recur and often grow in an infiltrative, destructive manner. They almost never metastasize but typically require wide excision with a margin of normal tissue to achieve control. Desmoid fibromatosis is the prototype.

Intermediate (rarely metastasizing): These tumours combine locally aggressive behaviour with a small but real risk of distant spread. The metastatic rate is < 2% and is not predictable by histology. When metastasis occurs, it usually involves lung or lymph nodes. Prototypical examples include plexiform fibrohistiocytic tumour and angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma.

Malignant: Malignant soft tissue tumours (sarcomas) have both locally destructive growth and substantial metastatic potential. The risk of distant spread varies widely by histological type and grade, from about 20% to nearly 100%. Some low-grade sarcomas have metastatic rates of only 2–10%, but recurrent disease may progress to higher grade, as seen in myxofibrosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma.

It is important to note that these biological categories do not correspond to histological “intermediate grade” in sarcomas, nor do they align exactly with ICD-O behaviour codes. Locally aggressive tumours with no metastatic potential are usually coded as /1, whereas rarely metastasizing tumours are coded as /3.

Grading and staging of sarcomas
Grading

Histological typing alone is insufficient to predict clinical course in most sarcomas. Grading is therefore used, based on the intrinsic features of the untreated primary tumour, while staging incorporates tumour extent {23780383}.

Early attempts at grading were made by Broders in 1939, with systematic studies emerging in the 1970s {907970}. Modern grading systems, developed in the 1980s, rely mainly on mitotic activity, necrosis, and differentiation {6692258; 6846018; 6693192; 8297645}. To improve reproducibility, WHO recommends reporting mitotic activity per defined area in mm2 rather than per 10 high-power fields, as field size varies across microscopes. This also facilitates digital pathology.

The two most widely used systems are those of the US National Cancer Institute (NCI) {6692258} and the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) {6693192}. The FNCLCC system is more precisely defined and reproducible and is now the preferred system, endorsed by the College of American Pathologists and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) {20367297} [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. It scores differentiation, mitotic activity, and necrosis, with the sum determining the grade. Differentiation scoring varies by histological type, which is the most challenging component.

Grading predicts the risk of distant metastasis and survival both across sarcomas as a group and within common histological types such as UPS and synovial sarcoma {11346874}. It has little value for predicting local recurrence, which is determined more by surgical margins. Grading is less informative for tumours with distinctive biology, such as myxoid liposarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, alveolar soft part sarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma, and clear cell sarcoma; it is not applied to tumours that rarely metastasize {17090186; 16359536}.

Limitations of grading include sampling error in core biopsies, which can underestimate grade in as many as 40% of retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcomas. In biopsies, only high-grade assignments can be considered definitive; low- and intermediate-grade assignments should be regarded as provisional. Ancillary methods may improve accuracy, such as imaging to assess necrosis or proliferation markers (Ki-67, phosphohistone H3) as alternatives to mitotic count {12209729}. Despite limitations, grading remains the most important prognostic factor and is expected by clinicians to guide therapy.

Molecular approaches may further refine grading. The complexity index in sarcomas (CINSARC) gene expression signature, based on chromosomal complexity and mitotic regulation, has outperformed histological grading in sarcomas with complex genomic profiles and in gastrointestinal stromal tumours {28710396; 20581836; 22167411; 29860427}. Independent validation studies are ongoing {35848358; 40423526}.

Staging

Staging combines histological and clinical information. The most widely used systems are those of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and the AJCC. The TNM system incorporates grade, tumour size, depth, nodal status, and presence of metastasis {20180029}.

Important prognostic factors for relapse and death include patient characteristics (age, sex), tumour features (histology, grade, size, site, depth, and metastasis at diagnosis), and quality of management (expert biopsy, planned surgery, adequacy of margins). Nomograms have been developed to integrate these variables and provide individualized outcome prediction {27068860; 23530096; 22153892; 40712530; 37753660}.

Significant changes in soft tissue tumour classification since the fifth edition
Adipocytic tumours

One new tumour type is introduced: lipoblastoma-like tumour. The diagnostic criteria for atypical spindle cell / pleomorphic lipomatous tumours are clarified; they are now separated into atypical spindle cell and atypical pleomorphic subtypes. Reproducible morphological criteria distinguishing non-lipogenic areas of cellular well-differentiated liposarcoma from cytologically bland dedifferentiated liposarcoma are established; the designation “low-grade dedifferentiated liposarcoma” is no longer recommended. More precise grading parameters for myxoid liposarcoma are specified, with explicit distinction between low-grade and higher-grade disease.

Fibroblastic and myofibroblastic tumours

Two new tumour types are included: PRRX1-rearranged fibroblastic tumour and nodular necrotizing fibroblastic tumour. There are small changes in nomenclature: “EWSR1::SMAD3–positive fibroblastic tumour” has been modified to “SMAD3-rearranged fibroblastic tumour” (anticipating the future identification of fusion partners involving other members of the FET family of genes) and the word “pleomorphic” has been added to superficial CD34-positive pleomorphic fibroblastic tumour (given the importance of cytological pleomorphism in both recognition of this tumour type and possible confusion with pleomorphic sarcomas). Calcifying fibrous tumour was inadvertently omitted from the 2020 classification; it has been included. Notably, adult fibrosarcoma has been removed from the classification; essentially all tumours diagnosed as adult fibrosarcoma in the past can be classified as other sarcoma types (e.g. monophasic synovial sarcoma, MPNST, fibrosarcomatous subtype of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans) using contemporary methods. Finally, UPS has been moved from tumours of uncertain differentiation (where it was described within the section on undifferentiated sarcoma) to this section, since in many cases the tumour cells appear to show fibroblastic and sometimes myofibroblastic differentiation (despite the “undifferentiated” designation).

Fibrohistiocytic and synovial tumours

Two new tumour types are included: chondroid synoviocytic neoplasm and keratin-positive giant cell–rich tumour. Deep fibrous histiocytoma has been removed from this classification, since it is a subtype of cutaneous benign fibrous histiocytoma (dermatofibroma), which is included in the fifth-edition Skin tumours volume of this series.

Pericytic (perivascular) tumours

One new tumour type is included: SRF-rearranged myoid neoplasm, which includes a group of mostly benign tumours with myoid or immature smooth muscle differentiation, characterized by SRF gene fusion with various partners.

Smooth muscle tumours

Inflammatory leiomyosarcoma has been omitted from this section; tumours in this category are now designated inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumour (see the sections on section skeletal muscle tumours).

Skeletal muscle tumours

Inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumour has been introduced in this volume, including tumours formerly designated inflammatory leiomyosarcoma. Spindle cell / sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma has been split into two separate entries: fusion-associated spindle cell rhabdomyosarcoma and MYOD1-mutant spindle cell / sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma, given the distinct genetics and differences in clinical behaviour. Ectomesenchymoma has been removed from the classification; this is now recognized to represent embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma with intermixed neuroectodermal elements.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumour

SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour is now a separate tumour type, given the distinct molecular pathogenesis, implications for possible familial syndromic associations, and differences in clinical behaviour.

Chondro-osseous tumours

No significant changes have been made to this section.

Peripheral nerve sheath tumours

No significant changes have been made to this section.

Tumours of uncertain differentiation

A large number of recently recognized tumour types are included for the first time in this volume, including OGT-rearranged mesenchymal neoplasm, acral fibrochondromyxoid tumour, GLI1-altered mesenchymal tumours, superficial FET-ETS family neurocristic tumour, pseudoendocrine sarcoma, NUT-rearranged sarcoma, and FET-CREB family epithelioid mesenchymal tumour. The former entity NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm has been expanded to kinase-altered spindle cell neoplasms, since it is now recognized that a wide range of kinase gene fusions (as well as point variants) can be identified in this tumour class. PATZ1-rearranged sarcoma has been introduced into this section; this tumour was included within the section on round cell sarcomas with EWSR1::non-ETS fusions in the fifth edition, but such tumours show more morphological heterogeneity than round cell sarcomas.

Vascular tumours of soft tissue and bone

In the fifth edition, separate sections on vascular tumours were included in both the soft tissue and bone tumour chapters; in this edition, these sections have been combined, in order to limit redundancy, given the similarities in nearly all such tumour types, whether they arise in soft tissue or bone. The most significant change in this section is the nomenclature for lesions formerly known as haemangiomas and lymphangiomas, introducing the term “vascular anomalies” (and “lymphatic malformations”), to align the classification with the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA). Spindle cell haemangioma was inadvertently omitted from the 2020 classification; it has been included. YAP1::TFE3 haemangioendothelioma has been separated from epithelioid haemangioendothelioma, given differences in histology and clinical behaviour.

Undifferentiated spindle and small round cell sarcomas

Given the fact that some of these high-grade sarcomas show not only round cell but also spindle cell morphology, the chapter name has been modified accordingly. As mentioned above, the fifth-edition section on round cell sarcomas with EWSR1::non-ETS fusions has been split into PATZ1-rearranged sarcoma (included in this chapter, under section tumours of uncertain differentiation) and NFATC2-rearranged sarcoma (included in section Undifferentiated spindle and small round cell sarcomas).
