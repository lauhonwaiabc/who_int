4.0.2 Enchondromatosis (IDH1, IDH2)

Definition

Enchondromatosis is a heterogeneous skeletal disorder in which patients have multiple symptomatic cartilaginous neoplasms in the medulla of bone or periosteum causing bone expansion, often associated with postzygotic IDH1 or IDH2 mutations.

Subtype(s)

Ollier disease is the most common enchondromatosis subtype and is characterized by the occurrence of multiple cartilaginous masses originating in the medulla of bone, particularly affecting the short and long tubular bones of the limbs. The disorder is referred to as Maffucci syndrome when cutaneous, soft tissue, or visceral haemangiomas are also present. Other subtypes are extremely rare. Metachondromatosis is associated with osteochondroma-like lesions and enchondromas. The enchondromatosis subtypes can be distinguished based on the distribution of the enchondromas, other accompanying symptoms, and the mode of inheritance.

Localization

Enchondromas in Ollier disease are predominantly located in the femur (47%), tibia (30%), humerus (27%), pelvis (21%), fibula and radius (14% each), ulna (13%), and scapula (11%). In the axial skeleton, the skull base is the most common site affected by enchondromas (11%), followed by the ribs (7%) and the spine (1%) {32144835}. In Maffucci syndrome, enchondromas are predominantly found in the femur (29%), tibia (26%), humerus and ulna (19% each), radius (15%), fibula (14%), and pelvis and scapula (14% each). In the axial skeleton, the ribs are the most common site affected by enchondromas (10%), followed by the skull base (6%) and the spine (1%). In both syndromes, the small tubular bones of the hands are more often affected with enchondromas (43% and 63%) than those of the feet (19–29%). Unilateral presentation is seen in 58% and 46% of patients with Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome, respectively. Vascular lesions in Maffucci syndrome present most frequently in the upper extremity (70%), lower extremity (52%), and the viscera (16%) {32144835}. In metachondromatosis, enchondromas are seen in the pelvis and long bones, particularly of the lower limb, with osteochondroma-like lesions most frequent in the short tubular bones of the hands and feet {24432109}.

Clinical features

Ollier disease is non-hereditary and usually manifests in early childhood. Enchondromas in the metaphysial regions of long bones result in growth disturbance (such as limb length inequality or deformity) {5553576}; finger swelling, deformity, and growth arrest can occur with lesions in the hand {27603196}. Pathological fractures occur occasionally. The extent of orthopaedic complications depends on the number and skeletal distribution of enchondromas. There is wide clinical variability with respect to size, number, location, age of onset, and requirement of surgery. Malignant transformation is usually associated with pain {24645839} and faster growth.

Maffucci syndrome is also non-hereditary and presents at birth or in early childhood with haemangiomas of the dermis, subcutis, or internal organs. Spindle cell haemangioma, a vascular lesion with a high propensity for local recurrence but no potential for metastasis, is overrepresented among patients with Maffucci syndrome {7560353; 7661276}. The skeletal features in Maffucci syndrome are indistinguishable from those in Ollier disease.

Orthopaedic surgical intervention may be needed to treat the deformities. Excision of a symptomatic lesion, osteotomy or guided growth to correct malalignment, or bone lengthening is performed in appropriate patients. Affected individuals should be instructed to pay close attention to signs or symptoms heralding malignant transformation {10199270}. Patients with Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome are at increased risk of developing chondrosarcomas. The median age for developing malignancy is 25 years in Ollier disease and 30 years in Maffucci syndrome {32144835}. Patients with Ollier disease also have an increased incidence of gliomas and juvenile granulosa cell tumours {20661403}. In Maffucci syndrome, angiosarcomas, astrocytomas, pituitary adenomas, juvenile granulosa cell tumours, pancreatic adenocarcinomas, and single other tumours have been reported {19934970; 20661403}.

Metachondromatosis is a hereditary disorder whose lesions are mostly asymptomatic, although they may cause complications due to mass effects {24432109}. Most lesions regress in adulthood {24432109}.

Imaging

In long bones, lucent columns of cartilage with thin sclerotic margins extend longitudinally from the physis into the metaphysis {5553576}, often reaching the diaphysis. They may be associated with abnormal bone modelling such as metaphysial broadening, diaphysial shortening, and angulation {29197346}. Similar lucent columns can be seen in the pelvis at the ischial and iliac apophyses (the latter giving the pathognomonic iliac-fan appearance) {1604350}. In short tubular bones, enchondromas occur in the proximal phalanges, metacarpals, middle phalanges, and then distal phalanges, in order of frequency {27603196}. Multiple tumours in a single ray or affecting multiple rays in a single nerve distribution have been observed {35575990}; lobular medullary tumours cause expansion, endosteal scalloping, and cortical thinning {23478067}, with occasional cortical absence {11553836}. Surface tumours (periosteal chondromas) scallop the cortical surface and are often covered by an incomplete shell of periosteal new bone {1604350}. The tumours increase in number and size until skeletal maturation and show progressive chondral-type mineralization with age. Enchondromas are hyperintense on fluid-sensitive MRI sequences and show enhancement typical of hyaline cartilage; low signal due to intervening sclerotic bone and matrix mineralization is seen {11061692}. The development of a secondary central chondrosarcoma in long bones is suggested by cortical destruction, marrow oedema, periosteal reaction, and an extraosseous mass {33484272}. Extraosseous tumour may also indicate malignant transformation in the hands and feet {26189572}, but some studies have shown no predictive features in short tubular bones {33484272} {16624212}.

In metachondromatosis, there is usually no growth disturbance or deformity. Osteochondroma-like lesions point towards the adjacent joint and may resolve spontaneously. There may be osteonecrosis / Perthes-like changes in the femoral heads due to vascular compression by femoral neck osteochondromas; calcific foci can be seen in soft tissues {24432109}.

Epidemiology

Enchondromatosis is rare, but the exact incidence is unknown; the estimated prevalence of Ollier disease is 1 case per 100 000 people {16995932}. About 75% of patients with Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome are diagnosed before the age of 20 years {22147000; 32144835}, with a median age at the time of diagnosis of enchondroma of 7 years and 11 years in Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome, respectively {32144835}. Eight percent of patients are diagnosed with an enchondroma before the age of 1 year {32144835}. In Maffucci syndrome, vascular tumours are usually diagnosed 2–3 years after the enchondromas; however, they can also be diagnosed before enchondromas are found, even before the age of 1 year (median: 13 years) {32144835}. Enchondromatosis has been described in many different ethnic groups, and there is no significant difference in distribution by sex.

Etiology

Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome are not inherited. Most patients carry postzygotic mutations in the isocitrate dehydrogenase genes IDH1 or IDH2 in their enchondromas (87%), chondrosarcomas, and spindle cell haemangiomas (70%) {22057234}. In total, 65 of 75 patients (87%) with Ollier disease and 17 of 21 patients (81%) with Maffucci syndrome were shown to carry mutations in their tumours {22057234; 22057236}. Mutations mostly affect the p.Arg132 position in exon 4 of IDH1 and include p.(Arg132Cys) (R132C) (~65%), p.(Arg132His) (~15%), p.(Arg132Gly) (~5%), and other very rare variants affecting the p.Arg132 position. Mutations in IDH2, p.(Arg172Ser), are rare {22057234; 22057236}. In Maffucci syndrome, variants other than the p.(Arg132Cys) substitution are rare. Multiple tumours within the same patient were shown to carry identical variants. Also, variants were found at a very low frequency in normal tissue in these patients {22057234; 22057236}. In addition, variants in the PTH1R gene, encoding a receptor for PTH and PTHrP, may account for rare patients with Ollier disease {11850620}; however, such variants have not been detected in other studies {15523647; 22057234; 22057236}.

In addition to somatic mutations in IDH1 and IDH2, rare germline (constitutional) missense variants have been reported in genes involved in the HIF pathway (HIF1A, VHL, IDH1) in a subset of patients {36480544}, postulated to predispose to tumour development {39587599}.

For some other conditions with multiple enchondromas, the genetic background has also been elucidated. Germline heterozygous putative loss-of-function alterations of PTPN11, which encodes a protein tyrosine phosphatase involved in RAS/MAPK signalling, were found in patients with autosomal dominant metachondromatosis {20577567; 21533187}. Germline biallelic loss-of-function alterations in ACP5 were found in patients with autosomal recessive spondyloenchondrodysplasia {21217755; 21217752}. ACP5 encodes for TRAP, which is required for maintenance of osteopontin, a bone matrix protein.

Pathogenesis

IDH1 and IDH2 are metabolic enzymes involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle. IDH mutations were previously found in other tumours, mainly gliomas and acute myeloid leukaemia. Heterozygous IDH1 mutations lead to gain of function, and the mutant enzyme is responsible for increased production of the (onco)metabolite D-2-HG {22057236}. This causes, among other things, epigenetic changes including altered histone methylation and DNA hypermethylation {22057234}. D-2-HG has been shown to promote chondrogenic differentiation and inhibit osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, the presumed precursor cells of enchondroma {26046462; 26161668}. Thus, when IDH1 and IDH2 mutations occur in the mesoderm early after gastrulation, causing a somatic mosaic distribution of mutations within the mesenchymal tissues, this results in multifocal local production of D-2-HG in the developing skeleton, promoting chondrogenic differentiation over osteogenic differentiation, causing multiple enchondromas during skeletal maturation.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross specimens are identical to those of sporadic enchondroma.

Histopathology

Enchondromas present as well-circumscribed nodules (see section Enchondroma). The cartilage is hypercellular and the nuclei are enlarged and irregular. Within the context of Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome, increased cellularity and some nuclear atypia are not sufficient to diagnose atypical cartilaginous tumour / chondrosarcoma, grade 1 (ACT/CS1); the distinction should be made in a multidisciplinary team, taking radiological features such as cortical destruction and soft tissue extension into account.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of pathogenic IDH variants in non-affected tissue from the affected limb may be helpful.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: diagnosis is based on radiographic appearance and clinical features; radiological presence of at least 2 enchondromas combined with other features such as growth disturbance, the typical hand or foot deformities, or columnar lucencies in long-bone metaphyses; in milder forms of presentation in adults, these other typical features may be lacking, and ≥ 3 enchondromas, usually anatomically related, may warrant the diagnosis.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with Ollier disease have a markedly increased risk of developing secondary chondrosarcomas. There are no large studies showing the incidence of malignant transformation, but estimates have ranged from 5% to 50% {3805090; 10637885; 3815310; 22147000}, and the risk is dependent on the location of the tumours. When patients have enchondromas restricted to hands and feet, the risk is less (~15%) than for patients in whom long bones are also affected (43–46%) {22147000}. About 26% of the patients develop multiple chondrosarcomas {22147000}. The risk of developing chondrosarcoma is possibly even higher among patients with Maffucci syndrome, with incidence figures as high as 53% {22147000}. In contrast, in metachondromatosis, the lesions often spontaneously regress and the risk of malignant progression is low {24432109}.
