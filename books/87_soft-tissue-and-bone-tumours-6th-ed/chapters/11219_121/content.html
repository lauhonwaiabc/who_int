1.12.19 Synovial sarcoma

Definition

Synovial sarcoma (SS) is a monomorphic spindle cell sarcoma showing variable epithelial differentiation, characterized by SS18::SSX1/2/4 fusion genes.

ICD-O coding

9040/3 Synovial sarcoma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B5A.Y & XH9B22 Synovial sarcoma, other specified primary site & Synovial sarcoma NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: synoviosarcoma; synovial cell sarcoma; tenosynovial sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Monophasic, biphasic, poorly differentiated.

Localization

The majority (70%) of SSs arise in the deep soft tissue of the lower and upper extremities, often in juxta-articular locations. About 15% arise in the trunk and 7% in the head and neck region {19514087}. Unusual sites of involvement include male and female external and internal reproductive organs, kidney, adrenal gland, retroperitoneum, stomach, small bowel, lung, heart, mediastinum, bone, CNS, and peripheral nerve {15330802; 15725802; 21295819}.

Clinical features

SS usually presents as a swelling (sometimes longstanding), which may be painful. The initial growth of SS is often slow, and a small circumscribed tumour may give the wrong impression of a benign lesion by clinical examination and imaging {20858835}. SS may have radiologically detectable stippled or spiculated forms of calcification {23169746}. SS with aggressive growth may erode or invade adjacent bone.

Epidemiology

SS may occur at any age and is equally distributed between the sexes {19514087}. More than half of the patients are adolescents or young adults, and 77% of cases occur before the age of 50 years. The relative frequency of SS compared with other soft tissue sarcomas is age-dependent, ranging from 15% in patients aged 10–18 years to 1.6% in patients aged > 50 years {19514087}. SS18::SSX1–positive SSs show an approximately even sex ratio, whereas SS18::SSX2 SSs are significantly more common in women {26947017; 11782370}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

SS harbours a pathognomonic t(X;18)(p11;q11) translocation {11782370}, by which SS18 on chromosome 18 is fused to one of the SSX genes (SSX1, SSX2, or SSX4) on the X chromosome {11107170}. Although there is some variability, the most common fusion transcript links exon 10 of SS18 with exon 6 of the partner SSX gene {17334349}. Approximately two thirds of SS cases carry an SS18::SSX1 fusion, one third carry SS18::SSX2, and only rare cases carry SS18::SSX4 {11107170; 17334349} or SS18L1::SSX1 – the latter arising from a t(X;20) translocation {12696068}.

Conditional expression of SS18::SSX induces SS in genetically engineered mouse models, supporting its function as an oncogene when expressed in permissive mesenchymal progenitor cells {17418413; 23921231; 26947017; 29202462}. Additionally, SS18::SSX1 has been shown to transform primary cell lines {11274403}. Recent evidence suggests that the SS18::SSX fusion protein disrupts epigenetic control and blocks mesenchymal differentiation by complementary mechanisms including competitive binding with, and displacement of, native SS18 in the SWI/SNF (BAF) chromatin-remodelling complex {23540691}, inducing dependency on BRD9-containing alternative SWI/SNF complexes (ncBAF complexes) {30397315; 30431433}, and involving a role for SMARCB1 (BAF47) / INI1 {34078620}. SS18::SSX was found to retarget BAF complexes on chromatin to oppose PRC2-mediated repression, and to associate with KDM2B-PRC1.1, a non-canonical polycomb repressive complex 1. This complex aberrantly activates the expression of developmentally regulated transcription factors {29502955; 29861296}, with target specificity mediated by the SSX-portion of the fusion protein {37735617}. Interaction of SS18::SSX with ATF2 and TLE1 was found to repress ATF2 target genes {25614489}. A recently described specific transcriptomic signature identified in a single-cell RNA sequencing approach characterizes an SS18::SSX-regulated cell state of SS associated with poor prognosis and marking immune-deprived tumour regions {33495604}. Oncogenic pathways functionally involved in SS comprise IGF1R, β-catenin, and YAP signals {23921231; 30814111}. DNA methylation analysis was shown to perform with high sensitivity for SS {33479225; 37921028}.

Individual, histologically classic SS cases with EWSR1::SSX and MN1::SSX fusions were described, the latter showing a divergent DNA methylation profile {34504309}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most SSs are 30–100 mm in diameter at diagnosis. Minute lesions < 10 mm occur especially in hands and feet {16723849}. On cut surface, tumour colour and consistency are proportionate to cellularity, collagenization, and myxoid change or haemorrhage. Areas may be tan or grey, yellowish or pink, and soft or firm {6256705}. SS is frequently multinodular and can be multicystic. Calcification, metaplastic ossification, and necrosis may be present.

Histopathology

Histologically, SSs are classified as biphasic (in approximately one quarter to one third of cases) or monophasic (in the majority). Biphasic SS comprises both epithelial and spindle cell components, in varying proportions. Of note, almost all SS18::SSX2 cases show monophasic histology, whereas SS18::SSX1 cases show an approximate 2:1 ratio of monophasic to biphasic histology {26947017; 10718206}. The epithelial cells are arranged in solid nests or cords, or in glands with a tubular or sometimes alveolar or papillary architecture. In glandular areas, epithelial cells are cuboidal or columnar, with ovoid vesicular nuclei and more-abundant palely eosinophilic cytoplasm, compared with the spindle cell component, and may contain eosinophilic material. Occasionally, the glandular component can predominate, and it may be confused with adenocarcinoma; however, a scant spindle cell component is virtually always found. Rarely, epithelial cells show (keratinizing) squamous metaplasia or granular cell change.

The spindle cells in biphasic SS resemble the spindle cells found in monophasic SS. These delicate spindle cells are fairly uniform and relatively small, with high N:C ratio, sparse cytoplasm, and ovoid, hyperchromatic nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli. Typically, in both monophasic and biphasic SSs, the spindle cells are arranged in dense cellular sheets or vague fascicles, with occasional tigroid nuclear palisading or a herringbone architectural pattern. The amount of collagen is variable, with a subset containing strands of ropy or wiry hyalinized collagen. Myxoid change is typically focal and rarely predominates, with alternating hypocellular and more-cellular areas, as well as retiform cords or microcysts {10349982}. Many SSs focally display a staghorn-shaped vascular pattern, reminiscent of solitary fibrous tumour. In as many as one third of SSs, areas with calcification and/or ossification are found, which are sometimes abundant. In areas with ossification, the osteoid has a lace-like pattern mimicking osteosarcoma, and bone tissue can mature to the point of being lamellar and trabecular {7684201}. Metaplastic cartilage is rarely seen.

In otherwise typical biphasic or monophasic SS, poorly differentiated areas with increased cellularity, greater nuclear atypia, and high mitotic activity (> 6 mitoses/mm2 or > 10 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.17 mm2) may be found {15364967}. Poorly differentiated areas may be composed of fascicular spindle cells (reminiscent of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour), small round hyperchromatic tumour cells (reminiscent of Ewing sarcoma), or epithelioid cells {10375108}. Compared with typical biphasic or monophasic SS, poorly differentiated areas more often contain areas of necrosis, a branching vascular pattern, and thin fibrovascular septa separating groups of tumour cells {12940774}. It has been suggested that tumours should be classified as poorly differentiated SS if such areas comprise > 20% {10078916}.

By immunohistochemistry, EMA is expressed more often and more widely in SS than are keratins, especially in the monophasic and poorly differentiated subtypes {12409719}. Epithelial cells in biphasic SSs consistently express EMA and keratins and may contain almost any keratin subtype {11037348}. Poorly differentiated areas of SS are often focally EMA-positive, whereas keratin expression is seen in only about 50% of these tumours {11037348; 12409719}.

Antibodies to the SS18::SSX fusion protein and the C-terminus of SSX have recently emerged as the most sensitive and specific markers of SS, obviating the need for molecular genetic confirmation in many instances. The sensitivity/specificity of the SS18-SSX and SSX C-terminus antibodies is 96%/100% and 100%/96%, respectively {32141887}.

Focal S100 expression may be detectable in as many as 40% of SSs {12409719}, while SOX10 is rarely positive {23929265}. CD34 immunostaining is extremely rare in monophasic SS {12409719}. Moderate or strong nuclear staining for the transcriptional co-repressor TLE1 is found in the large majority of biphasic, monophasic, and poorly differentiated SSs {21571956}; however, TLE1 staining is not specific to SS and also occurs in histological mimics {12409719; 21571956}.

Cytology

Aspirates show high cellularity with tissue fragments of uniform oval to fusiform cells with scant indistinct cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei, and small nucleoli (and some single similar cells) in monophasic SS. An epithelioid component of glandular or acinar cells is added in the biphasic SS. Capillaries can be seen in some tissue fragments, and mitoses can be present. Poorly differentiated SS has small round cells. The differential diagnosis is broad, and specific diagnosis relies on ancillary studies on a cell, including SSX and SS18::SSX immunohistochemistry and FISH {3444168; 32141887}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FISH or targeted RNA sequencing for the presence of SS18 gene rearrangements or SS18::SSX fusions can be performed to confirm the diagnosis {26970672}, although immunohistochemical surrogates are sufficient.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

For monophasic SS: fascicles of uniform spindle cells with minimal cytoplasm; thin-walled, ectatic, branching vessels; limited keratin and EMA expression For biphasic SS: uniform spindle cells and epithelial elements For poorly differentiated SS: round cell morphology

Desirable: demonstration of SS18 rearrangement or surrogate immunohistochemistry.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control or American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system may be used.

Prognosis and prediction

SS has a variable prognosis. Metastatic disease commonly occurs in lungs and bone. Most distant tumour recurrences develop within a few years after initial diagnosis, but late recurrences do occur, even after 10 years {20716627}. Major prognostic determinants are tumour stage at presentation, tumour size, and French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) tumour grade {15364967; 18997619; 20716627}. Tumours with > 20% poorly differentiated areas show a more aggressive behaviour {9888710; 10078916; 10375108}. The best outcomes are seen with tumours that are < 50 mm in diameter or that have < 6 mitoses/mm2 (10 mitoses in 1.7 mm2), with no necrosis {10375108; 15364967}. Minute SSs (< 10 mm) have an excellent prognosis {16723849}. Children fare better than adults {19514087}, and extremity-based SSs have a better prognosis than SSs involving the trunk or the head and neck area {19514087; 19088035}. A prognostic nomogram based on data available preoperatively from patients with SS has been developed {19088035}. Fusion type is not regarded as being prognostically useful {26217552; 19088035}. US population–based SEER Program data indicate that the 5-year and 10-year disease-specific survival rates, respectively, are 83% and 75% in children and adolescents (age: < 19 years) and 62% and 52% in adults {19514087}.
