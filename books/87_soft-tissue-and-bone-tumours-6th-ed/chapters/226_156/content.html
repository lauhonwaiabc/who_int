2.2.6 Parosteal osteosarcoma

Definition

Parosteal osteosarcoma is a low-grade malignant bone-forming neoplasm that arises on the cortical surface of bone, above the periosteum.

ICD-O coding

9192/3 Parosteal osteosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH8HG5 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Parosteal osteosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: juxtacortical osteosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Approximately 70% of parosteal osteosarcomas are located at the posterior aspect of the distal femur in the metaphysial region {8126042; 24786129}; the next most common sites are the proximal portions of the tibia and humerus. Other less frequently involved sites include the craniofacial bones, ribs, and small bones of the hands and feet {17615569; 30633158}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

Patients present with a mass, which is occasionally painful. Distal femoral lesions are associated with restricted knee flexion. A long history, frequently over a year, is typical {8126042}; the history is sometimes shorter with primary high-grade transformation {8918422}.

Imaging

Parosteal osteosarcoma presents characteristic radiological features. Radiographs show a densely mineralized lobulated exophytic (cauliflower-like) mass with central dense ossification (denser centrally than peripherally) adjacent to the bone at the bone surface {32681279}. Attachment to the bone (tumour stalk) may be slender or broad, with the remainder of the lesion separated by a lucent cleavage plane (string sign), representing histologically intact periosteum interposed between the cortex and the tumour mass {21071381}. The underlying cortex may be normal, thickened, or destroyed. The tumour stalk becomes more broad-based with time, obliterating the lucent cleavage plane in later stages {17331824}. CT shows better the cortical changes, the lucent cleavage plane, and ossified intramedullary extension (which is seen with equal frequency in low-grade and high-grade transformed (dedifferentiated) tumours {8918422; 8939240}. However, CT does not always reveal the presence and/or the precise extent of the medullary invasion (if not ossified). Lucent areas on CT within dense tumours may be seen, containing either benign tissue or high- or low-grade tumour; CT does not differentiate among these different tissues {3872580}. MRI shows usually a hypointense and heterogeneous mass on T1- and T2-weighted MRI sequences, but appearances suggesting fatty marrow may be seen in well-differentiated tumours {28917546}. Lucent areas on radiographs and CT, which are hyperintense on fluid-sensitive MRI sequences, raise the possibility of focal high-grade transformation {8939240}, particularly when they are large, located deeply, and enhancing {11484126; 18814136}. On MRI, a thin, usually incomplete, hyperintense rim corresponding to cartilage is occasionally seen on the tumour surface (cartilage cap) {8939240; 22613734}.

Epidemiology

Parosteal osteosarcoma accounts for about 1–6% of all osteosarcomas, and it is the most common type of surface osteosarcoma. It occurs in young adults, with a peak occurrence in the third decade of life. A slight female predominance is reported in published cases {8126042; 26637687; 15852358; 38545575}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Parosteal osteosarcomas are cytogenetically characterized by one or more supernumerary ring chromosomes, often as the sole aberration. The chromosome number is typically near-diploid, and the tumours do not show the massive chromosomal rearrangements associated with high-grade osteosarcomas [[Mitelman F, Johansson B, Mertens F. Mitelman Database of Chromosome Aberrations and Gene Fusions in Cancer [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2019. Available from: https://cgap.nci.nih.gov/Chromosomes/Mitelman.]]. The ring chromosomes contain amplified material from chromosomal region 12q13-q15 {11793439; 16732325; 9162194}. Potential target genes in these amplicons include MDM2 and CDK4, which are amplified in > 85% of the cases {22074548; 21336260; 16732325; 20196171; 9989829}. The same genes may be amplified in high-grade osteosarcoma with ring chromosomes, but the overall frequency of amplification is about 10% in conventional high-grade osteosarcomas, some of which may represent unrecognized low-grade osteosarcomas with high-grade transformation {20196171; 24703847; 28643781; 22074548; 22301501}. Gene amplification is usually accompanied by increased expression of MDM2 and CDK4 at the RNA and protein levels {21336260; 20196171}.

Macroscopic appearance

Parosteal osteosarcoma presents as an exophytic, lobulated mass, typically attached to the underlying cortex by a broad base. Large tumours may encircle the involved bone. Satellite nodules may be noted, particularly in recurrent lesions. The cut surface shows a tan-white tumour with a hard gritty texture. Nodules of cartilage may be present. Occasionally, the cartilage may form an incomplete cartilage cap on the outer surface, simulating an osteochondroma {9609176}. Penetration of the underlying cortex with or without invasion into the medullary cavity may be seen. Soft and fleshy areas, if present, suggest high-grade transformation of the tumour. Focal necrosis, haemorrhage, and fluid cavities may be present.

Histopathology

Parosteal osteosarcoma is composed of well-formed bone trabeculae with intervening fascicles of spindle cells. Typically, the tumour is hypocellular with minimal atypia and low mitotic activity. At the periphery of the tumour, the spindle cell component may show invasion into the adjacent skeletal muscle. Matrix formation can be very focal in some tumours, leading to histological overlap with desmoplastic fibroma. In about 20% of the cases, the tumour is more cellular and the spindle cells show moderate atypia. The bone trabeculae are arranged mostly in a parallel fashion and may or may not show osteoblastic rimming. Some tumours contain irregular anastomosing and curved bone trabeculae, simulating woven bone in fibrous dysplasia. Approximately 50% of cases display cartilaginous differentiation, in the form of small, scattered nodules or a cartilaginous cap. High-grade transformation occurs in 15–43% of cases, either at presentation or (more often) at the time of recurrence {8126042; 8918422; 15852358; 35932377}. The high-grade component can be osteosarcoma or undifferentiated spindle cell sarcoma {15852358; 31128127}, or very rarely rhabdomyosarcoma {10525797}.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical study of MDM2 and CDK4 may provide a useful diagnostic tool in parosteal osteosarcoma, particularly in small biopsies and/or when molecular access is limited {20601938; 21336260}. Analysis of MDM2 and CDK4 may also help identify high-grade transformation in parosteal osteosarcoma with limited low-grade components {22301501}.

Differential diagnosis

Because of its deceptively bland appearance, parosteal osteosarcoma must be differentiated from a variety of benign fibrous and fibro-osseous lesions, including florid reactive periostitis, juxtacortical myositis ossificans, and desmoplastic fibroma. In addition to the clinical, radiological, and pathological features, MDM2 amplification assay or the surrogate immunostaining of MDM2 and CDK4 is helpful in the distinction, because the above-mentioned mimics are negative for MDM2 amplification. Unlike in osteochondroma, the cartilage cap in some parosteal osteosarcomas is mildly hypercellular and the chondrocytes show mild atypia and lack the perpendicular columnar arrangement seen in osteochondromas. Parosteal osteosarcoma can be morphologically distinguished from periosteal osteosarcoma and high-grade surface osteosarcoma, the other two subtypes of surface osteosarcoma. The presence of a focal low-grade spindle cell component and/or MDM2 amplification suggests high-grade transformation of parosteal osteosarcoma over high-grade surface osteosarcoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of MDM2 amplification can be used to confirm the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a bone tumour with compatible imaging; parosteal location; low-grade spindle cell tumour with woven bone formation.

Desirable: MDM2 amplification.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification of malignant tumours [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]] does not recommend applying the TNM staging system for bone tumours to surface/juxtacortical osteosarcoma. However, other staging systems, such as the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020.]], do include these tumours in the bone staging system. See also the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is excellent, with a 90% overall survival rate at 5 years {8126042; 31128127}. For pure low-grade tumours, wide surgical excision is curative. Incomplete resection results in local recurrence {31128127}. Metastasis, usually a late phenomenon, occurs rarely in low-grade tumours, whereas tumours with high-grade transformation metastasize at a high rate, leading to a poor prognosis. Chemotherapy is reserved for transformed (dedifferentiated) tumours, which show a poorer response to a standard chemotherapy regimen than conventional osteosarcoma, while the clinical outcomes in some studies were not markedly different {31154837; 35932377}.
