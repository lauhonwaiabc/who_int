1.8.3 Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma

Definition

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) is a fusion-negative malignant soft tissue tumour with morphological and immunophenotypic features of embryonic skeletal muscle.

ICD-O coding

8910/3 Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B55.Z & XH83G1 Rhabdomyosarcoma & Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: sarcoma botryoides; mixed embryonal and alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma; gangliorhabdomyosarcoma; ectomesenchymoma.

Subtype(s)

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma with divergent neuroectodermal differentiation (Former Ectomesenchymoma)

Localization

Approximately one half of ERMSs occur within the head and neck region (including the orbit), and the other half within the genitourinary system {25267746; 30091945; 10742419; 22535600; 26872011}. Less frequently, ERMS arises in the biliary tract, retroperitoneum, or abdomen. In contrast to alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS), ERMS rarely involves the soft tissues of the extremities.

Clinical features

ERMS presents with a variety of clinical symptoms, generally related to mass effects, or it may be indolent. Head and neck lesions can cause proptosis, diplopia, or sinusitis; genitourinary lesions may produce a scrotal mass or urinary retention; biliary tumours may cause jaundice.

Epidemiology

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children and adolescents, with 4.5 cases per 1 million people aged 0–20 years {30617281} [[Ries LAG, Smith MA, Gurney JG, et al., editors. Cancer incidence and survival among children and adolescents: United States SEER Program 1975–1995. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute, SEER Program; 1999. NIH Pub. No. 99-4649.]]. ERMS is the most common subtype, accounting for 70–75% of all RMS cases in children, with one third of cases occurring in children aged < 6 years {10742419; 22535600; 26872011; 36069287}. About 4% of ERMS cases affect infants. ERMS with neuroectodermal differentiation is exceedingly rare, characteristically occurring in the first 2 years of life {10742419; 22535600; 26872011; 36440693}. ERMS also constitutes 20% of all adult rhabdomyosarcomas {19398574}. It is slightly more common in boys than in girls (M:F ratio: 1.5:1) {19536876}. About 80% of rhabdomyosarcomas in North America occur in White people, compared with 15% in African Americans {19536876}.

Etiology

ERMS is associated with several syndromes involving alterations of the RAS signalling pathway, for example, Costello syndrome (HRAS gene variants), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1 gene variants), and Noonan syndrome (variants in RAS pathway genes). Cases are reported in association with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (dysregulation of imprinting in the 11p15.5 region), DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome, and Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53 variants) {30617281}. Recent studies show that as many as 7% of patients with RMS harbour a germline cancer predisposition variant, and those with ERMS are more likely to harbour a germline cancer predisposition variant than those with ARMS {38546643}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic cases of ERMS are aneuploid with whole-chromosome gains including polysomy 8, followed by extra copies of chromosomes 2, 11, 12, 13, and/or 20. Whole-chromosome losses include monosomy 10 and 15. In most ERMSs, a genomic event such as chromosome loss, deletion, or uniparental disomy results in the loss of one of the two alleles at many chromosome 11 loci. This loss of heterozygosity involves chromosomal region 11p15.5, which contains imprinted genes that encode a growth factor (IGF2) and growth suppressors (H19 and CDKN1C) {26349418}. Genomic amplification in the form of double minutes or as ascertained by comparative genomic hybridization is frequent in ERMS with anaplasia {11807989; 18985676}.

Genomic studies of ERMS have identified somatic driver variants involving the RAS pathway (NRAS, KRAS, HRAS, NF1, FGFR4), involving effectors of PI3K (PTEN, PIK3CA), or in genes that control the cell cycle (FBXW7, CTNNB1) {24436047; 30617281}. Mutation of a RAS isoform occurs in one third of ERMSs, and mutation of a RAS pathway member has been found in < 50% of ERMSs. Within this group, HRAS variants are enriched in the infant population (70% of infants aged < 1 year had HRAS or KRAS variants), and NRAS variants are enriched in adolescents. Similar HRAS variants and upregulated myogenesis-related genes (MYOG, CHRND, and MRLN) have been found in ERMS with a neuroectodermal component {26872011}. NF1 variants occur in 10% of cases. Epigenetic modifications may also play a role, and BCOR point mutations or focal homozygous deletions are described in 15% of ERMSs. Variants in TP53 occur in approximately 10% of ERMSs and may be associated with anaplasia {24382691; 34166060}. About 30% of ERMSs harbour more than one driver variant, whereas one quarter have no driver variant identified {24436047; 34166060}.

Macroscopic appearance

ERMS forms poorly circumscribed, fleshy, pale-tan masses that impinge upon neighbouring structures. Botryoid ERMS has a characteristic polypoid appearance with clusters of small, sessile, or pedunculated nodules that abut an epithelial surface.

Histopathology

Analogous to embryonic skeletal muscle, ERMS contains primitive mesenchymal cells in various stages of myogenesis. Stellate cells with sparse, amphophilic cytoplasm represent the most primitive end of this spectrum. Differentiating rhabdomyoblasts acquire more cytoplasmic eosinophilia and elongation, manifested by tadpole or spider cells. Terminal differentiation with cross-striations or myotube formation may be evident. Differentiation often becomes more evident after therapy {17006261}. Typical ERMSs are composed of variably differentiated rhabdomyoblasts within a loose, myxoid mesenchyme, with alternating areas of dense and loose cellularity. The relative amount of myxoid matrix and spindled cells is highly variable. Occasional poorly differentiated ERMSs consist largely of primitive round cells, simulating ARMS (dense pattern) {23765537}.

Botryoid ERMS has a polypoid or protuberant growth and contains linear aggregates of tumour cells (the cambium layer) that tightly condense below an epithelial surface, along with hypocellular myxoid stroma invested by radially oriented capillaries perpendicular to the overlying mucosal surface that may appear deceptively benign {39210566}.

ERMS may also have prominent spindle cell morphology, particularly in paratesticular tumours, although small foci of more-typical ERMSs are usually present as well {8625211; 8434703}. Heterologous cartilaginous differentiation is occasionally present and is most commonly observed in the female genitourinary tract, particularly common in tumours harbouring DICER1 alterations {33135284; 34017064}.

Anaplastic ERMS is defined by the presence of markedly enlarged, atypical cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, often with bizarre, multipolar mitotic figures {18985676}. Anaplastic features may be focal or diffuse. In children, tumours with extensive pleomorphism are considered anaplastic ERMS rather than pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma. Although the clinical significance of anaplasia in ERMS remains unclear, the presence of anaplasia is associated with both TP53 somatic and germline mutations {33302115; 24382691; 37194615}.

A small percentage of ERMSs are intermixed with neuroectodermal elements. The latter cover the entire spectrum of neuroblastic phenotype, ranging from scattered ganglion cells and neuropil, to mature ganglioneuroma, intermediate ganglioneuroblastoma, and primitive neuroblastoma. The ERMS can be botryoid, classic with a spindle cell component, or dense cellular {21803398; 26872011; 10742419}. The neural element can be minimal and may be missed especially in biopsies {32769567; 12469232}.

Immunohistochemistry

By immunohistochemistry, ERMSs are essentially always positive for desmin, although the extent of immunoreactivity is variable. The skeletal muscle–specific nuclear regulatory proteins myogenin (MYF4) and MYOD1 are also positive in essentially all cases, although the number of positive nuclei may be highly variable; the dense cellular ERMS may show positivity similar to that seen in ARMS {16861966; 23765537}. MSA and SMA are frequently positive. Aberrant expression of keratins, S100, CD56, synaptophysin, and NFP may be seen {2455782}. It is important that this aberrant expression is not interpreted as evidence of a neuroblastic component, and the morphology and expression of neural markers are correlated {10742419; 26872011}. Expression studies of ERMS have identified markers that distinguish FOXO1 fusion–negative rhabdomyosarcoma (HMGA2 and EGFR [HER1] overexpression) from FOXO1 fusion–positive rhabdomyosarcoma (AP-2β and P-cadherin overexpression) and may help in assigning subtype {19469909; 24618610}. p53 immunohistochemistry may help identify patients with anaplastic tumours that require further testing for somatic and germline alterations {9065719; 12574910; 38364651}. Expression of H3K27me3 is lost in more than half of the cases and cannot be used to distinguish ERMS from malignant triton tumour {39957203}.

Cytology

Aspirated material shows high cellularity, with primitive small, round, spindled and stellate cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm seen singly or in tissue fragments with myxoid matrix. Scattered large tadpole- or ribbon-like rhabdomyoblasts with eccentric cytoplasm and rare cross-striations and occasional binucleated or multinucleated cells are present {10934943; 17636492}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Absence of the PAX3::FOXO1 fusion and PAX7::FOXO1 fusion distinguishes ERMS from ARMS {19147825}. In addition, lack of MYOD1 p.(Leu122Arg) mutations {39511703; 36980529} and absent recurrent translocations involving VGLL2/VGLL3, NCOA2/NCOA3 genes and rare others help differentiate ERMS, especially those with a prominent spindle cell component, from MYOD1-mutant spindle cell / sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma and fusion-associated spindle cell rhabdomyosarcoma {30181563; 26501226}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: primitive round and spindle cell morphology with scattered differentiated rhabdomyoblasts; positivity for desmin and heterogeneous nuclear staining for myogenin and/or MYOD1; lack of FOXO1 rearrangement.

Desirable: exclusion of MYOD1 p.(Leu122Arg) mutations and translocations involving VGLL2/VGLL3 and NCOA2/NCOA3 genes.

Staging

Rhabdomyosarcoma may be staged based on site of origin using the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system for soft tissue sarcomas {36631406} [[International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting [Internet]. ICCR; 2020. Soft tissue sarcoma resection specimens; cited 2025 Jun 10. Available from: https://www.iccr-cancer.org/datasets/published-datasets/soft-tissue-bone/soft-tissue-sarcoma-resection-specimens/.]]. In children, the TNM system is used, but a modified TNM staging system is used by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and by the College of American Pathologists {40007148} [[International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting [Internet]. ICCR; 2020. Paediatric rhabdomyosarcoma; cited 2025 Jun 10. Available from: https://www.iccr-cancer.org/datasets/published-datasets/paediatrics/paediatric-rhabdomyosarcoma.]]. The Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group (IRSG) grouping system is used for surgicopathological evaluation, which includes assessment of margins and residual disease {15059650}.

Prognosis and prediction

The tumours are highly sensitive to chemotherapy, and the outcome of ERMS is more favourable compared with that of other types of rhabdomyosarcoma {35129294}. In children and adolescents, risk stratification predictive of outcome is based on the following prognostic factors: fusion status, IRSG grouping system, primary site, stage, size, and age {38400828}. Patients aged 1–9 years have better outcomes than adolescents {31456361} or adults. Lymph node involvement can be seen, especially in vulvovaginal tumours {28634755}.

TP53 variants convey an unfavourable prognosis, and assessment of both TP53 and MYOD1 mutation status is typically required for clinical trial enrolment. Patients with either of these high-risk molecular features may shift to a higher-risk stratification and treatment approach, making these important molecular markers {34166060; 39913888}.
