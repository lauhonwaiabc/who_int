9.1 Non-epithelial tumours of the ovary: Introduction

In this sixth-edition WHO classification, ovarian tumours are divided into two separate chapters comprising epithelial and non-epithelial tumours. Non-epithelial ovarian tumours are much more uncommon than epithelial neoplasms. Currently these neoplasms are classified as sex cord stromal tumours (SCSTs), germ cell tumours, and miscellaneous tumours, and in the sixth edition, the classification is largely unchanged from the fifth edition. Exceptions are the removal of fibrosarcoma, neuroectodermal-type tumours, Wolffian tumour, and rete adenocarcinoma. Fibrosarcoma has been removed since this is an extremely rare neoplasm and most cases in which this diagnosis is suspected represent other tumour types, such as cellular mitotically active fibroma, other sarcomas, poorly differentiated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour (SLCT), or other SCSTs. Similarly, rete adenocarcinoma is vanishingly rare, with only a single reported purported example {2852648}. Wolffian tumour has been moved to the chapter on tumours of the broad ligament and other uterine ligaments (section Wolffian tumour of the broad ligament and other uterine ligaments), and neuroectodermal-type tumours are now covered in the chapter on mesenchymal tumours (section CNS-type tumours).

One area of ovarian pathology in which significant advances have been made, even since the publication of the fifth edition, is the elucidation of the molecular events in ovarian SCSTs and some other non-epithelial neoplasms (#54968Table 9. XXX) {39492459}. These represent a heterogeneous group of relatively uncommon neoplasms that in their classic forms are often relatively easy to diagnose. However, there may be considerable morphological overlap between the various tumour types, and immunohistochemistry, although useful in confirming an SCST, is of limited value in distinguishing between the different types. Significant advances include the elucidation that adult granulosa cell tumours contain somatic FOXL2 pathogenic variants (PVs) in > 90% of cases {19516027}, and a significant majority of moderately and poorly differentiated SLCTs harbour DICER1 PVs (somatic or germline) {28654427}. The available morphological and molecular evidence currently indicates that well-differentiated SLCTs represent a distinct tumour type, separate from moderately/poorly differentiated and retiform SLCTs {36583307; 39592485}. Ongoing studies are elucidating the molecular events in several other tumour types within the SCST category. For example, microcystic stromal tumour contains CTNNB1 or less frequently APC PVs and may occasionally be an extracolonic manifestation of familial adenomatous polyposis {25820106; 29076875; 26200099}. In problematic cases, demonstration of the appropriate molecular abnormality assists in tumour classification and may dictate the need for genetic referral for investigation of a possible germline cause. However, it should also be stressed that none of these molecular alterations are totally specific or sensitive for a particular tumour type and that in problematic cases, the clinical, pathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular parameters should all be considered in formulating a diagnosis {39492459}. In this sixth edition, more guidance is provided as to when to diagnose gynandroblastoma (a mixed SCST with two or more distinct elements) and SCST-NOS. For example, gynandroblastomas containing components of moderately to poorly differentiated SLCT and juvenile granulosa cell tumour usually exhibit DICER1 PVs and represent morphological variants of pure SLCT, with areas exhibiting close mimicry of juvenile granulosa cell tumour {32815828, 25844550}. Similarly, adult granulosa cell tumour with tubular areas, which (out of context) could be interpreted as a Sertoli cell tumour component, should not be regarded as a gynandroblastoma, since a component of tubules is still compatible with adult granulosa cell tumour {40326456}.

There are some minor amendments to the WHO classification of germ cell tumours in the sixth edition. Somatic neoplasms arising from teratomas are covered in the section on mature teratomas rather than having their own separate section. Mucinous neoplasms arising within teratomas are discussed in the same section, and an alternative terminology, akin to that used for appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, is recommended for large intestinal–type mucinous neoplasms arising in teratomas {33963606} (section Mature teratoma (including somatic neoplasms arising from teratoma). Carcinomas with yolk sac tumour elements (so-called somatically derived yolk sac tumour) may occur in the ovary and endometrium {27334714} and are covered in the sections on high-grade serous carcinoma (section High-grade serous carcinoma), “clear cell carcinoma” (section Clear cell carcinoma), endometrioid carcinoma (section Endometrioid carcinoma), and other endometrial carcinomas (section Other endometrial carcinomas). These are not true germ cell tumours and should not be managed as germ cell tumours. Germ cell tumours of various other sites in the female genital tract are briefly mentioned in sections where germ cell tumours are covered in this chapter. <<52633>>Table 9.XXX lists the expected immunohistochemical profile of malignant ovarian germ cell tumours.

Small cell carcinoma of the ovary of hypercalcaemic type exhibits deleterious germline or somatic mutations in a single gene, SMARCA4, in almost all cases {24658002; 24658001; 24658004; 27504996}; SMARCA4 is part of the SWI/SNF complex, which is implicated in the pathogenesis of a growing number of malignancies. Demonstration of this mutation and/or loss of immunohistochemical staining for SMARCA4 may, in the appropriate morphological context, be crucial for the diagnosis of this highly aggressive neoplasm {26645725; 27100627}.

Mesenchymal tumours of the ovary are covered in a stand-alone chapter devoted to these neoplasms at all sites in the female genital tract (section Mesenchymal tumours); the only ovarian mesenchymal tumour, which is retained in the current chapter, is ovarian myxoma since it is not covered in the mesenchymal chapter. Mixed epithelial and mesenchymal tumours are also covered in the mesenchymal chapter, which covers these neoplasms at all sites in the female genital tract. Mesothelial tumours are covered in section Peritoneal mesothelial tumours and metastatic tumours in section Metastasis to the female genital tract (section Metastases to the ovary).
