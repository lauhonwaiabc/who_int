4.3.1.1 Adenocarcinoma in situ, HPV-associated

Definition

Adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), HPV-associated, is an intraepithelial glandular neoplasm caused by persistent HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: high-grade cervical glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, HPV-associated.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Squamocolumnar junction and proximal endocervix / endocervical canal

Clinical features

Most patients are asymptomatic, and diagnosis is usually made following an abnormal cervical cancer screening test, either cytology and/or positive high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) test {25951104}. Approximately 30–60% of HPV-associated AIS are detected incidentally during follow-up for squamous abnormalities {12352181; 30980692; 12942122; 20949623; 24678677; 27908632}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of HPV-associated AIS has increased over the past few decades, especially among women in their fourth decade of life {14983500; 19019325; 28102052}. Among women aged 30–39 years and residing in the USA, the incidence rate was 6.6 cases per 100 000 person-years in 2008 and increased to 11.2 cases per 100 000 person-years in 2015 {30980692; 10502426; 19019325; 32168211}. In contrast, it declined among younger women, which is likely due to HPV vaccination and/or HPV-based screening, which is better than cytology in the detection of glandular neoplasia {31255301; 29859731}. The mean patient age at diagnosis is in the fourth decade of life (35–37 years), 10–15 years younger than the mean age for invasive HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinoma {990231; 7248903; 7053829; 9207802}.

Etiology

HPV-associated AIS is caused by persistent HR-HPV infection, most commonly HPV18 and HPV16 and less commonly HPV52 and HPV45 {36419751; 37064129}. Of the AIS cases that are diagnosed by cervical cytology sampling and/or biopsied on colposcopy, 99% are HPV associated {22323075}. Of these, > 90% are positive for HPV16 and/or HPV18, a proportion similar to that seen in invasive HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinoma {22323075}. The higher prevalence of HPV18 in invasive adenocarcinoma versus in invasive squamous cell carcinoma {20473886} is also seen for AIS (~50%) versus HSIL (CIN 2/3) (~20%) {22323075}.

Risk factors for the development of HPV-associated AIS are similar to the risk factors for cervical carcinoma in general, and include smoking, immune suppression, and long-term oral contraceptive use {11303584; 32114321; 12686037}.

Pathogenesis

HPV infection likely occurs in the reserve cells, which can differentiate into glandular and/or squamous in situ or invasive neoplasms resulting in AIS or concurrent AIS/HSIL, and the oncogenic development pathway is the same as in squamous lesions {12942122; 6856213; 1372156; 31444787}. See section Squamous intraepithelial lesions. It has also been proposed that some cases of AIS arise from progenitor cells in the endocervical glandular compartment that have unidirectional glandular differentiation {20567153}. Viral integration is an early event in HPV-associated AIS, in contrast to squamous lesions {18172324; 17054308}. In HPV-associated AIS, HPV18 demonstrates higher chromosomal integration frequencies compared with HPV16, while HPV-associated AIS infected by HPV16 (but not HPV18) shows a decrease in APOBEC3 (an antiviral host-defense enzyme)-related nucleotide substitution {34175494}.

Macroscopic appearance

Colposcopically, HPV-associated AIS is subtle, as it does not have the typical acetowhite or mosaic vascular pattern of HSIL, and it is difficult to appreciate especially when high in the endocervical canal {2792895; 25950554; 17043583}.

Histopathology

HPV-associated AIS is a neoplastic proliferation that is confined to pre-existing glands. The distribution may be largely restricted to the surface epithelium (sometimes referred to as superficial or early AIS) and can also replace normal endocervical glands/crypts with preserved lobular architecture {16327433; 3342374; 3605256; 10872657; 12352181}. Rarely, colonization of the lower uterine segment or endometrial epithelium may occur, and involvement of adnexal tissues through direct extension or exfoliation with implantation has also been described {19952931; 18813124}. Typically, HPV-associated AIS is composed of columnar cells with pseudostratified, enlarged, oval, and hyperchromatic nuclei with easily identified apical mitotic figures (floating mitoses) and basal apoptotic bodies/karyorrhexis {10872657; 12657929; 9537470; 2792895; 3342374}. AIS can have more subtle features, such as mild nuclear atypia and relative lack of apoptosis or prominent mitotic figures {10872657; 16327433}. Cilia may be present rarely {10872657; 35782103; 32947332}. The cells are most commonly mucin-depleted, but abundant intracytoplasmic mucin of endocervical or intestinal (goblet cells, argyrophilic granules) type may be present. Stratified mucin-producing intraepithelial lesion (SMILE) is composed of mucin-containing cells in a multilayered and stratified arrangement and can sometimes show palisading of the basal cells {11023104; 25039487; 25039487 }. It is best considered a morphological pattern of AIS (HPV-associated AIS, SMILE-pattern), although some consider it as a distinct category of reserve cell dysplasia {32482967}, and these lesions may have previously been reported as adenosquamous carcinoma in situ in the older literature {14308224}. When SMILE has minimal mucin, it can be misclassified as HSIL, especially since HPV-associated AIS is often associated with a concurrent HSIL {15387866}. There is no low-grade glandular equivalent to low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) {16327433; 3342374; 3605256; 10872657; 12352181}.

The extent to which HPV-associated AIS can display intraglandular complexity, including cribriform arrangements, and/or exophytic papillary architecture is controversial. When these findings are present focally in a well-sampled excision/resection specimen, it is generally within the allowable spectrum of AIS. However, when extensive and/or very well developed, a diagnosis of HPV-associated adenocarcinoma with non-destructive invasion is a consideration (i.e. Silva pattern A adenocarcinoma – see section Adenocarcinoma, HPV-associated).

Ancillary testing

The tumour suppressor protein p16 shows immunohistochemical overexpression (block-type staining) in nearly all cases of HPV-associated AIS as a result of compensatory upregulation related to interference of the HPV oncoprotein E7 with cellular RB1 (the retinoblastoma protein) {36396551}. Although p16 immunohistochemistry is not required in every case, it should be applied when there is any diagnostic uncertainty involving hyperplastic, reactive, metaplastic, or other benign cervical glandular lesions {27801764; 36396551}. Because these non-neoplastic proliferations may show some p16 staining, it is critical to distinguish between block-type (or every-cell) and negative/non–block-type staining. It is important to note that some other glandular neoplasms in the differential diagnosis of HPV-associated AIS can display block-type p16 staining, especially in limited biopsy or curettage specimens. Examples include serous carcinoma of the endometrium, tube-ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma, high-grade endometrioid carcinoma of the endometrium, and even HPV-independent cervical adenocarcinomas {17581420; 19623034; 20727021; 38445509}.

HPV in situ hybridization will identify nuclear signals (DNA-based) or nuclear and cytoplasmic signals (mRNA-based); mRNA-based in situ hybridization is more sensitive and specific than DNA-based methods {29135516}. PCR may be used to confirm HPV infection, but sensitivity and specificity lack because analysis may underperform in archival formalin-fixed tissue, and these tests do not provide specific information on the presence of HPV within neoplastic cells {28403015}. Direct HPV testing may also have value in selected cases, such as those with an equivocal p16 staining pattern or cases with block-type staining of a lesion lacking the characteristic morphology of HPV-associated AIS. mRNA in situ hybridization can help adjudicate the HPV status of these problematic cases and inform the correct diagnosis {29135516}. However, this test is not widely available in most laboratories; therefore, a useful adjunct to p16 in determining an HPV-driven process is RB1 immunohistochemistry. Due to HPV E7 binding and partial degradation of RB1 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, there is partial loss of RB1 expression in HPV-associated neoplasms, including HPV-associated AIS {38445509}. Diffuse block-type p16 positivity and partial loss of RB1 expression is highly sensitive and specific to a HR-HPV–driven lesion.

Characteristically, there is loss of ER expression (may be focally and weakly positive) and, more specifically, PR expression in HPV-associated AIS {17365826}. Benign mimics of HPV-associated AIS tend to be positive for ER and PR {24678677; 17197900}.

Other markers that can be useful in distinguishing endocervical glandular neoplasia from benign mimics include PAX2, HNF4α, and CAIX. PAX2 is a urogenital transcription factor expressed in Wolffian ducts, developing kidneys, and Müllerian ducts during embryonic development, which has been found to be positive in benign endocervical glands, including mesonephric remnants. In contrast, most cervical adenocarcinomas and AIS are PAX2 negative, regardless of HPV status {17529925; 20061933; 23202787}. HNF4α is a transcription factor widely expressed in tissues such as liver, kidneys, pancreas, stomach, and intestine and is a sensitive and specific marker of gastrointestinal-type tumours because of its relationship to MUC5AC {2279702; 22554936}. It has been shown that HNF4α is expressed in both HPV-associated and HPV-independent glandular neoplasia of the cervix (in situ and invasive) but not in normal endocervical glands {39798855}. CAIX is a transmembrane protein originally discovered in the HPV18-positive cervical adenocarcinoma HeLa cell line {8486430}. It is expressed in invasive and in situ endocervical glandular neoplasias, also regardless of HPV status, and can be useful when differentiating neoplastic from benign/reactive glandular lesions {19670419; 21157448 ; 8080042; 8084592; 23391777; 23299542}.

Cytology

The Bethesda system provides standardized terminology and diagnostic criteria for reporting cervicovaginal cytology specimens {25931431}.

Cervical cytology specimens have a higher false negative rate for the detection of AIS and cervical adenocarcinoma compared with squamous neoplasia {34583894}. HPV testing increases the sensitivity of screening for cervical adenocarcinoma {38373111}. Primary prevention by HPV vaccination is the most effective strategy for the elimination of cervical adenocarcinoma {28720447}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

High-risk HPV testing may be useful in diagnosis, but the diagnosis can generally be made on morphology with or without p16 staining.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: columnar cells, either mucin-depleted or with evident intracytoplasmic mucin, with pseudostratified, enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei and easily identifiable apical mitotic figures and karyorrhexis or basal apoptotic bodies.

Desirable: HPV detection, either indirectly (block-type p16 staining) or directly (HR-HPV testing).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The natural history of HPV-associated AIS is not as well defined as that of HSIL, mostly due to the lower incidence. An interval of 5–13 years from clinically detectable HPV-associated AIS to progression to invasive adenocarcinoma has been reported {10964335; 10502426}. When HPV-associated AIS is diagnosed on a cervical biopsy, approximately 15% will be associated with an invasive adenocarcinoma on a subsequent excision specimen {8684768}. The spontaneous regression rate of AIS, if this occurs, is not known.

In most cases, HPV-associated AIS can be successfully treated with excisional biopsy (e.g. cold knife cone / loop excision) with negative margins and close cytological follow-up {19019325; 38733955}. Hysterectomy is sometimes undertaken. For conservative management, close follow-up by colposcopy, cytology, and HPV testing is essential, with HPV status being a powerful indicator of complete removal (when negative) versus persistent/progressive disease (when positive) {18085613; 19019325; 22188786}.
