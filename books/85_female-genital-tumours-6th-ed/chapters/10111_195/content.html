10.1.1.1 Leiomyoma

Definition

Leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle neoplasms with a wide range of morphological patterns.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

None.

Localization

Uterus is the most common site, and uterine leiomyomas may be intramural, submucosal, or subserosal. Leiomyomas can also arise in extrauterine gynaecological sites including ovary, fallopian tube, broad ligament, vulva, and vagina.

Clinical features

In general, leiomyomas occur more frequently in patients who are receiving progestogen therapy {21398323; 3182168; 12636944}.

Lower genital tract leiomyomas typically present as slow-growing painless masses and may be clinically mistaken as a cyst.

Uterine leiomyomas are often asymptomatic, but one third of patients present with menorrhagia; pelvic pain; or symptoms related to size, location, and number of tumours.

Leiomyomas of the ovary may be an incidental finding or may present as a pelvic mass that may be rapidly growing and associated with ascites (including Meigs syndrome) {15489647; 1417447; 9891243}.

Broad ligament leiomyomas may be associated with massive ovarian oedema and pseudo–Meigs syndrome {24901402; 9704185}.

Epidemiology

In the lower genital tract, leiomyomas are the most common mesenchymal tumours, with an estimated incidence of 5.4–77% among women of reproductive age, and they usually present in the fourth to sixth decade of life {8669526; 28786880; 34343794; 28296146}.

Uterine leiomyomas are most prevalent among African American women and least common among Asian women {31960950; 18534913; 28296146} and usually affect women in their fifth decade of life; they represent the most common tumour at this site. Approximately 10% of uterine leiomyomas show a variable histological appearance (e.g. cellular, myxoid; described below). Described geographical or racial variability may partly be related to different epigenetic factors {23818951; 22428009; 25922306}.

Leiomyomas are rare in the ovary and account for < 1% of all ovarian tumours.

Etiology

Most cases are sporadic, but rare tumours arise in the setting of hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma (HLRCC) syndrome (see section Hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma (FH)) or Alport syndrome-diffuse leiomyomatosis {28275241}. Patients with HLRCC syndrome present with symptomatic uterine leiomyomas and cutaneous leiomyomas in the second to third decades of life.

Risk factors include family history, premenopausal state, obesity, hypertension, dietary factors, and > 5 years since most recently giving birth. The use of oral contraceptives decreases the risk of leiomyomas {9757871; 8813312; 33067250}.

Pathogenesis

Most leiomyomas originate from a single transformed somatic stem cell {22633281; 24890270; 16202732; 8529956}, although multiple concurrent tumours may have a common clonal origin with distinct secondary subclonal genetic aberrations {25964426; 24713395}. Most leiomyomas harbour MED12 mutations, HMGA2 aberrations, RAD51B alterations, or biallelic loss of FH gene (see #53456Table 10.XXX) {25106763}. A minority harbour HMGA1 rearrangements, RAD51B fusions, COL4A5 and COL4A6 deletions, and chromosome 1p deletion {34626221; 37466765}. These alterations are mutually exclusive {28592321}. Epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin remodelling, play a key role in the tumorigenesis of uterine leiomyomas. These changes affect genes involved in tumour suppression, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix formation, contributing to leiomyoma growth {34349258; 35199155; 25922306}. Spindle and myxoid leiomyomas have relatively simple genomic profiles {25932961; 31189997}; in contrast, leiomyomas with bizarre nuclei have both simple genomic profiles (biallelic inactivation, loss of heterozygosity, and FH mutations) and complex genomic profiles (TP53 and RB1 alterations) {28863073; 28664937; 27363490; 39961604; 38481055}.

Macroscopic appearance

Leiomyomas from all the gynaecological sites show similar gross features. They are well circumscribed but unencapsulated, range widely in size, and characteristically have a bulging, firm, whorled, white cut surface. Some tumours are soft, in particular if oedematous, highly cellular, or epithelioid. Progestogen therapy may induce multiple discrete haemorrhagic foci (apoplectic change) {26685083}; haemorrhage and infarction may also occur in larger tumours. Cystic change may be seen. Differences in macroscopic appearance can be associated with variant histology or hormonal milieu. Hydropic leiomyomas usually have a vaguely lobulated, white-grey, and water oedematous cut surface that may be granular. Cellular leiomyomas and lipoleiomyomas often have a tan to yellow cut surface. Myxoid leiomyoma has a gelatinous and sticky cut surface, which does not exude oedematous fluid on compression. Leiomyomas in pregnant patients may have a beefy-red appearance (so-called red degeneration).

Vulvovaginal leiomyomas frequently harbour myxohyaline change resulting in a homogeneous and glistening cut surface.

Uterine leiomyomas are often multiple, form discrete masses, and may occur in a submucosal, intramural, or subserosal location. In contrast, diffuse leiomyomatosis shows diffuse involvement of the myometrium by numerous poorly defined or confluent small nodules. Mitotically active leiomyomas are often submucosal. The serosal component of cotyledonoid dissecting leiomyoma may appear as beefy bulbous protrusions mimicking placental tissue.

Histopathology

Leiomyomas from all gynaecological sites, including uterus, broad ligament, and lower genital tract, show essentially similar histological features. Most leiomyomas have a well-demarcated border and are composed of spindle cells arranged in intersecting fascicles. Cells have indistinct borders, eosinophilic fibrillary cytoplasm, and cigar-shaped nuclei with small nucleoli and rare mitoses. Nuclear palisading resembling schwannoma may be seen. Prominent hyalinization may lead to compartmentalization of tumour cells, imparting a pseudoepithelioid appearance {26715170}. Ischaemic necrosis (defined by the presence of a band of granulation tissue with or without associated haemorrhage or fibrosis between viable and non-viable tumour) may occur. The non-viable areas have a mummified appearance in which both tumour cells and blood vessels are devitalized. At an early stage, only single or groups of apoptotic cells are seen; in longstanding leiomyoma, calcification may occur {17667546; 20179432}. Treatment of leiomyomas by several modalities, including GnRH agonists, embolization, and antifibrinolytic agents, can modify morphological features. Common findings related to treatment include increased cellularity, inflammatory infiltration, and regressive changes, such as ischaemic necrosis, hyalinization, and calcification {12548162; 20407327; 15958862; 17667546; 27801754; 14615835}. No tumour cell necrosis is seen.

Fumarate hydratase (FH) deficiency, either due to germline or somatic mutation, is seen in as many as 1–1.5% of uterine smooth muscle tumours, predominantly leiomyomas. FH deficiency may be seen in various patterns of leiomyomas, as described subsequently {28664937; 27454940; 26457356; 23211287, 31471882}. Characteristic features may include staghorn vasculature, alveolar-pattern oedema, scattered bizarre nuclei, ovoid nuclei sometimes arranged in chains, eosinophilic cytoplasmic (rhabdoid) inclusions, and prominent eosinophilic nucleoli surrounded by perinucleolar haloes {27454940; 26574848; 24309325; 39835370}. All show overexpression of 2SC, and many show loss of FH expression by immunohistochemistry. These morphological and immunohistochemical features do not discriminate germline versus somatic mutation {37312573}, and therefore the risk of HLRCC syndrome merits clinical correlation.

Cellular leiomyoma shows significantly increased cellularity compared with the surrounding myometrium {7793473}. The neoplastic cells have a diffuse arrangement (often in the centre) or grow in fascicles (typically at the periphery). Thick-walled vessels and cleft-like spaces are common. Cells typically have scant cytoplasm and lack atypia, and mitoses are rare. The border can be irregular and merges with the surrounding myometrium {647634; 24901397}.

Mitotically active leiomyomas are usually seen in women of reproductive age. They are associated with secretory endometrium, pregnancy, and drugs (progestogens and tamoxifen). The mitotic count can range as much as 8.42 mitoses/mm², corresponding to 20 mitoses/10 HPF (field diameter: 0.55 mm), but higher mitotic counts should be classified as STUMP. Cellularity may be increased, but there is no cytological atypia or tumour cell necrosis {2307449; 3337336; 1728856}.

Hydropic leiomyoma has conspicuous zonal, watery oedema, which separates tumour cells into thin and delicate cords or nests and may surround thick-walled vessels in a perinodular pattern {30292626; 1309411}.

Leiomyoma with ischaemic necrosis may show multiple stellate zones of haemorrhage surrounded by a hypercellular rim of cells – some with eosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nuclei, and increased mitoses (tumours with this constellation of features were formerly called apoplectic leiomyoma). The cells in these areas may be associated with myxoid change. Most importantly, away from these areas, the smooth muscle cells lack atypia {3182168; 4073354; 26685083}. These changes are associated with progestogen therapy and pregnancy; there may be hydropic change and cells with a deciduoid or signet-ring appearance {21398323}.

Leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei is characterized by the presence of bizarre cells that may be mononucleated or multinucleated and may have eosinophilic or globular cytoplasm, smudged chromatin, nuclear pseudoinclusions, or vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli, which can be focal, multifocal, or diffuse. The background may show typical leiomyoma {27015034; 28664937; 28700441; 25140893}. Mitotic count is typically low (< 2.1 mitoses/mm2, equating to < 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter), but karyorrhectic nuclei, which may mimic atypical mitoses, are not uncommon {9351564; 25140893}. Most tumours have a well-circumscribed border.

Lipoleiomyoma has variable numbers of mature adipocytes admixed with the smooth muscle cells {16810060; 37972871}. Some tumours may have a chondroid appearance or resemble hibernoma {15842648; 9891251}. The cells have no significant atypia and only rare mitoses {1582747}.

Epithelioid leiomyomas display round or polygonal cells with appreciable eosinophilic granular or clear cytoplasm arranged in sheets, cords, trabeculae, or nests. They may be admixed with a spindle cell component {56982; 9130984}. Tumours exhibiting a plexiform growth pattern and measuring < 10 mm are termed “plexiform tumourlets”. They are characterized by abundant extracellular matrix, which surrounds and separates the tumour cells into cords {6092289; 33103268}. Tumours have < 0.8 mitoses/mm2 (equating to < 2 mitoses/10 HPF 0.55 mm in diameter). No tumour cell necrosis should be seen. Rarely, cells have a rhabdoid morphology {9130984; 16175075}.

Myxoid leiomyoma is a well-circumscribed and hypocellular tumour with cells widely separated by myxoid acid-mucin stroma (Alcian blue–positive). Cells lack cytological atypia and mitotic activity, and no tumour cell necrosis should be present {22175894; 28787279}. Myxoid leiomyoma is rare and is a diagnosis of exclusion once other entities in the differential diagnosis have been excluded (e.g. inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour and endometrial stromal tumours).

Dissecting leiomyoma shows irregular dissection by nodules of bland smooth muscle cells of the myometrium, often with conspicuous hydropic change and occasionally with intravenous extension {8944038; 10478662; 23370645}. There may be extension outside the uterus (cotyledonoid dissecting leiomyoma) {22710314}.

Diffuse leiomyomatosis is characterized by innumerable, poorly circumscribed hypercellular nodules of bland smooth muscle cells diffusely involving the myometrium {3692674; 8835266}. It may be seen in patients with HLRCC or Alport syndrome {31162287}.

Rarely, heterologous elements including bone {26458678}, cartilage {19225797}, skeletal muscle {1877531; 10088557}, haematopoietic or lymphoid cells {2670790}, and osteoclastic-type giant cells {26166717; 25851705} may be found.

Lower genital tract leiomyomas show similar histology to that of their uterine counterparts; morphological patterns are unusual, but they are often hyalinized or oedematous {450336; 418977}. The classification criteria are probably similar to uterine leiomyomas, but the data are limited {8669526; 1879812; 28786880; 32687944}. Recent literature shows that malignant potential in lower genital tract smooth muscle tumours can be defined per uterine criteria {28786880; 32687944}. Uterine criteria for malignancy are also applicable to smooth muscle tumours of the ovary, broad ligament, and other uterine ligaments {15489647; 30702465}.

Immunohistochemistry

Leiomyomas express desmin, h-caldesmon, SMA, MSA, calponin, transgelin, and SMMHC {26715170; 11257619; 11176075; 34626221}. They are also positive for ER, PR and WT1 (nuclear) {19734847}. CD10 is expressed in as many as 40% of cellular leiomyomas {11396634; 11914617}. Expression of p16 and abnormal p53 pattern is rarely seen in usual leiomyomas, but these markers may be positive/aberrant in leiomyomas with bizarre nuclei and FH-deficient leiomyomas {18580309; 39961604; 39869100; 36788080}. Hypercellular areas in apoplectic leiomyomas may show increased p16 expression {28609585}. HMGA2 expression may be seen {30292626}. ALK stain should be negative {35426837}. FH-deficient leiomyomas show positive staining for 2SC and may show complete loss of staining for FH {26574848; 24441663; 24309325;31162287; 26457356}. However, some tumours with missense FH mutations may retain FH staining {30741757; 26574848; 28664937; 26457356; 34678832}. Overall, 2SC is a more sensitive marker for FH deficiency {34678832}.

Usual leiomyomas and different patterns typically have a benign course, although experience with some patterns are limited {8179071; 19417585; 20179432}. Leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei may recur after myomectomy {25140893; 28700441; 34754271}. There are also rare examples of leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei progressing to leiomyosarcomas {35093974; 39691991; 19633649; 34754271; 36457718; 39691991}. Lower genital tract leiomyomas may recur after incomplete excision, but the risk is low {8669526; 28786880}. Referral for genetic counselling to exclude HLRCC syndrome should be considered for women with an FH-deficient leiomyoma {30741757}.

Cytology

Smears show mostly low cellularity and, when cellular, comprise cohesive tissue fragments with smooth edges, bland slender spindle cells with blunt-ended nuclei displaying vesicular chromatin and without cell borders against a clean background. The cells lack nuclear atypia, mitotic figures, and necrosis {8498132;12760289}.

Prognosis and prediction

Leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei may recur after myomectomy {25140893; 28700441; 34754271}. Usual-type leiomyoma

Intersecting fascicles of bland spindle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and cigar-shaped nuclei lacking cytological atypia or tumour cell necrosis and with very low mitotic count

Cellular leiomyoma

More cellular relative to surrounding myometrium; thick-walled blood vessels, clefted spaces; may have irregular border; cells have scant cytoplasm but lack cytological atypia; low mitotic count; no tumour cell necrosis

Mitotically active leiomyoma

Leiomyoma with < 8.42 mitoses/mm2 (6–19 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter); no cytological atypia; no tumour cell necrosis

Hydropic leiomyoma

Leiomyoma with prominent oedematous stroma that leads to compartmentalization of the smooth muscle cells resulting in an epithelioid appearance

Apoplectic leiomyoma

History of progesterone use or pregnancy; stellate zones of haemorrhage; zonation phenomenon; early infarct necrosis; may see mild to moderate cytological atypia and increased mitoses immediately adjacent to the necrosis but absent in areas distant from the necrosis

Leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei

Focal, multifocal, or diffuse bizarre cells; low mitotic count (< 2.1 mitoses/mm2, typically < 5 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter); no cytological atypia; no tumour cell necrosis

Lipoleiomyoma

Admixture of mature adipocytes and smooth muscle cells

Epithelioid leiomyoma

Rounded or polygonal cells with eosinophilic granular or clear cytoplasm; no cytological atypia; no tumour cell necrosis; mitotic count < 0.8 mitoses/mm2 (equating to < 2 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter)

Myxoid leiomyoma

Circumscribed, hypocellular, and myxoid; no cytological atypia or mitotic activity; no tumour cell necrosis; ALK negative

Cotylenoid dissecting leiomyoma

Irregular nodular dissection of bland smooth muscle cells within the myometrium

Diffuse leiomyomatosis

Innumerable, poorly circumscribed hypercellular tumour nodules within the myometrium; no cytological atypia or mitotic activity; no tumour cell necrosis

Fumarate hydratase (FH)–deficient leiomyoma

Staghorn blood vessels; alveolar oedema; chain-like arrangement of smooth muscle cells; eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions; prominent eosinophilic macronucleoli surrounded by clear perinucleolar haloes; these FH-deficient morphological features may be seen in usual-type leiomyoma, cellular leiomyoma, or leiomyoma with bizarre nuclei.
