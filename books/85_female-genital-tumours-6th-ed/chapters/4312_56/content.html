4.3.1.2 Adenocarcinoma, HPV-associated

Definition

Adenocarcinoma, HPV-associated, is an invasive adenocarcinoma of the cervix or vagina, caused by persistent high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the cervix (transformation zone mostly) but also rarely in the vagina {31857682}; before diagnosing a primary vaginal HPV-associated adenocarcinoma, spread from the cervix should be excluded.

Clinical features

Symptomatic patients present mostly with abnormal vaginal bleeding and/or a mass. Some cases are detected through cytology-based / HPV testing–based screening, as either glandular or squamous abnormalities.

Epidemiology

The relative frequency of adenocarcinomas of the cervix has increased to 10–25% of all cervical carcinomas, particularly in high-income countries, predominantly because of the impact of cytology-based screening on detecting and treating squamous precancers {10926787; 14983500; 22816437; 31255301}. There is also some evidence that the absolute incidence rate of adenocarcinomas is also increasing, the cause of which remains undetermined {40180806}. Approximately 85% of cervical adenocarcinomas are HPV-associated, and the relative frequency of these is anticipated to decrease with the wider adoption of primary HPV-based screening {24192252, 29135516; 39052435}. The mean patient age at presentation of HPV-associated adenocarcinoma is early to mid-forties, significantly younger than that of HPV-independent adenocarcinoma {30679193;30720532}. Adenocarcinoma of the vagina is uncommon and accounts for 8–10% of primary carcinomas at this site {34669198}. The small number of patients reported with primary vaginal HPV-associated adenocarcinoma have been 38–51 years of age at the time of diagnosis {31857682}.

Etiology

These tumours are caused by persistent infection with HR-HPV. For neoplasms based in the cervix, the most common HPV types are 16, 18, and 45, accounting for about 95% of cases {22323075; 22684765; 26096203}. Coinfection with multiple HPV types, as seen in HPV-associated squamous cell carcinoma, occurs in 5–10% of cases {24762548; 15502807; 26096203}. Specific etiological data for primary vaginal HPV-associated adenocarcinomas are limited.

Pathogenesis

Most adenocarcinomas are etiologically associated with HPV clades A9 (typically HPV16) and A7 (typically HPV18). The HPV viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 inactivate the p53 and RB1 proteins, respectively; this inactivation is associated with integration of HPV into the host genome, genomic instability, and accumulation of somatic mutations {20592731}. The presence of HR-HPV is a defining molecular feature of HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinoma, which is otherwise a molecularly heterogeneous entity {28397825; 28112728; 24390348}. When stratified by Silva pattern of invasion, different genomic groups emerge {28731050; 36849702; 37920148}. High-risk adenocarcinomas (pattern B with lymphovascular invasion and pattern C) are enriched for SNV in PIK3CA, ERBB2, and ATRX. APOBEC-high signature is seen exclusively in pattern C tumours and is associated with an increased tumour mutation burden. Copy-number alteration differences with gains and losses in specific loci and overall increased alterations in pattern C tumours compared with patterns A and B have also been reported. Variable potentially actionable mutations in some genes (PIK3CA, ERBB2, KRAS) may be present {31855952; 36849702}. TP53 mutations have been documented in some tumours (including some with wildtype p53 staining), and these alterations are not considered to be clinically significant drivers {36849702}. Specific diagnostic molecular findings of vaginal HPV-associated adenocarcinoma are unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours mostly present as an exophytic mass or ulcerated lesion. Endophytic/diffuse involvement of the cervix is seen as expansion and induration of the wall (barrel-shaped cervix).

Histopathology

Cervical and vaginal neoplasms show the same morphology and histological hallmarks of HPV-associated adenocarcinoma, including apical mitoses and karyorrhexis/apoptoses, which are typically, although not always, conspicuous and identifiable at low magnification {29135516}. Tumour cell nuclei are enlarged, elongated, and hyperchromatic; however, overt pleomorphism is uncommon. Well-differentiated to moderately differentiated areas feature glands with smooth luminal borders lined by pseudostratified columnar epithelium. Villoglandular (elongated papillary architecture) or otherwise exophytic growth is also sometimes present. Solid growth is seen in more poorly differentiated / higher-grade tumours, and microglandular and micropapillary architecture may also be present {1471729; 30864975}.

The histological patterns of HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinoma are summarized in #53259Table 4.XXX. Distinction between the patterns is moderately reproducible {29877920}. The mucin-depleted, usual pattern is the most common morphological appearance that is encountered, but mucinous patterns also occur and may predominate. Mucinous differentiation can take the form of NOS (evident intracytoplasmic mucin in the majority of tumour cells on morphological examination but otherwise reminiscent of usual pattern), intestinal (well-developed goblet cells), or signet-ring (prominent mucin vacuoles causing nuclear displacement with or without extracellular mucin) morphologies. Invasive stratified mucin-producing carcinoma is an uncommon additional mucinous pattern that displays significant morphological diversity but that invariably shows some form of stratification/multilayering and peripheral palisading of nuclei {32235154}.

Regarding tumour grading, the International Society of Gynecological Pathologists has recommended that these neoplasms should be graded using a modification of the FIGO grading system used for endometrial endometrioid adenocarcinomas, although there is limited evidence base for this. Tumours with ≤ 10% solid growth are designated as grade 1, 11% to 50% solid growth as grade 2, and > 50% solid growth as grade 3. Tumours can be upgraded in the presence of marked nuclear atypia involving the majority (> 50%) of the tumour. There are some special circumstances, and tumours with a micropapillary, signet-ring, or an invasive stratified mucin-producing component (regardless of the quantity/percentage) should be considered automatically high-grade {33570864}.

All forms of HPV-associated adenocarcinoma can have destructive or non-destructive (adenocarcinoma in situ [AIS]-like) growth patterns {26715171; 12352181}. The pattern-based classification (Silva) system represents an attempt to correlate histological invasion patterns with outcome to spare lymphadenectomies in some patients. Although prospective studies to evaluate and confirm the retrospective results are necessary, this system may in the future become an important part of surgical planning and prognostication. It should be emphasized that the system is restricted to HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinoma and can only be applied to tumours that have been completely resected on loop/cone/trachelectomy/hysterectomy specimens. It divides invasive tumours into three groups encompassing the spectrum of stromal invasion from non-destructive (Pattern A), early/locally destructive (Pattern B), to diffusely destructive (Pattern C) {24071876; 25724003}. The system has moderate to good reproducibility {27174588; 27255163}, particularly in the distinction between indolent tumours (pattern A, non-destructive invasion) and potentially aggressive tumours (patterns B and C, destructive invasion). Recent studies have proposed a simplified binary pattern–based system with a low-risk category (pattern A and pattern B without lymphovascular invasion) and a high-risk category (pattern B with lymphovascular invasion and pattern C) {35501168; 36849702; 39164939}. The binary system has improved interobserver agreement and a higher proportion of evaluated cases with near perfect or perfect reviewer agreement {39014547}.

Cases with destructive invasion exhibit poorly formed angulated glands and cellular clusters that haphazardly infiltrate the cervical stroma, with an accompanying desmoplastic stromal response. Cases with non-destructive invasion include highly differentiated glands, often in a vaguely lobular configuration, which can sometimes be difficult to distinguish from AIS. The distinction between AIS and pattern A is often difficult and poorly reproducible {27174588}. In such difficult scenarios, an acceptable practice is to report the possibility of a pattern A adenocarcinoma and measure the depth of the focus in question, which can be used for staging purposes. such cases have a low risk of recurrence. Lymph node metastasis and conservative management can be considered particularly in cases following conization with negative resection margins {10638451; 33570863}.

There is a strong (but not perfect) correlation between HPV-associated pathogenesis and morphology, and while HPV testing and/or related surrogate tests are not always required for an accurate diagnosis, HPV-associated adenocarcinomas do not always demonstrate prominent mitoses and karyorrhexis/apoptosis {29135516}. Therefore, p16 immunohistochemistry should be used as a sensitive indirect test for HR-HPV infection since almost all HR-HPV–associated tumours show block-type staining {29135516}. Rare p16 negative/non–block-type cases may derive from methylation-induced inactivation {10535995}. Importantly, p16 results may not be reliable using old or poorly preserved tissue blocks {29135516}. It should be noted that p16 can also exhibit block-type staining in tumours that are not associated with HR-HPV {29851704}. RB1 immunohistochemistry has been shown to exhibit partial loss of expression (mosaic pattern, positive in 10–90% of tumour nuclei) in HPV-associated tumours, including HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinomas {38445509; 31743130}, in contrast to complete loss in small cell lung cancer due to RB1 gene alterations {35792876}. This mosaic pattern may represent heterogeneous degradation of RB1 by HPV E7 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and can help distinguish HPV-associated tumours from other carcinomas exhibiting block-type p16 expression; these carcinomas show either retained expression of RB1 in > 90% of the tumour or complete absence. The p16/RB1 immunohistochemical panel may be a reliable method to predict HR-HPV infection, especially when direct HPV testing is not available.

HR-HPV mRNA chromogenic in situ hybridization is roughly as sensitive as p16 immunohistochemistry but is more specific {29135516}; however, this test is not yet widely available. Rare cases that are negative with mRNA in situ hybridization are likely to be associated with HPV types not accounted for in the assay. HR-HPV DNA in situ hybridization will show punctate nuclear signals, while mRNA-based in situ hybridization can show punctate nuclear to diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic positivity and is more sensitive and specific than DNA-based in situ hybridization {29135516}. PCR may be used to confirm HPV infection, but sensitivity and specificity are questionable because the analysis may underperform in archival formalin-fixed tissue and does not provide for ascertainment that HPV is present specifically within neoplastic cells {28403015}. A specific PCR method involving laser-capture microdissection of paraffin-embedded, formalin-fixed tissue appears to be sensitive and specific, but this test cannot be used in a diagnostic setting {26334557}. Next-generation sequencing can also detect specific HPV types by mapping non-human, off-target reads to a library of known viral sequences and has been shown to correlate with other reference methods of detection, such as mRNA in situ hybridization {35331965}.

Positive immunohistochemical markers in most HPV-associated adenocarcinomas include CK7 and PAX8. Most cases exhibit wildtype p53 staining (mutation-type p53 expression should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses including HPV-independent adenocarcinomas and endometrial carcinomas) and are largely negative for ER and PR (although some exhibit focal and weak expression especially for ER), vimentin, CK20, p63, p40, and SATB2 {29851704; 29877920; 11781517}. CDX2 can be positive, particularly but not exclusively in those cases with intestinal differentiation in the form of goblet cells {18156982}. Claudin-18.2, a marker of gastric mucin, can rarely be expressed in cases with intestinal differentiation {40133876, 39025403}. It should be noted that invasive stratified mucin-producing carcinoma has a distinct immunohistochemical profile, more often showing diminished or no PAX8 expression with focal CK5/6, p40, and p63 positivity, indicating its reserve cell origin {34103667}. CAIX, a transmembrane protein originally discovered in the cervical adenocarcinoma HeLa cell line, can be expressed in HPV-associated lesions, including in situ and invasive squamous and glandular neoplasms, and can be useful when differentiating neoplastic from benign/reactive lesions {19670419; 21157448 ; 8080042; 8084592}, although this marker can also be rather frequently expressed in cases of gastric type, HPV-independent adenocarcinoma {23391777; 23299542}. Cervical HPV-associated adenocarcinomas are not known to show loss of mismatch repair protein expression. Mucicarmine histochemical stain can be used to highlight intracytoplasmic mucin, which can be especially helpful in stratified mucin-producing carcinomas with less conspicuous mucin {26523540}.

Cytology

The cytological appearance of HPV-associated adenocarcinoma often overlaps with that of HPV-associated AIS, although additional findings indicative of underlying invasion, such as tumour diathesis, may also be present. The morphology is characterized by syncytial aggregates and 3D clusters of malignant cells with finely vacuolated cytoplasm and enlarged pleomorphic nuclei with irregular chromatin distribution, nuclear membrane irregularities, and macronucleoli. Tumour diathesis, when present, may be less apparent on liquid-based preparations, appearing as granular and apoptotic debris clinging to the edges of tumour cell clusters [[Nayar R, Wilbur DC, editors. The Bethesda System for reporting cervical cytology: definitions, criteria, and explanatory notes. 3rd ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer, 2015]]. Identifying a background of AIS and/or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion helps classify the adenocarcinoma as cervical in origin. If a convincing in situ component is not identified, it is best to diagnose the case as adenocarcinoma NOS and defer definitive classification to a histological specimen {30351473}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HR-HPV testing may be useful in diagnosis, but the diagnosis can generally be made on morphology with p16 staining.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive adenocarcinoma with morphological features of HPV association.

Desirable: p16 block-type staining and/or HPV detection.

Staging

Tumours are staged according to the FIGO and TNM staging systems. Microscopic adenocarcinoma staging relies on pathological features, mainly depth of invasion. When admixed in situ and invasive components cannot be reliably separated, including in neoplasms with exophytic growth, the thickness of the entire lesion may be provided in lieu of the depth parameter, with an explanation; this thickness measurement can be used for tumour staging {33570866}.

Prognosis and prediction

HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinomas have significantly better disease-free and disease-specific survival than HPV-independent adenocarcinomas {30720532; 30679193; 33177150}. Stage is the most important prognostic variable overall, but there is some evidence, pending future corroborative studies, that a binary pattern of invasion classification may be more clinically significant, specifically in disease confined to the cervix {39164939}. Overall, the pattern-based classification has shown associations between tumour invasion patterns and risk of nodal metastases, recurrence, and survival. Pattern A lesions have been consistently shown to have no potential for nodal spread, although they may very rarely recur/metastasize, including in the adnexa. Ovarian involvement, both synchronous and metachronous, has been reported in non-destructive adenocarcinomas, including some very small adenocarcinomas {18813124; 34889854}, particularly in the context of endometrial and tubal involvement (secondary to transuterine and transtubal spread) and mucinous differentiation, and it does not necessarily portend a poor prognosis {37920148}. Patterns B and C confer a risk of about 4% and 25%, respectively, of nodal spread. Among pattern C lesions, those with micropapillary growth have a higher risk of nodal spread, and those with diffuse destructive and confluent architecture have a higher risk of recurrence and worse survival {28134668}. KRAS and PIK3CA mutations, which are frequent, are associated with destructive invasive patterns as well as prognosis {28731050; 24037752; 25669975; 26358014}. Because of the lack of prospective trials using pattern-based classification, management should be personalized and multidisciplinary. Tumours with an entirely exophytic growth may rarely behave aggressively {39689298}, but in general, purely exophytic usual pattern adenocarcinomas, including those with a villoglandular architecture, have an excellent prognosis. When present, stromal invasion confers a prognosis similar to that of other HPV-associated adenocarcinomas {17593209; 23552807}. Tumours with overt mucinous differentiation, including stratified mucin-producing carcinoma, may have a worse prognosis than other HPV-associated patterns {30720532; 30679193; 31385027; 32271191; 39477297}.

Since only very few primary vaginal HPV-associated adenocarcinomas have been reported, prognostic parameters are not well established.

Usual pattern

Mucinous pattern

This pattern accounts for ~75% of all cervical adenocarcinomas {29135516; 12207781}. Cells with abundant mucinous cytoplasm visible on morphology constitute 0–50% of the tumour, and the tumour otherwise shows mucin depletion.

This pattern accounts for ~10% of all cervical adenocarcinomas {29135516; 30679193}. There is intracytoplasmic mucin visible on morphology in the majority of the tumour, typically with a minor component of usual adenocarcinoma. Extracellular mucin may be seen. This pattern is subdivided into the following morphologies*:

Variable architectural arrangements including glandular, papillary, micropapillary, villoglandular, and solid morphologies are possible.

Mucinous NOS adenocarcinoma: mucinous cytoplasm resembling normal endocervix with pale purple staining on H&E

Intestinal adenocarcinoma: prominent goblet cell differentiation

Signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma: loose non-cohesive round cells with a mucinous vacuole displacing the nucleus

Stratified mucin-producing carcinoma: sometimes associated with adjacent stratified mucin-producing intraepithelial lesion and composed of invasive nests of stratified epithelium with intracytoplasmic mucin

*Although ≥ 50% of the tumour is classically required to show the mucinous morphology for designation as a specific mucinous pattern neoplasm, the presence of any of the mucinous appearances above, in any quantity, should be documented.
