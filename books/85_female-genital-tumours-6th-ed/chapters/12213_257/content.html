12.2.1.3 Invasive and metastatic hydatidiform moles

Definition

Invasive hydatidiform mole is a hydatidiform mole (usually complete or less often partial) that invades the myometrium and/or uterine vessels. Metastatic hydatidiform mole may manifest as lesions containing abnormal molar chorionic villi at sites beyond the uterus, most commonly the vaginal wall, pelvis, and lung.

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the uterus and at extrauterine sites in metastatic disease.

Clinical features

Vaginal bleeding with persistent elevation of serum hCG after primary evacuation of a hydatidiform mole is the most common clinical presentation {3010701; 25318458; 25607129}. Rarely patients present with acute abdomen or haemoperitoneum secondary to uterine perforation, and molar villi may be seen implanted on the pelvic organs such as the ovary, tube, broad ligament, or intestine {28377901}.

Epidemiology

Since hysterectomy is rarely performed for the treatment of invasive moles, the true incidence of this is difficult to estimate. A Slovak study (1993–2022) reported an incidence of invasive and metastatic hydatidiform moles at 1 case per 121 253 pregnancies (1 case per 86 589 live births) {38943503}. In the Republic of Korea, a population-based study (2009–2015) found an incidence rate of 20 cases per 100 000 pregnancies {30809458}. These findings highlight regional variations and underscore the need for comprehensive epidemiological data from other countries.

Etiology

In the majority of cases, invasive moles develop from complete hydatidiform moles {26535984}.

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

The lesion consists of invasive molar tissue extending into the myometrium. Hydropic villi may be visible, and uterine perforation may occur.

Histopathology

Most invasive hydatidiform moles are complete hydatidiform moles, which retain the villous histological characteristics but demonstrate myometrial and/or vascular invasion. Invasive partial moles also occur {25318458;22740971;8737309}. In cases of metastatic mole, molar villi are found in extrauterine locations.

In cases of degenerate invasive hydatidiform mole, where histological features may be compromised due to tissue necrosis or degeneration, p57 immunohistochemistry can aid in diagnosis.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: villi with abnormal trophoblastic proliferation within myometrium and/or uterine or extrauterine vessels.

Staging

See TNM staging of gestational trophoblastic neoplasms [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]].

Prognosis and prediction

In the absence of immediate complications (e.g. severe haemorrhage), chemotherapy is highly effective, with a cure rate of > 80%, depending on the extent of the disease {19369669; 26535984; 25607129}. Hysterectomy is usually not the first line of treatment in a young patient who desires fertility preservation. Studies have shown that gestational trophoblastic neoplasia occurs more frequently in heterozygous complete hydatidiform moles than in homozygous cases {20208060; 26535984; 31857680; 32404958}.
