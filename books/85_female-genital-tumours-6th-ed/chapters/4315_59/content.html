4.3.1.5 Adenocarcinoma, HPV-independent, clear cell type

Definition

Clear cell carcinoma is an invasive, malignant glandular neoplasm composed of clear or eosinophilic cells arranged in tubulocystic, papillary, and/or solid patterns and arising through mechanisms not associated with HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Sporadic cervical tumours typically occur in the endocervix. Tumours arising in association with in utero diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure typically occur in the ectocervix. These tumours also occur in the vagina, most commonly involving the anterior wall of the upper third.

Clinical features

The clinical features are similar to those of other cervical and vaginal carcinomas. Clear cell carcinomas of the vagina were previously seen mostly in young women in association with in utero DES exposure {9718055; 29124779; 5549830}, but they are now more commonly seen in older women independent of DES exposure {4636892; 4857957; 851158}. The tumours usually present with vaginal bleeding or discharge.

Epidemiology

Cervical clear cell carcinomas are rare, accounting for 3–4% of cervical adenocarcinomas and approximately one third of HPV-independent cervical adenocarcinomas in a population-based study in Northern Ireland {39052435}. They arise in two distinct settings: as sporadic tumours (median patient age: 51 years) {18394687} and in association with in utero DES exposure (mean patient age: 19 years) {10637063}. DES-associated risk can extend into adulthood {35767133; 37451413}.

These neoplasms are also rare within the vagina, where the effect of intrauterine DES exposure is better documented. The risk of DES-related vaginal clear cell carcinoma was highest in patients born between 1951 and 1956 {28689666}. Vaginal adenosis, the most important precursor to vaginal clear cell carcinoma, has been reported to arise in as many as 90% of patients exposed in utero to DES {1161238}. 80% of DES-associated clear cell carcinomas occurred between the ages of 15 and 31 years. The incidence of vaginal clear cell carcinoma has markedly declined since the discontinuation of DES use during pregnancy {28689666; 20204493}.

Etiology

Clear cell carcinoma is not associated with HPV. Currently only rare patients have in utero DES exposure {30733993}, and occasional cervical cases have been reported in patients with Lynch syndrome {29587389}. The etiology is otherwise unknown.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is largely unknown, although it has been proposed that some cervical examples develop due to malignant transformation of tubo-endometrioid metaplasia {33770055} and progression from an intraepithelial form of clear cell carcinoma {35283444}. Vaginal clear cell carcinomas can arise in association with vaginal adenosis (see Epidemiology). Epigenetic changes in epidermal growth factor may mediate the intergenerational effects of DES {37149030}, but risk does not extend into third generation offspring {39061214}. Non–DES-associated vaginal clear cell carcinomas may occur in association with adenosis or endometriosis, or without an identifiable precursor.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, cervical tumours can be endophytic with diffuse enlargement of the cervix (barrel-shaped cervix), exophytic with a protruding mass, or without a clinically evident mass. In the vagina, they most commonly form a polypoid, exophytic mass but may be flat and ulcerated.

Histopathology

The morphology is essentially identical to clear cell carcinomas that arise in other female genital organs, such as the ovary. Tumour cells are arranged in tubulocystic, papillary, and/or solid architectural patterns, often admixed to varying degrees. Tumour cells have clear and/or eosinophilic, sometimes granular, cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic boundaries can be prominent. Cells are cuboidal or flat, sometimes hobnailed, and only uncommonly stratified. Cuboidal tumour cells tend to have enlarged nuclei of relatively uniform size, frequently with a prominent nucleolus, whereas tubules and cysts can be lined by flat cells with hyperchromatic nuclei. The mitotic count is usually relatively low, and stromal hyalinization is common. Eosinophilic hyaline globules may be present, and occasionally there is a prominent inflammatory infiltrate. There is no grading system for these neoplasms, and they are regarded as automatically high-grade.

HNF1β, napsin A, and AMACR (racemase) are sensitive markers of clear cell carcinoma, but HNF1β is very nonspecific. Napsin A and AMACR are more specific, and staining can range from focal and weak to diffuse with strong intensity. The latter pattern is not typically seen in other cervical carcinomas {21490443; 29851704; 31361605}. The tumours are almost always diffusely positive for PAX8 and CK7 and negative for ER, PR, TTF1, and GATA3 {21490443; 29851704}. p16 usually exhibits negative/non–block-type immunoreactivity, but it is occasionally block-type despite an absence of high-risk HPV {20727021; 21490443; 23792604; 29851704}. p53 is usually wild type. Occasional cases exhibit loss of expression of mismatch repair proteins {29587389}.

Cytology

Cytological specimens show three-dimensional clusters and scattered single cells. Tumour cells have large round nuclei, coarse chromatin, nuclear membrane irregularities, and macronucleoli. The cytoplasm ranges from scant to abundant, is vacuolated and sometimes dense, and displays well-defined cell borders {34619807}. Vaginal smears have been shown to be effective for early detection of clear cell carcinoma in DES-exposed patients {10600286}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HPV testing, if performed, should be negative.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: adenocarcinoma composed of cells with clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm arranged in tubulocystic, papillary, and/or solid patterns.

Desirable: napsin A and/ or AMACR positivity; negative/non–block-type p16 staining and/or negative HPV testing.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

For cervical tumours, survival is similar to that for gastric-type adenocarcinoma and worse than that for HPV-associated adenocarcinomas {34985047}. Increasing stage, including lymph node status, is associated with decreased recurrence-free and overall survival {10684706; 34985047}. For FIGO stage II and higher, the median time to recurrence is approximately one year and the median overall survival is approximately 4 years {34985047}. For vaginal tumours, the 10-year overall survival for all stages is 80% for DES-associated cases and 60% for non–DES-associated cases {7959291}.
