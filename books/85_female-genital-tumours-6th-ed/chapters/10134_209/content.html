10.1.3.4 Adenosarcoma

Definition

Adenosarcoma is a biphasic neoplasm composed of a benign epithelial component and a malignant stromal component.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: Müllerian adenosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Adenosarcoma with sarcomatous overgrowth

Localization

Although adenosarcomas most commonly arise in the uterus, these tumours may also occur in the vagina and extrauterine sites including ovary, fallopian tube, and broad ligament, presumably arising from endometriosis {11293158}.

Clinical features

Patients with uterine adenosarcoma may present with abnormal uterine bleeding, an enlarged uterus, tissue protruding through the cervical os; a history indicating recurrent polyps is not uncommon. At other sites, patients may present with symptoms related to a pelvic mass.

Epidemiology

Uterine adenosarcoma is an infrequent malignancy that accounts for 5.5–9.0% of uterine sarcomas and about 1% of female genital tract neoplasms. Limited information on its epidemiology is available because of its very low incidence. A wide age range has been reported, but patients are most often postmenopausal [[Ashrafganjoei T, Talayeh M, Noei Teymoordash S, et al. Uterine Adenosarcoma: A Case Report and Review of Literature. Journal of Obstetrics, Gynecology and Cancer Research. 2021,7(1): 59–62. doi: 10.30699/jogcr.7.1.59]] {31139558}.

Etiology

This neoplasm is presumed to arise from neoplastic transformation of a Müllerian mesenchymal cell {26592504}, which subsequently stimulates reactive growth of the benign epithelial component.

Pathogenesis

This neoplasm lacks any highly recurrent genomic aberrations or specific chromosomal rearrangements as often found in other gynaecological sarcomas, but a wide variety of molecular alterations have been described in small percentages of adenosarcomas (see #55978Table 10.XXX). The pathogenesis is unknown, but some cases are associated with tamoxifen use {8811383}. In extrauterine sites, at least a subset arises from endometriosis {11293158}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours are typically solitary, exophytic, polypoid masses filling the endometrial cavity (mean size: ~50–60 mm) {2156771; 18941402}; they commonly prolapse through the cervical os. They may resemble typical endometrial polyps when small. In the ovary, they are usually unilateral and large with a solid and cystic or entirely solid cut surface, and polypoid fronds may be evident within the cysts {12360039}. Fallopian tube tumours may present as an intratubal polypoid mass. In large tumours, necrosis and haemorrhage are common, particularly in the setting of sarcomatous overgrowth {38688412}.

Histopathology

The tumour has broad leaf-like (referred to as phyllodes-like) growth pattern and/or dilated glands lined by benign endometrial or ciliated epithelium surrounded by a distinct cuff of neoplastic stroma, which is typically hypercellular relative to adjacent stromal tissue {2156771}. Stromal mitoses and atypia are generally present but are variable and may be inconspicuous. The sarcomatous component is most often of no specific type, but heterologous rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation may be present. The sarcomatous component may overgrow the epithelial component, referred to as sarcomatous overgrowth when pure sarcoma without epithelium accounts for at least 25% of the tumour volume. Sex cord differentiation may also be encountered but is not considered to represent a form of sarcomatous overgrowth. The sarcomatous overgrowth may be composed purely of low-grade sarcoma, but there may also be transformation to high-grade sarcoma, which is characterized by marked pleomorphism, brisk mitotic activity (often > 4.21 mitoses/mm2, equating to > 10 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter and 0.24 mm2 in area) and necrosis, and heterologous differentiation may be present. High-grade sarcoma may also occasionally be seen in the absence of sarcomatous overgrowth {28834809} and may be p53 mutation–type immunohistochemical expression. The epithelial component may display some cytological atypia and frequently has metaplastic changes. Immunohistochemically, adenosarcomas are often positive for CD10, ER, and PR, although these are often negative in sarcomatous overgrowth {18469708; 18941402}. Myogenin and MYOD1 can help confirm the presence of heterologous rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

These neoplasms lack the genomic aberrations and specific chromosomal rearrangements found in other gynaecological sarcomas {25231023}. Amplification of 8q13 and high-level copy-number gains of MYBL1 are seen in some cases, most often associated with sarcomatous overgrowth {26974998; 25231023; 32351899}. Other genetic alterations seen in adenosarcomas include DICER1 RNase IIIb domain mutations, PI3K/PTEN pathway alterations, NCOA2/3 fusions, and BAP1 deletion, with rare cases harbouring JAZF1::BCORL1 fusion {32011345; 36138078; 30266945}. High-grade adenosarcomas frequently harbour TP53 pathway alterations, high copy-number alterations, and ATRX loss {26592504; 36138078; 25231023; 11240741; 28834809}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: broad leaf-like (referred to as phyllodes-like) growth pattern and/or dilated glands lined by benign endometrial or ciliated epithelium; surrounded by a distinct cuff of neoplastic stroma, which is typically hypercellular relative to adjacent stromal tissue.

Desirable: stromal mitotic activity.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM and FIGO staging systems are used for this tumour. The staging of adenosarcoma differs from that of other uterine sarcomas; designation as pT1 is based on the presence and depth of myometrial invasion, not tumour size.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for uterine adenosarcoma is generally favourable compared with that of other gynaecological sarcomas. Sarcomatous overgrowth, deep myometrial invasion, and extrauterine involvement are adverse prognostic variables. High-grade atypia and aberrant p53 expression may also portend a worse outcome {28834809}. Adenosarcomas of the ovary are more aggressive than their uterine counterparts, with a propensity for surface involvement and rupture {12360039}. Similar to their uterine counterparts, their prognosis is worse with the presence of sarcomatous overgrowth and high-grade atypia.

Mutations

DICER1, TP53, ATRX, ARID1A

Fusions

ESR1::NCOA2, ESR1::NCOA3; JAZF1::BCORL1

Copy-number alterations

Amplification of 8q13 and high-level copy-number gains of MYBL1, MDM2/CDK4/HMGA2, CCNE1; BAP1 deletion, TERT promoter deletions; RB1 loss; high copy-number alterations broadly in high-grade tumours
