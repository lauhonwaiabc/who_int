9.3.0.6 Immature teratoma

Definition

Immature teratoma is a malignant germ cell tumour containing variable amounts of immature tissue in the form of primitive neuroepithelium.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most common site is the ovary. Immature teratomas of the uterine corpus are extremely rare, usually arise in older women; sometimes, but not always, those arising in association with an epithelial neoplasm are somatically derived {29106744; 28599587}. Occasional immature teratomas of the fallopian tube have been reported {10202679}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with symptoms related to a pelvic mass {992645}. Elevated serum AFP may occur (probably related to hepatocytic or intestinal-type elements), but when this is present it should prompt more extensive sampling to exclude a yolk sac tumour component.

Epidemiology

Ovarian immature teratomas account for about a third of all malignant germ cell tumours, with series reporting wide ranges from 3% to 50% of the cases {16648414; 38965563}. This is the second most common malignant ovarian germ cell tumour in most studies {16648414}, usually presenting within the first three decades of life {992645}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Immature teratomas may develop similarly to mature cystic teratoma via a common origin involving germ cells at the same developmental stage {16258281; 28664933}. They usually do not exhibit gain of 12p or isochromosome 12p unless they are part of a mixed germ cell tumour {9679978; 10850452; 16547466}. Molecular testing has revealed 2N near-diploid genomes, with extensive loss of heterozygosity and an absence of genes harbouring recurrent somatic mutations or known oncogenic variants {31911616}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours are usually unilateral, large, fleshy, greyish-tan, and solid-cystic, with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis {1260722}.

Histopathology

Variable amounts of immature tissues, mostly neuroectodermal tubules and rosettes, are admixed with ectodermal, mesodermal, and endodermal tissues of varying maturation. The neuroectodermal tubules and rosettes comprise cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, often with minimal cytoplasm, and usually exhibit obvious mitotic activity. However, the absence of frequent mitotic figures does not rule out that the tissue is immature neuroectoderm. Cellular, mitotically active glial tissue as well as immature mesodermal and (less frequently) endodermal components may be present; however, their presence alone is insufficient for the diagnosis of an immature teratoma. Pure immature teratoma should not contain foci of yolk sac tumour or other malignant germ cell tumour components. If these components are present regardless of size or amount, the tumour should be classified as a mixed germ cell tumour (see section Malignant mixed germ cell tumour). In some immature teratomas, a trabecular or nested proliferation of thin-walled blood vessels is present, mimicking a vascular neoplasm {11781518; 12068181}.

Embryonic-type neuroectodermal tumours (see << 10.1.9.9>>CNS-type tumours) may arise in immature teratomas. Additionally, in some cases of grade 3 immature teratoma, the presence of a diffuse proliferation of immature neuroectodermal elements may mimic CNS-type tumour, and the distinction may be difficult. The presence of clonal expansile growth of immature cellular neuroglial tissue that occupies at least one low-power field (40×/4× objective or 4.5 mm in diameter at minimum) without any other intervening teratomatous or non-teratomatous germ cell elements would support CNS-type tumour over grade 3 immature teratoma {35863107}.

SALL4 can be positive in immature neuroectodermal and intestinal elements {19295406; 23240671}. Glypican-3 can be positive in neuroectoderm. SOX2 is positive in primitive neuroepithelium but can also be expressed in fetal type glandular epithelium of endodermal origin. AFP may stain immature gastrointestinal-type glands.

Grading is based on the number of low-power microscopic fields (diameter: 4.5 mm) containing aggregated amounts of immature neuroepithelium in any one slide {1260722; 7814189} (see #53652Table 9.XXX); i.e. separate foci of immature neuroepithelium on a single slide are aggregated together to come up with a single measurement. Involvement of a low-power field corresponds to the aggregated length spanning the low-power field. Metastatic sites of immature teratoma are graded using the same criteria; pure gliomatosis peritonei (positive with GFAP) is considered mature (grade 0).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: the presence of tubules, rosettes, and/or sheets of primitive neuroepithelial cells.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour stage and grade of the primary tumour and grade of the extraovarian tumour (if present) are the main prognostic factors. The 5-year overall survival rate is > 90%. For stage I tumours, the 5-year survival rate approaches 100% {27423379; 21764111; 27222024; 7522313}. Current evidence indicates that gliomatosis peritonei is a benign lesion that arises in the peritoneum presumably from pluripotent Müllerian stem cells and not from the ovarian teratoma {5521737; 26564007}; some cases of gliomatosis peritonei occur in patients with benign ovarian tumours (especially mature teratomas) or even with no ovarian tumour {10726696}. Although currently there is controversy with respect to the role of adjuvant chemotherapy for advanced-stage disease even with immature elements, gliomatosis particularly is not treated after surgery {22820986; 38544795}. Therefore, extraovarian disease must be adequately sampled by the surgeon and the pathologist to exclude immature elements and another malignant germ cell tumour component. The current recommendation from the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) is that gliomatosis peritonei should be staged when associated with an ovarian immature teratoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]; however, there is no outcome-based evidence to support this guidance, and overtreatment should be avoided. In the absence of such evidence, many clinicians and pathologists discount the presence of pure gliomatosis peritonei and upstage the ovarian tumour only if the implants contain immature elements (i.e. grade 1 or higher). Whichever approach is taken, it is essential that the nature of the implants be carefully documented to allow the clinician to make the appropriate therapeutic decision. After chemotherapy, rare patients may develop extraovarian deposits of pure mature teratomatous tissue, the so-called growing teratoma syndrome {27258729; 26262454; 18043048}, which is typically surgically managed. Immature teratomas of the uterine corpus and fallopian tube are so rare that prognostic features are not known.
