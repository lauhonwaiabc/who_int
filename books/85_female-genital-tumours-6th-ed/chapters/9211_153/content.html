9.2.1.1 Ovarian fibroma

Definition

Ovarian fibroma is a benign pure stromal tumour composed of fibroblastic cells within a variably collagenous stroma.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: fibrothecoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Most ovarian fibromas are unilateral, but occasional cases are bilateral, most commonly in young patients with naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) {3547011}. Patients may present with symptoms attributable to an ovarian mass or occasionally with hormonal manifestations, usually estrogenic; Meigs syndrome (ascites and pleural effusion) occurs in a small proportion of cases.

Epidemiology

Fibroma is the most common ovarian stromal tumour, accounting for 4% of all ovarian neoplasms in clinicopathological series. Fibroma may occur at any age; it most frequently arises in middle age (average patient age: 48 years) and less commonly before the age of 30 years {7260859}. Fibromas occur in approximately 75% of female patients with naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (PTCH1) {3547011}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Trisomy and/or tetrasomy 12 is often found, and rarely IDH1 mutation is found {11852357; 23347143}. In cellular fibromas, loss of heterozygosity at 9q22.3 (PTCH1) and 19p13.3 (STK11) are frequent somatic mutations {16084949}. FOXL2 and DICER1 mutations are absent {23774170}.

Macroscopic appearance

The ovarian capsule is usually smooth, although sometimes capsular disruption and/or surface adhesions are present. The cut surface typically is hard, chalky, white or yellowish-white; cellular tumours may be tan and soft. Areas of oedema, cystic degeneration, haemorrhage, or necrosis may be present. Fibromas associated with naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome are often multinodular and calcified.

Histopathology

Fibromas are composed of intersecting fascicles of cells with bland, spindle to ovoid nuclei and scant cytoplasm within a variably collagenous stroma, sometimes including hyalinized plaques {7260859}. Calcification may be present, and rare tumours contain eosinophilic hyaline globules {19483628} or melanin pigment {29140884}. Mitoses are uncommon in most cases.

Approximately 10% of fibromas are densely cellular; when only mild nuclear atypia is present, such cases are referred to as cellular fibromas.

If cellular fibromas exhibit mitotic activity of more than 4/10 HPF without coagulative tumour cell necrosis and diffuse significant cytological atypia, they are referred to as mitotically active cellular fibroma {16861962}. Haemorrhage and infarct-type necrosis may occur and should not be misinterpreted as coagulative tumour cell necrosis.

Fibroma with minor sex cord elements contain sex cord components in various architectural patterns, which account for < 10% of the overall tumour. Rarely, luteinized cells can be seen.

The term “fibrothecoma” is not recommended, as careful application of the clinical, macroscopic, and microscopic findings distinguishes fibroma and thecoma from each other.

Reticulin stain demonstrates a completely intact pericellular pattern.

Fibromas are typically immunoreactive for sex cord markers such as inhibin, calretinin, FOXL2, CD56 and SF1; positive staining with these markers is often focal and/or weak {9688187; 12808064}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Absence of FOXL2 mutation may be helpful in diagnostically challenging cases where an adult granulosa cell tumour is in the differential.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: neoplasm composed of fascicles of bland fibromatous spindle cells in a variably collagenous stroma.

Desirable: pericellular reticulin is helpful in cases of cellular fibroma.

Staging

These are benign tumours and are not staged.

Prognosis and prediction

These are benign tumours, but a small proportion of cases (most commonly cellular fibromas) are associated with ovarian surface rupture and extraovarian adhesions. Such tumours are at risk of local recurrence, often after a long interval {7260859; 16861962}.
