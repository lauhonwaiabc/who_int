4.4.0.1 Carcinosarcoma

Definition

Carcinosarcoma is a malignant neoplasm showing mixed epithelial and mesenchymal differentiation resulting in a biphasic morphology.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: malignant mesodermal mixed tumour; malignant mixed Müllerian tumour; metaplastic carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Uterine cervix; can also arise in the vagina.

Clinical features

Cervical and vaginal carcinosarcomas most commonly present with abnormal vaginal bleeding or discharge that leads to the discovery of a cervical or vaginal mass {9656116; 11224604; 27113789; 21376478; 17640554; 25272303}. Sometimes a mass is seen protruding through the vagina, and some patients present with an abnormal cervical cytology sample {9656116; 11224604; 27113789; 21376478; 17640554; 25272303}. Because of the rarity of these tumours, secondary involvement from other primary sites, especially the uterine corpus, should always be excluded. Transvaginal prolapse of a uterine tumour may be misinterpreted as originating from the vagina {35377042}.

Epidemiology

Primary cervical, as well as vaginal, carcinosarcomas are rare and much less frequent than tumours arising in the uterine corpus or ovary. They occur mostly in postmenopausal women {35153524; 36769835}.

Etiology

A subset of tumours originating in the uterine cervix or vagina is associated with high-risk HPV infection {11224604; 17640554}. Occasional vaginal cases have been associated with prior pelvic irradiation {2984620}.

Pathogenesis

The most widely accepted theory for the pathogenesis is that these tumours represent carcinomas that undergo mesenchymal transformation to form sarcomatous areas; the epithelial and mesenchymal components are clonally related {17640554}. Therefore, similar to endometrial carcinosarcoma, this tumour is currently regarded as a type of epithelial neoplasm rather than a true mixed epithelial and mesenchymal tumour.

Macroscopic appearance

Carcinosarcomas of the uterine cervix and vagina usually comprise a large fleshy polypoid mass, sometimes with necrosis and haemorrhage. Cervical tumours are often seen protruding from the cervical os, and vaginal tumours may protrude through the vaginal introitus.

Histopathology

The carcinomatous component can comprise any type of primary cervical or vaginal epithelial malignancy (squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, adenoid basal carcinoma, mesonephric adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma). The mesenchymal component is often homologous but can occasionally show heterologous differentiation (rhabdomyosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma) {23533892; 25272303}. The epithelial and mesenchymal components are typically well demarcated, and a gradual transition should raise the possibility of spindle cell carcinoma or carcinoma with a spindle cell component. The immunophenotype of the epithelial component mirrors that of the corresponding pure carcinoma; for example, HPV-associated neoplasms exhibit diffuse block-type staining with p16. The mesenchymal component is usually negative for epithelial markers. Heterologous rhabdomyosarcomatous components are usually desmin, myogenin, and/or MYOD1-positive, chondrosarcomatous components are S100-positive, and osteosarcomatous components are SATB2-positive {27294605}.

Cytology

Carcinosarcoma can be recognized by the presence of a malignant epithelial component and a sarcomatous component. The sarcomatous component is absent in cervical cytology preparations in a significant number of cases {1312925}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an admixture of distinct carcinomatous and sarcomatous components; spindle cell carcinoma or carcinoma with a spindle cell component, as well as secondary involvement from another primary site, should be excluded.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is poor, with 2-year and 5-year overall survival rates of 60% and 32%, respectively, for cervical carcinosarcoma {28708788; 31912680}. Tumour stage is the single most important prognostic indicator, and the significance of heterologous elements remains undetermined. Cervical carcinosarcomas usually present at a lower stage (approximately 50% of cervical carcinosarcomas are FIGO stage IB at diagnosis), compared with those of the uterine corpus {9656116; 28708788; 36769835}.

Most vaginal carcinosarcomas present at FIGO stage I or II, although advanced-stage presentation can also occur {17640554; 27113789}. The prognosis for vaginal carcinosarcoma is also poor, with frequent early recurrence and metastasis {2984620}. Recurrence tends to be local (pelvic), although distant metastases may also occur {27113789}.
