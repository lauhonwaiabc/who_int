2.2.1.4 Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), HPV-associated, is a malignant squamous tumour, with stromal invasion caused by persistent HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

External female genitalia, including labia majora, labia minora, vestibule, clitoris, vaginal introitus, perineum, and urethral meatus

Clinical features

Most tumours present as an asymptomatic mass or ulcer, but some patients experience pruritus, a burning sensation, pain, or bleeding, especially in advanced disease.

Epidemiology

In 2022, vulvar cancer (irrespective of HPV status) was responsible for < 1% of all female cancer cases worldwide, with an estimated 474 000 new cases that year {38572751}. The proportion of vulvar cancers associated with HPV infection is 25% globally {31862245}, but it can be as high as 95% in some countries {36214794}. Peak incidence is in the seventh decade of life {27387669; 28815579}, but this tumour can also occur in young people {28815579}. In some high-income countries there is an increasing incidence among women aged < 60 years, which may be due to more frequent oncogenic HPV infections {28730615}. In the future, broader-coverage HPV vaccination may counter a rising burden from these cancers {31718338}, but such an effect may be observed only after a few decades. Women living with HIV, especially those with severe immunosuppression, are at high risk of vulvar high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia and cancer {39652424}. Women with other causes of compromised immune status as well as cigarette smokers are epidemiologically associated with an increased risk of HPV-associated SCC {28590952}.

Etiology

This tumour is caused by HPV infection, especially with high-risk types, with HPV16 being involved in > 70% of all HPV-associated vulvar tumours {23886586}. Exceptionally, low-risk HPV genotypes such as HPV6 and HPV11 have been identified as the sole cause of vulvar SCC {23886586; 24076770; 39886739}.

Pathogenesis

The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 lead to degradation of p53 and RB1 proteins, which in turn results in overexpression of p16. Therefore, immunohistochemistry typically shows strong and diffuse (block-type) p16 staining and a wildtype pattern for p53 staining {28815579; 28590952}. Only rarely are TP53 mutations identified in HPV-positive vulvar SCC {28899974}. See section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours frequently present as exophytic lesions. Multifocality is common. Most of these lesions arise from HPV-associated precursor lesions (high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions) {36794761}.

Histopathology

The tumour is composed of infiltrating islands of malignant squamous cells or extensive, complex, exophytic expansile growth. Typically, HPV-associated vulvar SCC is moderately to poorly differentiated. The tumours may show several histological patterns. Basaloid tumours are composed of basaloid cohesive nests of immature basal-type squamous cells that may resemble those in high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions. Warty (condylomatous) tumours have conspicuous superficial cell atypia and koilocyte-like changes {8380681}. Basaloid and condylomatous patterns can be mixed and may also show keratinization. One third of HPV-associated SCCs are keratinizing {28815579}. There is considerable histomorphological overlap between HPV-associated and HPV-independent vulvar SCCs; therefore, this distinction cannot be made on H&E alone {28815579}.

The identification of stromal invasion may be challenging in superficially invasive tumours and in biopsies {24076774}. Irregularly shaped nests, nests disconnected from the surface, loss of polarity, cytoplasmic eosinophilia of the invading cells, and the presence of desmoplasia favour invasion.

Ideally, the presence of HPV should be confirmed using validated molecular or immunohistochemical approaches with adequate sensitivity and controls. Diffuse, continuous block-type p16 staining is an acceptable surrogate marker for the presence of HPV with high interobserver agreement and is an important criterion for this diagnosis {28815579; 31771962; 39641228}. Adjacent high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion is often present.

Grading systems, based on either nuclear pleomorphism or differentiation, do not correlate with behaviour, and the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) recommends not to grade these neoplasms {36305532}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HPV detection and genotyping using PCR-based techniques or in situ hybridization allows identifying the presence of HPV. Other molecular tests are not clinically relevant.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma with squamous differentiation; p16 block-type immunoreactivity and/or positive molecular testing for HPV.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis in early-stage disease is good, with a reported 5-year survival rate of 80–90% {29090456; 30256833}. Stage and lymph node status are the most important prognostic factors for clinical outcome. Lymph node involvement decreases survival significantly. Depth of invasion is associated with lymph node involvement and is therefore included in clinical decision trees for groin surgery. HPV-associated vulvar SCCs are associated with better progression-free survival than are HPV-independent tumours, and they also have increased radiosensitivity {27864932; 32972785; 31771962; 37843640}.

Studies of prognostic factors in vulvar SCC have not consistently subclassified tumours as HPV-associated or HPV-independent. This makes it impossible to know what the risk factors for recurrence are specifically in HPV-associated vulvar SCC, which in most populations of high-income countries are less common than HPV-I SCC. This limitation notwithstanding, factors associated with an increased likelihood of recurrence include non-radical resection or tumour-free margins < 3 mm {31285094; 27497345; 27837710}, tumour size, depth of invasion, and the presence of lymphovascular and perineural invasion {37843640}.
