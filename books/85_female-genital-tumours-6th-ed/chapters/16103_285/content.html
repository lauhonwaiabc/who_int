16.1.0.3 Metastases to the peritoneum

Definition

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Metastatic carcinomas and sarcomas in the peritoneum are secondary malignant tumour localizations on the peritoneal lining of the abdominal cavity, with the histopathological appearances and neoplastic growth properties varying depending on the primary tumour type and site.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei (MCP) refers to deposits of mucinous tumour within the peritoneal cavity, primarily arising secondary to mucin-producing epithelial neoplasm mostly of appendiceal origin. This condition was formerly called pseudomyxoma peritonei, which is a clinical syndrome, but this term is no longer recommended.

Gliomatosis

Gliomatosis is characterized by the presence of mature glial tissue in the peritoneum.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

All sites of the peritoneal cavity (including the omentum and viscera)

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

All sites of the peritoneal cavity can be affected, including the omentum and ovary {21159590}. The mucinous tumour deposits may accumulate in dependent areas in the pelvic cavity due to gravity, or at sites where ascitic fluid is resorbed, such as the omentum and the right hemidiaphragm, a pattern known as the redistribution phenomenon {21159590}.

Gliomatosis

Peritoneum, omentum, lymph nodes {24421855}

Clinical features

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

The clinical features are often nonspecific, including abdominal discomfort, symptoms of intestinal obstruction, ascites, and weight loss {19325052}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

There can be symptoms of appendicitis, increasing abdominal girth, abdominal pain, voluminous mucinous ascites, or mucin within a hernia sac. Presentation may be with rapidly enlarging ovarian masses due to the involvement of the ovaries. Lymphatic and/or haematogenous metastasis is very rare, and metastasis beyond the peritoneal cavity are only rarely seen even in high-grade cases {28540829; 21159590}.

Gliomatosis

Patients usually have symptoms related to an ovarian teratoma. However, gliomatosis can cause abdominal/pelvic pain due to the formation of masses, or abnormalities may be seen on imaging studies when gliomatosis occurs as recurrent disease after adjuvant chemotherapy for ovarian immature teratomas, without elevation of serum tumour markers. In these circumstances, gliomatosis becomes part of the so-called growing teratoma syndrome {26564007}.

Epidemiology

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Peritoneal metastases are found in 50–80% of patients with advanced ovarian, colorectal, pancreatic, and gastric cancers {16432354; 24222942; 25891650; 28956065}. Peritoneal sarcomatosis is particularly associated with recurrence of intra-abdominal sarcomas, which represent about one third of all soft tissue sarcomas {30603133}. It is more common in children than in adults {25564159}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei affects women somewhat more often than men, usually in the fourth to seventh decades of life {28746986}.

Gliomatosis

Gliomatosis is rare. It occurs over a wide age range but is more common in young patients. It can be associated with immature teratomas, mature cystic teratomas, and mixed germ cell tumours {26564007}.

Etiology

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Metastatic cancers to the peritoneum are mainly derived from abdominopelvic organs such as the ovary, fallopian tube, uterus, colorectum, pancreas, biliary tract, and stomach {28956065; 30306591; 30503267; 30131104}, as well as from the retroperitoneum, and less commonly from the breast, lung, liver, and uterine cervix {24222942; 26457350; 28956065; 29233547; 30681378; 30589039; 23638212} or the extremities {9040804}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Most cases are associated with low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm. Extra-appendiceal primary sites include the colon, urachus, ovarian teratomas, or teratoma-associated mucinous borderline tumours, pancreas, and gallbladder {28449606; 23785561; 30531244; 31082160; 32904568}.

Gliomatosis

Proposed theories include maturation of implanted ovarian teratomatous elements and metaplasia of pluripotent Müllerin stem cells or supraperitoneal mesenchymal cells {15188134; 11438453}. Actually, gliomatosis peritonei can be identified very rarely in cases of mature teratomas or in the absence of ovarian tumour {10726696}. Such lesions represent peritoneal lesion associated with ventriculoperitoneal shunts {10726696; 2648802}.

Pathogenesis

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Peritoneum is a common site of metastasis due to its extensive area and the presence and movement of the peritoneal fluid {8849944; 17334873; 28956065}. Cancer cells can disseminate to the peritoneum by direct intraperitoneal spread in peritoneal fluid or via a lymphovascular route {17334873}. They adhere to mesothelium, permeate through mesothelial epithelium and stroma, and proliferate in a favourable tumour microenvironment, which involves a close interaction between tumour cells, mesothelial cells, macrophages, adipocytes, fibroblasts/myofibroblasts, as well as angiogenesis {29164988; 30503267; 32963283; 32439934; 36482201; 38575576}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

The genetic alterations in MCP reflect the primary organ. Most of the data relate to appendiceal tumours that have spread to the peritoneum {28746986}. The vast majority of low-grade tumours have KRAS mutations, and most have GNAS mutations, which lead to constitutive activation of the PKA pathway {24944587; 25274248; 26475379; 36723696}. High-grade tumours often have mutations similar to those in low-grade tumours, but with greater frequency and a higher tumour mutation burden. They often have additional mutations in other genes, including TP53, SMAD4, MYC, DAXX, and MYB {30962504; 24618609}. A notable exception is GNAS mutations, which are less common in high-grade tumours, suggesting that high-grade tumours do not necessarily arise from low-grade tumours {24944587}. Additional low frequency mutations can be identified in PIK3CA (4%), ATM (4%), BRAF (2%), PTEN (2%), and CDKN2A (1%) {37314541; 25274248}. Mismatch repair deficiency / microsatellite instability has not been identified in mucinous carcinoma peritonei {30692096; 36493333; 25274248}.

Gliomatosis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Peritoneal carcinomatosis is characterized by small, often superficial, whitish tumour deposits {29164988}. Peritoneal sarcomatosis forms discrete masses of variable size or diffuse thickening of the peritoneal surface.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Mucinous globules of tumour usually range in consistency from watery mucus to more solid mucinous masses, depending on the degree of fibrosis and tumour cellularity. As the mucinous tumour progresses, it tends to encase abdominal viscera and is often associated with large amounts of mucinous ascites. Extensive omental infiltration (omental cake) is common, and the ovaries can be transformed into mucinous masses.

Gliomatosis

It typically occurs as miliary lesions, measuring a few millimetres in size; however, larger lesions can be encountered.

Histopathology

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

The histopathology is the same as that of the primary malignancy and includes a broad variety of carcinomas and sarcomas. Peritoneal carcinomatosis of ovarian origin is most commonly of serous type {20407318}. Among cervical cancers, the unusual tendency of gastric-type adenocarcinoma for peritoneal and omental dissemination has been noted {26457350; 29233547}. In cases with dominant mucinous morphology and scant neoplastic cellularity, the differential diagnosis of MCP should be considered.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Since pseudomyxoma is merely a clinical term, and is a heterogeneous condition, microscopic features in each case should be interpreted in a context of clinicopathological correlations, combined with imaging studies, cytology, intraoperative findings, and so forth. In addition to abundant mucin, presence or absence of neoplastic mucin-containing cells and degree of cytological atypia should be focused, and mucinous ascites due to the rupture of the primary tumour and its organized form, called organized mucin, should be distinguished from MCP, including low- and high-grade lesions due to dissemination of neoplastic cells, which vary in degrees of tumour cellularity. Typically, accumulation of mucin pool with compartmentalization due to formation of fibrous septa, containing epithelial components, extends deeply in the subserosal adipose or fibroadipose tissue. If a peritoneal mucinous tumour arises from another organ, such as colon or stomach, then nomenclature and grading appropriate for that organ should be utilized (e.g., metastatic mucinous adenocarcinoma of the stomach, moderately differentiated). If the tumour is of unknown primary (for example, if only a biopsy is received) or morphologically compatible with an appendiceal primary, histological grading of the tumour using the criteria for MCP is appropriate. The histological grading is based on tumour cellularity, cytological and architectural atypia, and the presence of signet-ring cells (#55051Table 16.XXX) {24633196; 26492181}, and it is recommended to also examine the appendix (#56147Table 16.XXX). Most cases of MCP are grade 1, and to avoid overgrading, pathologists should exercise restraint when features of a higher-grade tumour are limited and focal. To classify as grade 2 MCP, high-grade cytological and architectural features should be present in at least 10% of the neoplastic epithelium. A diagnosis of grade 3 relies on the presence of signet-ring cells, which should comprise at least 10% of the neoplastic epithelium {24814804}. Signet-ring cells may be more prognostically important when they infiltrate tissue rather than only float in mucin {24814804}. True signet-ring cells must be distinguished from pseudo–signet-ring cells, representing degenerated tumour cells floating in mucin (usually a focal finding). Rarely, grade 3 tumours have other growth patterns, such as sheets of tumour cells. A peritoneal regression grading score (PRGS) should be given when there has been prior therapy (#56419Table 16.XXX) {30911613}.

Gliomatosis

There are discrete nodules of mature glial tissue that are occasionally associated with inflammation, haemorrhage, and fibrosis. Infrequently, gliomatosis is associated with endometriosis {24421855}. Immunohistochemistry shows the nodules to be positive for GFAP and SOX2 and variably positive for S100 {26564007}.

Cytology

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Cytological features reflect those of the primary neoplasm. Immunohistochemistry on cell block sections may help in ascertaining the site of origin. Sarcomas show a wide variety of cytological features depending on the type {23858317}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

There is mucin with varying numbers of atypical mucin-containing cells, singly and in 3D clusters. The use of “pseudomyxoma peritonei” as a diagnostic term is not recommended, and mucinous background alone without epithelial cells should be interpreted as negative. There are limited data on grading of MCP in cytological specimens. For this reason, grading in cytological specimens is not recommended.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Essential: intraperitoneal deposits of cytologically malignant neoplastic epithelial cells, showing infiltrative growth, with or without extracellular mucin; or sarcoma cells; clinical correlation.

Desirable: immunohistochemical profile consistent with primary site of origin (see #56407Table 16.XXX).

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Essential: intra-abdominal mucinous deposits composed of > 50% extracellular mucin with presence of neoplastic glandular epithelial cells.

Desirable: identification of the primary site of origin – usually the vermiform appendix; very occasionally the urachus, an ovarian teratoma, or unknown.

Gliomatosis

Essential: mature glial cell aggregates; absence of other teratomatous elements, particularly immature component.

Staging

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum are staged according to the FIGO and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging systems as appropriate to the primary origin of the tumour. In general, peritoneal metastases are pM1. Intraperitoneal metastases from appendiceal tumours (all types, not restricted to pseudomyxoma peritonei) are staged as pM1a. For colorectal cancer, peritoneal metastases are staged as pM1b.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Staging is according to the primary site of the neoplasm. For appendiceal primaries, the stage of MCP depends on the presence or absence of mucinous epithelium within the mucinous deposits (#55052Table 16.XXX). Acellular mucin is considered pM1a. Involvement of the peritoneal surface of abdominal organs such as the liver, spleen, and intestines with mucinous tumour deposits containing mucinous epithelial cells is classified as pM1b. Ovarian involvement, omental infiltration, and infiltration of an organ beneath serosal tumour deposits are also classified as pM1b. Stage pM1c is reserved for non-peritoneal metastases, such as parenchymal liver, pulmonary, or pleural metastases.

Gliomatosis

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Carcinomas and sarcomas metastatic to the peritoneum

The prognosis depends on the features of the primary cancer and may be influenced by local complications such as bowel obstruction and haemorrhage. The prognosis is poor irrespective of primary or recurrent disease and multimodal therapeutic approach {25564159; 28967566; 30603133}.

Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

Prognostic factors include the cellularity of the peritoneal mucin, histological tumour grade, and whether complete cytoreduction can be achieved. Patients with low-grade tumours and complete cytoreduction have a median survival time of 59–170 months, whereas patients with grade 2 and 3 tumours have median survival times of 45–59 months and 18.9–26 months, respectively {30017331; 31090036; 26597367; 30523470; 39435876}. One study {36493333} identified four prognostic molecular subtypes of appendiceal mucinous adenocarcinoma with peritoneal metastasis with RAS-mutated / GNAS wild type / TP53 wild type tumours having the most favourable outcomes and with TP53-mutated tumours having a worse prognosis {30458197; 36493333; 37314541}. However, not all studies have demonstrated the association of KRAS mutation with improved prognosis in MCP {39435876}. Adenocarcinomas lacking RAS, GNAS, and TP53 mutations (triple negative) had a worse prognosis {36493333}.

Gliomatosis

Gliomatosis has a favourable outcome {25594144; 26033179}. However, morbidity related to the expansive and compressive nature of enlarged glial masses {28656118} and rare malignant transformation, usually to high-grade gliomas, have been documented {2048608; 9195578}.
