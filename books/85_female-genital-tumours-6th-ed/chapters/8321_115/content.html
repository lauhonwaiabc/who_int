8.3.2.1 Mucinous borderline tumour

Definition

Mucinous borderline tumour (MBT) of ovary is an architecturally complex non-invasive mucinous neoplasm, with gastrointestinal-type epithelial differentiation.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: atypical proliferative mucinous tumour; mucinous tumour of low malignant potential.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours are localized in the ovary and may also arise in the retroperitoneum {19092632; 31488447}.

Clinical features

Patients most often present with symptoms related to an abdominopelvic mass {8600070; 10366144; 11075847; 11812936; 17414101; 21464732}.

Epidemiology

These are the second most common type of borderline tumour in North America and Europe, accounting for 30–50% of such tumours; but they are the most common subtype in Asia, where they account for about 70% of borderline tumours {21464732; 23346313}. MBTs occur across a wide age range, including paediatric patients (mean patient age: 45 years) {8600070; 10366144; 11075847; 17414101; 21464732; 23388124; 39323179}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

These tumours may arise from mucinous cystadenomas and have similar associations with mature cystic teratoma and Brenner tumours {31695154}. KRAS mutations are present in 30–75% of the tumours {8384077; 9118042; 8261457; 11444200}. The frequency of TP53 mutations is 19–35% in MBT, which is less than a half of that of mucinous carcinomas {32724153; 37500307}.

Macroscopic appearance

The mean tumour size is about 200 mm, with some cases as large as 500 mm {18162780}. The tumours are nearly always unilateral. They have a smooth external surface and are multiloculated, with smooth walls and mucinous contents; solid areas may be seen and should be preferentially sampled, because these may represent areas of invasion or mural nodules {8600070; 10366144; 11075847; 11812936; 17414101; 21464732}. It is recommended to sample one section per 10 mm of tumour for tumours < 100 mm and two sections per 10 mm for tumours ≥ 100 mm {15297960}. The presence of mucin, nodularity, or irregularity on external surface should prompt thorough sampling and raises concern for metastasis to the ovary.

Histopathology

These tumours contain multiple cysts lined by gastrointestinal-type mucinous epithelium showing variable degrees of nuclear stratification and cellular proliferation with tufting and villous or slender filiform papillae. Marked architectural complexity with confluent, sometimes cribriform, glands and/or papillae with little or absent intervening stroma warrants a diagnosis of mucinous carcinoma with confluent/expansile invasion (see section Mucinous carcinoma). However, care should be taken to avoid misinterpretation of tangentially sectioned crowded epithelium in MBT as confluent/expansile invasion {39523705}. The epithelium may resemble gastric pyloric epithelium or may contain goblet cells and (more rarely) Paneth cells. There is generally low-grade nuclear atypia. Mitotic activity is present predominantly in crypts and is less prominent on the luminal surface. A component of benign mucinous cystadenoma is often present. Epithelial proliferation must account for > 10% of the epithelial volume for a tumour to qualify as MBT; otherwise, the tumour should be classified as mucinous cystadenoma with focal epithelial proliferation {15297960}. The presence of focal or patchy marked cytological atypia, often associated with mitotic activity, warrants a diagnosis of intraepithelial carcinoma in these tumours {15297962; 15626914; 20737216; 21464732}. In the presence of diffuse marked cytological atypia, a diagnosis of mucinous carcinoma (primary or metastatic) should be considered. The presence of mucin or neoplastic epithelium on the external surface necessitates thorough exclusion of metastasis to the ovary. If metastasis is excluded, these findings may be evidence of preoperative rupture, particularly when associated with organizing or granulation tissue.

Single cells or small groups of cells may be present in ovarian stroma adjacent to glands. These were historically considered to represent microinvasion provided they (1) were unassociated with gland rupture; (2) did not show marked cytological atypia; and (3) measured < 5 mm in greatest linear extent without aggregating measurements across different foci {8666359; 9850171; 10366144; 11812936; 12090592; 21464732; 11075847}. This is a clinically insignificant finding in an extensively sampled specimen, and, therefore, the term “microinvasion” is not recommended. By contrast, marked cytological atypia in tumour cells within the stroma, regardless of the size of the focus, should prompt consideration of a diagnosis of invasive carcinoma. The size of invasive foci should be recorded in the pathology report.

Stromal luteinization, focal mucin extravasation, and mucin granulomas may be seen. Some tumours are associated with a Brenner tumour or a mature cystic teratoma. When associated or coexisting with teratoma, mucinous tumours should be classified as somatic tumours arising from teratomas (see section Mature teratoma (including somatic neoplasms arising from teratoma) {33963606}, after clinical exclusion of secondary involvement from an intestinal/appendiceal primary. These mucinous tumours arising from teratomas are more likely to be associated with pseudomyxoma peritonei {17527072; 18344868}.

Mural nodules are of two types: reactive or malignant {498013; 1563909; 12409723; 18300813; 32759977}. Rarely, nodules with mixed features are found. Mural nodules composed of carcinoma and/or sarcoma should be classified as carcinoma or sarcoma arising in MBT, rather than as MBT with a malignant component. This applies also to lesions that are found only microscopically. Reactive nodules are composed of a variable mixture of spindled/round mononucleated cells and multinucleated epulis-like giant cells, often associated with marked inflammation; variable mitotic activity may be present {12409723}. Anaplastic carcinoma shows a diffuse growth of cells with a variety of morphologies, including rhabdoid, spindle, and pleomorphic. Sarcomas are rare and usually display no specific line of differentiation, although rhabdomyosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma have been described {8112957}. Anaplastic carcinomas are usually positive for broad-spectrum cytokeratin and EMA, although staining may be focal. Reactive nodules are CD68-positive and typically negative for epithelial markers {18300813}. A recent molecular study suggests that most mural nodules are clonally related to MBT {32759977}.

MBT has the same immunohistochemical profile as mucinous carcinoma (see section Mucinous carcinoma). A mutation-type p53 pattern is observed in 20–33% of MBT compared with 61–85% in mucinous carcinoma {37406453; 37500307; 39523705} and is more frequently observed in the subset of MBT with intraepithelial carcinoma {37406453}. Currently, there is no molecular test or an immunomarker with sufficient discriminatory power in the distinction between MBT and mucinous carcinoma {33009577; 36222710; 36788074}. Metastatic tumours from the gastrointestinal tract (particularly low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm), pancreatobiliary system, and uterine cervix may exhibit a non-infiltrative growth pattern and a low-grade benign-like component, which closely mimics MBT (see section Metastasis to the ovary) {18813124; 21263249; 24300528}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: mucinous neoplasm with gastrointestinal-type differentiation and variable degrees of epithelial stratification, tufting, and villous or slender filiform papillae, in at least 10% of the tumour; low-grade nuclear atypia (except in intraepithelial carcinoma); absence of confluent/expansile growth or infiltrative/destructive stromal invasion.

Staging

MBT is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for these tumours is excellent because they are stage I at diagnosis. Recurrences have been reported after cystectomy or adhesions and can be seen as borderline or carcinoma; the latter is uncommon and, in most instances, probably explained by undersampling of the carcinomatous component, which is more likely in cases with a limited number of tumour sections {20173495; 24487471; 33963606}. The overall survival rate for women with MBTs with intraepithelial carcinoma is 95–100% {11075847}. The MBT-related death rate is < 5%, with adverse behaviour restricted to FIGO stage ≥ IC tumours {17414101; 21464732; 21836488; 39323179}. FIGO stage ≥ IC MBT is exceptional and should be reserved for the exceedingly rare and very well sampled primary ovarian tumour that was ruptured preoperatively.

There are insufficient data regarding the behaviour of focal carcinoma; however, rare patients with stage I tumours have been reported to have tumour recurrences and have died of disease {11013369; 21464732; 37406453; 39323179}. Anaplastic carcinoma often exhibits aggressive behaviour; it has been suggested that stage IA tumours may have a better prognosis, but data are limited {18300813; 32759977}.
