9.3.0.5 Malignant mixed germ cell tumour

Definition

Malignant mixed germ cell tumour is a tumour composed of two or more malignant germ cell components.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Abdominal pain, distension, and menstrual irregularities are the most common presenting symptoms. Less frequently, patients exhibit endocrine manifestations such as androgenic signs or features of precocious pseudopuberty {33284192}. Elevated serum tumour markers, including LDH, β-hCG, and AFP, are frequently observed and correspond to the specific histological components of the tumour.

Epidemiology

These account for 10–20% of all ovarian malignant germ cell tumours. They occur mainly in children and young women, typically spanning the ages of 3–55 years. These tumours are rare in the first 5 years of life and in women older than 35 years {33284192}. Rare cases occur in patients with abnormal karyotypes, sometimes arising in a gonadoblastoma {185553; 7814189; 16547466; 28372870; 29183227}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Many tumours exhibit a gain of 12p or isochromosome 12p {16547466}. Some tumours arise in a gonadoblastoma in patients with a normal or abnormal karyotype {29183227}. Amplifications of PIK3CA or AKT1 genes have been identified in a significant percentage of yolk sac tumours and mixed germ cell tumours containing yolk sac components. These alterations indicate activation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, which may contribute to tumorigenesis and could serve as potential therapeutic targets {30170975}.

Macroscopic appearance

These are mainly unilateral, typically large tumours, averaging 150 mm, with solid and cystic areas. The gross appearance depends on the elements that are present. Dysgerminoma is tan and fleshy. Yolk sac tumour areas typically contain small cysts and foci of necrosis. A choriocarcinoma component is haemorrhagic and necrotic, and embryonal carcinoma often contains zones of necrosis. Gritty areas of bone or cartilage may be present. Polyembryomas are typically grossly haemorrhagic and spongy {38963187}.

Histopathology

Most ovarian mixed germ cell tumours contain two malignant germ cell components, while a smaller subset exhibits three or more distinct elements. The morphological and immunohistochemical features of each individual component are identical to those of their counterparts in pure tumours {185553}.

Currently, the presence of any amount of a second component is sufficient to define a tumour as a malignant mixed germ cell tumour. The most common combination is dysgerminoma and yolk sac tumour, followed by immature teratoma with yolk sac tumour and embryonal carcinoma with yolk sac tumour. The two least common tumour types are embryonal carcinoma and choriocarcinoma. The components are often intimately admixed but may be present in separate areas of the mass with minimal intermixing. Tumours from patients presenting with precocious pseudopuberty frequently contain syncytiotrophoblastic giant cells. When reporting these neoplasms, the different morphological components should be listed and the percentage of each component in the overall tumour should be reported.

Two patterns of mixed germ cell tumour are sufficiently distinct to be separately categorized: polyembryoma and polyembryoma background.

Polyembryoma: This distinctive germ cell tumour is composed predominantly of embryoid bodies resembling the embryo before day 18 of development {33284192; 38963187}. The embryo-like (embryoid) bodies consist of a central plate of cuboidal to columnar embryonal carcinoma cells, a dorsal amnion-like cavity typically lined by flattened epithelium, and a ventral yolk sac–like vesicle composed of reticular and myxomatous yolk sac tumour. These bodies are surrounded by a myxoid, embryonic-type mesenchyme. A pure polyembryoma has not been encountered; tumours reported to date have all contained minor components of other germ cell elements, most often teratoma, yolk sac tumour, and/or embryonal carcinoma. The term “polyembryoma” should be reserved for neoplasms in which embryoid bodies comprise > 90% of the tumour, with other germ cell tumour patterns constituting minor components {36044302}.

Polyembryoma background: This shows random scattering of well-formed or fragmented embryoid bodies in nodular/lobular arrangements along with yolk sac tumour and/or embryonal carcinoma components. Embryoid bodies are a minor component of this mixed germ cell tumour. Single or small clusters of well-formed embryoid bodies can occur within an immature teratoma (focal embryoid bodies). As embryoid bodies are neoplastic formations, they are not always perfectly formed and can be seen as fragmented/imperfect bodies (or embryoid body remnants) with darkly staining embryonal carcinoma-type epithelium and associated cavities and cysts that recapitulate the amniotic cavity and yolk sac vesicle { 33284192; 38963187; 36044302}. Embryoid bodies, most often in remnant form, have been reported in about 25% of malignant mixed germ cell tumours and in 14% of immature teratomas {38963187}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: two or more malignant germ cell components.

Desirable: immunophenotype supportive of the individual components identified morphologically.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

The most important prognostic factor is stage. With current chemotherapy, the components that are present have less impact on the prognosis than in the past, but because the chemosensitivity of the various components differs {28372870}, the percentage of each component should be reported, as discussed. Some reports have suggested a favourable prognosis in polyembryoma compared with mixed germ cell tumours without a polyembryoma component; however, case numbers are small, treatment is variable, and follow-up studies are limited, so firm conclusions are not possible, particularly in the era of modern chemotherapy {38963187;11781529}.

There are significant differences in the clinical management of germ cell tumours between adult and paediatric populations. Historically, immature teratomas containing three or more foci of yolk sac tumour, exceeding 3 mm in diameter, were classified as mixed germ cell tumours; however, with a yolk sac tumour component less than this, a diagnosis of immature teratoma was made. In an effort to standardize diagnostic criteria and guide therapeutic decisions, it is now recommended that the presence of any yolk sac tumour component, regardless of size or number of foci, warrants classification as a mixed germ cell tumour. Nonetheless, for optimal clinical utility, it is advised that any minor yolk sac tumour component be clearly quantified in terms of both maximum diameter (i.e. ≤ 3 mm or > 3 mm) and its approximate proportion relative to the total tumour volume. Such documentation would avoid overtreatment and would enable meaningful comparisons between contemporary management outcomes and historical cohorts previously classified as immature teratoma with microscopic yolk sac tumour. This, in turn, will support accurate interpretation of emerging trial data in the context of prior classifications {38544795; 35863107}.
