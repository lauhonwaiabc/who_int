9.3.0.1 Dysgerminoma

Definition

Dysgerminoma is a primitive germ cell tumour composed of cells that are the neoplastic counterpart of primordial germ cells.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Dysgerminoma usually presents with abdominal pain or an abdominal mass, sometimes with an elevated serum LDH. Rarely, serum hCG may be elevated, particularly if syncytiotrophoblastic giant cells are present. Paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia may rarely occur. Most patients present with stage I disease. Bilateral ovarian involvement is present in as many as 10% of cases {10726633; 20097412; 29331749}. A subset of dygerminomas arise in phenotypically female patients with gonadal dysgenesis.

Epidemiology

Dysgerminoma is the most common malignant germ cell tumour. It accounts for about 1% of all ovarian malignancies {33284192; 15863154}. Dysgerminoma typically arises in children and young women (mean age: ~22–24 years), although rare cases have been documented to occur up to the seventh decade of life {33577182; 10726633; 20097412}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Chromosome 12 abnormalities, typically isochromosome 12p or 12p amplification, are seen in 80% of dysgerminomas {21523721}. A KIT mutation (exon 17, codon 816) is present in 30–50% of dysgerminomas {21523721; 22937135; 17274819} and KIT amplification in 30% {21523721}. In patients with gonadal dysgenesis, dysgerminoma may arise in association with gonadoblastoma (see section Gonadoblastoma).

Macroscopic appearance

Mean tumour size is 130 mm {33577182}. These neoplasms appear typically fleshy/rubbery, yellow or cream-coloured, solid, and lobulated. Cystic degeneration, haemorrhage, and/or necrosis may be present. Calcified areas may indicate a focus of gonadoblastoma in the tumour.

Histopathology

Dysgerminomas comprise sheets and nests of monotonous tumour cells separated by delicate fibrous septa. Less common architectural patterns include cords, trabeculae, solid tubules, and pseudoglandular formations. Tumour cells are polygonal, with well-defined cell borders, abundant clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm, and a central often angulated (squared-off) nucleus with one or two prominent nucleoli. Mitoses are common. Essentially all tumours contain a lymphocytic infiltrate within the fibrous septa, which ranges in intensity from scattered lymphocytes to a marked inflammatory infiltrate, including lymphoid follicles. Scattered syncytiotrophoblastic giant cells are present in a minority of tumours {33577182; 7270783}. The surrounding stroma may contain poorly formed granulomas, which may obscure the tumour, especially in metastatic sites. Rarely, dysgerminoma contains foci of spermatocytic tumour–like cells {28614211}. Dysgerminoma is typically immunohistochemically positive for SALL4 {19295406}, OCT4 {15371950}, LIN28 {22034885}, NANOG, KIT (CD117) {16056250}, and D240 {19483629}. Cytokeratins may be focally positive {16867864}. EMA, CD30, and glypican-3 are usually negative.

Cytology

Smears show large cells singly or in loosely cohesive tissue fragments, with high N:C ratios.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: uniform polygonal cells with clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm arranged in nests or cords separated by fibrous septa containing lymphocytes.

Desirable: positive immunohistochemical staining for markers such as OCT4, KIT (CD117), and D240.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

The 10-year progression-free survival rate is > 90%. Recurrence occurs in about 10% of cases, typically within 2 years of initial presentation. Stage is the main prognostic factor {29331749; 20097412; 22407668; 10726633}.
