17.1.0.2 Lynch syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)

Definition

Lynch syndrome (LS) is an autosomal dominant syndrome caused by a germline (constitutional) pathogenic variant affecting one of the four DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) and characterized by a high predisposition to a wide spectrum of cancers.

Subtype(s)

MLH1 Lynch syndrome; MSH2 Lynch syndrome; MSH6 Lynch syndrome; PMS2 Lynch syndrome; Muir–Torre syndrome (MIM number: 158320) {7224741}

Localization

Cancers occurring in the gynaecological tract are derived from endometrial tissue. The most common site of cancer is the endometrium, followed by the ovary; however, it can also rarely occur in the setting of extraovarian endometriosis {29241974}. Patients with LS are also at risk for synchronous and/or metachronous cancers of the colorectum, stomach, small bowel, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, renal pelvis and/or ureter, bladder, skin, or brain {30376427}.

Clinical features

Endometrial cancer is the most common cancer in women with LS and occurs as the sentinel cancer in slightly over half of cases. The median age of diagnosis of endometrial cancer is 50 years of age; cancers usually present at an early stage {15738026; 31337882; 19078925; 16421367}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of LS in patients with endometrial or ovarian cancer is approximately 2–3 % {16885385; 31086306; 36682091}. The cumulative risk for endometrial and ovarian cancer among patients with LS ranges between 40% and 60% and 9% and 17%, respectively, but is mutation dependent, as there is substantial variability in the penetrance of different MMR genes; the incidence in MSH6 mutation carriers is the highest {16885385; 21642682; 20028993; 21642682}. Founder mutations causing LS have been found in many populations {7584997; 28466842; 17039269; 29288294; 28369810}.

Etiology

LS most frequently results from autosomal dominant inheritance of a germline pathogenic variant in one of four DNA mismatch repair genes: MSH2, MLH1, MSH6, and PMS2 {9499183}. Other rare causes include a germline deletion of the 3′ end of the gene EPCAM, which results in the silencing of MSH2 as well as constitutional hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter {19455606; 21145788; 39831439}. De novo mutations have also been reported to occur rarely {21636617}.

Pathogenesis

The DNA MMR machinery recognizes and corrects errors (base pair mismatches, small insertions and deletions) that occur in DNA sequences. In LS, a secondary somatic hit occurs in the remaining MMR wildtype allele, leading to an MMR-deficient (dMMR) phenotype in which cells lack the ability to correct acquired errors. There is a subsequent accumulation of mutations (hypermutability) and carcinogenesis {40181086}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance is related to the tumour type and is not distinctive. Endometrial cancers arising in the lower uterine segment, which are rare in the general population, are enriched in women with LS {23765559}.

Histopathology

The histological type of cancers in the gynaecological tract in the setting of LS is diverse; however, most are endometrioid carcinomas of endometrium or ovary (section Endometrioid carcinoma, and section Endometrioid carcinoma). Other described histotypes include clear cell, mixed endometrioid / clear cell, un/dedifferentiated, and carcinosarcoma {32804882; 18469706; 36682091; 28114191; 19898223; 19238076; 24503759; 18469706}. Certain recurrent, yet nonspecific, histopathological features have been associated with dMMR endometrial cancers, including tumour heterogeneity, morphological ambiguity, and tumour-infiltrating and peritumoural lymphocytes {19238076; 24503759; 28079598}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although as many as 30% of endometrial carcinomas show MMR deficiency, most of these are sporadic and are either due to hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter or biallelic somatic mutations in one of the MMR proteins {33693762}. Repetitive regions, or microsatellites, are exquisitely sensitive to defects in the MMR system and readily accumulate errors, leading to changes in the length of tumour microsatellites, also known as microsatellite instability (MSI). Current methods that detect MSI are less sensitive in dMMR endometrial cancers compared with those of the colorectum because of a shorter median base pair shift, especially in patients with MSH6 mutations {39682157}.

As a screening tool, MSI has been largely replaced by immunohistochemistry due to its ease of performance and interpretation. MMR proteins form heterodimers (MLH1/PMS2, MSH2/MSH6) such that loss of expression of the first partner leads to instability and loss of the second; however, MSH6 and PMS2 may show isolated loss when the defect occurs in one of these genes {40181086}. Loss of expression of at least one MMR protein by immunohistochemistry shows a sensitivity of as much as 94% in detecting dMMR cancers. Tumours with missense mutations that produce non-functioning proteins but a recognizable antigen may show retained expression compared with truncating variants {31857677}. MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI do not differentiate between sporadic and germline origins of the tumour, and additional testing is required. Positive MLH1 hypermethylation in the setting of MLH1 and PMS2 loss is usually mutually exclusive with LS; however, there have been rare reports of co-occurrence, suggesting that the presence of methylation does not necessarily exclude LS {38061582; 38003003}.

If MLH1 hypermethylation is negative or other patterns of abnormal protein expression occur (MSH2/MSH6, MSH6 alone, PMS2 alone), testing for a germline pathogenic variant is required. When immunohistochemistry is used as a screening method, testing may be directed to the gene of interest based on the pattern of protein expression. Rarely, subclonal loss of MMR protein expression occurs in the setting of LS {36866757}. With the development of advanced sequencing technologies, germline testing with a panel of cancer predisposition genes versus single gene testing is increasing in use and may show an increased sensitivity compared with traditional screening methods in detecting LS {30376427; 40335734}. A subset of patients show negative genetic testing, despite abnormal protein expression or MSI, most of which can be attributed to biallelic somatic mutations, at times secondary to a hotspot POLE exonuclease domain mutation, both of which can be detected with somatic DNA sequencing {25111426; 25194673}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: germline mutation in a mismatch repair gene (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or EPCAM; or constitutional MLH1 promoter methylation.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with dMMR endometrial carcinomas have an intermediate prognosis compared with other molecular subtypes. Within dMMR endometrial carcinomas, patients with LS have been shown to have improved survival outcomes compared with those with non-LS endometrial carcinomas with MLH1 promoter methylation {33693762}.

dMMR cancers are characterized by exceptionally high mutational loads, which result in the generation of tumour-specific neoantigens that can subsequently be targeted by the immune system in the setting of immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Several recent randomized clinical trials have demonstrated that patients with dMMR endometrial carcinomas have significantly improved survival outcomes when treated with chemotherapy combined with ICB therapy {39102832; 36972026;36972022;37864337}. Furthermore, studies have shown patients with mutational dMMR tumours have higher response rates and longer survival than those with epigenetic dMMR tumours when treated with ICB therapy, suggesting that the molecular mechanism of dMMR is associated with response to ICB therapy {36301137}.

Tumours with dMMR appear to have a reduced response to hormonal therapy than MMR-proficient low-grade endometrioid carcinomas {39878274}.
