2.2.1.1 Squamous intraepithelial lesions

Definition

Squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) of the vulva are proliferations of squamous cells caused by HPV infection. The cells of these lesions show abnormal nuclear features and maturation abnormalities, and they are confined to the vulvar epithelial component. Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) of the vulva is, by definition, flat. Condyloma acuminatum is exophytic and is discussed here as it is also driven by HPV infection and has morphological overlap with LSIL.

Subtype(s)

Condyloma acuminatum

Flat low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL)

High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL)

Localization

Any area in the anogenital region may be affected by LSIL and HSIL. Vulvar condylomata acuminata occur most commonly in the labia minora and introitus. Lesions are commonly multicentric and multifocal {24719870}.

Clinical features

Condylomata acuminata usually present as painless papillae, bumps, warts, or verruca-like growths on the vulvar skin, sometimes complicated by pruritus, discharge, or bleeding. Vulvar LSIL can be subtle clinically, as the lesions are flat. The vast majority of low-grade lesions diagnosed in the vulva are condyloma acuminatum. In contrast, flat LSIL is uncommonly diagnosed in the vulva.

HSIL lesions present as white, erythematous, or pigmented macules, papules, or verrucous plaques, which may be confluent. Pruritus, irritation, and pain are common symptoms. Multifocal and multicentric/multizonal lesions (past, concomitant, or future HSILs of the cervix, vagina, and/or anus) {25987900} are frequent. Multifocality is more frequent in immunocompromised patients {9869880}.

Epidemiology

Before HPV vaccines were available, one cohort documented a peak incidence in 20- to 24-year-old women (6.3 cases per 1000 person-years) {23965766}. Incidence has declined due to HPV vaccination. {22420808; 27365492; 27527815}. Condylomata acuminata are typically more prevalent in the early years of sexual activity, with the median time between infection and development of lesions being approximately 5–6 months {24719870}. Patients with vulvar LSIL and HSIL have a wide age range (median: 45 years) {12920216; 37726173}.

HPV-associated precursors (HSIL) are much more often diagnosed than HPV-independent ones {37726173}. The global incidence of HSIL has increased over the past decades {10986677} but is expected to decrease in the near future because of increased HPV vaccination {27541373}.

Etiology

Condylomata acuminata are benign proliferative squamous lesions typically attributable to low-risk HPV types 6 or 11 {24719870; 22820980}. Low-risk HPVs are identified in approximately 70% of vulvar LSIL {12920216; 17122506}. High-risk HPVs have been identified in approximately 40% of vulvar LSIL, sometimes present as coinfection with low-risk types.

High-risk HPVs are the cause of HSIL. HPV16 is identified in > 70% of cases. Other high-risk HPV types are identified less frequently {23886586; 37726173}. Smoking and immunosuppression are epidemiologically associated with an increased risk of HSIL {28590952}.

HPV is sexually transmitted. Factors increasing the risk of infection include immune status, smoking, hormonal contraceptives, multiple sexual partners, and early coital age {24719870}.

Pathogenesis

HPV gains entry to the basal cells of the epithelium presumably through microabrasions or injuries of the basal cell layer and needs the cellular machinery of squamous differentiation to replicate.

As in other high-grade HPV-associated intraepithelial lesions of the genital tract, integration of transcriptionally active high-risk HPV is critical to HPV-associated vulvar squamous carcinogenesis. Viral oncoproteins inhibit cell-cycle regulators such as p53 and RB1, leading to uncontrolled proliferation.

Macroscopic appearance

Condylomata acuminata are soft, raised lesions with a verrucous, lobulated, filiform, or plaque-like appearance {24719870}. They tend to be non-pigmented. Vulvar LSIL may appear as a pale or erythematous flat area on the vulva.

HSIL presents as white, erythematous, or pigmented macules, papules, or verrucous plaques, which may be confluent.

Histopathology

Condyloma acuminatum typically shows acanthotic squamous epithelium with prominent papillomatous architecture {22820980}. Koilocytic features may be variably developed and are often less prominent in the vulva than in the cervix. Koilocytosis is best appreciated in valleys between papillae. A subset of condyloma acuminatum, and to a lesser extent flat LSIL, show morphological resemblance to seborrhoeic keratosis {12827609; 34170001}. Detection of HPV in genital seborrhoeic keratosis is rare in patients older than 50 years {24556611}. In younger patients, however, genital seborrhoeic keratosis-like lesions are often positive for low-risk HPV types (mostly HPV 6) {22583360}.

In the vulva, the term “LSIL” is reserved for flat lesions with histopathological features identical to the more common cervical LSIL, i.e. preserved squamous maturation and viral cytopathic changes in mid to superficial epithelial layers. These changes, described as koilocytosis, include rigid perinuclear haloes with eccentric nuclear positioning, enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei with irregular (raisinoid) contours and coarse chromatin, and binucleation. Mitotic figures are present in the lower third of the epithelium. Strict criteria should be applied to avoid overdiagnosis of vulvar flat LSIL, which is an unusual lesion.

Most vulvar LSILs show patchy, non-block p16 staining by immunohistochemistry {28719486}, although it can rarely show block positivity when associated with high-risk types. In addition to an increase of Ki-67 labelling in the lower part of the epithelium, there may be Ki-67 labelling of cells in the upper third of the epithelium {12920216; 24719870; 37726173}.

HSIL is characterized by loss of maturation of the squamous epithelium, with atypia present at the basal third of the epithelial thickness and beyond. This atypia includes loss of organization, nuclear enlargement and hyperchromasia, increased N:C ratios, and mitoses. As in other anogenital sites, HSIL can be stratified into grades 2 and 3 (following intraepithelial neoplasia nomenclature). Lesions graded as 2 have retention of some squamous maturation, with expansion of the atypical basaloid population and mitosis up to the lower two thirds of the epithelial thickness. Lesions graded as 3 have subtotal or total loss of maturation, with full-thickness extension of immature atypical cells and proliferation. Extension into skin appendages is common and may mimic invasion {1941803}.

Some lesions have a warty (condylomatous) appearance with florid exophytic growth, epithelial thickening, papillomatosis, and partial maturation with koilocytosis and multinucleation in superficial layers {28590952}. Attention to atypia, as defined above, involving basal epithelium, will alert to the presence of a high-grade lesion. Superimposed hyperkeratosis and parakeratosis (features of lichen simplex chronicus) can occur {29505429; 29750709}. Stromal melanophages are frequently seen. Dermal amyloid deposits {25149547} and mucinous areas are occasionally identified {19238078}. HSIL can coexist with lesions associated with β-HPV types that show epidermodysplasia verruciformis–like morphology (blue-grey cytoplasm, coarse keratohyalin granules, enlarged nuclei with minute nucleoli) {38180082}.

By immunohistochemistry, HSIL is characterized by block p16 expression, defined by continuous strong nuclear and cytoplasmic staining in at least the basal third of the epithelium {15213596; 28590952}. p53 immunohistochemical staining is wild type with two possible patterns: heterogeneous expression (variable intensity nuclear staining of scattered basal and suprabasal cells) and a basal-sparing pattern (in which the suprabasal epithelium shows intense nuclear labelling, but the basal epithelium shows heterogeneous or near to complete absence of nuclear staining) {29254796; 32203095}. The latter pattern is specific to HPV-associated lesions {36828360}. Immunohistochemistry is useful in confirming HSIL and excluding HPV-independent vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia. It can also help in the distinction from low-grade lesions (LSIL or exophytic condyloma), as a non–block-type p16 result will support a diagnosis of LSIL or condyloma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

HPV DNA or mRNA can be detected via in situ hybridization or PCR.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions/condyloma acuminatum

Essential: cytopathic features of HPV infection (koilocytosis: irregular hyperchromatic nuclei with perinuclear haloes and binucleation in mid to upper epithelial layers); retained squamous maturation and basal cell organization; mitoses confined to lower 1/3 of epithelium. For condyloma acuminatum, papillary architecture; acanthosis; hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis.

High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion

Essential: loss of maturation involving at least the basal one third of the epithelium but often extending through the full thickness; loss of organization with nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, and high N:C ratios; increased mitotic activity, with mitoses in the upper layers of the epidermis. If immunohistochemistry is available, p16 block-type overexpression, wildtype p53 expression.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Condylomata acuminata of the vulva are not considered to represent precancerous lesions. {17122506; 17721204; 26704327}. While indolent, recurrence is common due to repeated infection, persistence of virus in dermis or hair follicles, and contributing factors such as immunosuppression. There is very little information about the natural history of flat LSIL of the vulva.

HSIL carries a risk of progression to squamous cell carcinoma if untreated {16319258; 27864932}. The risk is relatively low compared with HPV-independent precursor lesions, with 11% absolute cancer risk, 8% cumulative incidence at 10 years, and median time to cancer diagnosis of 6 years in a recent series {37726173} (see section Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia, HPV-independent). Spontaneous regression is rare {22207153}. Risk factors for recurrence include age > 50 years, positive surgical margins, large size, and immunosuppression {22867736; 29126556}.
