16.1 Metastases: Introduction

The sixth edition of the Female genital tumours volume of this series integrates metastasis to the female genital tract organs, including the lower female genital tract, ovary, and peritoneum, which were previously separated and included in the different chapters of the fifth-edition volume, to provide a comprehensive review for common diagnostic approach and convenience of readers.

In this chapter, the term “metastatic” is used synonymously with “secondary” and therefore includes tumours that affect a specific anatomical site by direct spread from adjacent tissues or by haematogenous seeding. A wide variety of tumours metastasize to the female reproductive tract, including the vulva, vagina, cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. Each has different propensity to metastasize, depending on its anatomical relationship to nearby organs, including the vasculature. In the ovary, metastatic tumours account for 5–22% of all tumours {6692304; 3692671; 1730431; 8551738; 9331286; 11766746; 15982725; 15626916; 15871720}. Primary lesions are often in the large intestine, breast, endometrium, stomach, cervix, pancreas, appendix, and biliary system, with the gastrointestinal tract accounting for 80–90% of cases.

Metastasis to the cervix or body of the uterus is extremely rare {6322966; 3955507; 29750707}. In contrast, metastatic tumours are more common than primary tumours in the vagina [[Fu YS, Reagan JW. Pathology of the Uterine Cervix, Vagina, and Vulva. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 1989.]]. Metastatic tumours in the vulva account for 5–10% of cases.

The age range of onset is wide (10–86 years) {6322966; 12766584; 30918738}, and symptoms vary greatly depending on the anatomical location of the tumour and the extent of its spread. If it spreads to the cervix, it may be diagnosed by bleeding or a cervical smear {3955507; 15943996; 21979600; 22961708}. In the vulva, it may cause masses or ulcers {12766584}.

Most secondary tumours in the uterine corpus occur by direct extension from neoplasms of neighbouring organs. Among extragenital primary tumours metastatic to the uterine corpus, the most frequently reported are mammary lobular carcinoma, gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma, and colonic carcinomas { 29750707 ; 30918738 ; 15943994 }. Contiguous spread to the cervix from an endometrial primary carcinoma is most common { 14501374 ; 12883240 }, followed by metastases from the ovary, gastrointestinal tract, breast, and kidney { 3955507 }. Metastases to the vagina most commonly originate from cervical carcinomas. Other sites of origin include the endometrium, colorectum, ovary, vulva, urinary tract, and breast, as well as cutaneous melanoma, uterine leiomyosarcoma, and gestational trophoblastic tumours { 12649667 ; 18720923 }. Metastases to the vulva arise mainly from the gynaecological tract { 12766584 } but also from the gastrointestinal tract, skin (melanoma), and breast { 12766584 }.

To make the diagnosis of metastasis and determine or suggest the primary site, microscopic features should be combined with a constellation of findings including patient history and clinical presentation in addition to age, gender, history, clinical course, results of imaging study, and laboratory findings; frequent communication between pathologist and attending physician is mandatory. In this regard, clinicopathological correlation is an essential component for precise diagnosis of metastasis. For example, peritoneal disseminated carcinomatosis of an older woman of unknown origin may suggest high-grade serous carcinoma of tubo-ovarian origin, followed by gastrointestinal tract and pancreaticobiliary system. Unusual microscopic pattern may indicate metastasis as a diagnostic concern, although some primary and metastatic tumours share common morphology, including primary and secondary extramammary Paget disease; the latter might be of bladder or rectal origin. Ovarian metastasis of lobular carcinoma of the breast may be misinterpreted as sex cord stromal tumours, lymphoma/leukaemia, or desmoplastic stromal round cell tumour { 34931622 }.

Immunohistochemistry as an ancillary study may be contributory to a diagnosis, but it should be kept in mind that aberrant expression and limited specificity of diagnostic markers can be a source of confusion. Using a panel of antibodies is highly recommended rather than relying on a single marker to minimize such pitfalls (see #56407Table 16.XXX). For example, PAX8 or PAX17 appears to be diagnostic markers for epithelial tumour of Müllerian origin, but they have some limitations depending on histological types {36788073; 36853778}. SMAD4 (DPC4) loss is indicative of pancreatic origin, but sensitivity is as much as 60% {31569186}, and a subset of ovarian mucinous carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinomas can show same immunophenotype {33405429}. SATB2 is a rather specific marker for colorectal carcinoma, but ovarian mucinous tumour in association with mature teratoma can be positive for this marker {26059401} and shows better performance with aids of CK7 and PAX8 {26535987}.
