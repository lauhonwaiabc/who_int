4.1 Tumours of the uterine cervix: Introduction

According to the estimates published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 661 021 cases of cervical cancer and 348 189 deaths from the disease occurred in 2022 {38572751}. Cervical cancer was the fourth most common cancer in women globally (after breast, colorectal, and lung cancers) and one of the top three cancers in women aged < 45 years in 145 countries worldwide. The estimated age-standardized incidence rates vary considerably, with annual rates ranging from < 4 cases per 100 000 women in several countries in the Persian Gulf and northern/western Europe to > 40 cases per 100 000 women in several countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Cervical cancer was the leading cause of cancer-related death in women in sub-Saharan Africa {30207593; 31812369}. Due to their population size, China and India together contributed more than one third of the global cervical cancer burden {30207593}.

Cervical cancer incidence and mortality are directly linked to the effectiveness of cervical cancer screening {34758259}. Countries that introduced cervical cancer screening over the last decades have seen a strong subsequent reduction of cervical cancer incidence and mortality. Countries with high cervical cancer burden typically have little or no screening. More recently, countries that have introduced adolescent HPV vaccination have seen a further reduction in cervical cancer incidence among the youngest age groups {32997908}.

Despite the success of cervical cancer prevention through screening and vaccination, a large proportion of the world’s population is not reached by these efforts, and the cervical cancer burden is expected to increase. To address this, the WHO global cervical cancer elimination initiative has the ambitious goals of reducing cervical cancer incidence to < 4 cases per 100 000 women (defined as elimination) worldwide through scaling up HPV vaccination of girls, screening for and treating precancerous lesions, and reducing mortality by improving access to the diagnosis and treatment of invasive cancers. Compared with HPV vaccination alone, adding twice-in-a-lifetime cervical cancer screening can accelerate elimination by 1–3 decades and save millions of lives {32007141}. Many demonstration projects are underway, but widespread implementation will require large efforts and funding to achieve the WHO elimination goals.

The two major histopathological types of cervical cancer are squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, which develop through distinct pathways. Most cervical cancers originate in the squamocolumnar transformation zone, with SCCs more commonly developing in the ectocervical portion through squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs), while adenocarcinomas may develop in the endocervical canal through glandular precursors (adenocarcinoma in situ). The ectocervical region can be sampled more easily during screening and colposcopy. Therefore, cervical cancer screening is most effective at preventing SCC. Around the world, the proportion of adenocarcinomas among cervical cancers ranges from 5.5% in countries without screening to 18.7% in Australia and New Zealand. Between 2000 and 2018, about 68% of cervical cancers in the USA were SCC and 21% were adenocarcinoma {36455190}. In the highly screened population in Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), SCC incidence decreased after the introduction of HPV-based screening, while adenocarcinoma incidence remained stable, with adenocarcinoma contributing 44% of cervical carcinomas {38301311}. These data demonstrate that with increasing introduction of HPV-based screening worldwide, the relative proportion of adenocarcinoma and rare cancer types among all cervical carcinomas will increase, even if their absolute numbers remain stable; however, there is also some evidence that the absolute incidence rate of adenocarcinoma is increasing in some populations {40180806}. It is noted that the incidences of various cancers take some time to calculate as registries adjust to changes in pathology reporting.

Cervical epithelial pathology is dominated by HPV infection and its neoplastic consequences, and persistent HPV infection is necessary for the development of most cervical carcinomas {10451482}. However, it has become increasingly recognized that a small, albeit variable, proportion of cervical carcinomas (especially, but not exclusively, adenocarcinomas) are not associated with HPV infection and represent HPV-independent neoplasms. Although currently cervical HPV-associated and HPV-independent carcinomas are managed similarly, this will likely change because HPV-independent cervical carcinomas as in other anatomical sites, such as the oropharynx and the vulva, are more aggressive than HPV-associated carcinomas. Moreover, the expected progressive reduction in the next decades of HPV-associated carcinomas, as a consequence of mass vaccination programmes, will result in an increase in the percentage of HPV-independent carcinomas. Therefore, this sixth edition of the WHO classification, adhering to the fifth edition, divides cervical SCCs and adenocarcinomas and their precursors based on the role of HPV in pathogenesis. Uniform terminology is recommended, and the terms “HPV-associated” and “HPV-independent” are suggested to be used universally (rather than terms such as “HPV-related” and “non-HPV-associated”).

Almost all cervical SCCs are HPV associated. However, there are now increasing numbers of HPV-independent SCCs being reported {25675193; 40010435; 38959413; 37702216}. HPV-associated and HPV-independent SCCs cannot be reliably distinguished on the basis of morphological criteria alone; p16 immunohistochemistry or HPV molecular testing is required. p16 positive expression (block or block-type) is a reliable surrogate marker of high-risk HPV association in premalignant lesions and invasive carcinomas. Rarely, the p16 result may be negative, leading to a misinterpretation {10535995}. Throughout this chapter when discussing p16 immunohistochemical staining, we use the terms “block” and “block-type” to denote the type of staining characteristically associated with high-risk HPV infection (https://wwwthebagp.org) (see section General Introduction). The term “block-positive p16” is used to characterize staining in cases of high-grade SIL (HSIL) only; as defined by the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology Standardization (LAST) Project for HPV-Associated Lesions, it comprises continuous strong nuclear or nuclear plus cytoplasmic staining of the basal cell layer with extension upwards involving at least one third of the epithelial thickness {22742517}. The term “block-type p16 staining” is used to describe strong nuclear staining (usually with accompanying cytoplasmic staining) in essentially all cells and is used for SCC and preinvasive and invasive glandular lesions and other tumour types. Block-type staining should be distinguished from negative/non–block-type staining (also referred to as mosaic staining). Although the percentage of HPV-independent SCCs in the cervix is very low, it is recommended that the HPV association of an SCC be documented in the pathology report for epidemiological purposes, cancer registration, or future clinical trials. A morphological diagnosis of SCC without differentiating the two categories is an acceptable alternative in cases where ancillary testing of the tumour is not feasible, the p16 results are inconclusive, or HPV status from testing of a proximate cervical cytology or tissue sample is not available. The designation SCC, HPV status undetermined, is appropriate in such cases, and it is important to note that most of these tumours will be HPV associated.

“SIL” is the terminology used for HPV-associated premalignant lesions and intraepithelial abnormalities. As in the prior fifth edition, they are divided into low-grade SIL (LSIL) and HSIL. In the LSIL category, both seborrhoeic keratosis-like lesion and papillary immature metaplasia are discussed as morphological patterns of LSIL, the former strongly associated with HPV42 {27792064; 40387309}. The importance of using cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) qualifiers for cases of HSIL (HSIL/CIN2 and HSIL/CIN3) is discussed, since this is of importance, especially in younger women, where cases of HSIL/CIN2 can often be managed conservatively under active surveillance with a good cytology-colposcopy correlation. A new entity in the sixth edition is CIN, HPV-independent, which is a possible precursor lesion of HPV-independent SCC. However, this is an evolving and not yet well-characterized entity, with few cases reported. Moreover, most reported cases have been associated with an HPV-independent SCC. Therefore, its biological behaviour, premalignant potential, and natural history remain largely unknown.

It has been recognized for some time now that although the majority of cervical adenocarcinomas are associated with HPV infection, a minority are not {29135516; 29877920; 29233547; 26645463}. As in the prior classification, the sixth edition continues to separate primary cervical adenocarcinomas into HPV-associated and HPV-independent categories. The former includes tumours showing a variety of morphological patterns (no longer considered as subtypes), and the growth pattern-based Silva system may be of value in stratifying these neoplasms into prognostic groups with different behaviours, including different risks of nodal spread. HPV-independent adenocarcinomas comprise gastric type, clear cell type, mesonephric type and others, mainly comprising endometrioid type and a category of endocervical adenocarcinoma NOS. Due to the occasional difficulties in the differential diagnosis with HPV-associated adenocarcinomas, negative/non–block-type p16 immunohistochemical staining and/or negative HPV testing is recommended before establishing a diagnosis of HPV-independent adenocarcinoma. Most adenocarcinomas previously reported as endometrioid represent mucin-depleted HPV-associated adenocarcinomas, and these tumours should be classified as HPV associated based on the typical morphological features and block-type p16 immunoreactivity. Notably, endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the cervix should be diagnosed only when an HPV-associated adenocarcinoma and other mimics (e.g. extension from an endometrial carcinoma) have been excluded. As in the fifth edition, serous carcinoma of the cervix is not included since there is no evidence that it occurs as a primary cervical tumour; adenocarcinomas reported in the past as primary cervical serous carcinomas represented HPV-associated or HPV-independent adenocarcinomas mimicking serous carcinomas or spread from a primary uterine corpus or tubo-ovarian serous carcinoma.

Adenocarcinoma in situ similarly is divided into HPV-associated and HPV-independent types. The latter is predominantly of gastric type {28394803}, with little published evidence of in situ equivalents of clear cell, mesonephric, or endometrioid adenocarcinoma. It should be recognized that HPV-independent gastric-type lesions (metaplastic or neoplastic) may occur at multiple sites within the female genital tract; this is referred to as synchronous/multifocal gastric-type lesions of the female genital tract and sometimes occurs in patients with Peutz–Jeghers syndrome {38477341; 19207943}. In such cases, it may be difficult to distinguish between synchronous independent lesions or metastasis from one site to another.

In this sixth edition, cervical adenosquamous carcinomas are also now divided into HPV-associated and HPV-independent categories. The majority are HPV associated, and in most of the reported HPV-independent examples, the glandular component has been of gastric type {36811841}. As in the fifth edition, mucoepidermoid carcinoma is no longer recognized as a distinct category and is now regarded as a form of adenosquamous carcinoma due to morphological overlapping and absence of universal diagnostic criteria, although there have been occasional reports of primary cervical neoplasms exhibiting the molecular alterations of mucoepidermoid carcinomas of the salivary gland (CRTC1::MAML2 fusion) {19092631}. In addition, as in the fifth edition, adenoid cystic carcinoma is no longer included as a distinct category of cervical carcinoma. Most cases previously diagnosed are now considered to represent a morphological pattern of SCC showing a basaloid pattern or (less commonly) HPV-associated adenocarcinomas showing an adenoid cystic carcinoma–like morphology.

In the fifth edition, neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the cervix were included in a separate chapter of neuroendocrine neoplasia; however, in this edition, cervical NENs are covered in this cervix chapter with categories of neuroendocrine tumour (NET) and neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC). Mesenchymal and melanocytic tumours of the cervix are covered in stand-alone chapters devoted to these neoplasms at all sites in the female genital tract (section Mesenchymal tumours and section Melanocytic lesions respectively). Cervical adenomyomas and adenosarcomas are covered in the mesenchymal chapter that covers these neoplasms at all sites in the female genital tract. Müllerian papilloma of the cervix is now covered in the vaginal chapter (section Tumours of the vagina) since these neoplasms are more common at that site.

As with other chapters, non-neoplastic squamous and glandular lesions are no longer included in the sixth-edition classification. These lesions, which appeared as separate sections in the fifth edition, may be considered as differential diagnoses in a variety of circumstances and are listed in #56252Table 4 XXX.

In many of the sections on cervical adenocarcinomas, primary vaginal adenocarcinomas are covered rather than having a separate iteration in the vaginal chapter. This is because many types of primary vaginal adenocarcinomas are essentially identical to those that occur in the cervix, and they are usually more common in the latter location. Adenocarcinomas of the vagina that are discussed in this cervix chapter are adenocarcinoma, HPV associated, endometrioid adenocarcinoma, clear cell carcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, gastric-type, and mesonephric adenocarcinoma. Adenosquamous carcinoma of the vagina is also discussed in this cervical chapter.
