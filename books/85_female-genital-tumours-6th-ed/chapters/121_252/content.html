12.1 Gestational trophoblastic disease: Introduction

GTD comprises a spectrum of trophoblastic proliferations ranging from hydatidiform moles to trophoblastic neoplasms. Each entity exhibits distinctive histological and cytological features that recapitulate specific trophoblastic cell types of the placenta {11898845}.

Placental trophoblasts are broadly classified into three major subtypes: syncytiotrophoblasts, cytotrophoblasts, and intermediate trophoblasts. Syncytiotrophoblasts are fully differentiated, multinucleated cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, located external to the cytotrophoblast layer on the surface of chorionic villi. These cells are primarily responsible for hormone production throughout gestation. Cytotrophoblasts are undifferentiated, proliferative cells with stem cell–like properties; they are typically small to medium-sized, polygonal to oval, and mitotically active. Intermediate trophoblasts are mononuclear cells of medium to large size with abundant cytoplasm. Based on their anatomical location and functional role, intermediate trophoblasts are subclassified into three types: implantation-site intermediate trophoblasts, located at the maternal implantation site; chorionic laeve intermediate trophoblasts, situated in the chorionic membrane; and villous or trophoblastic column intermediate trophoblasts, found in the anchoring villi {17498600; 24901393}.

Hydatidiform moles, characterized by abnormal proliferation of villous trophoblasts, are subclassified into complete and partial types. Recognizing hydatidiform moles and distinguishing them from non-neoplastic mimics is essential for appropriate postmolar surveillance and timely detection of persistent or aggressive GTD. Invasive hydatidiform moles, which may arise from either complete or partial moles, exhibit myometrial and vascular invasion. The spectrum of gestational trophoblastic tumours includes gestational choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumour, epithelioid trophoblastic tumour, and mixed trophoblastic tumour. Atypical villous lesions represent abnormal products of conception with histological features that overlap with those of partial mole. Tumour-like trophoblastic lesions include exaggerated placental site reaction and placental site nodule or plaque. Choriocarcinoma, the most common malignant trophoblastic neoplasm, recapitulates the trophoblastic proliferation characteristic of the previllous stage of early placental development. Both placental site trophoblastic tumour and exaggerated placental site reaction originate from implantation-site intermediate trophoblasts. In contrast, epithelioid trophoblastic tumour, placental site nodule, and atypical placental site nodule are derived from chorionic laeve intermediate trophoblasts. Introduced by FIGO in 2001 primarily for clinical management, gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN) is a unified clinical classification that includes persistent moles, invasive moles, choriocarcinoma, placental site trophoblastic tumour, and epithelioid trophoblastic tumour {12092010}. In cases of postmolar GTN, treatment decisions are now based mainly on clinical findings and serum hCG levels, without the need for pathological assessment of tissue specimens {34686354}. As a result, the precise pathological process remains undefined in most patients with a clinical diagnosis of postmolar GTN.

In accordance with the general framework of the sixth edition of the WHO classification, non-neoplastic trophoblastic lesions – including abnormal (non-molar) villous lesions, exaggerated placental site reaction, placental site nodule, and atypical placental site nodule – are no longer presented as separate sections within the chapter.
