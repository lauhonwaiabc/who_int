7.2.2.2 High-grade serous carcinoma

Definition

High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) is an invasive high-grade malignant epithelial neoplasm with serous differentiation originating in the fallopian tube.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Fallopian tube

Clinical features

Most patients present with symptoms of a pelvic mass. In low-stage disease, there may be a mass of the fallopian tube or features of hydrosalpinx. More commonly there is both tubal and ovarian involvement, with the ovarian tumour often larger than the tubal tumour, or replacement of the tube and ovary by tumour. Rare patients do not exhibit any clinical symptoms, and the tumour is only detected incidentally in salpingectomy specimens removed for unrelated reasons or removed for risk reduction in the setting of a germline mutation in a hereditary ovarian cancer gene.

Epidemiology

A current understanding of the epidemiology of fallopian tube HGSC remains to be established since the criteria for assigning the primary origin of HGSC involving the fallopian tube, ovary, and/or peritoneum have evolved in the past decade, resulting in many cases that may have historically been assigned as primary ovarian HGSC to be assigned as primary fallopian tube HGSC using current criteria {36305537; 26827965}. The incidence is low (0.63 cases per 100 000 person-years in the United States from 2001to 2014) but has been increasing in recent decades and mainly affects women aged 70–74 years {29281053; 29514737}.

Etiology

Fallopian tube HGSC is thought to arise from genotoxic injury to the fallopian tube epithelium, particularly in the fimbrial ends, during ovulation cycles. Risk is also increased with hormone replacement therapy {22561401; 28931330; 28054715; 27625309}. A reported 16–43% of cases have been associated with germline pathogenic mutations in BRCA1/2 or other genes involved in homologous recombination {11263928; 14615451}.

Pathogenesis

TP53 mutation is thought to be the earliest and essential (but not sufficient) event in initiating fallopian tube HGSC. CCNE1 amplification, MYC amplification, and high levels of aneuploidy contribute to approximately half of the cases, while the remaining cases are associated with defective homologous recombination genes, including BRCA1 or BRCA2 {23780408; 28931330}. There is evidence of lineage continuity between the precursor serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC), tubal HGSC, and disseminated HGSC {30560554; 21990067; 29061967}.

Macroscopic appearance

In patients with symptomatic disease, the tumour is generally a solid mass either replacing the fallopian tube or confined to the lumen of the fallopian tube. However, in some patients with macroscopic disease involving the ovaries and/or peritoneum, the fallopian tube HGSC may be visibly occult and only detectable by microscopic examination. In patients with asymptomatic tumour detected incidentally in salpingectomy from unrelated surgery or risk-reducing salpingectomy, the tumour is often not visible by macroscopic examination and only rarely forms a small nodule {21353295; 24820399}. Enhanced tissue processing and examination by the SEE-FIM (sectioning and extensively examining the fimbriated end) protocol to identify occult fallopian tube HGSC is advised in all cases of extrauterine HGSC and in risk-reducing salpingectomies {16434898}.

Histopathology

The histopathology and immunohistochemistry are identical to those of ovarian HGSC. In the setting of visibly occult tumour, HGSC is typically localized to the fimbriae of the fallopian tube and is generally distinguished from STIC by the presence of invasion into the submucosal stroma. Rarely, malignant tubal epithelium may exhibit an exophytic, papillary pattern growing from the tubal mucosa, without stromal invasion. For such cases, there is no outcome-based evidence to define the distinction between classification as exophytic STIC versus classification as exophytic HGSC.

Cytology

Vaginal/cervical cytology is positive in 14% of cases {12789923}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not usually necessary for clinical diagnosis. However, in specimens showing synchronous tubal HGSCs, uterine serous carcinoma, or peritoneal disseminated HGSCs, molecular studies comparing their somatic mutation profiles may be warranted to clarify their clonal relationships {30560554}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a high-grade carcinoma with serous differentiation growing in the fallopian tube.

Staging

In the setting of multiple-site extrauterine involvement by HGSC, the consensus is to assign the primary origin to the fallopian tube when it is involved by STIC and/or HGSC, regardless of the relative sizes of the foci of HGSC in the involved organs (see section Epithelial tumours of the ovary: Introduction, , and #56078Table 8.XXX).

This entity is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]]).

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year survival rate is > 80% with stage I disease, in particular with adherent fimbria {25149685; 10053109; 11066048; 29281053}. The predictive variables are the same as those for ovarian HGSC.
