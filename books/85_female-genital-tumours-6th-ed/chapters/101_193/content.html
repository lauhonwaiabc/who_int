10.1 Mesenchymal tumours: Introduction

This introduction summarizes key changes made to the classification of mesenchymal tumours of the female genital tract since the previous edition.

Mesenchymal tumours of the uterus are a rare and heterogeneous group of neoplasms that arise from the uterine mesenchymal tissues, including smooth muscle, connective tissues (such as the endometrial stroma), and other mesenchymal elements. These tumours account for approximately 3–7% of all uterine malignancies and encompass both benign and malignant entities, with uterine leiomyomas representing the most common benign neoplasm, and uterine sarcomas – including leiomyosarcomas (LMSs), endometrial stromal sarcomas (ESSs), and undifferentiated uterine sarcomas – constituting the malignant spectrum {19853898; 15047237; 27177280}.

Clinically, patients with malignant mesenchymal tumours often present with nonspecific symptoms such as abnormal uterine bleeding, pelvic pain, or a rapidly enlarging uterine mass. Because these features overlap with those of benign uterine conditions, especially leiomyomas, accurate preoperative diagnosis remains challenging {39322612; 18189292}. Imaging, particularly MRI, can aid in differentiation, but histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis.

The staging of uterine sarcomas is defined by the FIGO or TNM classification systems, which reflect both tumour size and extent of spread, including extrauterine and metastatic involvement {26433666; 36305534} [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]] [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. Prognosis is generally poor for high-grade uterine sarcomas, particularly LMS, which are associated with a high risk of recurrence and distant metastases, even when diagnosed at an early stage. Five-year overall survival varies by histological subtype and stage, ranging from > 80% for low-grade ESS to < 30% for advanced LMS {40434060}.

Understanding the unique biological behaviour and clinical course of each tumour type is critical for optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes. According to the most recent 2024 ESGO/EURACAN/GCIG guidelines, case management should be centralized in expert referral centres with multidisciplinary tumour boards, guided by advanced imaging and molecular diagnostics, and include en bloc surgical excision without morcellation to optimize outcomes {39322612}. Molecular sequencing may have direct clinical relevance, particularly when it identifies potentially targetable alterations that can inform therapeutic decision-making.

A major revision in the sixth edition is the consolidation of all mesenchymal tumours of the female genital tract into a single unified chapter. Tumours previously addressed in organ-specific chapters are now grouped by mesenchymal origin, with site-specific features discussed under each entity. For example, smooth muscle tumours – the most common group of mesenchymal neoplasms in the female genital tract (60%) {19853898} – are no longer classified by anatomical site but as a unified group. Previously described so-called subtypes of leiomyomas, including leiomyomas with bizarre nuclei, epithelioid, and myxoid features, are now recognized as morphological patterns of leiomyoma, not separate biological entities, since they do not differ in clinical behaviour, treatment, or molecular profile.

Leiomyoma with fumarate hydratase (FH) deficiency is an indicator of genetic risk and is classified as a pattern. This tumour may occur sporadically or be associated with hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma syndrome. It shows distinctive histological and immunohistochemical features that should prompt consideration of genetic counselling in the appropriate context (see section Hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma). The clinical significance of uterine leiomyomas with FH deficiency largely depends on the presence or absence of hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma syndrome and associated renal cell carcinoma {17895761; 12772087}. Among malignant smooth muscle tumours with FH deficiency, biologically malignant behaviour has only rarely been reported {27454940; 26574848; 16477632}.

Although the distinction between benign and malignant smooth muscle tumours remains primarily based on morphological criteria, differing from those applied in soft tissue pathology, recent molecular studies have substantially improved diagnostic precision. Uterine LMS commonly harbour complex genomic alterations, including mutations or loss in TP53, RB1, ATRX, DAXX, PTEN, MDM2, and CDKN2A, among others. These findings have led to the development of immunohistochemical algorithms that support malignancy risk assessment and help stratify recurrence risk {29100075; 29321523; 35705560; 32299819; 33876771; 36788080; 33099852; 25932961; 29327710}.

Significant updates to the diagnostic criteria of epithelioid and myxoid smooth muscle tumours have also been introduced. For epithelioid LMS, recent evidence {34419987} supports requiring at least two of the following for diagnosis: diffuse cytological atypia, tumour cell necrosis, and ≥ 1.7 mitoses/mm2 (equating to ≥ 4 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm diameter). Revised criteria for myxoid LMS similarly require at least two of the following: infiltrative tumour margins, diffuse atypia, tumour cell necrosis, and > 0.8 mitoses/mm2 (equating to > 2 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter) {26866354}.

Low-grade LMS remains a controversial entity. Although clinical and molecular data support its existence, reproducible diagnostic criteria are lacking {21921786}. Some consider it part of the spectrum of smooth muscle tumours of uncertain malignant potential that recur late and follow a more indolent course than conventional LMS {37699075}.

The diagnostic criteria for smooth muscle tumours of uncertain malignant potential have also been revised. A mitotic count threshold of ≥ 8.4 mitoses/mm² (equating to ≥ 20 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm diameter) is now recommended to distinguish these from mitotically active leiomyomas, replacing the earlier cut-off point of 6.31 mitoses /mm2 (equating to 15 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.55 mm diameter) {39711162}. These criteria, often based on limited case series, are intended to support, rather than strictly dictate, diagnostic judgement.

Undifferentiated uterine sarcomas not classified further (NCF) are now listed under section miscellaneous mesenchymal tumours, not under section endometrial stromal and related tumours, reflecting their morphological and molecular heterogeneity.

The category adenofibroma was already removed in the fifth edition. Tumours previously classified as adenofibromas are now considered either low-grade adenosarcomas or benign endometrial polyps with atypical architecture {25118811; 26715175; 20179434}.

Lipomatous tumours of the female genital tract are now described in greater detail and subclassified according to the sixth edition of Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours.

SMARC-deficient neoplasms are subdivided into SMARC-deficient uterine sarcomas and SMARCB1-deficient vulvar neoplasms.

Epithelioid sarcoma, myoepithelial carcinoma, and myoepithelial-like vulvar tumour are grouped under SMARCB1-deficient vulvar neoplasms.

NTRK-rearranged spindle cell sarcomas are designated as kinase-altered spindle cell neoplasms in the Soft Tissue and Bone Tumours classification; however, the term “sarcoma” is preferred in the Female Genital Tract Tumours classification, as these tumours in the female genital tract show clinical behaviour consistent with malignant mesenchymal neoplasms. Previously listed as provisional entities, they are now recognized as a distinct category because of their risk of recurrence and death {35713627}.

Central primitive neuroectodermal tumours (PNETs) of the CNS type are now separated from Ewing sarcomas based on genetics: EWSR1 fusions with ETS family genes (e.g. FLI1) define Ewing sarcoma, while CNS-type tumours show divergent biology and require different classification.

In endometrial stromal tumours, molecular genetics has led to refined classification. Well-defined subsets of high-grade endometrial stromal sarcomas can now be suspected based on immunohistochemical surrogates, although definitive diagnosis requires molecular confirmation. This improved characterization has reduced the use of the undifferentiated uterine sarcoma, NCF category, which may be phased out in future editions. In settings without access to molecular diagnostics, classifying tumours with similar therapeutic implications as undifferentiated uterine sarcoma, NCF, may remain valid.

ESR1 mutations have been identified in LG-ESS cases with high-grade transformation or altered hormone receptor status; testing is now recommended in such cases {34961764; 39322612}.

Mesenchymal tumours with epithelioid cytology and nuclear uniformity should be assessed for TSC2 mutations or gene fusions because of implications for targeted therapy {34561551; 39322612}.

Superficial myofibroblastoma, previously described as a separate entity in the fifth edition, is now included in the myofibroblastoma section on the basis of recent literature demonstrating overlapping clinicopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular features {11486170; 15693885; 21820148; 22575260}.

Entities well recognized in soft tissue pathology, such as synovial sarcoma and low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, are now included in section Other mesenchymal tumours. Several provisional tumour types – such as KAT6A/B::KANSL1 uterine sarcoma and COL1A1::PDGFB sarcoma – are also summarized in a dedicated synoptic table in that section.
