7.2.2.1 Serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma

Definition

Serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC) is a proliferation of cytologically malignant epithelial cells with serous differentiation in the fallopian tube mucosa with an intraepithelial distribution and lacking stromal invasion.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: carcinoma in situ; fallopian tube intraepithelial neoplasia; high-grade tubal intraepithelial lesions.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These are localized to fallopian tube mucosa (preferentially occurring in the fimbria). STIC is usually a solitary focus and unilateral; however, occasional cases may be multifocal or bilateral.

Clinical features

No symptoms or signs are directly attributable to STIC itself. STIC is typically an incidental microscopic finding in the setting of a risk-reducing salpingectomy, salpingectomy performed as part of surgery for ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma, or salpingectomy performed for indications unrelated to risk reduction or ovarian cancer.

Epidemiology

In the risk-reducing setting (usually those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 germline mutations), most patients with STIC are in the fifth to seventh decades of life {21392246; 25199754; 30232083; 34465478; 35302882}. However, in the risk-reducing population and as a sporadic finding in procedures performed for other indications, STIC is detected in 0.7–10% {35302882} and < 0.01–1.1% {24820399; 26630221; 28479065; 31360879} of cases, respectively. For patients in whom STIC is detected as a sporadic finding in procedures performed for other indications, the mean age has ranged from 57 to 63 years {25517954; 25517955; 40386497}. The age range for patients with STIC and a synchronous ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma (41–77 years) is similar to that for such patients without STIC {29042553}. STIC is identified in as many as 61% of cases with ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma {20861711; 27003156; 28187099; 35859323}.

Etiology

A significant proportion of cases of STIC are associated with BRCA1 and BRCA2 germline (constitutional) variants {19543244}.

Pathogenesis

STIC is the earliest morphologically recognizable step in the development of high-grade serous carcinoma of the fallopian tube or ovary. A carcinogenesis sequence has been proposed in which the earliest lesion in this pathway is the secretory cell outgrowth {18156967; 20733351; 21196340; 28106106}. The secretory cell outgrowths with immunohistochemical overexpression of p53 are referred to as p53 signatures (p53 signatures are relatively common within the general population {20871594}). A subset of p53 signatures contain TP53 mutations {17117391; 30560554}. With continued cellular proliferation and gene dysregulation of a p53 signature, malignant transformation into STIC is thought to occur. Atypical lesions intermediate between p53 signature and STIC exist, and these have been referred to by multiple descriptive terms, including serous tubal intraepithelial lesion (STIL). Nearly all STICs have a TP53 mutation, and various other molecular alterations have been reported {17117391; 21990067; 31107721; 32817081; 33011111; 38048050}. High-grade serous carcinoma then develops after the occurrence of stromal invasion into the fallopian tube stroma or exfoliation of cells onto the ovary or peritoneal surfaces (with the formation of a mass or infiltration into these tissues).

Macroscopic appearance

STIC is a microscopic finding without any detectable gross features by itself.

Histopathology

STIC is a microscopic finding ranging in size from 0.1 to 8 mm (mean: 1.7 mm) {25675178}. The detection rate of these small lesions depends on the extent of tissue sampling. In risk-reducing salpingectomy samples, the SEE-FIM protocol is recommended {23722507; 24309326}. As STIC displays an overlapping spectrum of appearances and the histological features are inconsistent from case to case, the morphological findings that establish this diagnosis should include some variable combination of atypical features, including mucosal thickening / epithelial stratification / loss of polarity, cellular detachment and exfoliation of cells, loss of cilia, nuclear moulding, longitudinal cleavage artefacts, pleomorphism, nuclear enlargement and increased N:C ratio, hyperchromasia, nuclear rounding, prominent nucleoli, irregular chromatin distribution, irregular nuclear membranes, mitotic figures, and/or apoptotic bodies {34850262; 35859323; 36939551}. Some STICs may have a flat mucosal surface, while the surface in others can be more irregular. In particular, compared with flat STICs, STICs with an irregular surface and detached cells/clusters are more frequently associated with high-grade serous carcinoma, a higher Ki-67 proliferation index, epithelial stratification, increased lymphocyte density, and DNA copy-number gain/amplification at the CCNE1 or MYC loci {38298022}. Criteria have not been established to distinguish STIC with notable exophytic growth from expansile-type high-grade serous carcinoma without stromal invasion.

Occasionally, STICs may be attenuated and not entirely obvious at low magnification. In uncommon cases, the cells of STIC may be distributed in a pagetoid fashion in which lesional cells can be admixed with normal ciliated cells (for such cases, careful correlation between immunohistochemical stains for p53 and Ki-67 and the morphological findings is important to ensure that only the lesional cells are assessed for p53/Ki-67) {35859323}. Although the frequency of STIC may be lower after neoadjuvant chemotherapy {23722507}, it nonetheless can persist in some cases after such treatment {25083961}.

As the interobserver agreement between pathologists for diagnosing STIC based only on histological features is not high, performing immunohistochemical stains for p53 and Ki-67 improves diagnostic reproducibility {20567141; 21989347; 22498942}. Immunohistochemistry should only be performed for cases with atypia, and these stains are only scored in the atypical foci. Cases with atypia, aberrant p53, and a Ki-67 proliferation index > 10% can be more reproducibly diagnosed as STIC {21989347; 22498942; 35859323}. For problematic instances when the Ki-67 index is close to 10%, borderline cases can be resolved when the atypical focus exhibits more proliferation than the background non-atypical epithelium. However, based on a consensus report from the STIC Consortium, it was considered mandatory to perform immunohistochemical stains for p53 but not for Ki-67 when establishing a diagnosis of STIC {36939551}. Atypical foci lacking aberrant p53 immunohistochemical expression or having a Ki-67 index < 10% have been classified as STILs {21989347; 22498942; 35859323}. Foci with only aberrant p53 staining but lacking atypia and exhibiting a Ki-67 index < 10% have been referred to as a p53 signature, which has not known clinical significance and is not reportable.

Cytology

A subset of cases with STIC have positive peritoneal washings; however, morphological data/descriptions in the literature are limited {27733814}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: severely atypical epithelium with aberrant p53 immunohistochemical expression.

Staging

STIC is currently not staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Long-term outcome data are limited. A meta-analysis of women with BRCA germline mutations undergoing risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy demonstrated that the 5- and 10-year risks for developing subsequent peritoneal carcinoma were 11% and 28%, respectively {35302882}.
