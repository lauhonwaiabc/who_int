2.2.3.1 Paget disease

Definition

Paget disease of the vulva is a primary intraepithelial adenocarcinoma of the vulvar skin, with or without underlying invasive adenocarcinoma.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: extramammary Paget disease; primary vulvar Paget disease.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Vulvar Paget disease can arise from either the labium minus or the labium majus and can extend onto extravulvar skin and/or involve the vaginal or (rarely) cervical mucosa.

Clinical features

Patients present with a pruritic, erythematous, eczematous lesion mimicking a vulvar dermatosis or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (VIN 3).

Epidemiology

Primary vulvar Paget disease is uncommon, typically affecting White women aged > 60 years {26971063}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Vulvar Paget disease is thought to originate from a pluripotent stem cell in the epidermis or skin adnexa.

Macroscopic appearance

Paget disease is typically slightly raised and reddened compared with adjacent normal skin, with poorly defined margins.

Histopathology

Large round Paget cells with pale cytoplasm and a large nucleus, frequently with a prominent nucleolus, are present within the epidermis; these are predominantly located in the basal layers of the epidermis, sometimes with extension of isolated cells or small nests of cells upwards through the superficial layers of the epidermis. Involvement of the epithelium of skin adnexa is common. The cytoplasm contains mucin, demonstrable with PAS or mucicarmine stains, in most cases. A prominent host inflammatory response in the superficial dermis is common, with neovascularization; this correlates with the eczematous clinical appearance. Dermal invasion is usually in the form of individual or small groups of cells, sometimes forming glandular structures. A mimic of Paget disease, epidermodysplasia verruciformis-like lesion caused by a beta-HPV, has larger cells and a characteristic blue coloration of the cytoplasm, often presenting in immunocompromised individuals {38180082}.

The cells of Paget disease consistently express CK7, which serves to distinguish them from the cells of high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion, HPV-independent VIN, and melanoma {10542944}. CK7 may also assist in identifying small foci of dermal invasion. EMA, CAM5.2, CEA, and GATA3 are typically diffusely positive. Melanoma markers are negative and HPV cannot be detected, but p16 may be diffusely expressed {23370646; 31033795}. The cells are also typically negative for uroplakin-3, CDX2, and CK20 (although occasional cases are focally positive for CK20). Expression of these markers, especially if diffuse, should suggest secondary involvement of vulvar skin by urothelial carcinoma or rectal adenocarcinoma {26971063}. Secondary involvement of vulvar skin has been referred to as secondary Paget disease. The cells of the epidermodysplasia verruciformis-like lesion are negative for CK7.

Paget cells can express breast cancer biomarkers and AR. Most express ER and PR; ERBB2 (HER2) expression is less common, found in up to one third of lesions, and the reported frequency of ERBB2 (HER2) amplification varies {33287150; 15272283; 17701193; 28418164; 31297606}. Mutations in genes encoding the PI3K/AKT cascade have been found to significantly correlate with CDH1 hypermethylation {22821211; 28291131}. A study using comparative genomic hybridization revealed amplification at chromosomes Xcent-q21 and 19, as well as loss at 10q24-qter {16086738}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large epithelial cells with abundant cytoplasm, concentrated in the lower layers of the epidermis but extending upwards, as single cells or small groups of cells.

Desirable: CK7 immunoexpression; negative melanoma markers.

Staging

The staging of invasive Paget disease is the same as that of other vulvar carcinomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Local recurrences are very common, especially, but not exclusively, after incomplete excision, as Paget cells often extend further than is appreciable clinically (35–60%) {28124023; 29324542; 30458205}. Disease-related mortality is uncommon (< 5%) unless there is invasion {30458205}, but local control can become increasingly difficult with multiple recurrences. Depth of invasion and inguinal lymph node metastasis are associated with increased mortality. The presence of rare, isolated tumour cells in the superficial dermis is not associated with a worse prognosis {10542944; 22989161; 23519285; 30458205}. Patients with ERBB2 (HER2)-positive tumours may benefit from targeted treatment if clinically warranted {19519604; 30854041}.
