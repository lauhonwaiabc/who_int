9.3.1.3 Ovarian carcinoid

Definition

Ovarian carcinoid is a teratoma comprised of a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumour.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: grade 1 neuroendocrine tumour; well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Ovarian carcinoids may be detected incidentally or cause signs and symptoms related to a pelvic mass or ascites. Carcinoid syndrome (including cardiac manifestations) is seen in only 30% of insular carcinoid cases (in contrast to metastatic carcinoid, which is more frequently associated with carcinoid syndrome) {1157010; 8626144}; chronic constipation due to PYY secretion rarely occurs in strumal and trabecular carcinoid {25473750; 26630222}. Endocrine changes related to stromal luteinization can result in virilization and Cushing syndrome. Rare association with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 is reported {24818031}.

Epidemiology

Ovarian carcinoid is a rare tumour that accounts for 0.5–1.7% of all carcinoid tumours and 1% of all ovarian tumours {12569593; 41244794). The most common type is insular carcinoid (~50%) {1157010}, followed by strumal carcinoid (~40%) {7427909}. Trabecular carcinoid {834404; 7104979} and mucinous carcinoid {11342766} are rare. Ovarian carcinoid occurs over a wide age range, with most patients being postmenopausal (mean age: 53 years) {41244794; 6715397; 834404}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

It is considered to be of teratomatous derivation. The origin is presumed to be neuroendocrine cells within teratomatous components such as intestinal-type epithelium of a mature teratoma {39370906}.

Macroscopic appearance

Primary ovarian carcinoids are almost always unilateral and small (average size: 34 mm), but they may be larger (> 70 mm) {1157010}. Mucinous carcinoids are typically large (mean: 90 mm) {11342766}. Carcinoid tumours are typically solid but occasionally can be cystic or form a nodule within a mature cystic teratoma or mucinous tumour. They have a homogeneous yellow to tan cut surface, which can be glistening if mucinous {11342766}.

Histopathology

Insular carcinoid is composed of variably sized solid nests that may be punctated by acini, resulting in a pseudoglandular, tubular-like, or cribriform appearance. Luminal secretions may be present as well as calcifications. Retraction artefact around tumour nests may mimic lymphovascular space invasion. Trabecular carcinoid is composed of parallel, often interanastomosing, ribbons and trabeculae or cords. Strumal carcinoid is composed of trabecular or insular carcinoid intimately admixed or juxtaposed with thyroid follicles {7427909}. Mucinous carcinoid is composed of small glands/acini floating in pools of mucin, lined by goblet cells with compressed nuclei admixed with columnar cells, some showing neuroendocrine granules. Teratomatous elements {6715397; 834404; 7427909}, fibromatous stroma, and/or stromal luteinization may be present in all types.

The tumour cells are uniform and round to oval, with pink cytoplasm and centrally located bland nuclei with salt-and-pepper chromatin. Reddish-brown argentaffin granules may be seen at the cell base. Mucinous carcinoids may show crowded glands, confluent growth, and cribriforming. Carcinoma arising in mucinous carcinoid shows solid growth, single or signet-ring cells, severe cytological atypia, brisk mitotic activity, and mucin depletion.

Carcinoid is positive for neuroendocrine markers such as INSM1, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and CD56. A subset is positive for CDX2 and SATB2, regardless of whether the carcinoid is of primary ovarian origin or is metastatic to the ovary {19047909; 39370906}. Ki-67 is typically low {28735441; 29349725; 39370906; 37059424}.

Because primary ovarian carcinoid tumours exhibit similar morphology to their counterpart well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumour (NET) arising in other organs such as the gastrointestinal tract or lung, a diagnosis of primary ovarian carcinoid requires exclusion of metastasis from an extraovarian site. Distinguishing primary versus metastatic origin of a carcinoid in the ovary requires integrating the clinical history and the macroscopic and microscopic pathology, but the criteria are imperfect. Small size, unilateral involvement, and/or coexisting teratomatous elements favour a primary ovarian origin. Large size, bilateral involvement, absence of teratomatous elements, multinodularity, and/or lymphovascular space invasion favour a metastatic origin { 4815582; 17475313}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: neuroendocrine tumour with or without coexisting teratomatous elements.

Desirable: immunohistochemical positivity for neuroendocrine markers.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is generally excellent, with rare exceptions, especially if the tumour is a poorly differentiated mucinous carcinoid. Advanced-stage disease, albeit uncommon, is an independent prognostic factor for adverse outcomes {35538309; 37059424}.
