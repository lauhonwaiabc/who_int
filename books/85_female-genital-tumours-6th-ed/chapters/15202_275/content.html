15.2.0.2 Congenital melanocytic naevus

Definition

Congenital melanocytic naevus is a benign melanocytic neoplasm present at birth or appearing in the first year of life.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: congenital naevocytic naevus; congenital naevus syndrome; neurocutaneous melanosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Giant congenital melanocytic naevus with garment distribution frequently affects the genitalia. Small lesions most often involve the vulva.

Clinical features

Congenital melanocytic naevi are well-defined (but not sharply demarcated), tan to brown papules, nodules, or plaques. Small congenital naevi are < 15 mm in diameter, intermediate congenital naevi are 15–200 mm, and giant congenital naevi are > 200 mm {23182059; 19503980}. Congenital melanocytic naevi may continue to grow and change colour {3777972}. They usually darken, get thicker, and increase the volume of terminal hairs. Satellitosis is often seen. A hyperproliferative and multinodular phenotype often associated with severe pruritus can be seen in a subset of giant congenital naevi {37716647}.

Epidemiology

Small congenital melanocytic naevi affect approximately 1% of newborn infants.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most large congenital melanocytic naevi harbour exon 3 NRAS mutations; smaller congenital melanocytic naevi can also harbour BRAF NM_004333.4:c.1799T>A p.(Val600Glu) (BRAF V600E) mutations {31111470}. Gene fusions involving BRAF, RAF1, and ALK have also been documented as tumorigenic drivers in congenital naevi {37716647; 30945443; 17301836; 30359577; 29464327}. Congenital naevi are related to the migration of neural crest precursors (melanoblasts) during embryogenesis {24460190}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Junctional congenital melanocytic naevi display intraepidermal single and nested melanocytes in a background of variably hyperplastic epidermis. The dermal and compound types exhibit single and nested small dermal melanocytes lacking cytological atypia and demonstrating maturation. Perivascular and periadnexal growth can be noted. Melanocytes can be seen infiltrating arrector pili and/or nerves. If biopsy is performed within the first weeks of life, during the active growth phase of the naevi, a concerning pagetoid scatter and high dermal mitotic activity (as many as 27 mitoses/mm2) can be present {23348144}. Desmoplastic stroma has been described in a subset of fusion-driven congenital naevi {40036344; 29464327}. Longstanding congenital melanocytic naevi can develop dermal proliferating nodules, some of which are composed of atypical melanocytes and show mitotic activity.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular studies can be informative in the differential diagnosis between benign proliferative nodules and possible melanoma arising in a congenital naevus. Copy-number variations in the form of gains or losses of whole chromosomes can be seen in benign proliferative nodules, a finding that differs from the multiple clonal segmental chromosomal aberrations (gains or losses) typically found in melanoma {25517953}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a pigmented lesion present at birth or appearing within the first year of life.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Giant congenital melanocytic naevus is the main risk factor for developing melanoma in childhood {30782194}. The risk of developing melanoma appears to be proportional to the size of the lesion. In giant lesions, the lifetime risk can be approaching 10%. The highest incidence of melanoma arising in congenital melanocytic naevi is within the first 5 years of life, with a second peak from puberty into early adulthood {23182059; 27849631; 18671780; 16792745}.
