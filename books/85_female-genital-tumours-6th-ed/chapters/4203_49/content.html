4.2.0.3 Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), HPV-associated, is an invasive and/or exophytic malignant epithelial tumour exhibiting squamous differentiation and caused by persistent HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Cervix

Clinical features

Patients with small tumours may be asymptomatic; larger tumours commonly present with abnormal vaginal bleeding, discharge, and pain. Involvement of the parametrium can cause ureteric obstruction. Anterior and posterior growth with extension beyond the cervix leads to urinary frequency, pain, haematuria, tenesmus, and vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistulas.

Epidemiology

Cervical cancer is the fourth most frequent cancer in women worldwide, with an incidence of 661 021 new cases and 348 189 deaths worldwide in 2022 {38572751}. Approximately 90% of cervical cancers worldwide occur in low- and middle-income countries {38572751} lacking organized screening and HPV vaccination programmes. The highest incidence and mortality rates are seen in sub-Saharan Africa, where cervical cancer is also the most frequent cancer in women, followed by South-Eastern Asia {38572751}. The average age at diagnosis in women from high-income countries is in the sixth decade of life (median age: 51 years {30911077}); in low-income countries, the average age at diagnosis is younger, particularly in women living with HIV {37915091}. 80–90% of cervical cancers are SCCs, although the proportion is less in high-income countries {20952254; 33204265}. The large majority of cervical SCCs (> 90–95%) are HPV associated. The disease is largely preventable, and in high-income countries cervical cancer incidence and mortality have markedly diminished over the past 30 years, since the introduction of screening programmes and, in recent years, HPV vaccination {31382121}.

Etiology

Persistent infection with high-risk HPV genotypes causes the vast majority (> 90–95%) of SCCs {30911077; 20952254}. Twelve HPV types are classified by WHO as oncogenic (high-risk): 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59 {28893940; 23189750}, but two types (16 and 18) are responsible for approximately 70% of all SCCs {20952254; 12556961}. Another eight HPV types (26, 53, 66, 67, 68, 70, 73, and 82) have been rarely but consistently identified as single HPV infections in about 3% of cervical carcinomas and are classified as probably/possibly carcinogenic {25043390; 23189750}. Very rarely, low-risk HPV genotypes such as 6 and 11 have been identified as the sole cause of cervical SCC {24076770; 30312218: 39886739}.

Pathogenesis

HPV-associated cervical SCC develops from an HSIL as a result of high expression levels of the viral oncogenes E6 and E7 in dividing epithelial cells (a so-called transforming infection). The progression of high-grade lesions to SCC requires the accumulation of additional, not yet completely understood epigenetic and genetic alterations, a process that may take 20–30 years. Hypermethylation of CpG islands in promoter regions of tumour suppressor genes has been recognized as a molecular alteration involved in the progression from HSIL to cervical cancer {26219541; 31067838}. Several factors have been associated with an increased risk of HPV persistence and progression, including immunosuppression (particularly due to HIV) {37915091}, multiparity {39031901}, and smoking {37501128; 38510348}. Recent evidence overall fails to support an independent association between oral contraceptive use and cervical SCC risk {28759170}.

More than 70% of HPV-associated SCCs exhibit genomic alterations in either one or both of the PI3K/MAPK and TGF-β signalling pathways {28112728}. ERBB3 (HER3), CASP8, HLA-A, SHKBP1, and TGFBR2 have been reported as significantly mutated genes {28112728}.

Macroscopic appearance

Small invasive carcinomas may not be grossly visible or may present as red, erosive, or raised areas. More-advanced tumours can be either exophytic or endophytic (with minimal surface changes and infiltrative growth). Tumours may involve the full circumference of the cervix resulting in a barrel-shaped cervix or may involve one or more quadrants. The cut surface frequently shows necrotic areas and has a friable consistency.

Histopathology

SCCs are characterized by infiltrating, angulated, irregularly sized and shaped nests, anastomosing cords, and solid sheets of cells separated by an intervening desmoplastic or inflammatory stroma. Nuclear pleomorphism and prominent mitotic activity are typically present. Early stromal invasion from HSIL is associated with small irregular ragged epithelial nests, increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia (paradoxical maturation), stromal loosening, or desmoplasia. The diagnosis of invasion in papillary or exophytic lesions may be extremely difficult or impossible in small biopsies {33323853}.

Grading systems, based on either nuclear pleomorphism or differentiation, do not correlate with behaviour {29665326}, and the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) does not recommend grading these neoplasms {36305535}. Other grading systems for cervical SCC, such as a binary system and a growth-based grading system, have also been proposed, but these are not currently recommended {29665323; 29665326; 38085957}.

Several histological patterns have been described {37638549}, and these may occur in combination. These different patterns are of no established prognostic significance. Non-keratinizing and basaloid patterns are the most frequently identified patterns. Non-keratinizing SCCs are composed of polygonal squamous cells growing in nests or sheets. Intercellular bridges or individual cell keratinization can be seen, but keratin pearls are not present. Keratinizing SCCs are characterized by the presence of keratin pearls. Tumour cells show a mature appearance, with polygonal cytoplasm and intercellular bridges. Basaloid SCCs show nests of immature, basaloid appearing squamous cells with scant cytoplasm, resembling the cells commonly seen in HSIL. Individual cell keratinization can be present, but keratin pearls are rarely seen {12195218}. Occasional cases exhibit an HSIL-like growth pattern (CIN 3-like invasive SCC) and may be misinterpreted as non-invasive or minimally invasive disease, especially in limited biopsy specimens {27681166}. Warty (condylomatous) SCC shows an exophytic surface, sometimes with koilocyte-like changes. Papillary SCC shows exophytic growth of fibrovascular cores lined by a multilayered abnormal epithelium with squamous differentiation. Underlying stromal invasion may be present, but is often inappreciable at the time of biopsy, leading to underdiagnosis as HSIL {33323853}. When biopsy samples show florid exophytic growth typified by abundant papillary fragments without underlying stroma, it is therefore important to raise the possibility of a papillary SCC. Importantly, invasion may be inapparent or extremely focal on complete excision even in the setting of metastatic disease, underscoring the importance of a carcinoma, rather than SIL, diagnosis in this setting {9255254}.

Occasionally, these papillary tumours may show a transitional-like appearance, and the term “squamotransitional carcinoma” has been used in the past {7573672; 9255254}. The occasional reported cases of SCC associated with low-risk HPV types tend to show this morphology {24076770}, and a large condyloma or a benign squamous lesion with a papillary/polypoid architecture (such as seborrhoeic keratosis-like lesion {27792064}) should be excluded in these cases. Lymphoepithelioma-like SCC is a rare pattern showing a dense stromal inflammatory infiltrate. These represent HPV-associated SCCs, and EBV is not involved in their pathogenesis {15863191; 17674013}. Rare cases of HPV-associated SCC exhibit spindle cell or acantholytic morphology, although this pattern is usually focal {28700422}.

Most HPV-associated SCCs show strong and diffuse p16 overexpression by immunohistochemistry in both the nuclei and the cytoplasm (block-type positive p16 staining) {30911077}. Very occasional cases lack it, typically due to uncommon molecular events or, rarely, in the setting of low-risk HPV-driven disease {32304153; 30312218; 39886739}. High-risk HPV with mRNA in situ hybridization targeted against E6/E7 is highly specific and may be performed in cases with equivocal or challenging to interpret p16 results; additional testing for low-risk HPV mRNA may be performed if high-risk HPV is unexpectedly negative, or when warty or papillary morphology raises concern for a low-risk HPV-driven neoplasm {28403015; 39886739}.

Because both HPV-independent and low-risk HPV-associated SCCs are now recognized in the cervix, p16 immunohistochemistry and/or HPV typing using in situ hybridization based or other molecular methods are recommended for the definitive diagnosis. In cases with negative/non–block-type p16 immunohistochemical staining, HPV testing is recommended before classifying an SCC as HPV-independent. If these tests are not available, the SCC, HPV status undetermined, category should be used (see section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV status undetermined).

Cytology

Non-keratinizing and basaloid SCCs exhibit syncytial aggregates and single cells with high N:C ratios, coarse chromatin, macronucleoli, and irregular nuclear contours. Cell borders are poorly defined and cytoplasm is delicate. Tumour diathesis and haemorrhage are commonly present. Keratinizing and warty (condylomatous) SCCs have tumour cells that are heavily keratinized and have low or high N:C ratios, with intermixed keratin pearls, tadpole cells, and spindle cells. The nuclei are hyperchromatic, with dense or coarse chromatin. In contrast to non-keratinizing tumours, nucleoli and tumour diathesis are uncommon.

Although cytology has a very high sensitivity for the identification of a squamous lesion in patients with cervical SCC, the cytological diagnosis in these patients often falls short of carcinoma {26030246}. SCCs with early invasion or predominantly endophytic growth often show only HSIL on cytological sampling because of sampling limited to the surface component. Basaloid and papillary SCCs will similarly often be interpreted as HSIL on cytology due to cytomorphology that closely resembles that of HSIL, although in the case of papillary SCC the presence of cohesive clusters associated with fibrovascular cores may provide a diagnostic clue. Keratinizing SCCs exhibit low N:C ratios, which may occasionally lead to a misdiagnosis of low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion. Paucicellular or unsatisfactory cytology specimens may be seen in occasional patients with SCC.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Routine HPV testing is not required, but detection of high-risk HPV by in situ hybridization or PCR-based methods may be helpful to confirm an HPV association.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma exhibiting squamous differentiation; block-type p16 immunohistochemical staining as a surrogate marker of HPV association (occasional exceptions).

Desirable: positive direct HPV testing (in cases where p16 is negative/non–block-type or equivocal).

Staging

Tumours are staged according to the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Tumour stage is the most important prognostic factor. Histological patterns, HPV type, and grading do not seem to have prognostic implications {29665326; 29665323}.
