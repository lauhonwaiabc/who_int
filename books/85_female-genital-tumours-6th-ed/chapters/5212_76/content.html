5.2.1.2 Endometrial atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia

Definition

Endometrial atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia (EAH/EIN) is the simultaneous change of epithelial cytology and an increased number of endometrial glands in comparison with the stroma (crowded gland architecture), often within a morphologically defined region distinct from the surrounding endometrium or from entrapped normal glands.

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: complex atypical endometrial hyperplasia; simple atypical endometrial hyperplasia; endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Endometrium

Clinical features

Abnormal uterine bleeding is the most common presenting symptom.

Epidemiology

In a large case series, the incidence was 56 women per 100 000, with an average patient age of 50–60 years {19393600}. Hereditary susceptibility parallels that of heritable syndromes associated with increased risk for endometrioid endometrial carcinoma, including Cowden syndrome and Lynch syndrome {35001185; 32854222}.

Etiology

Hyperestrogenism, polycystic ovary syndrome, obesity, and diabetes are risk factors associated with an earlier age of onset {35001185; 37290247}. Endometrial polyps are another risk factor {20966706; 18637968}.

Pathogenesis

EAH/EIN emerges as a clonal expansion of neoplastic glands that begins as a localized lesion and may expand to involve the entire endometrium {10699996}. EAH/EIN harbours many of the genetic and epigenetic changes in endometrioid endometrial carcinoma. These include defective mismatch repair, PAX2 inactivation, and mutation of PTEN, CTNNB1 (encodes β-catenin), and KRAS {20631067; 38734880; 33506979}.

Macroscopic appearance

Many lesions have no distinguishing macroscopic features; others present as focal or diffuse thickening. EAH/EIN may arise within endometrial polyps {19851198; 18637968}.

Histopathology

EAH/EIN is composed of crowded aggregates of altered tubular or branching glands, cytologically distinct from the background architectural and cytological pattern. Increased intraglandular stratification and cribriforming may be seen, although the latter should not be prominent {3385513}. In an area of EAH/EIN, the glands exceed the stroma, which leads to a reduction of the stromal volume. The lesion must be of sufficient size that artefactual crowding can be excluded, and coincidental change of architectural and cytological alterations is evident. Nuclear appearance varies between patients but is distinct from that of the background from which the lesion has emerged. High-grade nuclear atypia is not a feature of EAH/EIN and, when present, may indicate a serous carcinoma. Metaplastic changes frequently occur, most commonly squamous morular (associated with CTNNB1 mutation), but mucinous, secretory, or tubal/ciliated changes can be seen {18637968; 35834400}. EAH/EIN mimics (basalis, polyp, dyssynchronous-phase endometrium, cystic atrophy) must be excluded. Endometrial hyperplasia without atypia and EAH/EIN can be distinguished based on a combination of architectural (whole-field vs geographical, respectively) and cytological features (uniform field vs changed from background, respectively) {7074572; 7694941}. A panel of PAX2, PTEN, and β-catenin might be a helpful diagnostic tool since aberrant immunohistochemical expression of ≥ 1 of these markers occurs in > 90% of EAH/EIN {20631067; 34545858; 38785042}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphologically defined endometrial changes with crowded glandular architecture (gland area greater than stromal area) and altered epithelial cytology; distinct appearance of epithelium relative to adjacent and/or entrapped non-neoplastic glands, which are usually evident.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

EAH/EIN is associated with a significant risk of progression to endometrioid carcinoma {4005805; 20576254}, calling for either surgical or medical management. With surgery, one third of women with EAH/EIN on biopsy are diagnosed with endometrial carcinoma in the hysterectomy specimen {16400639; 32343732}. EAH/EIN may be treated conservatively with hormones (oral progestins or progestin-secreting IUD), but the risk of recurrence is high, and these patients need close clinical follow-up and serial endometrial sampling {26630538; 17592264}.
