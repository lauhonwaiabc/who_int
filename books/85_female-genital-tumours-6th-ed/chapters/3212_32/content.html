3.2.1.2 Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), HPV-associated, is an invasive and/or exophytic malignant epithelial tumour exhibiting pure squamous differentiation and caused by persistent HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours primarily develop in the upper third of the vagina and are more frequently found on the posterior wall {18980291; 11812092; 32972784}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms include vaginal bleeding and discharge, postcoital bleeding, urinary symptoms, pelvic pain, and a foreign body sensation within the vagina {7295606}. The presence of simultaneous cervical or vulvar SCC, or a history of such cancers within the past 5 years, must be excluded before making a diagnosis of a vaginal primary.

Epidemiology

SCC is by far the commonest vaginal cancer, and HPV-associated vaginal SCC is more common than HPV-independent SCC. There is a temporal trend of a rising incidence globally. However, in 2020, vaginal cancers accounted for < 1% of all female cancer cases worldwide, with an estimated 17 908 new cases that year and about 8000 vaginal cancer deaths per year worldwide compared with 18 800 new cases and 8238 deaths in 2022 {38924674; 33538338; 38572751}. A significant geographical variation in the disease burden exists with a 4-fold difference between the regions with highest (some African and Asian regions) and lowest incidences {38924674; 33538338}. Most affected patients are in their seventh to ninth decades of life. As in cervical SCC, there is a positive association between the incidence of vaginal SCC and higher rates of HIV infection and sexual activity that carries a high risk of acquiring sexually transmitted diseases {38924674}. Most cases of vaginal cancer (78%) are linked to HPV infection {31862245}.

Etiology

This tumour is linked to persistent HPV infection. Most cases are associated with high-risk HPV types, with HPV16 being the most prevalent (~60%) {21166704; 25155250; 24785610}. Low-risk HPV types have been reported rarely {18500261}. Additional risk factors include a history of and treatment for other HPV-associated anogenital cancers, particularly cervical cancer; smoking; and immunosuppression, including HIV infection {16803507}.

Pathogenesis

HPV-associated vaginal SCC develops from a high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) with a similar pathogenesis to cervical HPV-associated SCC. See section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated. In one study using massively parallel sequencing, PIK3CA activating mutations were the most common genomic event in HPV-associated vaginal SCC {34650187}.

Macroscopic appearance

Vaginal SCC can display a variety of gross appearances, from ulcerated plaques to polypoid lesions or strictures.

Histopathology

The histological features are essentially identical to those of cervical HPV-associated SCCs. See section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated of the uterine cervix. As in the cervix, morphological examination cannot reliably distinguish between HPV-associated and HPV-independent SCCs, and p16 immunohistochemistry and/or HPV testing is necessary. Most HPV-associated SCCs show block-type p16 immunohistochemical expression. p53 immunohistochemistry is typically wild type. High-risk HPV testing may be performed in cases with equivocal or challenging to interpret p16 results.

The International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) does not recommend grading primary vaginal SCCs {35703457}.

Cytology

See section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated, of the uterine cervix.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of high-risk HPV by in situ hybridization or PCR-based methods may be helpful to confirm an HPV association.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: no previous (≤ 5 years) or concomitant SCC of cervix or vulva; invasive carcinoma exhibiting squamous differentiation; block-type p16 immunohistochemical staining as a surrogate marker of HPV association (occasional exceptions).

Desirable: positive HPV testing (in cases where p16 is negative/non–block-type or equivocal).

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis depends on factors including patient age, tumour stage, and tumour size {23488292; 16803507; 31873914; 29065399; 35703457}. For high-stage disease, the prognosis is poor, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 50% for all stages {30139838; 31873914}. In one study, the 5-year relapse-free and cancer-specific survival rates were 68% and 76%, respectively {31873914}.

Several studies have identified the presence of high-risk HPV as an independent favourable prognostic indicator for both disease-free and overall survival in primary vaginal SCCs. Specifically, HPV16-positive tumours have been linked to improved survival outcomes {22226684; 23402906; 21666483}. Furthermore, it is estimated that prophylactic HPV vaccination could reduce the incidence of HPV-related vaginal carcinoma by 60% {31360870}.
