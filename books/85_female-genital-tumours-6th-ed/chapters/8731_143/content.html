8.7.3.1 Malignant Brenner tumour

Definition

Malignant Brenner tumour is an ovarian carcinoma resembling an invasive urothelial carcinoma, associated with benign or borderline Brenner tumour.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

This tumour occurs in the ovary.

Clinical features

Patients present with an abdominal mass or pain. Some may have abnormal vaginal bleeding {3570630; 4005816; 38539441}. Tumours are usually unilateral (> 80%) and occasionally bilateral {3570630; 27130406; 38539441}.

Epidemiology

According to pathological case series and case reports, malignant Brenner tumours account for < 5% of all Brenner tumours {5040741; 2321577; 38539441}. These tumours occur in women aged > 50 years, with a median age of 56–60 years {38539441}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Malignant Brenner tumours arise from benign and borderline Brenner tumours. Molecular alterations include homozygous CDKN2A (p16) deletions; FGFR3, PIK3CA, and TP53 mutations; and MDM2 and CCND1 amplifications {19033864; 28639280; 33077920; 32322877}. Mutations in the TERT promoter, typical of urothelial bladder carcinomas, have not been found {27299795}. It has been suggested that activating PIK3CA mutations and CDKN2A loss may be early events, whereas FGFR3 and MDM2/TP53 alterations, which are mutually exclusive, indicate two different pathways for malignant Brenner tumour pathogenesis and are likely late events {33077920}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours have a median size of 100 mm {27130406}. They may be solid or cystic with mural nodules.

Histopathology

The tumour is composed of irregularly shaped masses of cytologically atypical urothelial-type cells, sometimes with focal squamous differentiation. Cystic areas within the tumour are lined by multilayered epithelium, which resembles invasive urothelial carcinoma with hyperchromatic pleomorphic nuclei, variably prominent nucleoli, dense eosinophilic cytoplasm, and prominent mitotic activity. Invasion may be difficult to detect because of the densely fibromatous background of the tumour, but a desmoplastic stromal reaction is helpful in identifying unequivocal stromal invasion. Rarely, the invasive component appears to arise directly from a benign Brenner tumour, without a borderline component {23202779}. Mucinous glandular elements and (more rarely) mucinous adenocarcinoma may coexist within the Brenner component. The absence of a benign or borderline Brenner component should raise the possibility of high-grade serous or endometrioid carcinoma with transitional cell–like differentiation, urothelial carcinoma, or squamous cell carcinoma {23018212; 23108017}.

Immunohistochemistry

These tumours are usually negative for WT1 and negative or weakly positive for ER and PR. Immunostaining for p16 and p53 may be variable depending on gene status. Immunohistochemical loss of p16 occurs in tumours with CDKN2A loss {32322877}. Most tumours have a wildtype p53 pattern {23018212; 19033864}. The data for GATA3 and p63 are very limited. Positive and negative GATA3 tumours have been reported, in contrast to benign and borderline Brenner tumours, which are positive. p63 has been reported to be positive in all Brenner tumours, including carcinomas {19092634; 32322877; 38539441}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive carcinoma with urothelial differentiation; a benign or borderline Brenner tumour component in the background.

Staging

Malignant Brenner tumour is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Fifty percent of tumours are confined to the ovary at diagnosis {38539441}. Patients with stage I tumours have a recurrence rate of 24% and a disease-specific 5-year survival rate of 94.5%. Patients with extraovarian spread (stages II, III, IV) have a recurrence rate of 64% and a disease-specific 5-year survival rate of 51.3% {27130406; 38539441}.
