17.1.0.6 Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53)

Definition

Li–Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) (TP53) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic germline variants in TP53 and characterized by multiple primary neoplasms in children and adults, with a predominance of soft tissue sarcomas, osteosarcoma, breast cancer, brain tumours, and adrenocortical carcinoma.

MIM numbering

151623 Li–Fraumeni syndrome; LFS

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: sarcoma family syndrome of Li and Fraumeni.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

LFS is associated with cancers of the breast, soft tissue, bone, brain, adrenal glands, and less frequently female genital tract.

Clinical features

LFS is characterized by the early onset of a broad spectrum of cancers and a high lifetime cancer risk. The five core LFS-related cancers are adrenocortical carcinomas, breast cancer, CNS tumours, osteosarcomas, and soft tissue sarcomas, which constitute about 70% of LFS-related neoplasms {29076966}. In addition, a broad range of other tumours, including lymphomas, gastrointestinal malignancies (gastric and colorectal cancers), melanoma, and lung cancer have been described in LFS families {19204208; 24706533; 26101242}. More recent studies have also reported cancers of the female reproductive organs (ovarian and endometrial carcinomas), the urogenital tract (carcinomas of the kidney and the prostate), and the eyes {14583457; 28509937}.

The classic definition of LFS required sarcoma in a patient before age 45 years, a first-degree relative with any cancer before age 45 years, and a first- or second-degree relative with any cancer before the age of 45 years or sarcoma at any age {29039119; 3409256}. This clinical definition has since been expanded to allow a broader recognition of individuals and families with LFS.

Patients and families without the full spectrum of classic criteria have been designated as having Li–Fraumeni–like syndrome (LFL), which has been further defined clinically by two sets of criteria, proposed by Birch and Eeles, respectively {29039119; 8118819; 8718514}. The Birch criteria are close to those of classic LFS but do not require a sarcoma, whereas the Eeles definition of LFL includes two first- or second-degree relatives with LFS component tumours at any age, rather than the three required by the classic criteria {29039119; 8718514}. The investigation of other family genes and non-coding regions of the TP53 gene may explain the occurrence of LFL {30086788}.

Epidemiology

More than 1500 germline (constitutional) variants associated with LFS, LFL, and probands with a family history or fulfilling the Chompret criteria are included in version R21 (January 2025) of the TP53 Database from 1990 onwards [[The TP53 Database [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 [cited 2025 Feb 21]. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov.]] {35352025}. The incidence of TP53 germline variants in Europe and North America seems to be 1 case per 5000–20 000 individuals {19204208; 12672316}. In southern Brazil, the founder mutation of TP53 NM000546.6: c.1010G>A p.(Arg337His) occurs with an incidence of 1 case per 300 individuals and is present in 0.3% of the general population {16494995; 24936644}. Within this patient population, the p.Arg337His mutation detected by tumour genomic profiling is frequently germline in origin {34270331}. There is recent evidence of the occurrence of de novo TP53 variants in about 10–20% of individuals with LFS; about 20% of these seem to occur during embryogenesis {29070607}. The highest pick-up rate of TP53 germline variants appears to be in patients with early-onset breast cancer {30240537}. Gynaecological tumours are rare. Ovarian cancers account for 1.9% of malignancies associated with LFS (52 of 2741 included tumours), with an even lower occurrence of tumours at other gynaecological sites [[The TP53 Database [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 [cited 2025 Feb 21]. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov.]] {35352025; 27328919}. Ovarian cancer in LFS presents at a median age of 39.5 years, compared with 64.3 years for sporadic cases {14583457}. Uterine cancers account for an even smaller number of malignancies in LFS. In a study of 151 subjects with uterine serous carcinoma, 2 had germline variants in TP53, even with no personal or family history suggestive of Li–Fraumeni syndrome {22811390}.

Etiology

The genetic basis of autosomal dominant LFS is the presence of a germline pathogenic variant in the TP53 tumour suppressor gene {1978757; 2259385}. A broad spectrum of TP53 variants across the coding regions of the gene typically resulting in a dominant negative or loss-of-function effect have been found in LFS families; however, 20–40% of LFS individuals and the majority of LFL families may lack detectable variants {14583457; 12619118}. The lack of 100% concordance between TP53 variants and the classic LFS phenotype may be explained by posttranslational alterations, complete deletion, or the effects of modifier genes on the phenotype generated by the presence other genetic events {21779515}. The most frequent TP53 variants are missense variants at specific hotspot codons, mainly clustering in the DNA-binding domain and interfering with DNA binding or disrupting the binding surface structure necessary to modulate target gene transcription {20182602}. Other alteration types include nonsense, splice site, frameshift, large deletions, insertions, and complex rearrangements, with some variants occurring outside of the DNA-binding domain {27984644; 26014290}.

Four patterns of germline variants account for about 80% of the alterations: G:C>A:T at CpG islands in almost 50%, followed by A:T>G:C, G:C>A:T, and deletions (in ~10% each), predominantly involving 11 codons within the coding regions of exons 5–10, with codons 175, 213, 245, 248, 273, 282, and 337 most commonly (> 3%) [[The TP53 Database [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health; 2025. Version R21, Jan 2025 [cited 2025 Feb 21]. Available from: https://tp53.cancer.gov.]] {35352025}. Lesions within introns or the regulatory regions of the gene have been identified, although their functional significance is unclear and there are open questions about the significance of some TP53 variants occurring in cancer {28254861}. There is some evidence that genes of the Fanconi pathway, RECQ family genes, and other genes found in breast cancer are associated with LFL cancers {30086788}. Widespread use of next-generation and other sequencing technologies are uncovering higher numbers of variants in TP53, as well as in other genes in LFS and LFL {28509937}. This is leading to changes in the classification of Li–Fraumeni spectrum to include phenotypic Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria in the absence of a pathogenic TP53 variant; Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals meeting clinical LFS criteria with a pathogenic TP53 variant; attenuated Li–Fraumeni syndrome for individuals with a pathogenic TP53 variant who do not meet LFS testing criteria, and incidental Li–Fraumeni syndrome for those individuals who carry a pathogenic variant without a family history of cancer {34709361}.

Pathogenesis

The p53 protein is a multifunctional transcription factor involved in a wide range of biological processes {32404993; 28886379}. Its best characterized functions are in the control of cell-cycle progression, DNA integrity, evolution of genomic alterations in the context of tumour progression {35978189}, and the survival of cells exposed to DNA-damaging agents. Evidence indicates that p53 also regulates other important processes, such as cell oxidative metabolism, the cellular response to nutrient deprivation, fertility, ferroptosis, and stem cell maintenance. The extent and consequences of the biological response elicited by p53 vary according to stress, the specific mutation conferred {28948977}, and cell type {28886379}. The functions of p53 rely mainly on its transcriptional activity, but it can also act via interactions with various proteins {21941372; 30525089}.

In LFS-associated tumours, TP53 is usually inactivated by a second hit involving the wildtype allele. Mutant p53 isoforms differ from each other in the extent to which they have lost suppressor function and in their capacity to inhibit wildtype p53 in a dominant negative manner {12826609; 17311302}. In addition, some p53 mutants seem to exert oncogenic activity, but the molecular basis of this gain-of-function phenotype is still unclear {24651012; 30224644}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of tumours in LFS is similar to that of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Most gynaecological tumours have been included in larger studies of tumour sites in LFS patients, and information on specific tumour histology is sparse; case reports have generally included more detailed information on tumour histology. Tumours that have been observed in the context of a germline TP53 variant include endometrioid endometrial adenocarcinomas {33990091, 37344881, 25588929}, uterine serous carcinoma {22811390}, uterine mixed endometrioid and serous carcinoma {22811390}, small cell carcinoma of the ovary (hypercalcaemic type) {28177947}, yolk sac tumour {37344881}, undifferentiated carcinoma and endometrioid carcinoma of the fallopian tube and peritoneum {22006311}, and pleomorphic liposarcoma of the uterine cervix {28177947}. Gestational choriocarcinomas have been reported both in a woman with LFS and in a woman without LFS carrying a fetus with a paternally derived TP53 pathogenic variant {28477316, 29581140}. Incidental bilateral serous tubal intraepithelial carcinomas have been observed, raising the question of risk-reduction surgery in this patient group {29945152}. Similar to BRCA variant carriers, the p53 signature has also been documented in the distal fallopian tubes of patients with LFS; however, the natural history of these lesions is uncertain at this time {19834951}.

Other cancers may involve many other organs, with breast, soft tissue and bone sarcomas, brain tumours, and adrenocortical carcinomas being the most frequent {26014290}. The most common types of soft tissue sarcomas are leiomyosarcomas, rhabdomyosarcomas, liposarcomas, and undifferentiated sarcomas that can be challenging to classify. Osteosarcoma is the most frequent bone tumour. Among the brain tumours, glioblastomas and other types of gliomas, choroid plexus carcinomas, medulloblastomas, and ependymomas have been found.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Individuals who may be candidates for TP53 germline testing are selected based on the 2015 Chompret criteria (see #56165Box 17.XXX) {26014290} and the recommendations of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN; see #56166Box 17.XXX) {30333936}. Increased constitutional and somatic tumour testing has led to challenges in some cases in distinguishing among pathogenic germline variants, known polymorphisms, variants of unknown significance, somatic variants, and clonal haematopoiesis {31533767}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: pathogenic germline variants in TP53 and/or appropriate clinical history.

Staging

Stage according to the affected organ site.

Prognosis and prediction

In male patients, the specific cancer risks at ≤ 15 years, 16–45 years, and > 45 years are 19%, 27%, and 54%, respectively. In female patients, the respective risks in these same age groups are 12%, 82%, and 100%, with breast cancer incidence responsible for the observed sex difference {16912210}. Pathogenic variants in TP53 as seen in LFS confers an elevated risk of radiation-induced secondary malignancies and possibly increased sensitivity to low-dose radiation exposure by diagnostic methods such as mammography {23355100}. Current NCCN guidelines recommend that therapeutic radiation be avoided unless locoregional risk reduction or overall survival is greater than the risk of secondary malignancy. Diagnostic radiation should be minimized to the extent possible without sacrificing accuracy (https://www.nccn.org).
