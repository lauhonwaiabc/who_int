8.4.3.1 Endometrioid carcinoma

Definition

Endometrioid carcinoma of ovary is a malignant epithelial neoplasm displaying varying proportions of glandular, papillary, and solid architecture, with the neoplastic cells showing endometrioid differentiation, similar to endometrioid carcinoma of the uterine corpus.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Ovary

Clinical features

Patients typically present with symptoms related to a pelvic mass, or the tumour may be an incidental finding.

Epidemiology

Endometrioid carcinoma accounts for approximately 10% of ovarian carcinomas {20407318; 29718305}. The mean patient age is 55 years {29718305}.

Etiology

These tumours may occur in the setting of Lynch syndrome {25025451; 29716739}, and a genetic susceptibility locus (5q12.3) has also been identified {28346442}. Other risk factors include endometriosis (odds ratio: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.67–2.48) {22361336}, hormone therapy, and a first-degree family history of breast cancer {27325851}, whereas early menopause, tubal ligation, and high parity are protective factors {23315066}.

Pathogenesis

Most tumours (85–90%) arise in endometriosis / endometriotic cysts, less frequently from endometrioid adenofibromas or borderline tumours. As many as 25% of patients have concurrent endometrial hyperplasia or endometrioid carcinoma {2258081; 28194593; 26832770; 26832771}, with most synchronous malignancies demonstrating a clonal relationship {26832770; 26832771; 37922918; 40145935}. However, there is a shift in DNA methylation signatures between endometrial and ovarian tumours {37652400}, and clinical behaviour appears to depend on the histological criteria and molecular features (e.g. mutation-type p53), rather than the clonal origin {37922918}.

The most common molecular alterations in ovarian endometrioid carcinomas affect the WNT/β-catenin signalling pathway (CTNNB1 mutations, 53%), the PI3K pathway (PIK3CA, 40%; PTEN, 17%), the MAPK pathway (KRAS, 33%), and the SWI/SNF complex (ARID1A, 30%) {23765252; 17418409}. The associated precursor lesions often exhibit shared gene variants, including PIK3CA, ARID1A, KRAS, and CTNNB1 {29604057; 20942669; 30657901; 16429393; 28776572; 30256257}. Analogous to their endometrial counterparts, ovarian endometrioid carcinomas can be classified into four molecular subtypes defined by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): (1) hypermutated/microsatellite instability-high due to mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR; 7–19%); (2) ultramutated/POLE-mutated due to variants in the POLE exonuclease domain (3–11%); 3) copy-number high/mutation-type p53 (11–24%); and (4) copy-number low/no specific molecular profile (NSMP; 58–66%) {28776572; 22189970; 26799366; 26166557; 30256257; 31340883; 33020491; 32384322; 34446267; 34158040}.

Macroscopic appearance

Endometrioid carcinomas are usually unilateral and large (mean size: 11 cm), with a smooth outer surface {28264438}. They have a variably solid and/or cystic cut surface. Haemorrhage or necrosis may be extensive. If arising within an endometriotic cyst, the tumours may form intracystic polypoidal nodules.

Histopathology

Endometrioid carcinomas show a wide spectrum of appearances similar to those seen in uterine endometrioid carcinomas. The most common pattern is a confluent back-to-back or cribriform glandular proliferation. The glands have smooth luminal borders and are lined by neoplastic cells with round to oval nuclei, open chromatin, and low to moderate cytological atypia. Less commonly, a destructive pattern of invasion with desmoplastic stromal reaction is present. Solid architecture usually constitutes a minor proportion of the tumour, but it can be predominant in approximately 10% of cases, often in association with geographical necrosis. Morular or squamous differentiation occurs in approximately 50%, with round aggregates of bland ovoid to spindled cells lacking keratinization (morules) or, less often, with overt squamous differentiation with keratin formation and intercellular bridges, occasionally associated with the formation of keratin granulomas. Mucinous differentiation may be seen and, when extensive, may result in misclassification of the tumour as mucinous carcinoma. Other cytoplasmic changes include secretory features, characterized by supranuclear and subnuclear vacuoles resembling secretory endometrium and nonspecific clear cell change, which may result in misclassification as clear cell carcinoma. Tumour cells may form small tubular glands or nested and corded arrangements, mimicking sex cord stromal tumours (Sertoli cell tumour, Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour, or adult granulosa cell tumour) {38418687}. Other less common morphological patterns include spindled cell {7503356}, oxyphilic {7814190}, ciliated {8811387}, and corded and hyalinized {15644772}. Rare associations with undifferentiated carcinoma (dedifferentiated carcinoma) {16306785} and neuroendocrine carcinoma {8886877; 1384368} have been reported. Endometrioid carcinoma may show yolk sac tumour–like {27334714; 8764742, 32558949}, trophoblast-like {16527336}, or pilomatrix-like morphology with solid nests of basaloid cells and prominent ghost cell keratinization {36553153}.

Tumours previously referred to as seromucinous carcinoma are composed predominantly of serous and endocervical-type mucinous epithelium, often with foci containing clear cells and areas of endometrioid and squamous differentiation {25723110}. In view of their overlapping morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular profiles, most are now reclassified as a pattern of endometrioid carcinoma, usually endometrioid carcinoma with mucinous differentiation {28125452; 32023964}. In a minority, the tumour is reclassified as low-grade serous carcinoma (with mucinous differentiation) and, in a small subset, as mucinous carcinoma {28125452}.

Endometrioid carcinomas are typically graded using the FIGO grading system for endometrial endometrioid carcinoma. Most tumours are grade 1 or 2. An alternative system is Silverberg grading {9486579}. Grade 3 endometrioid carcinoma is a diagnosis of exclusion after exclusion of high-grade serous carcinoma and metastatic tumours.

Most endometrioid carcinomas (81–85%) are positive for PAX8 and estrogen and progesterone receptors {26974996; 29901523; 30480647}. However, PAX8 may be negative (11%) {18724243}, especially in tumours with sex cord–like pattern (67%) {38418687}. A subset is positive for CDX2 (48%) or SATB2 (15%) {30326146; 31239549}. Napsin A and WT1 are usually negative, although napsin A may be positive in tumours with secretory change and WT1 may be expressed in 10–14% {29309296}.

Cytology

Cytology reveals cellular specimens, often with large cohesive cell clusters exhibiting gland formation. Pap staining reveals round to oval/elongated nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli, mild nuclear membrane irregularities, low to intermediate N:C ratios, and wispy cytoplasm. Squamous differentiation may be present. It may be impossible to distinguish a grade 3 endometrioid carcinoma from high-grade serous carcinoma, although the latter is significantly more common; cell blocks are helpful.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a tumour with confluent or destructive stromal invasion; low to moderately atypical endometrioid glands with variable solid growth pattern.

Desirable: squamous, secretory, or mucinous differentiation; associated endometriosis, endometriotic cyst, endometrioid adenofibroma, and/or endometrioid borderline tumour.

Staging

Endometrioid carcinoma is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Stage is the most important prognostic factor. At presentation, most tumours are confined to the ovary (stage I) {20407318}. Lymph node involvement is extremely rare {37567599}. In a SEER analysis, the survival rate was > 95% for stage IA/IB disease, 89% for stage IC/II, and 51% for stage III/IV {29718305}. Peritoneal keratin granulomas, in the absence of tumour cells, do not warrant upstaging {1698340}. Candidate prognostic biomarkers include estrogen and progesterone receptors {23845225; 28264438}, p16 {30062862}, p53, POLE {28776572}, CDX2, CTNNB1 {30326146}, and CD8 {29049607} by immunohistochemistry, as well as POLE {28776572} and CTNNB1 {30326146} gene mutations. TP53-mutated tumours have greater genomic complexity with frequent copy-number alterations and are associated with poor prognosis, whereas patients with POLE-mutated and MMR-deficient tumours have excellent survival rates {28776572}. In addition to providing a prognostic value, MMR immunohistochemistry should be performed where possible in all tumours {30256257; 26799366} for Lynch syndrome screening, followed by MLH1 promoter methylation testing in MLH1/PMS2-deficient tumours. Germline testing for Lynch syndrome should be performed in patients with MSH2-/MSH6-, MSH6- or PMS2-deficient tumours and those with MLH1-/PMS2-deficient tumours in the absence of MLH1 promoter hypermethylation.

Patients with CTNNB1-mutated tumours, which are often low-grade with squamous differentiation and low genomic complexity, have a favourable prognosis, in contrast to patients with CTNNB1-mutated endometrial endometrioid carcinomas, which are associated with worse outcomes {33020491; 10433945; 30326146; 28651933; 32769429; 10433945; 11329142; 30326146}. Thus, the prognostic value of the TCGA subtypes in ovarian endometrioid carcinomas appears similar to that of their endometrial counterparts {34446267}, and the ProMisE (Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for Endometrial Cancer) algorithm based on immunohistochemistry for MMR proteins and p53, and POLE sequencing can be applied to these tumours.

Synchronous (co-existent) endometrioid carcinomas of the endometrium and ovary have been shown to be clonally related {26832770; 26832771}. Their indolent behaviour {24300466} supports conservative management when the following criteria are met: both tumours are low-grade and confined to the ovary and uterus, there is < 50% myometrial invasion, and no lymphovascular invasion or additional metastases are present {30723764; 33604759; 30413340}. Thus, tumours fulfilling these criteria should be managed as independent synchronous (rather than metastatic) primary neoplasms. The FIGO 2023 staging system recognizes both the clonal nature of these tumours and their associations with indolent behaviour by considering them endometrial primaries and assigning them to the IA3 category in cases of a unilateral ovarian neoplasm without surface involvement (pT1a) {37337978}. Currently, there is no guidance for tumours that do not fulfil the FIGO 2023 criteria for stage IA3 (e.g. superficially invasive endometrial endometrioid carcinoma with bilateral ovarian endometrioid carcinomas or non-myoinvasive endometrial endometrioid carcinoma with focal ovarian surface involvement), and these should be considered metastases.
