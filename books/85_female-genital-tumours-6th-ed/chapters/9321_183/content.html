9.3.2.1 Gonadoblastoma

Definition

Gonadoblastoma is a distinctive form of in situ germ cell neoplasia consisting of germ cells, similar to those of dysgerminoma / seminoma / germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS) of the testis, arranged in nests with admixed, incompletely differentiated sex cord cells.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the gonads.

Clinical features

About 50% of patients appear as virilized female patients, 30% as non-virilized female patients, and 20% as male patients with hypospadias and cryptorchidism {4193741}. Most cases are found during investigation for a possible disorder of sex development, usually because of ambiguous external genitalia in infancy. Some cases present later, typically with primary amenorrhoea or findings related to a malignant germ cell tumour. Features of a syndrome (see Etiology, below) may also lead to discovery.

Epidemiology

Gonadoblastoma is rare and almost always restricted to patients with gonadal maldevelopment caused by anomalies in genes involved in gonadal embryogenesis {31805291; 34560684}. Such dysgenetic gonads lead to gonadoblastoma in as many as 50–60% of patients {4193741; 16882833; 22423509; 25129544}. Patient age ranges from neonatal to the fourth decade of life {4193741; 34560684}. There are no established geographical, racial, or environmental associations.

Etiology

Patients with germline or acquired mutations in the genes WT1 (Denys–Drash syndrome, Frasier syndrome) and SRY (Swyer syndrome) develop dysgenetic gonads and gonadoblastomas. Also required is the GBY region of the Y chromosome, including the candidate TSPY1 gene, in gonadal tissue {8533770; 19306348}. As many as 30% of patients with Turner syndrome and Y-chromosomal material develop gonadoblastoma {25535833; 27052295; 36305565}, as do approximately 5% of patients with androgen insensitivity syndrome {27862157; 26608236}.

Pathogenesis

Most patients are phenotypically female, but the involved gonads carry Y-chromosomal genes; they are identifiable as testes in 20% and as streaks in 20% of cases {4193741}. None are recognizable ovaries. Because of mutations in the pathway of testicular development or absence of functional androgen, SOX9 expression is inadequate to support formation of normal seminiferous tubules and Sertoli cells, with FOXL2 predominating in sex cord stromal cells {18348162; 21682576}. As a consequence, germ cells have delayed maturation, with coexpression of OCT3/4 (POU5F1) and TSPY {15948118; 15998778}, which, with enhanced KIT/KITLG signalling, are believed to promote their neoplastic transformation {18566970; 28544305}. TSPY1 is thought to act as an oncogene in germ cells lacking adjacent functioning Sertoli cells {10090875}. Activating KIT mutations are found in > 50% of dysgerminomas in patients without disorders of sex development but in only 6% of dysgerminomas in patients with disorders of sex development {22937135}. The pathogenesis of gonadoblastoma in patients with normal karyotypes and with no evidence of disorders of sex development is unclear {30252730}.

Macroscopic appearance

They are mostly poorly defined tan to grey lesions with a gritty texture. With progression to invasive germ cell tumour, features specific to that entity are seen.

Histopathology

Multiple variably sized round nests are distributed in a fibrous to cellular gonadal stroma; testis or streak gonad may be found at the periphery. The nests contain three components: germ cells, small sex cord cells, and round globular basement membrane deposits. Some germ cells are identical to those of dysgerminoma/seminoma/GCNIS, but others may resemble spermatogonia. The sex cord cells may surround the germ cells or the basement membrane deposits or form a palisade at the periphery of nests. Leydig-like cells may be present in the surrounding stroma, and laminated psammomatous calcifications are frequent on the basement membrane deposits. The calcifications may coalesce, and their presence may be the only sign of a burnt-out gonadoblastoma. Occasional cases show large, anastomosing nests resembling germinoma (dysgerminoma/seminoma) {27454939}. In two thirds of cases, the surrounding areas have a corded pattern of the same component cells {27454939}. These have been termed both “dissecting gonadoblastoma” and “undifferentiated gonadal tissue” {27454939; 16608895}. In the single largest study, about half of the cases showed germinoma, with other germ cell tumours (embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, or teratoma) in 10% {4193741}. These areas show the characteristic morphology of those tumours.

Immunohistochemical staining is positive in a subset of the germ cells for markers characteristic of germinoma/GCNIS (SALL4, OCT3/4, podoplanin (D2-40), PLAP). The sex cord cells are typically positive for inhibin, calretinin, SF1, FOXL2, and (weakly) SOX9 {24766183; 21682576; 27454939; 23722510; 26107563}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: in situ germ cell neoplasia arranged as rounded nests containing heterogeneous germ cells (some resembling germinoma/GCNIS), small sex cord cells, round deposits of basement membrane.

Desirable: expression of germ cell and sex cord stromal immunohistochemical markers.

Staging

Gonadoblastoma is an in situ malignancy and should not be staged.

Prognosis and prediction

Surgical excision of gonadoblastoma is curative. Bilateral gonadectomy is indicated because of the high risk of contralateral gonadoblastoma and subsequent development of a malignant germ cell tumour. If a malignant germ cell tumour has developed, the prognosis depends on its histological components and stage.
