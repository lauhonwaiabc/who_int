8.2.3.2 High-grade serous carcinoma

Definition

High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) of ovary is a high-grade epithelial neoplasm demonstrating serous differentiation.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Ovary

Clinical features

Presenting symptoms are nonspecific and relate to involvement of abdominopelvic organs. More than 80% of patients present with FIGO stage III or IV disease. Elevated serum CA125 levels (> 35 units/mL [> 35 kU/L]) are found in > 90% of patients, but this is a nonspecific marker {25528497}.

Epidemiology

HGSC is the most common ovarian carcinoma (~70%) {30121132}, with higher incidence in White populations. Patients present over a wide age range (50–70 years). Age > 60 years, a family history of breast/ovarian carcinoma, and infertility are risk factors, whereas multiparity, breastfeeding, oral contraceptive use, late menarche, and early menopause are protective factors {22343225; 17240246; 19107446}.

Etiology

Approximately 20% of patients with HGSC have germline alterations in BRCA1 or BRCA2 (also see section BRCA-related cancer predisposition syndromes (BRCA1, BRCA2)).

Pathogenesis

Nearly every tumour harbours a deleterious TP53 mutation {20229506; 26166714; 35328131} (with non-synonymous mutations being more common than frameshift mutations or deletions) and complex high-level copy-number alterations. Amplifications of CCNE1, MYC, and MECOM and deletions of PTEN, RB1, and NF1 are some of the more common copy-number alterations {21720365; 26017449}. Homologous recombination–deficient (HRD) tumours (~50% of HGSC) lack the ability to repair double-strand DNA breaks {24240112}, and CCNE1 amplifications are mutually exclusive with BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathway disruption. Approximately 30% of HGSCs have alterations in BRCA1 or BRCA2 – 20% with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations (15% germline, 5% somatic) and 11% with BRCA1 epigenetic silencing via hypermethylation {21720365; 23257362; 29587661} (also see section BRCA-related cancer predisposition syndromes (BRCA1, BRCA2)). A small percentage (~2%) are associated with germline mutations in moderate penetrance genes such as RAD51C/D, ATM, BRIP1, PALB2, and CHEK2 {29054568; 32359370; 25622547} and genomic aberrations in other homologous recombination genes {21720365}. Other patients may have an enigmatic familial predisposition to developing ovarian carcinoma.

HGSCs arise from tubal-type epithelium, usually in the fallopian fimbria and less commonly on the ovarian surface or within ovarian epithelial inclusion cysts. Rarely, transformation from serous borderline tumours or low-grade serous carcinomas has been reported {39389422; 17592266; 32801341; 22082603}. A number of morphological and molecular studies have generated abundant evidence supporting that the majority of HGSCs of the ovary or peritoneum arise from serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma {18757330; 21502498; 24332043; 29061967; 31493898; 33011111; 35859323}. Although this view is not unanimously agreed upon, the vast majority of pathologists and clinicians in a survey accept this concept {27801755}. A subset of high-grade serous ovarian carcinomas may develop from high-grade transformation of a low-grade serous tumour {17592266; 39028145; 39389422}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours are usually bilateral, large, and exophytic, and they demonstrate solid and papillary growth and fluid-filled cysts. The solid areas are tan to white and frequently display extensive necrosis. There is commonly extensive extraovarian involvement {26630225}.

Histopathology

These tumours typically exhibit solid, papillary, labyrinthine (with slit-like spaces), glandular, and/or cribriform architecture. Nuclei are large and markedly atypical (nuclear size variability of > 3-fold), with high mitotic activity, typically > 5 mitoses /mm2 (equating to > 12 mitoses /10 HPF of 0.55 mm in diameter and 0.24 mm2 in area), including atypical mitoses. Necrosis and multinucleated cells are common. Unusual morphologies include microcystic architecture with intraluminal and intracytoplasmic mucin, cytoplasmic clearing, and (very rarely) signet-ring cells {11603214}; follicular patterns; and spindle cells. Occasional tumours may architecturally mimic serous borderline tumour {26232113}. Rarely, a component of somatically derived yolk sac tumour or choriocarcinoma may be present {27334714; 32404953; 32558949} (see section Yolk sac tumour and section Non-gestational choriocarcinoma, p. XXX). For yolk sac tumour, the most commonly encountered patterns are microcystic/reticular and glandular {27334714}, which may exhibit morphological overlap with the carcinomatous areas. A small subset of ovarian serous carcinomas are morphologically ambiguous, which may make subclassification as low-grade or high-grade difficult {31876585; 40327333}.

HGSC with HRD may display variant architecture, which includes SET (solid, endometrial-like, transitional) patterns {27150160; 22193042}. Micropapillae, giant cells, and lymphocytic infiltrate may also be seen.

Approximately 90% of tumours demonstrate nuclear expression of WT1 (usually diffuse), and approximately 95% exhibit a mutation-type pattern of p53 expression represented by strong diffuse staining in at least 80% of cells, no expression (with an intact internal control), or (rarely) diffuse cytoplasmic staining with weak nuclear staining {27840695; 30059451; 33899789}. See Chapter 1: General Introduction. p53 immunohistochemistry is used as a surrogate marker for TP53 mutations, but as many as 10% of HGSCs may demonstrate discordant immunohistochemical and mutational findings {35328131}.

CK7, CA125, and PAX8 are typically positive, and ER is frequently expressed. p16 demonstrates strong diffuse (block-type) immunoreactivity in ≥ 50% of tumours {26974996; 24336157; 26173002}. CK20, CEA, HNF1β, and napsin A are typically negative in these tumours, while PTEN and ARID1A expression is retained (a subset of cases have loss of PTEN expression) {18830127}.

Cytology

HGSCs are characterized by 3D tissue fragments of pleomorphic tumour cells, with numerous variably sized cytoplasmic vacuoles, marked nuclear atypia, and a range of N:C ratios, with high N:C ratios being most common in dissociated cells. Cells may be seen in papillae or nondescript tissue fragments, or singly. Psammoma bodies are commonly seen. Mutation-type p53 (overexpression, null, or cytoplasmic) and block-type positive p16 (CDKN2A) can be helpful in distinguishing high-grade from low-grade serous carcinoma when morphological features are indeterminate {23558569}, although it should be noted that strong diffuse p16 immunoreactivity is not present in all HGSCs {24336157; 26173002}.

Because a significant number of serous carcinomas of the adnexa and the peritoneum present with ascites, this often constitutes the only sample for a diagnosis that leads patients to treatment by neoadjuvant chemotherapy, before a biopsy or histopathological examination is performed, in certain regions of the world. Cell blocks are useful for performing a wide range of immunostains that allow a specific diagnosis of primary serous carcinoma, including high- versus low-grade and in differentiating primary versus metastatic adenocarcinoma {32769333}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: high-grade serous tumour; marked nuclear atypia (nuclear size variability of > 3-fold) and/or high mitotic activity; exclude tubal carcinoma including serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma to establish an ovarian primary site (see Table 8.0.1).

Desirable (in selected cases): WT1 immunoreactivity; mutation-type p53 expression.

Staging

HGSC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

More than 95% of patients have extraovarian disease at presentation. Median 5-year survival rates are between 15% and 55%, with stage, extent of residual disease after debulking, platinum sensitivity, and HRD status being the major prognostic factors {31002578; 31046081; 38960218}. Despite high initial responses to chemotherapy in about 65% of patients, most cases recur and are clinically characterized as platinum-sensitive (favourable; recurrence > 6 months after treatment) or platinum-resistant (unfavourable; recurrence < 6 months after treatment) {1999708}. About 10–15% of patients with HGSC have long-term survival, but molecular explanations for exceptional response remain unclear.

In the absence of a germline BRCA mutation, molecular somatic testing in the upfront setting should include identification of BRCA1 or BRCA2 status, loss of heterozygosity, or HRD status, which may also determine indications for poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor maintenance therapy. HRD tumours are more platinum-sensitive than proficient tumours {24240112}, and patients with BRCA1 or BRCA2 germline mutations have significantly better prognosis {22274685; 25398451; 33563640}. The introduction of PARP inhibitors has been an important development in the treatment of women with ovarian cancer, and targeting DNA damage repair with PARP inhibitors as a maintenance therapy significantly improves progression-free survival for both BRCA1 and BRCA2 carriers and women with HRD {30345884; 31562799}. Restoration of BRCA function by reversion mutation, in which secondary mutations within homologous recombination repair genes restore previously impaired protein function, is a common mechanism of acquired resistance to platinum chemotherapy and PARP inhibitor therapy, in HRD tumours {24202391; 21709188}.

CCNE1 amplifications (20%), lack of CD8-positive tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, and hypermethylation of the TAP1 promoter region in 6p21.3 are associated with a poor prognosis. Predictive markers include ERBB2 amplification and/or overexpression (for anti-ERBB2 therapy), and FRα (FOLR1 immunohistochemistry for antibody-drug conjugate therapy) {26017449; 30734108; 26625204; 19258951; 24728075; 28152503; 37212825; 36716407}.

The response to chemotherapy may be assessed by clinical, radiological, serological, and histological methods. The pathological chemotherapy response score (CRS), assessed in the omentum, has been widely adopted {26089092}, because it shows good correlation with overall and progression-free survival {31846531}. CRS1 corresponds to no or minimal response, CRS2 to appreciable tumour response but with residual tumour, and CRS3 to complete or near-complete response {31118141}.
