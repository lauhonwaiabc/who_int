4.5.2 Neuroendocrine carcinoma

Definition

Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) is a high-grade malignant epithelial neoplasm with neuroendocrine morphology, which generally exhibits immunohistochemical evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: mixed neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Carcinoma admixed with small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma/ Combined small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Carcinoma admixed with large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma/ Combined large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma

Localization

These tumours are localized to the cervix.

Clinical features

Patients with cervical NEC typically present in a similar manner to other cervical malignancies, usually with abnormal vaginal bleeding and/or detection of a cervical mass. NECs are extremely aggressive neoplasms and often present at high stage with spread beyond the cervix and with nodal and/or distant metastases.

Epidemiology

Based on a 2018 systematic review of the literature, cervical NEC is a rare tumour accounting for 1.4% of all cervical cancers. Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), and other subtypes were identified in 80.4, 12.0, and 7.6% of cases, respectively {29728073}. Although SCNEC is the most frequent subtype, it is a rare tumour with an annual incidence in a high-income country of 0.06 cases per 100 000 women {18515524}. This tumour is diagnosed at a mean age of 49 years {18515524; 25341572}, whereas SCNEC at other sites typically presents in older patients {26945341; 29019870; 38789995}. LCNEC of the uterine cervix is more uncommon than SCNEC, typically affecting premenopausal women (mean age: 34 years) {9255253; 29728073}. Combined SCNEC and LCNEC are even more uncommon neoplasms.

Etiology

Most SCNECs, LCNECs, and mixed neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine carcinomas of the uterine cervix are associated with high-risk HPV infection, most commonly types 16 and 18, and represent HPV-associated neoplasms {29248196; 34023130}. Although HPV18 is more common than HPV16 in SCNECs, LCNECs show a similar proportion of HPV16 and HPV18 infection, suggestive of a different biology in these different histological types of NEC {29248196}. The HPV independence of some NECs is further underscored by a large study, which demonstrated that nearly one third of cervical NECs are HPV negative {38625939}.

Pathogenesis

Cervical SCNECs exhibit recurrent molecular alterations involving the KRAS, PIK3CA, PTEN, KMT2D (MLL2), NOTCH2, ARID1A, ERBB2, MYC, RPL10, WDFY3, RB1, and TP53/BRCA pathways {29505425; 36069807; 37920164; 38625939}. TP53 and/or RB1 alterations tend to be enriched in HPV-independent tumours {36069807}. SCNECs also harbour frequent copy-number gains (3q27.1 and 19q13.12) and copy-number losses (1p36.21, 5q31.3, 6p22.2, 9q21.11, 11p15.5). An integrated analysis involving genomic alterations, HPV integration, and transcriptomic sequencing revealed that SCNEC may have different transcriptome subtypes {38721894}. LCNECs show some overlapping molecular alterations with SCNECs, including PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and MAPK pathway alterations, and MYC {36069807}. Phylogenetic analysis of mixed tumours has demonstrated that adenocarcinoma and NEC elements originate from a common precursor with shared genetic alterations, supporting their clonal relationship {27532149; 38625939}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours, when pure or mixed, usually comprise a friable cervical mass lesion; the macroscopic appearances are not distinct from other cervical malignancies.

Histopathology

SCNEC is characterized by the presence of a monotonous population of small to medium-sized cells with ovoid or slightly spindled hyperchromatic nuclei, dispersed chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm {28735441}. Nuclear moulding, brisk mitoses, apoptotic bodies, nuclear fragmentation, necrosis, and crush artefact are frequent features. The growth pattern is usually diffusely solid, but nests, trabeculae, pseudoglandular and rosette-like structures are sometimes present {27098591}. Lymphovascular space invasion is common.

LCNEC is composed of large cells with polygonal shape, moderate to abundant cytoplasm, large nuclei with coarse chromatin, sometimes with prominent nucleoli, and exhibiting a high mitotic rate {28735441}. The tumour typically displays an admixture of architectural patterns, including diffuse, trabecular/cord-like, insular, nested, pseudoglandular, and rosette-like. There is often extensive geographical necrosis. Nuclear palisading can be seen around the periphery of cell nests, and eosinophilic cytoplasmic granules are present in some cases. Similar to SCNEC, lymphovascular space invasion is common.

In some neoplasms, there are areas of SCNEC and LCNEC, and in some cases there is overlapping morphology such that it is difficult to distinguish between SCNEC and LCNEC {28735441; 39726011}. There may be an admixed preinvasive or invasive endocervical glandular or squamous lesion {28735441}. In mixed tumours, HPV-associated usual-type adenocarcinoma is the most common non-neuroendocrine component; squamous cell carcinoma is less common. On rare occasions, gastric-type adenocarcinoma, mesonephric adenocarcinoma, or adenoid basal carcinoma has been reported to be admixed with an NEC {15712186; 27532149; 39726011}. When reporting such mixed neoplasms, it is recommended to document the individual tumour types and their approximate percentages.

Synaptophysin, chromogranin, INSM1, and CD56 are the most frequently used immunohistochemical markers for diagnosis of NEC. Synaptophysin and INSM1 are the most consistently expressed and chromogranin and INSM1 are the most specific; staining with chromogranin may be very focal {20182342; 28735441; 33264139; 33323849}. CD56 is a nonspecific marker, and positivity should only be regarded as evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation in the proper histological context. Broad-spectrum cytokeratins are usually positive, sometimes with punctate cytoplasmic staining. TTF1, CDX2, and SATB2 expressions are present in some cases, and immunoreactivity for these markers does not exclude a gynaecological origin {20182342; 35091815}. Diffuse block-type p16 immunoreactivity is common because of the association with high-risk HPV. SMARCA4 (BRG1), SMARCA2 (BRM), and SMARCB1 (INI1) are retained. Cervical NECs are generally mismatch repair stable tumours, are usually (but not always) PDL1 negative and exhibit PARP1 expression {37593242; 32727929}.

A diagnosis of SCNEC can be made in the absence of neuroendocrine marker positivity if the morphological appearances are typical, but most cases are positive with one or more neuroendocrine marker {28735441}. In contrast, expression of at least one neuroendocrine marker (synaptophysin, chromogranin, INSM1, or CD56) is necessary to establish the diagnosis of LCNEC. When CD56 is the only marker expressed, histological features typical of LCNEC must be present. Cases without compatible neuroendocrine morphology, for example squamous cell carcinomas or adenocarcinomas, should not be reported as NEC in the presence of neuroendocrine marker immunoreactivity, and these should not be reported as exhibiting neuroendocrine differentiation. In mixed carcinomas, the immunophenotype of each component mirrors that of the corresponding pure neoplasms.

Cytology

Cervical cytology preparations of SCNEC are usually moderately to highly cellular. The cells are arranged in sheets and as single dispersed cells with scant cytoplasm. The nuclei have finely granular/stippled chromatin, often with nuclear moulding and chromatin streak artefact {20891001; 34241929}. Preparations from LCNEC show highly atypical cells with moderate to abundant cytoplasm, in groups and singly {30461217}. In mixed carcinomas, there may be an admixture of cell types, which mirror those of the corresponding pure neoplasms.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A positive molecular test for HPV may help confirm a cervical primary {24875120; 29248196; 31263021}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: high-grade carcinoma exhibiting neuroendocrine morphology (small cell or large cell); immunohistochemical evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation in cases with large cell morphology.

Desirable: immunohistochemical evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation in cases with small cell morphology.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Pure SCNECs, LCNECs, and mixed carcinomas with an NEC component of the uterine cervix are highly aggressive, with a high propensity for extracervical, lymphatic, and haematogenous spread. Distant metastasis at presentation is common. Mortality is high, even among patients diagnosed at an early stage {25341572; 37269846; 40057385}.
