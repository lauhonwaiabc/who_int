9.2.2.2 Juvenile granulosa cell tumour

Definition

Juvenile granulosa cell tumour (JGCT) is a pure sex cord tumour containing granulosa cells typically arranged in nodules with macrofollicular formations in an oedematous or myxoid fibrothecomatous background.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Prepubertal patients often present with isosexual pseudoprecocity as a consequence of hyperestrogenism {6465418; 39561630}, although rarely androgenic manifestations may be seen. Abdominal pain or distension and menstrual irregularities are common symptoms in postpubertal patients {6465418; 39561630}. Haemoperitoneum secondary to rupture is an occasional presentation {6465418}. Rare patients have Maffucci syndrome, Ollier disease, or DICER1 syndrome {1553918; 6365306; 21501861}, although tumours in patients with the latter syndrome may represent Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours (SLCT) with close morphological mimicry of JGCT {32815828; 29037807}.

Epidemiology

This is a rare tumour accounting for approximately 5% of all granulosa cell tumours. JGCT is typically encountered in the first three decades of life (mean age: 13 years), although it may occur at any age, including in postmenopausal women {6465418}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The molecular events in JGCT are varied. Activating AKT1 and GNAS alterations have been reported in 29–60% and 30% of cases, respectively {35031544; 31409083; 16507630; 26137586; 39773640}. The genomic landscape of JGCT highlights several other recurrent gene variants affecting epigenetic modifiers (KMT2C [52%], ARID1A [39%], KMT2D [35%]) and telomere elongation (TERT variant in 19% of tumours, TERT rearrangement in 13%) {35031544}. DICER1 mutations are found in 6–23% of cases {26033501; 32910017; 35031544}, sometimes associated with DICER1 syndrome {21501861}. Somatic IDH1 and IDH2 variants have been found in tumours associated with Ollier disease or Maffucci syndrome {31409083; 37324278}. Rare reports describe an association with tuberous sclerosis and germline TP53 and PTEN mutations {21501861; 16516278; 18669439}. JGCTs lack the FOXL2 NM_023067.4:c.402C>G p.(Cys134Trp) point mutation characteristic of adult granulosa cell tumours {35031544; 32910017}. Tumours with bizarre nuclear atypia may exhibit TP53 mutations and MYC family amplifications {41036825}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most tumours (98%) are unilateral, with a variable size (average: 125 mm). The tumours are typically solid and cystic, and the cystic foci may contain haemorrhagic fluid. Both a uniformly solid and a predominantly cystic appearance are also seen {35353158}. The solid tissue is typically yellow-tan or grey and may show areas of haemorrhage, especially if ruptured {6465418}.

Histopathology

Tumours typically show a low-power nodular or diffuse architecture with scattered follicles of varying size and shape, although often predominantly of intermediate size (not microfollicular or macrofollicular); the follicles may contain basophilic secretions. Occasionally, a pseudopapillary architecture is present {18301054}. Tumour cells typically have an epithelioid appearance, with round vesicular nuclei that usually lack grooves, and typically have abundant eosinophilic, grey, or vacuolated cytoplasm. A variable degree of cytological atypia is seen, including the presence of bizarre cells with marked cytological atypia {41036825}. Mitotic activity is variable but usually brisk with an average of 4.9 mitoses/mm2 (equating to an average of 7 mitoses/10 HPF assessed in 40 HPF 0.425 mm in diameter and 0.142 mm2 in area) and ranges from 0.7 mitoses / mm2 to 22.6 mitoses/mm2 (ranging from 1–32/10 HPF). Apoptotic bodies are often prominent {6465418}. The stroma is typically oedematous, myxoid, or collagenous/hyalinized, and a fibromatous or thecomatous component may be present. Occasionally there is extensive sclerosis. Predominantly cystic tumours contain a similar cell population that lines large follicles and often invaginates into the surrounding stroma {35353158}. Some moderately to poorly differentiated SLCTs harbour areas of follicular differentiation resembling JGCT, and these tumours typically harbour DICER1 alterations, and their clinical features closely resemble those of SLCT; thus, tumours with any component of SLCT and with a DICER1 alteration should be considered a variant of SLCT rather than JGCT or gynandroblastoma (see section Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour) {32815828; 29037807}. JGCTs usually express sex cord markers SF1, inhibin, calretinin, WT1, CD99, CD56, and FOXL2. Cytokeratins may be positive. EMA is usually negative but rarely positive {15693901}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing is typically not required for diagnosis but may be useful in excluding alterations more typical of other tumours with overlapping morphological features such as adult granulosa cell tumour (FOXL2) or small cell carcinoma of hypercalcaemic type (SMARCA4). In the presence of a DICER1 alteration, as discussed, an SLCT with close morphological mimicry of JGCT should be considered {32815828; 29037807}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: granulosa cells with diffuse or nodular growth punctated by irregularly shaped follicles.

Desirable: positive staining for sex cord markers.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with tumours confined to the ovary have an excellent prognosis. More than 80% of JGCT are stage I at diagnosis {39561630}. Rupture, positive peritoneal cytology, and extraovarian spread are associated with increased risk of recurrence (most often within the first 3 years) {6465418}. In one study, tumours with bizarre nuclear features exhibited aggressive behaviour {41036825}.
