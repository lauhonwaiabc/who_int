4.4.0.4 Adenoid basal carcinoma

Definition

Adenoid basal carcinoma is an epithelial tumour composed of morphologically bland basaloid cells arranged in small, rounded nests or tubules caused by persistent high-risk HPV infection.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: low-grade adenoid basal tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours are localized to the cervix (usually at the transformation zone).

Clinical features

Patients are generally asymptomatic. Unless associated with another tumour type, this tumour is usually discovered as an incidental finding in patients who are being managed for an abnormal cervical screening test result or are undergoing surgery for another reason {16911774; 9706976}.

Epidemiology

In the largest published series of 82 patients, age ranged from 28 to 89 years (median: 69 years) {35722427}.

Etiology

High-risk HPV has been detected in almost all cases where this has been investigated {15712186; 22809729; 31393622}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is largely unknown. Based on the expression of prostatic markers (NKX3-1 and PAP in the tubular component), pure adenoid basal carcinoma may be related to ectopic prostatic-type tissue and could represent the malignant counterpart of ectopic prostatic tissue, although this is not proven {33032811; 33021555}.

Macroscopic appearance

A gross abnormality is usually absent unless another type of carcinoma is also present.

Histopathology

Adenoid basal carcinoma is an epithelial tumour composed of small, rounded nests or sometimes cords of relatively monomorphic, bland basaloid cells with ovoid nuclei and scant cytoplasm {9706976}. The nests of tumour cells may display peripheral palisading, cystic change, clear cell change, and focal squamous differentiation. Typically, the nests contain small tubules. There is little in the way of nuclear atypia or mitotic activity. A stromal reaction is usually minimal or absent, and vascular invasion has not been reported in morphologically pure lesions. Adenoid basal carcinoma is often associated with a high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (> 90% of cases) or with an invasive carcinoma of another type, most commonly squamous cell carcinoma but occasionally adenocarcinoma or neuroendocrine carcinoma {15712186; 22809729; 31393622}. Therefore, a definitive diagnosis of pure adenoid basal carcinoma requires evaluation of the entire tumour. Adenoid basal carcinoma associated with another invasive carcinoma type should be reported as a mixed carcinoma, with a description of the constituent histotypes and their proportions. Adenoid basal carcinoma may be colonized by a concurrent high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion, whereby nests of overtly atypical high-grade squamous cells partly or completely replace the nests of adenoid basal carcinoma, sometimes with a retained peripheral cuff of basaloid cells; this should be distinguished from a mixed adenoid basal carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma {37991261}. Adenoid basal carcinoma should also be distinguished from adenoid basal hyperplasia, a non-neoplastic lesion of reserve cell origin that exhibits negative/non–block-type staining for p16 and is HPV negative {9706976; 22809729}.

Immunohistochemically, the tumour cells are positive for broad-spectrum cytokeratins and p63 and typically exhibit p16 block-type immunoreactivity {16911774; 15712186; 22809729}. The tubular elements are often positive with prostatic markers NKX3-1 and (less frequently) PAP but are usually negative with PSA {33032811; 33021555}.

Cytology

Rarely identified in cervical cytology, pure adenoid basal carcinoma comprises crowded sheets and three-dimensional clusters of small, uniform cells with hyperchromatic oval nuclei, sometimes with peripheral palisading {8964176; 26278519}. When mixed with a high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion or invasive carcinoma, the cytological features are of those entities.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

High-risk HPV testing may be useful in diagnosis, but the diagnosis can generally be made on morphology.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: infiltrative small nests or cords of morphologically bland basaloid cells; absent or minimal stromal reaction.

Desirable: block-type positivity for p16 and/or detection of high-risk HPV.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Adenoid basal carcinoma has no known metastatic potential when occurring in pure form {16911774; 9706976}, and therefore the term “low-grade adenoid basal tumour” has been suggested. When diagnosing a pure adenoid basal carcinoma, it should be conveyed to the clinician that these neoplasms have no known metastatic potential and do not require radical treatment, although excision with clear margins is recommended to exclude another tumour component. The outcome of mixed carcinomas with an adenoid basal carcinoma component depends on the pathological prognostic features of the other component {16911774; 15712186}.
