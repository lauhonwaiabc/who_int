3.1 Tumours of the vagina: Introduction

Benign, premalignant, and malignant lesions are much less common in the vagina than at many other sites in the female genital tract. Primary vaginal cancer is rare, constituting only approximately 0.1% of all cancers and 2% of all female genital tract malignancies in adults {38572751; 37336757}. Within the malignant category, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the most common, although a wide range of non-squamous malignancies may also occur {36731045}. As in the cervix, vaginal epithelial pathology (mainly premalignant and malignant squamous lesions) is dominated by HPV infection and its neoplastic consequences. Most primary vaginal SCCs are HPV-associated, most commonly HPV16 {22226684; 21166704; 23402906}. However, some primary vaginal carcinomas (a minority of SCCs and most adenocarcinomas) are unrelated to HPV infection and represent HPV-independent neoplasms. Of note, the presence of HPV is a favourable prognostic factor for vaginal SCC {22226684; 23402906}.

The classification of SCCs of the vagina parallels that of the cervix and vulva, with HPV-associated and HPV-independent SCCs recognized as separate entities. Although there are currently no significant differences in management between HPV-associated and HPV-independent tumours, it is recommended that the type of vaginal SCC (HPV-associated or HPV-independent) be documented in the pathology report. A morphological diagnosis of SCC, HPV status undetermined, without appending an HPV association is an acceptable alternative when tests required to make this distinction (p16 immunohistochemistry and/or HPV testing) are not available, such as in low-resource settings. Regardless of whether ancillary techniques are available, it is important to note that most neoplasms will be HPV associated. Before making a diagnosis of a primary vaginal HPV-associated SCC, a cervical or vulvar primary should be excluded, and a diagnosis of a cervical or vulvar SCC in the past 5 years is usually taken as excluding a primary vaginal SCC {37336757}. HPV-associated squamous lesions may occur at multiple sites within the lower female genital tract (cervix, vagina, vulva) and perineal/anal/perianal regions.

A category of CIN, HPV-independent, has been introduced in this edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours series to parallel the corresponding lesion in the vulva [VIN, HPV-independent]. However, an HPV-independent precursor lesion of HPV-independent SCC is not yet included in the vaginal classification, as it is not well-established in the published literature.

Primary vaginal adenocarcinomas are rare, with most vaginal adenocarcinomas representing metastasis from elsewhere. Although a small number are HPV-associated with a morphology similar to HPV-associated cervical adenocarcinomas {31857682}, most primary vaginal adenocarcinomas are HPV-independent (e.g. gastric-type adenocarcinoma, clear cell carcinoma, endometrioid carcinoma, mesonephric adenocarcinoma, adenocarcinoma of Skene gland origin, and intestinal-type adenocarcinoma) {29664741}. Broadly, the same categories of adenocarcinoma occur in the cervix, and they are usually more common in this location. Because of this, most of these adenocarcinomas are covered in the section Epithelial tumours of the uterine cervix. Adenocarcinoma, intestinal-type, and adenocarcinoma of Skene gland origin are covered in this chapter since exact equivalent neoplasms do not exist in the cervix.

As with other chapters, non-neoplastic squamous and glandular lesions are no longer included in this edition of WHO classification. #53874Table 3.XXX lists those non-neoplastic lesions that appeared as separate sections in the fifth-edition volume of WHO Classification. Carcinosarcoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, adenosarcoma, sarcomas, and germ cell tumours are also covered in other chapters, reflecting the sites where they more commonly occur. Some benign lesions, such as villous adenoma, mixed tumour, Müllerian papilloma, and fibroepithelial stromal polyp, are retained in this chapter. Fibroepithelial stromal polyp is retained, because the evidence that some of these are associated with RB1 and other molecular abnormalities and are presumably neoplastic {37922949}. Adenoid basal carcinoma of the vagina has been removed as a diagnostic category since there are few to no published cases.
