9.4.0.5 Wilms tumour

Definition

Wilms tumour is composed of a variable mixture of blastemal, epithelial, and stromal elements identical to its renal counterpart.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: nephroblastoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

The clinical presentation is variable but is usually with symptoms of an abdominal mass, pain, or abnormal vaginal bleeding {31815892; 28018955; 30701637}.

Epidemiology

These are extremely rare primary ovarian neoplasms. The patient age has ranged from 4 to 36 years {31815892; 30701637; 38084641}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of ovarian pure Wilms tumour is unknown, but these neoplasms may arise from persistent mesonephric duct remnants or cells with persistent embryogenic potential. Additionally, and more commonly, Wilms tumour may develop in the context of an ovarian teratoma, usually a mature teratoma {31815892; 28018955}. A study of five ovarian Wilms tumours identified DICER1 pathogenic variants in two pure Wilms tumours, while three tumours associated with teratomas were found to be DICER1 wild type. Furthermore, pure and teratoma-associated ovarian Wilms tumours displayed divergent copy-number variation and DNA methylation patterns. These results suggest that the two cases diagnosed as pure primary ovarian Wilms tumours may represent moderately to poorly differentiated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours (almost always associated with DICER1 pathogenic variants), while the teratoma-associated neoplasms are likely true Wilms tumours {38084641}. This is further supported by the observation that the pure Wilms tumours showed at least focal expression of sex cord markers. Before diagnosing a pure ovarian Wilms tumour, a moderately to poorly differentiated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour should be considered and excluded because of the morphological overlap between these two tumour types.

Macroscopic appearance

Pure Wilms tumour occurs as a cystic and solid, almost always unilateral, mass, usually measuring > 100 mm.

Histopathology

Wilms tumour typically shows a combination of blastemal, epithelial, and stromal elements; however, monophasic or biphasic tumours may also rarely occur. The blastemal component consists of densely packed, small to medium-sized cells with scant cytoplasm, nuclei with coarse chromatin, numerous mitoses, and apoptotic bodies arranged in variable patterns (solid, serpentine, nodular). The blastemal component often has a basaloid morphology. The epithelial component includes rosette-like, papillary, tubular, glandular, or glomeruloid structures, lined by columnar or cuboidal cells with elongated hyperchromatic nuclei. Heterologous differentiation may occasionally be seen in the form of squamous or mucinous epithelium. The stromal component is represented by variably cellular, sometimes mitotically active, spindle cell areas with smooth muscle or fibroblastic differentiation, sometimes displaying heterologous elements in the form of rhabdomyoblasts, cartilage, fat, bone, ganglion cells, or neuroglial tissue {24713986; 31815892; 10872672}.

Immunohistochemically, the blastemal component is usually diffusely positive for WT1 (nuclear staining with antibodies directed to the C-terminal and N-terminal portions of the WT1 protein), PAX8, and vimentin, variably positive for cytokeratins, EMA, CD56, CD57, CD10, and desmin, and negative for other muscle markers. The epithelial component is typically positive for cytokeratins, EMA, and CD56, and variably positive for PAX8, WT1, and CD10. The stromal component expresses vimentin, with variable desmin, CD34, BCL2, and WT1; myogenin and MYOD1 are positive if there is heterologous rhabdomyoblastic differentiation. Synaptophysin, chromogranin, calretinin, SF1, and inhibin are usually negative {31815892}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant in most tumours. However, in cases raising a differential diagnosis of pure ovarian Wilms tumour and moderately to poorly differentiated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour, DICER1 mutation analysis is recommended to guide diagnosis since variants in this gene are extremely common in the latter neoplasms.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a primary ovarian tumour with a variable mixture of blastemal, epithelial, and stromal elements.

Desirable: presence of a teratoma; negative DICER1 mutation analysis when no teratoma is present.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the ovarian FIGO and TNM staging systems. The US National Wilms Tumor Study considers all extrarenal Wilms tumours to be at least stage II, mandating chemotherapy; however, most of the reported ovarian Wilms tumours have had favourable outcomes. It is therefore recommended to stage ovarian Wilms tumours as a primary ovarian neoplasm.

Prognosis and prediction

Experience is limited; however, no documented deaths of disease have been reported after a follow-up ranging from 3 to 108 months, even in patients with advanced-stage disease or recurrence shortly after surgery {31815892; 30701637}.
