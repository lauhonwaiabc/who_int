10.1.1.4 Intravenous leiomyomatosis

Definition

Intravenous leiomyomatosis (IVL) is an intravascular growth of benign smooth muscle cells in the absence of or beyond the confines of a leiomyoma, sometimes with pelvic or extrapelvic extension.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: intravenous leiomyoma; intravascular leiomyomatosis; intravascular leiomyoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

IVL typically originates in the uterus but may occasionally arise in the broad ligament {25675187}. In a subset of cases, IVL may extend into the adnexal and/or other pelvic veins and inferior vena cava {25675187; 34525488; 3692675}. In rare cases, it can reach the heart (intracardiac leiomyomatosis) and pulmonary vessels {32337287; 37365113}, with unilateral (usually right-sided) extrauterine spread more common than bilateral {36332557}.

Clinical features

IVL typically occurs in patients with a history of uterine leiomyomas (ULs), sharing similar symptoms especially when confined to uterus {3202247; 8112951; 21777942; 29699902}. Extrauterine extension is seen in a subset of patients (pelvic: 30–40%; extrapelvic: as much as ~60%), which may present with lower extremity oedema, chest pain, dyspnoea, syncope, or pulmonary embolism, rarely leading to sudden death {3675155; 29351101; 36332557; 36793517; 39395469}. IVL exhibits distinct ultrasonographic features {37492474}. Pelvic MRI (with MR angiography/venography) may help with early detection {17179374; 36332557; 37365113}; contrast enhanced CT (with CT-angiography) can evaluate extrauterine extension {26858203; 29395222; 37365113}; and transthoracic echocardiography is commonly used for intracardiac involvement {36332557}.

Epidemiology

Intravenous leiomyomatosis is rare, with very low clinical incidence and representing < 5% of uterine smooth muscle tumours {21777942; 27631266; 36793517; 34343794}. It occurs predominantly in premenopausal women with a mean age of 46 (+ 9.3 years) {36332557}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The frequent presence of concurrent UL along with shared molecular alterations such as HMGA2 rearrangements (der(14)t(12;14) and/or gain of 12q) with increased HMGA2 expression suggests a common clonal origin with UL {12508249; 26892441; 29699902; 32341498}. However, rare cases arising in large veins away from uterus support a vascular wall origin in at least a minority of IVLs {3174546;11078622; 28214494}. In addition, the common MED12 mutations in UL are less frequently reported in IVL {25033729; 31889549; 32240666}. IVL has more complex alterations than UL, including 1p, 10q, and 22q losses and 12q gains, similar to disseminated peritoneal leiomyomatosis and benign metastasizing leiomyoma, but with less chromosome instability than leiomyosarcomas {11904348; 25033729; 26892441; 31889549; 32341498}. Transcriptomic and proteomic profiles further highlight both overlap and distinction from UL {26892441; 30723247; 35740573}. Overall, integrated clinical and molecular features are compatible with an intermediary phenotype that is relatively distinct from typical UL and leiomyosarcoma counterparts; however, currently no pathognomonic molecular alteration has been characterized to fully explain this phenomenon {38108079}.

These tumours do not exhibit fusions detected in other mesenchymal neoplasms that may extend into vasculature (e.g. JAZF1, PHF1, KANSL1, ALK, etc.) {34856302; 37307879}.

Macroscopic appearance

Multiple white, rubbery, and nodular masses are sometimes seen as coiled, worm-like tumour plugs in uterine/extrauterine vessels or may be free-floating. Similar to that of UL, the size has a wide range from microscopic foci to > 100 mm; and subtle lesions may be difficult to visualize grossly. Thorough sampling of peripheral myometrial sections, adjacent vasculature, and lower uterine segment soft tissue may prevent diagnosis, particularly in samples with high clinical suspicion {3202247; 21777942; 25675187; 29699902; 33429819}.

Histopathology

Intravascular growth of benign smooth muscle cells resembling those in UL (including its various histological types), occur in the absence of or outside the confines of a UL {3202247; 25675187}. Hydropic change, hyalinization, and thick-walled vessels are frequent {8112951; 3202247}. Uncommon patterns include those with adipocytes (intravenous lipoleiomyomatosis) {3202247; 16919562; 32341498} and vascular-rich forms {32341498}. Rarely, benign endometrial stroma and glands may be present (intravenous adenomyomatosis), often with coexisting endometriosis {24076777; 34856302}. Smooth muscle markers (desmin, h-caldesmon) and hormone receptors (ER, PR) are generally positive, while CD10 can be negative to variably positive {15171320; 15285309; 29699902; 34856302; 40276735}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intravascular growth of benign smooth muscle tumour cells in the absence of or outside the confines of a leiomyoma.

Desirable: tumour cells usually express smooth muscle markers (desmin, h-caldesmon) and hormone receptors (ER, PR).

Staging

This neoplasm is not currently recommended to be staged by standardized guidelines.

Prognosis and prediction

Risk of recurrence is associated with incomplete resection {39395469; 40276735}, extrapelvic extension, tumour size (> 15 cm) {38508588}, and age ≤ 45 years {36793517}, with a median time to recurrence of 16–33 months (range: 0–178) in 18–25% of patients. Recurrences may occur within veins or rarely as benign metastasizing leiomyoma {1203870; 21777942; 25675187; 3202247}. Myomectomy or isolated tumour resection increases recurrence risk compared with hysterectomy, while bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy provides no definitive additional benefit {34525488; 35692495}. For unresectable disease or as neoadjuvant therapy, tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, or GnRHa have been used because of IVL’s positive hormone receptor status; however, their efficacy remains uncertain {34525488; 36332557; 38508588}. Ultimately, complete surgical excision remains the primary strategy to prevent recurrence and posttreatment clinical follow-up is recommended, especially for patients with increased risk factors.
