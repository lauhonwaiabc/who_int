2.1 Tumours of the vulva: Introduction

Vulvar cancer is less common than cancers of cervix, uterine corpus, or ovary/fallopian tube; but it is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide (#53724Fig. 2.XXX). Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is by far the most frequent malignant vulvar tumour, and in the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours series, both precursors and invasive carcinomas were subclassified based on their association with HPV infection, as HPV-associated (HPV-A) or HPV-independent (HPV-I). This classification is typically done based on the presence of block-type p16 immunoreactivity (HPV-A) or absence of such p16 expression (HPV-I). This approach to subclassification of SCC precursors and invasive carcinomas has been supported by subsequent studies {32972785; 33246713; 37099408; 36828360; 37726173; 37840151; 38518213}. There is variation worldwide in the relative frequency of HPV-A and HPV-I vulvar SCC, with the former predominating in sub-Saharan Africa {36214794} while the latter is more common in Europe and North America {36214794}. The SCCs that arise via an HPV-I pathway occur in older patients and behave more aggressively than HPV-A carcinomas.

In this edition of the WHO classification, the HPV-I vulvar SCC and precursors are further subclassified into a more common subtype that is associated with pathogenic mutations in TP53 and mutation-type p53 expression on immunostaining and a less common subtype characterized by wildtype TP53 and wildtype p53 expression by immunohistochemistry. These subtypes of HPV-I squamous neoplasia are usually diagnosed based on p53 immunostaining #56613Fig. 2.XXX, but sequencing of TP53 can be done for tumours in which the p53 expression pattern is indeterminate. It is recommended that, where available, both p16 and p53 immunohistochemistry be performed for classification {32972785; 33246713; 37099408; 36828360; 37840151; 38518213}. Block immunoreactivity for p16 with wildtype p53 expression indicates an HPV-A SCC or precursor, while patchy or negative p16 expression and mutation-type p53 expression are characteristic of the most common HPV-I SCCs (p53 aberrant [p53abn] molecular subtype). Patchy or negative p16 and wildtype p53 expression identify the subtype of HPV-I SCCs (p53 wild type [p53wt]) with a prognosis intermediate between HPV-A and HPV-I (p53abn) vulvar SCC.

The precursors of HPV-I SCC remain diagnostically challenging. Those associated with mutation-type p53 expression [HPV-I (p53abn) VIN] often show histopathological features of differentiated VIN (dVIN), with significant basal atypia as well as altered maturation of the neoplastic keratinocytes. VIN that are HPV-I (p53wt) have only been recognized relatively recently and are characterized by verruciform and acanthotic growth with altered maturation but minimal cytological atypia (verruciform acanthotic VIN or vaVIN) {33105449; 35437330}. Although there are some general morphological differences between HPV-A and HPV-I SCC and precursor lesions, and between HPV-I (p53abn) and HPV-I (p53wt) SCC and precursors, it is not possible to accurately classify an individual lesion without the use of biomarkers {26630231; 28815579; 29505429; 37726173}. The algorithm shown in #56613Fig. 2.XXX is applicable to both SCC and precursor lesions. Nevertheless, despite the prognostic implications of molecular subtype, all SCCs are treated similarly at this time. Thus, in settings where immunohistochemistry is not available, a morphological diagnosis without further classification (SCC, HPV status undetermined) is an acceptable alternative and allows for appropriate surgical management to be delivered.

Tumours originating in mammary-type glands and lesions derived from Bartholin glands are covered in this chapter, as are Paget disease and other rare primary vulvar adenocarcinomas. For invasive Paget disease or mammary gland–type adenocarcinomas, it is recommended that breast cancer biomarkers be assessed, where available. They are not routinely indicated in Paget disease that is completely in situ.

Benign and malignant tumours of adnexal origin other than the above-mentioned tumours of mammary-type glands and lesions of Bartholin glands are not covered in this volume. Although a variety of these tumours have been reported in the vulva, they are very uncommon. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 12). https://publications.iarc.who.int/650.]] for additional information on these neoplasms not listed in this volume.

In this update, condyloma and seborrhoeic keratosis-like lesions no longer have separate sections and are covered together with squamous intraepithelial lesions (section Squamous intraepithelial lesions). Mesenchymal and melanocytic tumours of vulva are covered in the separate chapters devoted to section mesenchymal and section melanocytic tumours (chapters 10 and 15, respectively). Vulvar adenocarcinomas of intestinal type are now covered in the section devoted to such lesions arising in the vagina, where they are more commonly encountered (section Adenocarcinoma, intestinal type). Finally, in keeping with the decision to remove benign/non-neoplastic lesions from this edition, vulvar cysts, including Bartholin gland cysts, and Bartholin gland hyperplasia are not covered in this sixth edition.
