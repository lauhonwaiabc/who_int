8.2.2.1 Serous borderline tumour

Definition

Serous borderline tumour (SBT) of ovary is a non-invasive, low-grade, proliferative serous epithelial neoplasm.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: non-invasive low-grade serous carcinoma; atypical proliferative serous tumour; serous tumour of low malignant potential; serous borderline tumour with microinvasion.

Subtype(s)

Serous borderline tumour, micropapillary/cribriform

Localization

Ovary (most common), but may also occasionally be seen in fallopian tube or peritoneum

Clinical features

Patients present with symptoms attributable to a pelvic mass.

Epidemiology

Approximately 50% of all borderline ovarian tumours are serous {33010027}. Patients present over a wide age range (16–97 years). The median age is 50 years {15897738; 24924123}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

RAS/MAPK pathway genetic alterations are present in 60–80% of SBTs. Somatic mutations of KRAS (codon 12) or BRAF (Val600Glu) are most common and tend to be mutually exclusive. BRAFV600E mutations are less frequent in advanced stage than in stage I tumours. Alternative RAS/MAPK pathway–activating genetic alterations, including HRAS, ERBB2, or non-Val600Glu BRAF mutations (but not NRAS mutations), are present in a minor subset of SBTs {12644542; 22102435; 26506417; 28873354; 31839880; 34568720}. Most extraovarian implants show concordant BRAF or KRAS mutations as their associated primary ovarian SBT {24307542}. Shared mutations between SBT and adjacent cystadenoma, associated endosalpingiosis, and recurrent low-grade serous carcinoma (LGSC) have also been reported, consistent with a clonal neoplastic process across these lesions {15466181; 31576556; 31343420}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours are generally > 50 mm and may be intracystic (and lined by excrescences) and/or exophytic with surface involvement. Approximately one third are bilateral (more common in the micropapillary/cribriform subtype) {15897738; 28248817}.

Histopathology

Usual SBT is characterized by hierarchical branching papillae with variable amounts of stroma in the cores. The epithelium is stratified, with tufting and cell detachment. There is an admixture of cell types (including ciliated, non-ciliated, eosinophilic, hobnail, or mesothelial-like), and the nuclei are heterogeneous. Some tumours have a prominent adenofibromatous appearance. Tumour cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, often exfoliating in cell clusters or single cells, are particularly prominent in BRAFV600E-mutated SBTs {25188864; 31839880; 36867514}. A 10% threshold is commonly used for the minimal amount of epithelial proliferation required for distinction of an early serous borderline tumour from cystadenoma/adenofibroma with focal proliferation {18156968; 15297961}.

The micropapillary/cribriform subtype has elongated micropapillae, at least 5 times longer than wide, usually without stromal cores, that directly emanate from large papillae (the so-called Medusa head appearance) and/or small punched-out cribriform spaces. An area of pure micropapillary/cribriform growth measuring ≥ 5 mm is required for the tumour to be classified as the micropapillary/cribriform subtype {15897738; 28248817}. The cells have a high N:C ratio, and the nuclei are small and uniform, often with small nucleoli, imparting a more monotonous appearance compared with the cellular heterogeneity associated with typical SBT. The nuclear features should not be high-grade, and mitoses are infrequent. Occasionally, there is confluent epithelial proliferation, almost reaching solid growth; formal diagnostic criteria are lacking to distinguish between the upper limit of a florid micropapillary/cribiform SBT versus LGSC with micropapillary/cribiform SBT–like growth pattern. However, as such cases are often associated with other areas showing definitive stromal invasion and/or extraovarian disease, a diligent search for foci of invasive LGSC is warranted {12766576; 27487741}.

Within the stroma, some SBTs harbour foci of individual tumour cells or small clusters of tumour cells with abundant dense eosinophilic cytoplasm and/or small, simple papillae with cytological features of SBT. The individual foci are typically < 5 mm in greatest dimension and have been designated as stromal microinvasion. Immunohistochemical evidence suggests that these eosinophilic tumour cells and small papillae are biologically inert, with a senescent or terminally differentiated phenotype. Moreover, they are not associated with an adverse prognosis {15297961; 28248817; 24441661}. The term “microinvasion” is therefore no longer recommended; optionally, these findings can be reported descriptively.

Low-grade serous carcinoma arising in serous borderline tumour

A more concerning morphological pattern that can be seen in some SBTs consists of a haphazard arrangement of larger cell nests, complex papillae, branching glands, micropapillae or macropapillae, which exhibit the cytological features of low-grade carcinoma. These patterns of invasion are often associated with a stromal response and are morphologically identical to LGSC (see Histopathology in the section on LGSC for further details). Although foci with this appearance that are limited in extent (< 5 mm in greatest dimension) have been designated as microinvasive carcinoma, the prognostic significance of this term is based on limited data and this term is no longer recommended. Adequate sampling of the tumour, including submitting additional sections where appropriate, is warranted for such cases to exclude larger or multiple foci of LGSC {30480646}. If foci of LGSC measuring > 5 mm can be excluded, then a diagnosis of focal LGSC arising from SBT can be rendered. Although the size of invasive foci has not been shown to be a prognostic indicator {28248817}, providing a size measurement may be useful to demonstrate the focality of this finding. In cases where the features are indefinite for invasion by LGSC, use of terminology such as “indeterminate” or “equivocal” as qualifiers may be appropriate.

Implants

The term “implant” is used to describe extraovarian disease associated with SBT of the ovary. Non-invasive implants are lesions that involve peritoneal surfaces (or submesothelial invaginations) or fibrous septa between omental fat lobules. Invasive implants (extraovarian LGSCs) are defined by their infiltrative growth. As invasive implants behave like LGSC, they should be designated as such, and the use of the term “invasive implant” is discouraged.

Non-invasive implants can be further subdivided into epithelial and desmoplastic patterns {3179935; 27428735; 15297961}. The epithelial pattern consists of hierarchically branching papillae or detached clusters of cells within smoothly contoured epithelium- or mesothelium-lined spaces. The desmoplastic pattern consists of single cells or clusters of cells embedded in reactive-appearing (granulation tissue–like or fasciitis-like) or desmoplastic stroma that predominates over the epithelial component and appears tacked onto the peritoneal surface. The epithelium frequently blends into the surrounding stroma. If a desmoplastic implant is present on the ovarian surface or within the cyst of an ovarian SBT, the term “autoimplant” is used {16625091}.

Extraovarian LGSCs (invasive implants) associated with SBT exhibit a variety of patterns, including densely packed small nests or papillae, small or haphazard micropapillae, and inverted macropapillary and glandular patterns {11257616; 12218568; 27428735; 28248817; 15297961}. The defining feature is destructive, infiltrative growth, which morphologically resembles LGSC in the ovary. In most cases, the epithelial component predominates, often with a micropapillary pattern associated with retraction artefact lacking an epithelial/mesothelial lining. Tumour cells are sharply demarcated from the surrounding stroma. There is often an associated stromal reaction (i.e. fibrosis or desmoplasia, which differs qualitatively and quantitatively from the florid granulation tissue–like appearance of desmoplastic non-invasive implants). Multiple studies have demonstrated increased risk of recurrence and worse survival outcomes associated with this progression to LGSC {11257616; 12218568; 15297961; 27428735; 28248817; 3179935}.

Rarely, implants exhibit an appearance that is concerning for invasion but cannot be definitively classified as LGSC because of ambiguous morphology; these lesions should be designated as indeterminate type {15897738; 27428735}.

Lymph node involvement

Lymph node involvement by SBT, which is more common in subcapsular sinuses than parenchyma, may be seen as single cells, papillary clusters, or simple glandular structures that resemble non-invasive epithelial implants. Lymph node involvement by SBT is not equivalent to metastatic carcinoma but should be staged as N1 disease. Lymph node involvement is frequently associated with endosalpingiosis; however, endosalpingiosis in isolation does not warrant assigning a higher stage.

Rarely, nodular intraparenchymal aggregates of tumour resembling LGSC may be seen in lymph nodes; these should be designated as involvement by LGSC {16699316}.

Cytology

SBT is characterized by papillary tissue fragments with relatively uniform small cells, mild to moderate nuclear atypia, and increased N:C ratio. Psammoma bodies are commonly seen. Based on cytopathological features alone, SBT cannot be reliably distinguished from LGSC {11668465}. A diagnosis of involved by serous neoplasm can be issued with definitive classification deferred to the surgical specimen.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: serous epithelial proliferation; papillary hierarchical branching or micropapillary/cribriform pattern; low-grade cytology.

Staging

SBT is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system. Ovarian or extraovarian LGSC (invasive implant(s)) should be staged separately.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with FIGO stage I SBT show no significant difference in overall survival compared with the general population; in contrast, patients with advanced disease have decreased survival {24924123}. A reported 4–7% of women with SBT develop subsequent carcinoma, usually low-grade but rarely high-grade {15897738; 28248817; 31343420}. Risk factors for subsequent development of carcinoma include micropapillary/cribriform subtype, advanced stage, bilaterality, ovarian surface involvement, and residual disease after surgery; the presence of invasive implants (extraovarian LGSC) is the most important adverse prognostic factor {27836204; 28248817}. Lymph node involvement is not considered an adverse prognostic factor, except in rare cases of involvement by nodular tumour aggregates {10836293; 15297961; 16699316}. The BRAFV600E mutation, associated with prominent eosinophilic tumour cells, is associated with decreased risk of subsequent carcinoma, after adjusting for age and stage {31839880; 36867514}. The extremely rare SBTs that present with synchronous or metachronous high-grade, including anaplastic or poorly differentiated, carcinoma are associated with particularly poor prognosis {30565721; 39028145; 39389422; 40327333}.
