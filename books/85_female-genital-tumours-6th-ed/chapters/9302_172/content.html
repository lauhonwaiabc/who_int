9.3.0.2 Yolk sac tumour

Definition

Yolk sac tumour (YST) is a malignant neoplasm resembling extraembryonic structures, including the yolk sac, allantois, and extraembryonic mesenchyme.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: endodermal sinus tumour; primitive endodermal tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary and rarely in the endometrium, uterine cervix, vagina, and vulva {28599587; 27631522; 31033797; 6086471; 30814243; 22178761; 26076163; 27959583; 35733602; 34157379}.

Clinical features

Patients typically present with symptoms related to an ovarian mass such as abdominal pain. Most patients have a high level of serum AFP. YST may coexist as a component of a mixed germ cell tumour as well as with ovarian (and endometrial) cancers of varying histotypes in older patients; these have been referred to as somatically derived YSTs (see section High-grade serous carcinoma) {27334714; 27631522; 29106744; 34469331; 35639393}. Tumours arising in the uterine corpus, cervix, and vagina tend to present with abnormal vaginal bleeding, and vulval tumours usually present with a mass.

Epidemiology

Ovarian YST accounts for approximately 20% of malignant ovarian germ cell tumours and occurs mostly in the second and third decades of life {34469331}; there is a second peak in older women, and most of these neoplasms are probably somatically derived. Endometrial cases are also usually somatically derived and tend to occur in older patients. Vaginal and cervical YST usually occurs in children aged < 4 years {6086471; 30814243; 22178761}, and most vulval examples arise in women aged < 30 years (mean: 19 years; range: 1–52 years) {26076163; 27959583}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Abnormalities in chromosome 12, mostly isochromosome 12p, have been documented in over half of YSTs. In older women with coexisting ovarian or endometrial carcinoma, the YST is thought to be somatically derived {27334714; 29106744; 30170975; 32404953}.

Macroscopic appearance

YST is typically unilateral and large (average: 150 mm), with a solid and cystic cut surface. Haemorrhage and necrosis are common {34469331}.

Histopathology

Multiple patterns are usually present, most commonly a reticular/microcystic pattern (a meshwork of anastomosing spaces and cysts lined by a single layer of tumour cells) {22008025; 34469331}. Other patterns include papillary, solid, festoon (complex ribbons and undulating cords), and endodermal glandular (endometrioid-like or intestinal) patterns. Schiller–Duval bodies, which are round or elongated tumour cell–lined papillae with a large central vessel, protruding into a cystic space surrounded by tumour cells, are identified in a third of cases but are not usually prominent {22008025; 34469331}. Less common patterns are the polyvesicular-vitelline pattern (numerous vesicles and cysts within cellular stroma), parietal pattern (tumour cells embedded in linear bands of basement membrane material), mesenchyme-like pattern (tumour cells scattered in oedematous or myxomatous connective tissue), and hepatoid pattern {23348213; 22008025; 7139531}. Tumour cell appearance depends on the growth pattern, but the cells usually exhibit significant nuclear atypia with hyperchromatic nuclei and clear cytoplasm, which in some patterns manifests as supranuclear and subnuclear vacuoles, and hyaline globules. Eosinophilic hyaline globules and areas of necrosis are commonly seen. In older women with a coexisting ovarian or endometrial carcinoma (somatically derived YSTs), the most commonly encountered patterns are microcystic/reticular and glandular {27631522}.

YST expresses SALL4, a marker of primitive germ cell tumours {19295406; 24444105}, and usually one or both markers considered YST characteristic: AFP (often focal and weak) and/or glypican-3 (more sensitive than AFP but less specific) {18277882; 22008025; 24444105}. Other markers may be expressed reflecting differentiation of the endoderm including CDX2 and villin (intestinal-type patterns), HepPar1 (hepatoid pattern), and TTF1 (foregut/respiratory pattern) {24444105}. When the differential diagnosis includes a Müllerian-type carcinoma, lack of CK7, ER, and PAX8 expression supports a diagnosis of YST {29106744}.

Cytology

Smears show papillary tissue fragments of cells with cytoplasmic vacuoles and interspersed hyaline globules {7571973}. The differential diagnosis includes clear cell carcinoma and other germ cell tumours, namely embryonal carcinoma and choriocarcinoma {7571973; 19500123}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: primitive germ cell tumour exhibiting endodermal, extraembryonic, or somatic patterns.

Desirable: glypican-3 and/or AFP positivity by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year survival rates for ovarian YSTs are > 95% for stage I–II, 70% for stage III, and 50% for stage IV {28803748; 29194189}. Somatically derived YST has a worse prognosis {27334714; 27631522; 29106744}. Vaginal YSTs are malignant, but most patients achieve complete remission with combination chemotherapy (with or without surgical resection) {22178761; 30814243; 16200632}. Cervical and vulval YSTs are extremely rare, and prognostic factors are not known.
