15.2.1.1 Mucosal melanoma

Definition

Mucosal melanoma is a malignant melanocytic neoplasm arising within the female genitourinary tract in a non-cutaneous site, usually in the vulva, vagina, or urethra and rarely in the cervix.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

vulvar melanoma; vaginal melanoma; vulvovaginal melanoma; genital melanoma

Subtype(s)

Mucosal lentiginous melanoma (87460/3); nodular mucosal melanoma (87211/3)

Localization

These uncommon tumours mostly arise in mucosa of the vulva, vagina, or urethra {12409721; 25384702; 37760467}. Rare cases may arise in the uterine cervix {30044323}. Multifocality is common in mucosal melanoma. Vulvar melanoma primarily occurs in the labia majora; unilateral involvement is more common compared with bilateral disease, followed by labia minora and clitoris, while vaginal melanoma is mostly diagnosed in the anterior wall and lower one third of the vagina {27864417; 28064232}. Mucosal melanomas of urethra or cervix can secondarily extend to vaginal and vulvar mucosa and vice versa {32702401}.

Clinical features

Symptoms and signs depend on the anatomical location and include the presence of a mass, a macule, or a non-pigmented lesion, and/or bleeding; pruritus is a common presenting symptom of vulvar melanomas {12409721; 27864417}. Tumours may be pigmented or amelanotic {28064232, 38906758}. Conventional dermoscopic findings include structureless areas, grey areas, irregular black-brown dots, and blue-white structures {31389062}.

Epidemiology

Primary mucosal melanoma accounts for approximately 5–6.4% of malignancies of the vulva and vagina {38071449}. Vulvovaginal melanomas represent approximately 1% of all melanomas in most European populations but about 25% of melanomas in dark-skinned individuals {24272143; 39447759}. Most patients are postmenopausal, with a median age of 65 years {25384702}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Unlike cutaneous melanomas, mucosal melanomas have not been associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure {28467829; 31320640}.

Pathogenesis

In contrast to cutaneous melanomas, vulvovaginal melanomas show a low mutation burden, without a UV signature. Instead, they are characterized by numerous chromosomal structural variants and abundant copy-number changes, including multiple high-level amplifications {31320640; 30847022; 28467829; 23620124}. The most common somatic mutations involve KIT, NRAS, SF3B1, ATRX, TP53, and ARID2, while BRAF and TERT promoter mutations occur at a lower frequency compared with cutaneous melanomas; SF3B1 mutations may be correlated with shorter survival {38861917; 28734009; 35159047; 28026870; 28467829; 28720667}. KIT mutations are noted more commonly in vulvar melanomas (31.4%, compared with 6.2% in vaginal melanomas), where they correlated with longer survival {28734009, 28026870}, while NRAS mutations occur at relatively higher frequency in vaginal melanomas (18.9%, compared with 9.8% in vulvar melanomas) {28026870}.

Macroscopic appearance

Mucosal vulvar melanomas usually present as pigmented plaques, but amelanotic ulcerated nodules are also common {28064232}. Vaginal melanomas usually present as large, ulcerated, nodular masses and often involve the urethra and rarely cervix. An associated naevus is rarely seen {27864417}. Pigmentation is variable {28064232, 38906758}.

Histopathology

The tumour is usually formed by sheets or expansile nodules of large pleomorphic epithelioid or (less commonly) spindled melanocytes; necrosis is uncommon {38906758}. Nuclei often have vesicular chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and variable pleomorphism. Occasionally, small or naevoid cells may predominate. Melanin pigment is variable, but usually focally presents within both melanoma cells and macrophages. In situ component of vulvar and vaginal melanomas often shows a confluent nested and lentiginous growth pattern with variable pagetoid spread. Nodular type is relatively uncommon {27864417}. In rare instances, melanomas arising in the cutaneous surfaces of vulva may be of superficial spreading type, of which some may extend across the mucocutaneous junction to involve the vulvar mucosa {27864417}.

Metastasis to uterus is exceedingly rare and may involve the endometrium, myometrium, serosa, or cervix, but should be considered in the differential diagnosis, especially in patients with known history of primary cutaneous melanoma {7127256; 30354020}, including acral melanoma {33999583}, or (less commonly) primary uveal {16998600} or even brain {29848531} origin.

Immunohistochemically, there is usually reactivity of the tumour cells for S100, SOX10, HMB45, MITF, melan-A (MART1) and sometimes KIT (CD117) {12409721; 28734009} [[Moran JMT, Hartsough EM, Hoang MP. Vulvovaginal Melanocytic Lesions: A Review. Diagnostic Histopathology 2024;30:15–24.]].

Cytology

Typically shows a largely dispersed cell population with scattered poorly cohesive tissue fragments. The cells are large, round to epithelioid or sometimes spindle shaped {17276948}, with a variable amount of well-defined cytoplasm that may or in many cases may not contain melanin pigment. Nuclei are large, varying from monotonous round to more pleomorphic, with granular chromatin, and often prominent nucleoli. Intranuclear pseudoinclusions are often present. Occasional giant cells may be present. Pigmented macrophages containing melanin pigment may be seen in the background.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

BRAF, NRAS, and KIT tumour mutation testing may be useful for patients for whom targeted therapies are being considered. KIT mutations in vulvar melanomas are associated with longer progression-free survival {28734009}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: melanocytic tumour with malignant features.

Desirable: reactivity for melanocytic markers (if needed).

Staging

Vulvar melanoma is staged using the cutaneous melanoma staging system. There are no Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging criteria for melanomas occurring elsewhere in the female genital tract. However, it has been recommended that the cutaneous melanoma staging system may also be applied to vaginal melanomas {25384702}.

Prognosis and prediction

Unlike that for patients with cutaneous melanoma, the overall survival for patients with primary melanoma of the female genital tract is relatively poor {29748886; 28600969; 31784896; 33514483}, with non-vulvar location (vaginal, urethral, or cervical) being associated with a worse outcome {36229264; 38906758}. This is partly attributable to delayed detection. Increased tumour thickness, ulceration, necrosis, and high mitotic count are poor prognostic features in vulvovaginal melanoma {25384702; 38906758; 27864417}. The reported 5-year survival rate for patients with vulvovaginal melanoma is approximately 20%. Data on outcomes after systemic immunotherapy for metastatic mucosal melanoma are limited. Even though immunotherapy is associated with improved overall survival in patients with vulvar and vaginal melanomas, the outcomes appear to be poorer than in patients with cutaneous melanoma {27533633; 40170584; 38861917}. Notably, improved overall survival rates was noted in patients with vulvar melanoma at advanced stage, and among surgery-naïve patients with vulvar and vaginal melanomas {40170584}. Also, combination anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy resulted in longer survival rates compared with monotherapy {36324592}.
