4.2.0.1 Squamous intraepithelial lesions

Definition

Squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) of the uterine cervix are proliferations of squamous cells caused by HPV infection. These cells show abnormal nuclear features and maturation abnormalities but do not extend below the basement membrane.

Subtype(s)

LSIL; HSIL

Localization

SILs involve the cervical squamous epithelium and most commonly originate from the transformation zone. HSIL commonly arises from the region of immature squamous metaplasia near the current squamocolumnar junction (SCJ).

Clinical features

Women with cervical SIL are generally asymptomatic. Lesions are typically detected in the context of routine cervical cancer screening, including high-risk HPV testing (the preferred method) and/or cervical cytology, followed by colposcopic examination and tissue sampling for confirmation.

Epidemiology

The incidence and prevalence of cervical SIL vary widely across the world, reflecting differences in sociocultural factors, risk factors, access to screening, and HPV vaccine coverage. Data from high-resource regions are the most comprehensive. Although direct data on SIL prevalence in low- and middle-income countries are limited, the substantial burden of cervical cancer in these regions strongly suggests a high prevalence of HSIL {35875040; 34044477}. Contributing factors include high prevalence of HIV infection {39557497; 40086453; 39423451}, limited screening infrastructure, and low HPV vaccination coverage {34082174; 37370091}. These disparities highlight the urgent need to overcome barriers and expand global access to HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening {37237329; 34065961}.

Generally, cervical SIL affects women in their reproductive years, with the highest prevalence in young women, followed by a steady decline with age {32729638; 30140883}. HSIL is often identified at an older age than LSIL; however, there is considerable age overlap. Although uncommon, HSIL can occasionally develop within 1–2 years of HPV infection, even in adolescents {31030470}. Age-specific data from large US clinical trials show that LSIL prevalence is highest among women aged 21–24 years (6.5%), decreasing to 3.8% in those aged 25–29, 2% in women aged 30–39, and < 1% in women aged 40 years and older {21944226; 29681462}. HSIL shows a parallel trend, with a prevalence of 0.7% in women aged 21–24, 0.4% in those aged 25–39, and declining to 0.2% and 0.1% in women aged 40–49 and ≥ 50 years, respectively. In high-income countries, the introduction of HPV vaccination programmes around 2008 has resulted in significant reductions in HPV infections, LSIL, HSIL, and (more recently) cervical cancer incidence, as vaccinated populations enter the age range for cervical cancer screening {34741816; 30291040; 30944092}.

Etiology

Both LSIL and HSIL are caused by HPV infection. Approximately 40 HPV types can infect the cervix, with 12 high-risk and 6 low-risk types accounting for most lesions {39097395}. Among these, HPV16 is the most prevalent high-risk type detected in cervical HSIL and cervical cancer worldwide {21991433; 30632704}, whereas LSIL shows more variability in terms of HPV types {35312217}. Clinically validated high-risk HPV tests typically target 12–18 carcinogenic HPV genotypes {39221498}.

The distribution of HPV types varies significantly between LSIL and HSIL. LSIL can be associated with both low-risk HPV types (such as HPV6 and HPV11) as well as high-risk types, with HPV16 (26.3%), HPV31 (11.5%), HPV51 (10.6%), and HPV53 (10.2%) being the most common {15894666}. Among the possibly carcinogenic subtypes, HPV53 (10.2%) and HPV73 are the most common. In contrast, nearly all HSIL cases are associated with high-risk HPV types when sensitive molecular tests are used {26851121}. Approximately half of HSIL cases are associated with HPV16, although prevalence estimates range from 30% to 70% depending on geographical region. The next most common type detected in HSIL is HPV31 (8.6%), followed by HPV18 (6.9%) and other high-risk types such as HPV33, HPV52, and HPV58 {17405118}. HPV genotyping of cytology specimens indicates that coinfection with multiple HPV types is more common in LSIL than in HSIL {23447632; 19623178}.

LSIL reflects a productive HPV infection, whereas HSIL represents a transforming infection with malignant potential. Although HPV DNA is necessary for the development of SIL, it is not sufficient since many HPV infections are cleared by the host immune system and never progress to HSIL or cancer {21438669}. Additional contributing factors include smoking, high parity, early age at sexual debut, prolonged oral contraceptive use, immunosuppression (e.g. HIV infection or long-term immunosuppressive therapy), and coinfections (chlamydia trachomatis, HSV-2) {37501128; 33663309; 29215531; 26537763}. These factors may double or even triple the risk of developing HSIL and cancer, although the biological mechanisms by which they influence carcinogenesis remain poorly understood {17826171}.

Pathogenesis

Most cervical HSIL and subsequent cancers are believed to arise from the cervical transformation zone, particularly near the SCJ. Although the exact cell of origin remains controversial, several proposed models offer insight into this issue, supported by clinicopathological studies. One proposed model is that a discrete population of cuboidal epithelial cells at the SCJ appears uniquely susceptible to carcinogenic HPV and may serve as the origin of HSIL and cervical cancer {22689991; 24076771; 28425968}. Their distinct gene expression profile and immunophenotype support a dualistic model in which lesions arising from SCJ cells carry a higher risk of progression, whereas those originating from regular basal keratinocytes are more likely to be transient or result in LSIL. Another proposed model suggests that HPV infects basal cells of mature squamous epithelium, which results in LSIL or HSIL, which is more likely to be transient. Another proposed model suggests that HPV infects proliferating reserve cells beneath the endocervical columnar epithelium, leading to the development of thin HSIL, typically 10 cell layers or less in thickness {34655910; 40204232}. High-risk HPV-induced thin HSILs often progress to classic (thick) HSIL and show genetic alterations characteristic of invasive cancer, supporting their classification as true precursor lesions with malignant potential {38977078; 34619706}.

Whether HSILs arise from LSILs or develop independently remains a subject of ongoing debate. Although HSIL is often co-localized with LSIL, microdissection studies suggest that this often reflects a collision of lesions of different grades, driven by distinct HPV types, rather than true progression from LSIL to HSIL {22127961; 25353286}.

LSILs are generally DNA stable, with nuclei that are typically either euploid or polyploid. HPV viral gene expression in LSIL is coordinated with squamous cell differentiation. Compared with LSILs, HSILs more frequently exhibit aneuploidy, viral DNA integration, and chromosomal abnormalities involving 1p and 3q {14694518}. Uncoordinated expression of high-risk HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 is the key to the induction of genetic and cellular abnormalities that drive HSIL development {27905473}. HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins primarily target key cell-cycle regulators: E6 promotes degradation of p53, while E7 inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (RB1), which normally suppresses the transcription of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor gene p16 (INK4a) {10793105}. In high-risk HPV infections, E7-mediated inactivation of RB1 leads to marked overexpression of p16 (block-staining), whereas low-risk HPV E7 interacts weakly with RB1 and does not induce significant p16 upregulation {11291057}.

Recent research has identified a growing number of clinically relevant biomarkers aimed at improving screening accuracy and predicting prognosis. These include p16/Ki-67 dual staining on cytological specimens, HPV E6/E7 mRNA expression, squamous cell carcinoma antigen, macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), and VEGF {33044104; 33292364}. A wide array of RNA-based markers, such as mRNA, microRNA, circular RNA, and long non-coding RNA, are also under investigation {39839781; 25189639; 31905652}. DNA methylation of viral and host genes is a reliable biomarker for detecting HSIL and more severe lesions {31067838; 30098013; 38960143; 23093560}. The S5 classifier, combining EPB41L3 and HPV methylation targets, may help identify progressive CIN 2 {26790008; 31344234}. Cervicovaginal microbiome composition appears to influence host immunity and susceptibility to persistent HPV infection and CIN, and it is emerging as a potential predictive biomarker {34503848; 35276341}. Further studies are needed to validate their clinical utility and integration into routine practice.

Macroscopic appearance

Cervical SILs are typically not visible on gross examination unless exophytic. After the application of 3–5% acetic acid, they become apparent under colposcopy as acetowhite lesions {28953110; 28953109}. LSILs typically present as thin translucent acetowhite epithelium with fine vascular patterns and irregular borders. HSILs often present as thick dense acetowhite epithelium with coarse mosaic or punctation vascular patterns and sharply demarcated borders. Suspicious features for invasive cancer include atypical vessels, an irregular surface, necrosis, and ulceration.

Colposcopy is accurate for classical HSIL and cancer detection in women who are HPV-positive, but it has limitations in the adequate assessment of thin HSIL and HSIL arising in the endocervical canal {36796982}. Therefore, negative colposcopic findings do not rule out concerns raised by persistent high-risk HPV or abnormal cytology, particularly persistent HPV 16 and HSIL cytology.

Histopathology

“LSIL” and “HSIL” are the preferred diagnostic terms for both histological and cytological specimens {22742517}. This two-tiered system reflects the current understanding of HPV biology and its common carcinogenic pathway across anogenital sites and is more reproducible than the CIN system {7942772}. HSIL encompasses CIN 2 and CIN 3. Block-positive p16 immunohistochemistry supports the diagnosis of HSIL when there is diagnostic uncertainty.

LSIL

LSIL is characterized by proliferation of basal and parabasal-like cells, with an increased N:C ratio confined to the lower third of the squamous epithelium, often with mitotic activity, usually without atypical mitoses. LSILs have preserved epithelial maturation and differentiation often with koilocytic atypia. In the upper two thirds of the epithelium, cells lose their basal/parabasal morphology, but atypical nuclei persist and are accompanied by abundant cytoplasm. The nuclei commonly display hyperchromasia and membrane irregularities and may show binucleation or multinucleation. Pronounced nuclear atypia in the superficial layers does not, on its own, warrant a diagnosis of HSIL when a significant amount of cytoplasm is present. Koilocytosis is typically most prominent in the upper third of the epithelium but may extend into the lower layers. The perinuclear halo has a dense, sharply demarcated inner border, which helps distinguish it from glycogen-rich or reactive pseudohaloes {2171769}. Sometimes there is associated hyperkeratosis.

Proliferation of basal and parabasal cells often leads to the characteristic architectural abnormalities seen in LSIL. Recognized patterns include epithelial thickening with a slightly raised, undulating surface; papillary fronds with fibrovascular cores and pointed epithelial spikes (condyloma acuminatum); and slender filiform papillae often located near the proximal SCJ, known as papillary immature metaplasia. Papillary immature metaplasia typically shows mild nuclear atypia and low mitotic activity with inconspicuous koilocytic change and is associated with low-risk and occasionally high-risk HPV types; it may coexist with HSIL {29652189}. Seborrhoeic keratosis-like lesion is a rare HPV42-associated lesion with a plaque-like or stuck-on appearance lacking atypia and koilocytosis; a high-grade component has been reported in some cases {27792064; 35072991}.

LSIL diagnosis shows poor to fair reproducibility among pathologists, often due to overdiagnosis of reactive squamous proliferations that exhibit cytoplasmic vacuolization {11255427; 16183106}. Perinuclear haloes are nonspecific and may occur in the context of atrophy-related degeneration, glycogen accumulation, or non-HPV infections {2171450; 2174025}. To minimize overdiagnosis, LSIL should be diagnosed only when the perinuclear haloes are clearly accompanied by significant nuclear atypia.

HSIL

HSIL often shows marked basal and parabasal nuclear atypia, with immature basal-type cells extending above the lower third of the epithelium, accompanied by pronounced nuclear crowding, pleomorphism, and loss of normal cell polarity. In some cases, pleomorphism may be subtle, with only slight variation in nuclear size and shape. Cytoplasm is typically scant, resulting in a high N:C ratio and indistinct cell borders. Mitotic activity, including abnormal mitotic figures, is often observed above the basal epithelial layers. When an LSIL-like lesion exhibits atypical mitoses in the upper third of the epithelium, it raises concern for aneuploidy and HSIL {15662700; 3033572; 2852645}. Caution is warranted to exclude mimics of atypical mitoses such as karyorrhexis, hyperchromatic nuclei, and tissue artefacts. In such cases, p16 immunohistochemistry is recommended, especially when other definitive features of HSIL are absent.

The distinction between HSIL (CIN 2) and HSIL (CIN 3) is based on the extent of epithelial involvement by undifferentiated neoplastic cells. This distinction has clinical relevance for younger women, who may be candidates for conservative management of CIN 2, but is less critical in older women {32243307; 29487049}. HSIL (CIN 3) shows full-thickness atypia with minimal maturation in the superficial layers, whereas HSIL (CIN 2) exhibits more maturation and cytoplasmic differentiation in the upper epithelium. Both CIN 2 and CIN 3 can exhibit atypical mitotic figures throughout the epithelium, including in the superficial layers.

HSIL features considerable morphological variability, with several distinct patterns reported. The koilocytotic pattern refers to lesions showing prominent koilocytosis in conjunction with other diagnostic features of HSIL. Keratinizing HSIL is characterized by prominent surface keratinization and parakeratosis, often appearing as keratin plaques on colposcopy. Thin HSIL refers to lesions composed of 10 cell layers or less in thickness {27513079; 30927371}. Pleomorphic HSIL (HSIL with bizarre nuclei) is characterized by focal, striking nuclear atypia and multinucleation, often involving the endocervical crypts, and likely represents degenerative change {28666641; 39477401}. HSIL with an immature metaplastic phenotype is often found beneath columnar epithelium and shows greater nuclear density and pleomorphism than atypical, immature squamous metaplasia, with its diagnosis facilitated by block-positive p16 immunohistochemistry {17394499}. Rarely, HSIL extends upwards into the endometrial cavity {23776829}.

The reproducibility of HSIL diagnosis among pathologists is considered moderately good {11255427; 16183106}. However, when CIN 2 and CIN 3 are evaluated separately, CIN 2 is a poorly reproducible category, often subject to upgrading or downgrading on review {23429369; 17885496; 25389329}. CIN 3 is a more reproducible diagnosis than CIN 2, with expert pathologists agreeing on CIN 3 in > 80% of cases, compared with < 30% for CIN 2 {17885496}.

Benign conditions such as immature squamous metaplasia, atrophy, inflammatory and reparative changes can mimic HSIL morphologically, including thin HSIL. Conversely, extensive HSIL involving endocervical glands may resemble invasive cancer due to its depth of involvement. The term “expansile HSIL (CIN 3)” refers to lesions with extensive involvement and expansion of endocervical glands by HSIL, often with central necrosis and maturation. These cases may be associated with early invasion and typically warrant deeper tissue sections to exclude the possibility of invasive disease {7962647}.

Ancillary testing

p16, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, is a surrogate marker for high-risk HPV and is overexpressed (block-type staining) in nearly all HSILs and HPV-associated invasive cancers {11420459; 11291057; 19261387}. It is the only biomarker currently recommended by the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) Project to aid in the diagnosis of HPV-associated lesions {22742517}.

p16 immunohistochemistry is recommended in three specific contexts in the LAST project: (1) to help distinguish HSIL from its mimics, such as immature squamous metaplasia, atrophy, inflammatory and reactive epithelial changes, or tangential cutting; (2) to assist when the pathologist is entertaining a morphological diagnosis of HSIL (CIN 2), typically in clearly HPV-associated lesions, where the differential diagnosis includes LSIL versus HSIL, and for younger women when p16 expression status is factored into management; and (3) as an adjudication tool for cases with professional disagreement, provided the differential diagnosis includes HSIL {22742517}. p16 is also recommended in special circumstances where biopsy findings are ≤ LSIL but there is a high risk of missed high-grade disease, such as in patients with prior cytological findings of HSIL, atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC-H), atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASC-US) with HPV16 positivity, or atypical glandular cells (AGC).

Interpretation of p16 immunohistochemistry should follow the LAST criteria and consider staining intensity, extent, continuity, and location {27134110}. Only block-positive p16 staining is considered supportive of an HSIL diagnosis; this is defined as strong, continuous nuclear or nuclear plus cytoplasmic staining in the basal and parabasal layers (a key criterion), with upward extension involving at least one third of the epithelial thickness. Some authors propose a minimum horizontal extent of six contiguous positive cells, although this criterion is not well validated {25602791}. All other staining patterns, such as cytoplasmic-only, wispy, blob-like, scattered, or patchy staining, should be interpreted as negative/non–block-type and do not support a diagnosis of HSIL {28705710}.

The use of p16 should be reserved primarily for scenarios outlined in the LAST recommendations, which typically apply to < 25% of cervical biopsies, and its routine use in all cases is not recommended. In addition, p16 can be helpful in certain situations, such as evaluating a cauterized margin or assessing for superficial invasion in biopsies where significant inflammation obscures the epithelial-stromal junction.

Of note, approximately 30–58% of adjudicated LSIL (CIN 1) cases exhibit block p16 staining; however, such LSILs do not confer an actionable increased risk of cancer and, therefore, should not prompt reclassification as HSIL (CIN 2) {26541274; 25602791; 29697437}. To avoid overdiagnosing LSIL as HSIL, adherence to morphological criteria and the judicious use of p16 are essential {29697437}. Since the management of HSIL (CIN 2) and HSIL (CIN 3) may vary in different clinical situations, careful application of morphological features when subcategorizing HSIL is required to avoid the overuse of p16. However, it is currently not possible to predict which HSIL, based on microscopy alone, may progress to invasive cancer if left untreated.

Nearly all CIN 2 and CIN 3 are p16 block positive {29697437}. The natural history of rare p16-negative HSIL, particularly CIN 3, remains unclear. Therefore, downgrading a morphologically unequivocal HSIL based on negative/non–block-type p16 staining is not recommended.

Cytology

The Bethesda system provides standardized terminology and diagnostic criteria for reporting cervicovaginal cytology specimens {25931431}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

High-risk HPV testing may be useful in diagnosing HSIL, but the diagnosis can generally be made on morphology with or without p16 staining. HPV testing can help differentiate LSIL from HPV-independent reactive changes, reducing the risk of overdiagnosis, although this is not generally used {29112014}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

LSIL

Essential: proliferation of squamous cells with abnormal nuclear features, increased N:C ratios, and mitotic figures limited to the lower one third of the epithelium; minimal cytoplasmic maturation in the lower third of the epithelium; maturation begins in the middle third and is relatively normal in the upper third.

Desirable: HPV cytopathic effect (koilocytosis).

HSIL

Essential: proliferation of squamous cells with abnormal nuclear features, increased N:C ratios, and mitotic figures involving the middle or upper third of the epithelium.

HSIL (CIN 2)

Essential: full-thickness atypia and mitotic activity extending into the upper half to upper two thirds of the epithelium.

HSIL (CIN 3)

Essential: full-thickness atypia and mitotic activity throughout the epithelium.

Desirable: block-positive p16 immunohistochemistry in selected cases (see the subsection on ancillary testing).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

HPV-associated cervical SILs follow a dynamic course of regression, persistence, and progression; in general, higher-grade lesions are more likely to persist and less likely to regress. Caution is advised when interpreting these data, because of the inherent challenges in classifying lesions and the inconsistent application of p16 across studies. In young and immunocompetent individuals, approximately 60% of LSILs may regress spontaneously without treatment according to a meta-analysis {34176914}. Approximately 10% of LSILs, especially if HPV16-associated, may progress to HSIL. Progression of LSIL to invasive cancer is rare, estimated at 0.03%, likely reflecting true progression, with persistence of high-risk HPV or an HSIL missed at initial diagnosis.

Based on a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of conservatively managed SIL, HSIL (CIN 3) is estimated to regress in 28% of cases, persist in 67%, and progress to carcinoma in 2% {34176914}; HSIL (CIN 2) is more likely to regress spontaneously, as much as 50%, with 32% persisting and 18% progressing to CIN 3+ within 24 months {29487049}. The regression rate for CIN 2 is even higher in women under the age of 30 years, among whom 60% of CIN 2 lesions may regress, 23% persist, and 11% progress to CIN 3+. This supports the potential for conservative (non-excisional methods) management of CIN 2 in young patients {29487049; 39930620; 35861859}. HPV genotype significantly affects CIN 2 prognosis, with HPV16-associated CIN 2 showing lower regression (51%) and higher progression (47%) compared with non-HPV16 cases (83% regression, 16% progression) {35777432}.

HSIL typically precedes invasive cancer by about 20 years, indicating a prolonged period for potential malignant transformation. The risk of progression from untreated HSIL to cancer is estimated at 0.5–1% per year. For HSIL (CIN 3), the cumulative incidence of invasive cancer is approximately 30% over 30 years {18407790}. This long-term estimate is largely based on data from an unethical clinical study at New Zealand’s National Women’s Hospital (1965–1974), where one third of inadequately treated patients with HSIL (CIN 3) progressed to cancer. In comparison, cancer risk was only 0.7% at 30 years for women who received adequate treatment.

Most patients with cervical HSIL can be adequately treated using cryotherapy, thermal ablation, laser ablation, loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), or surgical conization {29126708; 35094363}. Recurrence after excision and ablation are about 1.6% and 2.9% at 6 months, increasing to 3.2% and 7.2% at 12 months {30306032; 32102650}.Risk factors for recurrence include incomplete excision, age ≥ 45 years, unclear or involved margins, persistent HPV infection, and abnormal follow-up cytology {17928267; 34434283. 14658599}. Women treated for cervical HSIL remain at significantly elevated risk of developing cancer not only in the cervix but also in the vagina, vulva, anal canal, and perianal region, highlighting the importance of comprehensive anogenital examination to screen for and prevent multifocal or multizonal neoplasia {24423603; 17959735; 31846057; 38297406}.
