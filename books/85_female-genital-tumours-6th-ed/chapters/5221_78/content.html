5.2.2.1 Endometrioid carcinoma

Definition

Endometrioid carcinoma of the endometrium is a malignant epithelial neoplasm displaying varying proportions of glandular, papillary, and solid architecture, with the neoplastic cells showing endometrioid differentiation.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Molecular subtypes: POLE-ultramutated endometrioid carcinoma (POLEmut); mismatch repair–deficient (MMRd) endometrioid carcinoma; TP53-mutant endometrioid carcinoma (p53abn); and no specific molecular profile (NSMP) endometrioid carcinoma.

Localization

Uterus

Clinical features

Patients typically present with abnormal/postmenopausal bleeding. Advanced disease presents with pelvic/abdominal symptoms that resemble those of a high-stage ovarian carcinoma.

Epidemiology

Although there are few data related to endometrioid carcinomas specifically, it should be noted that there have been striking increases in the incidence of endometrial carcinoma worldwide {38230766; 30574686; 39228624; 38381162}. In the United States, the largest increases were in Black Americans {38230766}. Approximately 40% of endometrial carcinomas are attributed to obesity, and most of these are endometrioid {33127429; 38274134}. The highest incidence is in countries where obesity is widespread {38274134; 37029916}. This affects the incidence of endometrial carcinoma and mortality to a greater degree than for other carcinomas {29160902}. The median age at presentation is 60–65 years [[https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/corp.html]].

Etiology

Prolonged exposure to unopposed estrogen and atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia is present in most but not all cases. Recognized risk factors for endometrial carcinoma are high body mass index {27557308} and use of postmenopausal hormone therapy or tamoxifen {23189749}.

Pathogenesis

Molecular alterations are shared with atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia. There are four molecular subtypes, as described above and detailed in the introduction. Frequently mutated genes include PTEN, PIK3CA, ARID1A, PIK3R1, KRAS, and CTNNB1 {21984976; 9699651; 22653804; 22383975; 21478295}. Most tumours have mutations in at least two of the genes. POLE, TP53, MSH6, and MLH1 mutations are less common and coexist with the frequently mutated genes. Patients with Lynch syndrome {31679916} and Cowden syndrome {29287922} account for < 5% of endometrioid endometrial carcinomas (EECs).

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours are exophytic or diffusely infiltrative. Varying degrees of necrosis and haemorrhage can be seen. Some cases arise within the lower uterine segment.

Histopathology

The distinction of well-differentiated EEC from endometrial atypical hyperplasia / endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia rests on the presence of stromal invasion or its approximate. Confluent glandular, cribriform, labyrinthine, and complex papillary architecture are manifestations of stromal invasion and are common EEC patterns. A desmoplastic reaction, in the appropriate context, can be used to support the impression of stromal invasion as well.

EEC typically displays at least focal glandular architecture. Glands typically have a smooth luminal contour and are lined by columnar cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm containing pseudostratified nuclei. Nuclear atypia is mild to moderate, with inconspicuous nucleoli, except in high-grade EEC. Metaplastic and cytoplasmic changes such as secretory, tubal, endocervical-type mucinous, morular, and squamous are frequent and when present support endometrioid differentiation. The mitotic count is highly variable.

Histological patterns include papillary, villoglandular, small nonvillous papillary, microglandular, trabecular, sex cord–like, spindled, and hyalinized and corded, with or without osseous metaplasia. EECs treated with progestins have the same architecture as untreated tumours but may display superimposed metaplastic changes and less nuclear atypia {17592264; 23010707}.

Rare EECs resemble poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. These have basaloid nests of highly atypical cells, comedo-type necrosis, and (in some cases) abrupt keratinization and ghost cells. The immunophenotype can be unusual (see following). Pilomatrix-like high-grade endometrioid carcinoma {40202384; 37192144; 33811207} is the best described member of a family of EECs with these features {39475375; 39475375}.

There are several myometrial invasion patterns: conventional, pushing, adenoma malignum-like, adenomyosis-like, and microcystic, elongated, and fragmented (MELF) {14501811; 23574770; 31766622}. The conventional pattern features irregular glands and nests with desmoplasia. The adenoma malignum and adenomyosis-like patterns lack both a desmoplastic response and associated endometrial stroma and can be recognized as invasive by virtue of a haphazard distribution of glands in myometrium. The MELF pattern elicits a fibromyxoid and inflammatory pattern that is disproportionate to the volume of the invasive focus. Invasive cells have a metaplastic, eosinophilic, and/or histiocytoid appearance. They may form spaces that resemble endothelial-lined channels.

Subtype histology

Endometrial carcinoma subtypes are defined by immunohistochemical and/or molecular genetic features. There is considerable histological variability within each category and across categories, but there are some characteristic features.

POLEmut subtype

5–10% of EECs are POLEmut {23636398}. These tumours typically demonstrate architectural, cytological, and immunohistochemical heterogeneity. Histologically high-grade components may resemble serous carcinoma {26416160} or feature bizarre cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm {25394778; 28795426; 26416160}. Peritumoural (Crohn-like) and intratumoural lymphocytes are common {35296668}. A combination of grade, peritumoural lymphocytes, DNA MMR proficiency, and aberrant p53 immunohistochemical staining can increase the probability of detection of a POLE mutation almost 5-fold {28795426}. Subclonal expression abnormalities for p53 and DNA MMR proteins may increase the probability further.

dMMR subtype

Approximately one third of EECs are dMMR {23636398}. These carcinomas may also have abundant lymphocytic infiltrates and tumoural heterogeneity {17949789}, but these features tend to be less well developed compared with POLEmut carcinomas.

p53abn subtype

Approximately 10% of EECs are p53abn {23636398}. These carcinomas almost always have pleomorphic nuclei, may be difficult to distinguish from serous carcinoma, and can therefore be challenging to recognize as definitely endometrioid (i.e. histological ambiguity) {19855378; 23629444; 24076778}. 10–15% have ERBB2 amplification, mutation, and/or immunohistochemical overexpression {33602681}. Uncommonly, p53abn EEC can present with minimal cytological atypia, mimicking a low-grade NSMP EEC {37773079}.

NSMP subtype

NSMP carcinomas comprise 50–70% of EECs {23636398; 31422589, 36241860}. More than 90% are FIGO grade 1 or 2 (low-grade) {23636398; 36241860} and tend to show combinations of glandular and solid architecture, frequently with metaplastic changes. Morules are more frequently seen in this subtype, compared with the others. The vast majority (90%) of NSMP EEC show strong and diffuse ER expression {36690721; 36788084}.

Grading

FIGO grading criteria are used: grade 1, 2, and 3 tumours, respectively, exhibit ≤ 5%, 6–50%, and > 50% solid non-glandular, non-squamous growth. The presence of severe cytological atypia in > 50% of cells increases the grade by one level, but serous carcinoma should be excluded in cases with nuclear atypia that is out of proportion to the architecture {21993268}. Note that many POLEmut and dMMR carcinomas contain components with such atypia. Binary grading is recommended {30550484}, whereby grade 1 and 2 tumours are classified as low-grade and grade 3 tumours as high-grade. The prognostic relevance of FIGO grading may be limited to NSMP EECs especially in uterus-confined disease.

Lymphovascular invasion

In stage I endometrial carcinoma, unequivocal lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) is present in approximately 10–15% of cases and correlates with higher grade, deeper myometrial invasion, and advanced stage {3978563; 26067863; 39893748}. LVSI is currently categorized as focal or extensive. The fifth edition of the WHO Female genital tumours required tumour emboli in ≥ 5 vessels to qualify as extensive LVSI as do the FIGO 2023 endometrial carcinoma staging criteria {37593813}. Much of the work from European cooperative groups, however, uses a cut-off value of ≥ 4 {33217679; 38335803}. For purposes of harmonization of future studies, a cut-off value of >/- 4 in one slide is recommended {38937066}. Although extensive/substantial LVSI, seen in approximately 5% of stage I endometrial carcinoma, is readily identifiable at low magnification with high interobserver agreement, focal LVSI often requires high-power examination, is prone to sampling limitations, and is associated with considerable diagnostic variability, particularly in the setting of artefact or MELF-type invasion. Particular artefacts, such as displacement of tumour cells in spaces and retraction of tissue due to delayed fixation, may mimic LVSI and should not be diagnosed as such {29356713}.

Immunohistochemistry

Low-grade EEC shows diffuse and strong immunoreactivity for PAX8, CK7, ER, and PR and patchy positivity for p16 (negative/non–block-type staining). High-grade EECs may be negative for the former markers and may show p16 block-type staining and mutation-type p53 staining {33021522}. Loss of staining for ARID1A, PTEN, or at least one of the MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6) supports endometrioid differentiation in the appropriate setting. Although there are exceptions, GATA3 {30148742} and napsin A {25871622} tend to be negative (seesection Mesonephric-like carcinoma and section Clear cell carcinoma). Pilomatrix-like high-grade endometrioid carcinoma has an unusual immunophenotype: negative PAX8, ER, and PR; aberrant nuclear expression of β-catenin; variable CK7, CDX2, synaptophysin, and p63 expression; and (rarely) positive CK20 {40202384; 37192144; 33811207}.

Several abnormalities that are associated with endometrioid carcinomas can be assessed using immunohistochemistry (i.e. ARID1A, PTEN, and DNA MMR proteins). Note that these aberrations are not specific to endometrioid carcinoma, but when present can support endometrioid differentiation in the appropriate context.

See section Epithelial tumours of the uterine corpus: Introduction and Prognosis and Prediction (below) for application of DNA MMR and p53 immunohistochemistry for the molecular classification algorithm.

Cytology

Pap testing is not a screening modality for endometrial carcinoma, but some tumours shed cells that can be recognized in Pap preparations. Endometrial carcinomas present as small, tight clusters or single round cells with scant, bluish, and frequently vacuolated cytoplasm. Neoplastic cells may have intracytoplasmic neutrophils. A tumour diathesis may be present {11480718}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology is only rarely necessary to confirm a diagnosis of endometrioid carcinoma, although it is frequently used for risk stratification, therapeutic prediction (see following), and Lynch syndrome screening (see Genetic tumour syndromes of the female genital tract).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: endometrioid-type glands with complex architecture and loss of intervening endometrial stroma and/or confluent papillary or solid growth.

Desirable: squamous, morular, or mucinous differentiation.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Anatomical staging (depth of myometrial invasion, cervical stromal invasion, uterine serosal and adnexal involvement, pelvic and extrapelvic spread, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis), as assigned by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC), provides robust prognostic information and can be used in low-resource settings.

Some patients with EEC will also have endometrioid carcinoma in one or both ovaries {11606097; 15297188}, some are metastatic from endometrium to ovary, and others are from synchronous endometrioid carcinomas of endometrium and ovaries” [[Scully RE, Young RH, Clement PB. Tumors of the ovary, maldeveloped gonads, fallopian tube, and broad ligament. Washington, DC: Armed Forces Institute of Pathology; 1998. (AFIP atlas of tumor pathology, series 3; fascicle 23)]]. or independent primary tumours {2982713}. The latter are more common in patients younger than 50 years. Distinction between the two is important, as the metastatic carcinomas have a worse prognosis. As such, the tumours are staged differently. According to the UICC, synchronous carcinomas are staged as independent primary tumours of endometrium and ovary (both stage T1a), while metastatic carcinomas are stage T3a endometrial carcinomas. Synchronous tumours are defined in FIGO 2023 as follows: (1) < 50% myometrial invasion; (2) absence of substantial LVSI; (3) no additional metastases; and (4) unilateral ovarian tumour without ovarian surface involvement {37593813; 30723764}. The carcinoma should be endometrioid and low-grade {30413340}. Many pathologists also use the size of the ovarian tumour (large favours synchronous) and the presence of endometriosis (favours synchronous), but this is not well validated. The term “co-existent endometrial and ovarian carcinoma” has been recommended as a neutral term to encompass these simultaneously occurring endometrial and ovarian carcinomas {40616871}. Almost all such tumours are clonally related, the exception being some Lynch syndrome–associated carcinomas {26832770; 26832771}. This paradox was recognized in the FIGO 2023 staging system by considering all synchronous tumours endometrial primaries, stage IA3, low-stage and low-risk disease.

FIGO grade has traditionally been considered the most important prognosticator after anatomical stage, but the prognostic relevance of FIGO grading may be limited to NSMP EECs especially in uterus-confined disease {37268062}. Nevertheless, it is still mandatory to report the grade.

Where the technology exists, endometrioid carcinomas are assigned a molecular subtype (POLEmut, dMMR, p53abn, and NSMP), which, as explained in the section Epithelial tumours of the uterine corpus: Introduction, has prognostic implications. For endometrioid carcinomas, FIGO 2023 staging {37337978} and ESGO/ESMO risk stratification {33397713} use anatomic staging, binary FIGO grade, molecular subtype, and extent of LVSI for prognostication and therapeutic prediction. Any amount of LVSI is prognostically important {39074932}, but there is a requirement to distinguish between focal and extensive/substantial LVSI, the latter being associated with a worse prognosis in much of the literature {33217679; 38335803; 39074932}. Most recurrences are identified in the vaginal cuff, pelvis, and regional lymph nodes, but p53mut carcinomas, those with extensive/substantial LVSI {40055122}, and pilomatrix-like high-grade endometrioid histology {39475375; 37192144} can metastasize to distant sites such as omentum, lungs, and liver.

Molecular subtyping also provides therapeutically relevant information. dMMR carcinomas may be more radiosensitive than others and are often targeted with checkpoint inhibitors when high-stage {40199646} and p53abn carcinomas may be more chemosensitive than others {32749941; 39854806}. Since 10–15% of p53abn carcinomas have HER2 amplification {33375706}, mutation and/or immunohistochemical overexpression, HER2 antibody-chemotherapy conjugates are used in the setting of high-stage disease or tumour recurrence when there is 3+ HER2 staining.
