12.2.1.1 Partial hydatidiform mole

Definition

Partial hydatidiform mole (PHM) is an abnormal gestation with trophoblastic proliferation and villous hydrops involving part of the chorionic villi, usually with embryonic development. The presence of a diandric, monogynic triploid genome is the fundamental etiology in most cases.

Related terminology

Acceptable: partial molar pregnancy; partial mole.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the uterus and less commonly are ectopic in the fallopian tube {15968202}.

Clinical features

Most patients present in the late first or early second trimester with missed or incomplete abortion. Vaginal bleeding is less common than with complete mole {7070731; 26588240}. Serum β-hCG is normal or mildly elevated {19369669}. Most cases are not suspected clinically prior to evacuation. Ultrasound may show focal placental cystic changes, and a fetus may be detectable {2644599; 7255725}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of hydatidiform moles shows a distinct geographical distribution, being highest in South-East Asia, the Middle East, and South America and lowest in western Europe and North America {29451409; 14602247; 26586414; 12972736}. However, the incidence rates of complete and partial moles are not separated in most studies, and epidemiological data specific to partial mole are lacking. Improvement in diagnostic accuracy in recent years may result in increased detection rates and a rise in the incidence of PHM due to an improved detection of new cases {27258730; 26586414; 29451409}. PHM tends to occur at a younger average age (32 years), and a very few women after the age of 40 years have triploid dispermic PHM in comparison with complete hydatidiform mole (CHM) {31857680}. Maternal age is probably not a significant risk factor for PHM {14602247; 26777992}.

Etiology

Partial moles have excess paternal chromosome complements in most cases, resulting in diandric monogynic triploidy. Rare tetraploid cases with three paternal haploid chromosome sets also exist {22123726; 25078332; 23887308; 33024305}.

Pathogenesis

Most PHMs (> 95%) arise as a result of two spermatozoa fertilizing one egg (dispermic/heterozygous PHM); the remaining cases result from reduplication of the paternal chromosome set (monospermic/homozygous PHM) due to failure of meiosis I or II after fertilization of one egg by one spermatozoon {7125594; 23887308; 28135560}. The exact mechanism by which the excess paternal genome leads to abnormal villous development is not yet understood.

Macroscopic appearance

Villous tissue may appear grossly normal or may show focal hydropic vesicles. Gestational sac, fetal parts, or an intact fetus may be present and show intrauterine growth restriction and malformations {2063893; 11746161}.

Histopathology

PHM typically exhibits a biphasic villous population comprising both small, fibrotic villi and enlarged, irregularly contoured villi characterized by scalloped outlines, variable degrees of hydropic change, and mild to moderate circumferential trophoblastic hyperplasia. Cistern formation and trophoblastic pseudoinclusions are frequently observed histological features {12469234; 23518914}. Fetal vessels and nucleated erythrocytes are commonly present within the villous stroma.

The morphological features of PHM are not entirely specific and may exhibit substantial overlap with a range of other entities, including CHM, hydropic abortion, chromosomal abnormalities such as trisomy syndromes, placental mesenchymal dysplasia, and twin gestations involving a complete mole with a coexisting normal fetus. Additionally, digynic triploidy may closely resemble PHM on ploidy analysis or conventional karyotyping. Therefore, the morphological diagnosis of PHM has substantial interobserver and intraobserver variability, with reported sensitivity of 56–93% and specificity of 58–92%.

Immunohistochemical staining for p57 typically reveals nuclear positivity in both villous cytotrophoblasts and stromal cells in < 99% of PHM cases {30500280; 24907943; 33024305} and is helpful in differentiating them from early CHM cases. Exceptionally, PHM may exhibit absent p57 expression, potentially because of maternal chromosome 11 deletion or other uncharacterized mechanisms.

The potential utility and limitations of various ancillary techniques {33024305;14511255; 39031756} in these contexts should be clearly understood and reported using the standardized terminology outlined in #54030Tables 12.XXX and #54031Table 12 XXX. DNA genotyping provides definitive confirmation by identifying diandric triploidy, the genetic basis of most PHMs {23518914; 28757408; 25078332; 32205485; 39031756}. When available, short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping should be used alongside histopathology to confirm the diagnosis (PHM–diandric triploid). Although genotyping using single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis through next-generation sequencing or microarray platforms can achieve comparable diagnostic accuracy, these methods are more costly and require a larger quantity of template DNA. In resource constrained settings {38421406; 25078332}, #54031Table 12 XXX may be used to guide diagnosis and using consistent terminology for clinical documentation, research and registry reporting in the following scenarios: (1) in the absence of ancillary studies, if a constellation of histological features is present, the recommended diagnosis is morphologically abnormal gestation, favouring PHM (morphology-based criteria); (2) if the histological features are not met, the case should be reported as abnormal villus lesion – unknown ploidy/genotype (cannot exclude PHM). Serial serum hCG monitoring is advised to assess for possible progression to postmolar gestational trophoblastic neoplasia.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

STR DNA genotyping provides precise diagnosis by identifying diandric triploidy {23518914; 28757408; 25078332}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: characteristic morphological features (#54030Tables 12.XXX); diandric triploidy by STR genotyping; definitive diagnosis requires molecular analysis.

Desirable: retained nuclear p57 immunohistochemical expression to exclude complete mole.

The alternative terminology (listed in #54031Table 12 XXX) is recommended if the essential criteria are not met.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Approximately 0.5–5% of PHMs are followed by postmolar gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, mostly invasive hydatidiform mole {26588240; 17086803; 17165438; 20673583}. The risk of choriocarcinoma after PHM is 0.1% {10892763}.
