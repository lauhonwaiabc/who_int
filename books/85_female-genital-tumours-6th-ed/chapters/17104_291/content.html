17.1.0.4 DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome (DICER1)

Definition

DICER1-related tumour predisposition (DICER1) syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder that increases the susceptibility of affected individuals to develop benign and malignant neoplasms across multiple organ systems, including the gynaecological tract. The condition is caused by heterozygous inactivating germline variants in DICER1, which plays an important role in microRNA biogenesis.

MIM numbering

606241 DICER 1, ribonuclease III; DICER1

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: DICER1 syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In the gynaecological tract, tumours typically arise in the ovary and uterine cervix. In patients with DICER1 syndrome, bilateral ovarian tumours may occasionally occur {32333409}.

Clinical features

Affected individuals are predominantly children and young adults. Approximately 5% of carriers develop their first neoplasm before the age of 10 years (female carriers: 4%; male carriers: 7%). The average incidence of neoplasms by the age of 50 years is 19%, with a substantially higher risk in female individuals (female individuals: 27%; male individuals: 10%). Gynaecological neoplasms predominate in older patients {30715996}. Key clinical phenotypes associated with germline DICER1 pathogenic variants are presented in #53065Table 17.XXX.

Epidemiology

The overall incidence is unknown. However, in one population-scale study, putative loss-of-function variants in DICER1 were present in 1 in 3700 individuals to 1 in 4600 individuals {33630087}.

Etiology

DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome is caused by germline pathogenic variants in DICER1 {19556464}, which may also arise de novo or cause somatic mosaicism {26475046}.

Pathogenesis

For tumour development to occur in patients with DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome, heterozygous germline loss-of-function variants is typically accompanied by a somatic missense mutation in the other DICER1 allele. These typically affect the RNase IIIb domain (compound DICER1 mutations) {22187960}. DICER1 is an RNase III endoribonuclease and is essential for processing microRNAs in the RNA interference pathway. Somatic missense variants in the RNase IIIb domain of DICER1 disrupt processing of the hairpin-shaped precursor microRNAs, resulting in an excess of 3p microRNAs and a deficiency of 5p microRNAs, leading to aberrant posttranscriptional regulation of multiple cancer gene networks {25176334}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome–associated neoplasms is nonspecific.

Histopathology

Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours in patients with DICER1 germline variants are often moderately to poorly differentiated, or retiform and may display heterologous elements, such as mucinous epithelium and fetal-type cartilage and/or skeletal muscle differentiation {28654427; 29037807; 38657450} (see section Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour). Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma of the female genital tract is commonly associated with DICER1 syndrome and most often arises in the uterine cervix. Tumours often display fetal-type cartilage {35941719}, a feature highly characteristic of DICER1 association {33846547} (see section Rhabdomyosarcoma). Histologically, both DICER1-associated Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours and embryonal rhabdomyosarcomas are indistinguishable from their non-syndromic counterparts {28654427; 22187960; 31900434}. Other rare tumours in the female genital tract associated with DICER1 syndrome include gynandroblastoma (see section Gynandroblastoma).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A germline DICER1 pathogenic variant must be confirmed.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: germline DICER1 pathogenic variant.

Staging

Staging is the same as for other neoplasms at the corresponding sites.

Prognosis and prediction

Outcome in patients with Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours and embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma of the female genital tract is generally favourable; however, certain features in Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours may be associated with adverse outcomes (see section Sertoli–Leydig cell tumour).
