8.3.3.1 Mucinous carcinoma

Definition

Mucinous carcinoma of ovary is an invasive mucinous neoplasm composed of gastrointestinal-type epithelium.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours are localized in the ovary.

Clinical features

Patients typically present with symptoms related to a pelvic mass. Most tumours are confined to the ovary at presentation. Advanced-stage disease is uncommon, accounting for 10–15% of patients {20862744; 35363158; 36222710; 38155475}.

Epidemiology

Mucinous carcinoma is more common in Asia, South America, and Africa {24556058; 38754054; 39720606} than in North America, where it accounts for 3–4% of all primary ovarian carcinomas {20407318}. Mucinous carcinoma is a more common histological type in Asian, Pacific Islander, and Hispanic ethnic groups {38775277}. The median patient age at presentation is 53 years {30917260; 36222710}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Many mucinous carcinomas develop from mucinous borderline tumours, although some may arise from a mature cystic teratoma or a Brenner tumour {31695154; 40028669}. The most common molecular alterations are copy-number loss of CDKN2A (76%) and KRAS mutations (64%), both considered to be an early event because they have been identified in precursor lesions at similar frequencies {31477716}. TP53 mutations occur in 64% of these tumours, a higher frequency than that in mucinous borderline tumours; this implicates TP53 mutation in the progression from mucinous borderline tumour to mucinous carcinoma {31477716}. ERBB2 (HER2) amplifications are detected in 15–26% of tumours and occur almost exclusively in a TP53-mutated background {31477716}. ERBB2 gene expression is higher among mucinous carcinomas with the confluent/expansile invasive pattern compared with those with the infiltrative/destructive pattern {36222710}. Mucinous carcinomas have overlapping gene expression with upper gastrointestinal and pancreatobiliary carcinomas {36222710}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours are typically large, unilateral, solid, and cystic, with an intact and smooth outer surface and mucoid contents; however, some tumours may rupture and be associated with adhesions. The median tumour size is 180 mm, with a wide size range from 40 to 500 mm {29466254; 35363158; 31782197}. Bilaterality is uncommon, occurring in only 0–4% of patients {29466254; 35363158; 31782197}. 98% of unilateral tumours measuring at least 130 mm are primary ovarian mucinous tumours. Tumours that are bilateral and/or smaller than 130 mm should raise concern for metastasis to the ovary {18162780}.

Histopathology

There is often a continuum of architectural and cytological atypia that includes benign, borderline, and carcinomatous areas. Carcinomas are characterized by two different patterns of invasion: confluent/expansile and infiltrative/destructive, each measuring at least 5 mm in linear extent. The two invasive patterns may coexist; however, the confluent/expansile pattern is more common. The confluent/expansile invasive pattern displays marked glandular crowding, with little or absent intervening stroma, creating a labyrinthine appearance. Papillary and cribriform areas may be present. The intervening stroma in this invasive pattern may contain fibroblastic proliferation resembling an adenofibromatous pattern {37406453; 39523705}. The infiltrative/destructive invasive pattern is characterized by irregular glands, nests, and single cells with malignant cytological features, often in a desmoplastic stroma. A minute focus of infiltrative/destructive invasion in a mucinous carcinoma with expansile invasion should be not considered a mucinous carcinoma with infiltrative/destructive invasion. Infiltrative invasion, in particular in the setting of bilateral ovarian involvement, should raise suspicion of metastatic mucinous carcinoma and prompt evaluation for an extraovarian source {18162780}. The tumours should be well sampled (see section Mucinous borderline tumour) to avoid missing infiltrative/destructive invasion. If an infiltrative/destructive component is present, the proportion of this component per total carcinomatous areas within the tumour should be estimated {33963606; 36305537; 39523705}.

The distinction between confluent/expansile invasion and crowded proliferation of intraepithelial carcinoma in mucinous borderline tumour (see section Mucinous borderline tumour) can be difficult, with interobserver variation in the diagnosis, reflecting the overlapping spectrum of these mucinous tumours {33963606; 36788074; 39523705}. In the setting of problematic cases in the distinction between mucinous carcinoma and mucinous borderline tumour with complex epithelial proliferation, the presence of a mutation-type p53 pattern involving a large proportion of tumour tissue (> 30%) favours the diagnosis of mucinous carcinoma {37406453; 39523705}.

For grading of mucinous carcinomas, a growth pattern–based system, based on the percentage or linear extent of the infiltrative/destructive component among the entire carcinomatous areas, is recommended. This shows better prognostic correlation than the Silverberg and FIGO grading systems {31782197; 35363158; 38155475; 39523705}. A 10% threshold for infiltrative/destructive proportion is used to distinguish low-grade from high-grade (see Prognosis and prediction). Linear extent may be more reproducible than a proportion, but a well-evidenced threshold has not been determined {38155475}.

Mural nodules as described in benign and borderline mucinous tumours may occur (see section Mucinous borderline tumour).

Ovarian mucinous carcinomas are typically diffusely positive for CK7 and variably positive for CK20, CEA, and CDX2 {16931958}. CA19-9 is often diffusely positive; CA125, WT1, napsin A, vimentin, ER, and PR are usually negative. PAX8, usually focal and weak, is positive in a subset of tumours {31239549}. p53 may show wildtype or mutation-type staining {32724153; 37500307}. p16 is usually negative or focally positive (non–block-type immunoreactivity) {17460447; 30062862}. Teratoma-associated mucinous carcinomas with lower intestinal morphology and immunophenotype should be considered somatic tumours arising from teratomas (see section Mature teratoma (including somatic neoplasms arising from teratoma) {33963606}. Diffuse strong positivity for SATB2 is present in < 10% of mucinous carcinomas {33706757} and in the absence of a teratomatous component should raise suspicion of metastatic disease from the lower gastrointestinal tract. A two-marker panel of CK7 and SATB2 is useful in distinguishing primary ovarian mucinous carcinoma from metastatic colorectal or appendiceal tumours, with PAX8 and CDX2 serving as secondary markers {31239549}. Metastatic adenocarcinomas from upper gastrointestinal and pancreatobiliary tracts and uterine cervix may exhibit overlapping immunohistochemical profiles with ovarian mucinous carcinoma (see section Metastasis to the ovary) {33706757}.

Cytology

Cytology specimens from patients with mucinous carcinoma may contain crowded atypical epithelial cells with variation in nuclear size and increased N:C ratio.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: mucinous tumour with cytological atypia and confluent/expansile and/or infiltrative/destructive invasion.

Staging

Mucinous carcinoma is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Most mucinous carcinomas are confined to the ovary (stage I) at presentation, and prognosis is very favourable. In a SEER analysis, the 5-year survival rate was 91% for stage I, 76% for stage II, and 17% for stage III/IV disease {29718305}. The survival of patients with advanced-stage mucinous carcinomas is worse than that of patients with high-grade serous carcinomas {21861962; 29718305}. Carcinomas with confluent/expansile invasion have a better prognosis than those displaying infiltrative/destructive stromal invasion {11812936; 23321546; 24487473; 29466254}. Recurrences tend to occur within 3 years of diagnosis and have low rates of response to chemotherapy {21861962; 29561302}. Most patients with extraovarian disease at presentation die of disease {20862744; 24487473}. Infiltrative/destructive invasion is confirmed in multivariable analysis to be associated with significantly worse prognosis within the first 2 years of diagnosis compared with confluent/expansile invasion, independent of tumour stage {36222710; 38155475}. Stage I patients with tumours containing infiltrative/destructive invasion are recommended to receive adjuvant chemotherapy {31048403}. A high proportion of infiltrative/destructive invasion (> 10% of total carcinomatous areas) is an unfavourable prognostic feature, whereas the prognosis for tumours with infiltrative foci < 1 mm or infiltrative proportion ≤ 10% is comparable to that of tumours with confluent/expansile invasion {31782197; 35363158; 38155475}.
