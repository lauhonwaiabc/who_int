9.2.3.2 Sex cord stromal tumour NOS

Definition

Sex cord stromal tumour NOS (SCST-NOS) is a sex cord stromal neoplasm that lacks definitive characteristics of a specific tumour type.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the ovary.

Clinical features

Patients may present with symptoms related to an adnexal mass or with hormonal manifestations, which may be estrogenic or androgenic. Occasional patients present with disseminated peritoneal involvement, sometimes with minimal or even absent ovarian involvement {27801763}.

Epidemiology

SCST-NOS is a rare diagnosis, but reliable data are scarce because this is a diagnosis of exclusion in a group of uncommon tumours and is subject to interobserver variability in diagnosis. Around 5–10% of ovarian SCSTs have been estimated to fall into this group; however, this may be an overestimate due to referral bias {24134930; 8651349}. In one large study, the mean patient age was 49 years {8651349}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Most molecularly characterized SCST-NOS lack known driver mutations that are characteristic of other SCSTs, such as FOXL2 and DICER1. Molecular analysis of FOXL2, DICER1, and STK11 assisted in establishing a diagnosis other than SCST-NOS in 84% (42/50) of cases in one series {32782216}, and FOXL2 testing resulted in reclassification of 30% (6/20) as adult granulosa cell tumour in another smaller series {23348906}. On the other hand, some morphologically unusual tumours may remain as SCST-NOS despite having molecular alterations characteristic of a specific SCST type {24136150}. Therefore, without supportive morphology and immunohistochemistry, molecular findings alone do not always justify classification beyond SCST-NOS. This is particularly important for newly emerging molecular alterations without extensive morphological correlation studies. For example, one SCST-NOS has been reported in a patient with germline BAP1 mutation and a family history suggestive of BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome {35348477}. Similarly, nonspecific molecular prognostic alterations (e.g., TP53, TERT) are not usually considered early drivers of the tumorigenesis and therefore do not influence diagnostic categorization.

Although molecular findings may assist in the reclassification of morphologically ambiguous tumours, the absence of a characteristic molecular alteration does not automatically revert neoplasms with clear morphological and immunohistochemical features of a specific tumour type back to the SCST-NOS category. For example, FOXL2 wildtype tumours with unequivocal AGCT morphology are not reclassified as SCST-NOS {39615884; 40326456}. In addition, the context of the alteration itself (e.g. mutation type, pathogenicity, etc.) is as important as the altered gene. Lastly, diagnosis of SCST-NOS requires a presumed sex cord stromal lineage on the basis of the available data; thus, newer entities with overlapping morphological and immunohistochemical features but unclear cell of origin (e.g. STK11-adnexal tumours, GLI1-fusion tumours) should not be considered as part of the SCST-NOS spectrum. Overall, even when the molecular results are available, they should only be interpreted in the context of integration of the clinical, morphological, and immunohistochemical features, as relying on isolated criteria risks misclassification.

Macroscopic appearance

The appearance is variable from predominantly solid to solid and cystic to cystic masses. They usually have a yellow or tan cut surface. Haemorrhage and/or necrosis may be present.

Histopathology

The histological features of SCST-NOS are variable, and judicious sampling may assist in picking up areas more diagnostic of a specific tumour type. Although characteristics of a SCST are evident, distinct diagnostic features of a specific SCST type are not present. Common histological features include cords, trabeculae, and tubules while some tumours are predominantly spindled; the degree of nuclear atypia and mitotic activity is variable, but high-grade nuclei may be present, including multinucleate cells {8651349}. Areas of necrosis may be present, especially in cases with high-grade nuclear atypia. Overall, the common defining feature is morphological and immunohistochemical evidence supporting sex cord stromal lineage. In pregnancy, these neoplasms may exhibit prominent oedema and/or luteinization of the neoplastic cells or ovarian stromal cells {6735262}. SCST-NOS should not be confused with gynandroblastoma, which represents an SCST composed of two or more different specific morphological types.

SCST-NOS typically exhibits positive staining with sex cord markers such as inhibin, calretinin, SF1, and FOXL2, although the distribution and intensity of staining may be variable. EMA is usually negative.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although not required for diagnosis, even a limited molecular panel (e.g. FOXL2, DICER1, STK11, CTNNB1) is encouraged, as it may assist in further classification of a subset of SCST-NOS. However, integration of the clinical, morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular features remains essential, as discussed above. Patients with DICER1- and STK11-mutated tumours should be considered for genetic counselling referral and germline testing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphological features supporting a sex cord stromal lineage, but absence of features of a specific tumour type.

Desirable: positive staining with sex cord stromal immunohistochemical markers.

Staging

These neoplasms are staged using the FIGO and TNM staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

There is limited literature regarding the behaviour of SCST-NOS. In the largest published series, most patients were diagnosed at stage I, and the 5- and 10-year survivals were 92% and 74% respectively {8651349}. Although systematic data are lacking, on the basis of the general understanding of SCSTs, tumours presenting at an advanced stage, exhibiting high-grade morphological features, or harbouring molecular alterations linked to poor prognosis (e.g., TP53 and TERT mutations) may carry an increased risk for adverse outcomes.
