16.1.0.1 Metastases to the lower female genital tract

Definition

Metastases to the lower female genital tract are tumours that secondarily involve the uterus, vagina, or vulva from other primary sites.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Uterus (corpus and cervix), vagina, vulva {6322966; 3955507; 29750707; 32910021; 12766584}

Clinical features

Patients may present with bleeding, abnormal cervical cytology {3955507; 15943996; 21979600; 22961708}, or a nodule/mass accompanied by ulceration or pain {12766584}. Patients may be asymptomatic if the infiltration affects the myometrium only. For most patients, the primary site of origin was already known at the time of the secondary site involvement, but occasionally, such metastasis may represent the first manifestation of disease, or the only site of metastasis {32910021; 22353422}.

Epidemiology

Most tumours that secondarily involve the uterine corpus, cervix, vulva, and vagina originated from elsewhere within the female genital tract {6322966}. The reported patient age range is wide (10–86 years) {6322966; 12766584; 30918738; 32910021}. Metastases to the vulva, uterine corpus, and uterine cervix are uncommon {6322966; 3955507; 29750707; 12766584}. The vagina is the most common site of metastases among the lower genital tract organs {6322966}, and metastases to the vagina are substantially much more common than primary vaginal malignancies [[Fu YS, Reagan JW. Pathology of the Uterine Cervix, Vagina, and Vulva. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 1989.]].

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Metastases to the uterine corpus, uterine cervix, vagina, or vulva occur through one or more mechanisms, including transmucosal/pagetoid spread, direct extension, or as a manifestation of lymphatic/haematogenous-based dissemination {34570018; 28461034; 6322966}. Secondary malignancies of the lower female genital tract are most commonly carcinomas {32910021; 6322966}, but a wide variety of other tumour types have been described. Most secondary tumours in the uterine corpus occur by direct extension from neoplasms of neighbouring organs, i.e. the uterine cervix, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. Among extragenital primary tumours metastatic to the uterine corpus, the most frequently reported are mammary invasive lobular carcinoma, gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma, and colorectal carcinomas {29750707; 30918738; 15943994}. Contiguous spread to the cervix from a primary endometrial carcinoma is most common {14501374; 12883240}, followed by metastases from the ovary, gastrointestinal tract, and breast {3955507; 22353422; 32910021}. Urothelial carcinoma of the urinary tract rarely metastasizes to the cervix, resembling primary papillary squamous cel carcinoma {27801758; 22510759}. Metastases to the vagina most commonly originate from cervical and endometrial carcinomas {6322966}. Other potential sites of origin include the colorectum, ovary, vulva, urinary tract, and breast, as well as cutaneous melanoma, uterine leiomyosarcoma, and gestational trophoblastic tumours, among others {12649667; 18720923; 20626179; 39637520}. Metastases to the vulva arise mainly from the gynaecological tract {11326939; 6322966}, but as much as 45% of non-squamous malignancies that involve the vulva may be secondary {12766584; 11326939; 36731045; 33032903; 28129884}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearances are variable. The metastases may be grossly undetectable, or they may present as single or multiple nodules and masses that may be ulcerated {12766584; 32910021}.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of metastatic disease requires careful correlation of clinical and pathological findings. Morphological features suggesting metastasis to the uterine corpus include a histological pattern that is unusual for primary endometrial carcinoma, diffuse replacement of endometrial stroma with sparing of benign endometrial glands, lack of premalignant changes in endometrial glands, and disproportionate carcinomatous involvement of the serosa or outer myometrium. Features suggesting metastasis to the cervix include histological patterns that are unusual for primary tumours of this site, multifocal tumours, extensive permeation of tumour cells in endocervical stroma with relative sparing of endocervical glands, signet-ring cells, lack of an in situ component, and extensive lymphovascular invasion {32910021; 2171127; 3955507; 23748795}. Secondary malignancies of both gynaecological and non-gynaecological origin may colonize native ectocervical and endocervical epithelium, thereby mimicking in situ carcinoma {20414103; 32910021}. Similarly, endometrial endometrioid carcinoma that secondarily involves the cervix may show deceptively bland morphology and be devoid of an associated stromal response, simulating primary cervical adenocarcinoma (including gastric-type HPV-independent adenocarcinoma) or even benign lesions such as mesonephric hyperplasia {12493029; 12883240; 20414103; 19117974}. The histopathology of metastases to the vagina varies with the type of tumour. Vulvar metastases commonly involve the dermis and subcutis. Pagetoid spread of rectal or bladder carcinoma to the lower genital tract {28461034; 22510759; 34570018} may mimic vulvar extramammary Paget disease {22510759}. Immunohistochemistry may help confirm the diagnosis and the origin of the metastasis (see #56407Table 16.XXX from section section Metastasis: Introduction).

Cytology

Isolated or clustered malignant tumour cells in a background of normal cervical cells without tumour cell diathesis are suggestive of metastatic nature {27434279}. Melanoma and sarcoma cells have been reported in cervical smears {30354020; 12040638}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnostic molecular pathology depends on the primary neoplasm.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: unusual histopathology pattern for the site; clinical correlation.

Desirable: immunohistochemical profile consistent with primary site of origin (see #56407Table 16.XXX).

Staging

Stage according to the primary neoplasm.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is poor because there are often accompanying systemic metastases {2171127; 12766584; 21941962}.
