4.3.1.7 Other adenocarcinomas

Definition

Other adenocarcinomas include invasive adenocarcinomas arising from the cervix or vagina that do not fit into the other well-established categories of primary adenocarcinoma. These tumours comprise endometrioid adenocarcinoma and adenocarcinoma NOS.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Acceptable: endometrioid adenocarcinoma of cervix or vagina; adenocarcinoma NOS of cervix or vagina.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Cervix and vagina; vaginal tumours have a variable location but are most commonly located in the apex {17895749}.

Clinical features

There is no specific information about primary cervical endometrioid adenocarcinoma or adenocarcinoma NOS other than the typical manifestations of a cervical mass, such as nonspecific pelvic symptoms, vaginal bleeding, and/or positive cervical cytology tests. In most reported cases of primary vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinoma, presentation was with a vaginal mass; some patients had a prior hysterectomy, some were taking unopposed estrogens, and none had a history of exposure to diethylstilbestrol {17895749}. Before diagnosing a primary cervical or vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinoma, spread from elsewhere within the female genital tract, especially the uterine corpus or ovary, should be excluded.

Epidemiology

Endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the cervix is rare, accounting for no more than 1% of all primary cervical adenocarcinomas in a large case series {29135516}. Because of its morphological similarities to those of usual-type HPV-associated adenocarcinoma and a lack of proper definitions, the reported prevalence was higher in older publications where many cases reported as primary cervical endometrioid adenocarcinoma represented HPV-associated adenocarcinomas {12207781; 11002225}. Primary vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinomas are also rare, with little epidemiological data available; in the largest reported series, patients’ age at presentation ranged from 45 to 81 years (mean = 60 years) {17895749}.

Despite studies reporting primary cervical serous carcinoma in the older literature {9422324}, the general consensus now is that most or all carcinomas with serous morphology detected in the cervix represent metastasis or direct extension from adnexal or endometrial serous carcinomas {20414103}, or represent other primary cervical adenocarcinomas with serous-like morphology, such as HPV-associated adenocarcinomas, including those with a micropapillary architecture, and HPV-independent adenocarcinomas {32699256}. Serous carcinoma is not recognized as a type of primary vaginal adenocarcinoma.

Etiology

Endometriosis is a possible risk factor for endometrioid adenocarcinoma of either site. Cervical endometrioid adenocarcinoma could arise in endometriosis or from an endometrioid-type adenocarcinoma in situ precursor lesion, although evidence is very limited {37030165; 38199133; 31883176}. In the largest reported series of primary vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinomas, endometriosis was identified in 14 of 18 cases (78%) {17895749}.

Pathogenesis

Cervical and vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinomas are considered to be HPV-independent neoplasms. Further specific underlying pathogenetic mechanisms are currently unknown. Adenocarcinoma NOS is an HPV-independent neoplasm.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours usually present as a visible polypoid or ulcerated cervical or vaginal mass, or they may be deeply seated in the cervical stroma or submucosa associated with endometriosis.

Histopathology

Endometrioid adenocarcinomas typically show confirmatory endometrioid features, including at least focal low-grade endometrioid glands lined by columnar cells with pseudostratified bland nuclei, with or without squamous or mucinous differentiation; there may be cytoplasmic vacuolation. Papillary and solid areas may also be present. Therefore, cervical and vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinomas have a histological appearance that is identical to that of endometrioid adenocarcinomas in the endometrium or ovary, and it is not possible to definitively determine tumour origin based on morphology alone {39328955}, particularly in biopsy specimens. Invasion into the cervical stroma by primary cervical endometrioid adenocarcinoma may be deceptive and can mimic a benign process due to bland morphology and lack of a desmoplastic reaction {8317607; 37922950}. It should be noted that a similar appearing pattern of secondary involvement of the cervix by endometrial endometrioid adenocarcinoma has also been described {12883240}. ER and/or PR should be positive in endometrioid adenocarcinomas. Most show patchy negative/non–block-type staining for p16, and all are negative for HPV. True endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the cervix should not be diagnosed in the presence of a mucin-poor endocervical adenocarcinoma. This diagnosis can only be established after primary endometrial carcinoma and HPV association are excluded.

A tumour that resembles serous carcinoma in the cervix, with papillary architecture, high mitotic rate, and high-grade nuclear atypia, probably represents a primary HPV-associated adenocarcinoma (sometimes with a micropapillary pattern {30864975}), an HPV-independent gastric-type adenocarcinoma, or a drop metastasis from an endometrial or extrauterine serous carcinoma and should be worked up accordingly. Primary serous carcinoma of the cervix is no longer an accepted entity.

Adenocarcinoma NOS is a diagnosis of exclusion when the features do not conform to any other primary cervical adenocarcinoma or when HPV status cannot be determined; a metastasis should also be excluded. Sometimes when this diagnosis is made on a biopsy specimen, areas diagnostic of other neoplasms may be present in a subsequent resection specimen.

Cytology

Primary HPV testing will not detect endometrioid-type adenocarcinoma or adenocarcinoma NOS. The morphological features of these tumour types in cytological preparations are not well described.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

If HPV testing is performed, endometrioid adenocarcinomas should be HPV negative.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: adenocarcinoma with endometrioid features (for endometrioid adenocarcinoma); ER and/or PR expression; exclusion of tumour spread from elsewhere in the female genital tract.

Desirable: association with endometriosis; negative/non–block-type p16 staining and/or negative HPV testing.

Staging

Tumours are staged according to the FIGO and TNM staging systems. Tumours arising in endometriosis may be centred in the cervical or vaginal wall, as opposed to the mucosa, making assessment of depth of invasion challenging, and an explanatory comment may be required in such circumstances.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for primary endometrioid adenocarcinoma of the cervix is unclear due to the previous lack of uniform criteria and limited published literature {32405523}. In the largest reported series of 18 primary vaginal endometrioid adenocarcinomas, outcome varied substantially: 2 patients died of their disease, 2 patients were alive with disease, and 11 patients were alive and well, with as many as 12 years of follow-up {17895749}. There is no specific information regarding cervical adenocarcinoma NOS.
