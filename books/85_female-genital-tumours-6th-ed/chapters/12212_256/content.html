12.2.1.2 Complete hydatidiform mole

Definition

Complete hydatidiform mole (CHM) is an abnormal gestation with diffuse trophoblastic proliferation and villous hydrops without embryonic development. The presence of androgenetic (paternal-only) genome is the fundamental genetic etiology in most cases.

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Acceptable: complete molar pregnancy; complete mole.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the uterus and less commonly are ectopic in the fallopian tube or ovary {24134929; 39537224}.

Clinical features

Well-developed CHM (typically evacuated in the second trimester) present with vaginal bleeding, excessive uterine size, marked elevation of serum β-hCG, and missed abortion {34669197; 39242292}. Other rare symptoms include hyperemesis, thyrotoxicosis, pre-eclampsia, and pulmonary embolism {39242292}. Ultrasound shows snowstorm appearance and absence of fetal development {28287945; 36763119}. Very early complete mole (evacuated in the first trimester, typically before 10 weeks of gestation) may not be suspected clinically and sonographically.

Epidemiology

The incidence of hydatidiform mole varies widely throughout the distinct geographical regions, being highest in South-East Asia (as many as 13 cases per 1000 pregnancies), the Middle East (as many as 5.8 cases per 1000 pregnancies), and South America (4.6 cases per 1000 pregnancies) and lowest in western Europe and North America (0.8–1.5 cases per 1000 pregnancies) {14602247}. Overall, worldwide population-based incidence rates of hydatidiform moles are approximately 1 to 2.5 cases per 1000 pregnancies on average {29451409; 36047375; 39242293; 39401964}. Asian countries report decreasing trends of gestational trophoblastic disease while the incidence rates increase in Europe and North America {39242293}.

Etiology

Development of sporadic hydatidiform moles has a multifactorial etiology {14602247} including genetic predisposition, ethnicity, environmental factor, or interaction among these factors {38811887; 39242293}. Significant risk factors for CHM include maternal age of < 20 or > 40 years, Asian race, a history of prior hydatidiform mole, and genetics (recurrent familial CHM) {14602247; 26777992; 33024305}. An increased risk of fertilization of an abnormal oocyte at the beginning and end of a woman’s reproductive life has been hypothesized {11843379; 26777992}. Germline mutations of maternal-effect genes account for the most familial biparental moles. Socioeconomic status and diet have also been suggested as possible risk factors for CHM.

Pathogenesis

Most sporadic CHMs have an androgenetic (paternal-only) genome harbouring two paternal haploid chromosome sets of either homozygous origin (80–90%) or heterozygous origin (10–20%) {23887308; 32404958; 33024305}. Rare tetraploid sporadic CHMs have four paternal haploid chromosome sets {8651859; 23633551}. CHM develops through global imprinting alteration in a preimplantation embryo due to de novo absence of the maternal genome. Overexpression of the paternal genome leads to trophoblastic hyperplasia and failure of fetal development {28135560}. Although the exact mechanism by which this occurs is not yet fully understood, defects in meiosis I is thought to contribute to the genesis of androgenetic CHMs {39545410}. Mutations in the maternal-effect genes NLRP7 (NALP7) and KHDC3L have been identified as causative events in 60% of recurrent hydatidiform moles (all associated with familial biparental CHM) {16462743; 21885028; 29463882; 39322462}. MEI1, TOP6BL, REC114, PADI6, and NLRP5 are less frequent maternal effect genes, accounting for 3% of cases {39322462}.

Macroscopic appearance

Well-developed CHM consists of voluminous, hydropic villi with visibly enlarged, semitransparent vesicles of variable sizes. Normal placental structures or fetal parts are absent, except in cases with a coexisting normal twin or multiple gestations {28132722; 31833013; 35796652}. Very early CHMs have minimal or no gross evidence of abnormal villi and are indistinguishable from non-molar products of conception.

Histopathology

Well-developed CHM is characterized by diffusely enlarged villi with marked hydropic changes, frequent cistern formation, and pronounced circumferential trophoblastic hyperplasia. Significant cytological atypia and mitotic figures are common. In a very early CHM, the villi may not be enlarged but have an irregular, bulbous, or polypoid appearance, with hypercellular myxoid stroma and prominent karyorrhexis. Trophoblast hyperplasia may be limited. There are no fetal or non-villous placental tissues present, although some exceptions exist. Rare cases with fetal vessels and red blood cells may indicate coexistence of a twin/multiple gestations or a CHM arising in association with an androgenetic/biparental mosaic/chimeric conception {23370656; 35796652}. Some cases, particularly well-developed CHMs, show prominent trophoblastic hyperplasia with detached atypical trophoblast fragments and infiltration of atypical intermediate trophoblasts at the implantation site. These findings fall within the spectrum of CHM-associated trophoblastic hyperplasia and exaggerated implantation sites and do not indicate a separate neoplasm such as choriocarcinoma or placental site trophoblastic tumour. However, a diagnosis of choriocarcinoma arising in CHMs or intramolar choriocarcinoma may be made in rare cases with abundant solid growth of biphasic atypical trophoblasts in the presence of scant associated molar villi (see section Invasive and metastatic hydatidiform moles) {28877059}. Immunohistochemically, p57 (KIP2) nuclear staining is absent in cytotrophoblast and villous stromal cells in both sporadic and familial biparental CHMs {23887308; 24907943; 33024305}.

The differential diagnosis includes partial hydatidiform mole, hydropic abortion, and non-molar early gestation that may share overlapping morphology with villous hydrops and trophoblastic hyperplasia but retained p57 nuclear expression in cytotrophoblast and villous stromal cells {28135560; 30500280; 39322461; 36729386}. Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping provides precise diagnosis of sporadic CHM by identifying androgenetic genome {20308840; 28135560; 30500280; 33024305; 34088998; 39801212}. Although single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis via next-generation sequencing or microarray platforms can achieve comparable diagnostic accuracy, these methods are more costly and require a larger quantity of template DNA.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

Well-developed CHM: a constellation of diffusely enlarged villi with marked hydropic changes; frequent cistern formation; and pronounced circumferential trophoblastic hyperplasia.

Very early CHM: normal-sized villi with irregular, bulbous, or polypoid configurations; hypercellular myxoid stroma; and prominent karyorrhexis.

Familial biparental diploid CHM: morphological features of either well-developed CHM or very early CHM; absence of p57 expression in cytotrophoblast and villous stromal cells; and balanced biparental genetic profile upon STR genotyping.

Desirable:

Very early CHM and well-developed CHM: absence of nuclear staining of p57 in cytotrophoblasts and villous stromal cells or androgenetic genotype on short tandem genotype testing.

Familial biparental diploid CHM: molecular analysis of germline mutations in NLRP7 (NALP7), KHDC3L, and other maternal effect genes.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

CHM carries a 15–20% risk of postmolar gestational trophoblastic neoplasia and a 2–3% risk of gestational choriocarcinoma, regardless of the gestational age at diagnosis {20673583; 25969351; 33416290; 39242292}. Studies have shown a higher frequency of GTN associated with heterozygous CHM than with homozygous cases {20208060; 26535984; 31857680; 32404958}. High serum hCG levels, recurrent mole, and older age are risk factors for developing GTN. The risk of subsequent CHM is 1–2% after one prior CHM and 15–20% after two consecutive complete moles, and the recurrent mole is likely a complete mole as well {39242292; 26202916}. Because risk stratification for GTN is not yet possible, all CHMs necessitate postmolar hCG surveillance.
