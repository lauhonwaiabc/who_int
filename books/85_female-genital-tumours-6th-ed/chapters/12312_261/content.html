12.3.1.2 Placental site trophoblastic tumour

Definition

Placental site trophoblastic tumour (PSTT) is a neoplasm consisting of implantation site–type intermediate trophoblast.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur in the uterine corpus, lower uterine segment, and occasionally in extrauterine sites {14501817; 24152517; 27513078}.

Clinical features

The most common presentation is abnormal vaginal bleeding, occurring in as many as 80% of the cases, followed by amenorrhoea {16085293; 27549089; 31047719; 36804610}. Less common symptoms include uterine enlargement, abdominal mass, and history of missed abortion. PSTT occurs more commonly (two thirds of the cases) following a normal pregnancy with a median latency of 12–18 months, but it can also occur following abortion and molar pregnancy. Some symptoms are determined by metastasis or related to ectopic pregnancy. Serum levels of β-hCG are generally low (< 1000 mIU/mL in as many as 80% of cases). At presentation, > 80% of the cases are FIGO stage I tumours {16246400}. FIGO stage II disease commonly involves adnexa or pelvic lymph nodes.

Epidemiology

The tumour occurs with an estimated incidence of 1 case in 100 000 pregnancies and 0.006 case per 100 000 female inhabitants per year, accounting for 0.25–5% of all trophoblastic tumours {27549089; 31047719; 38281479}. PSTT typically occurs in reproductive-aged women, with a mean age at diagnosis of 29–35 years (range 18–63 years) {16246400; 31047719; 35201361}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

PSTT is thought to arise from neoplastic transformation of cytotrophoblasts that differentiate towards implantation site–type intermediate trophoblastic cells {17613426}. The tumour typically exhibits rare genetic imbalances {14657956} and has a preferential requirement for the paternal X chromosome in the tumour genome {10879746; 17643092; 27246306}. Similar to other gestational trophoblastic tumours, PSTTs show a low tumour mutational burden, and microsatellite stability {31954539; 38301309}. Rare cases harbour somatic variants in ERBB2, KIT, KDR, APC, TP53, and SMAD4 {37260170; 38301309}.

Macroscopic appearance

PSTT generally involves the endomyometrium as nodular, solid masses with size as large as 10 cm and a fleshy, whitish tan to light-yellow cut surface. Nearly all tumours are myoinvasive, and deep myometrial invasion is seen in 50% of cases {16246400; 19552948; 36804610}. Focal haemorrhage and necrosis can be seen {27549089; 33139212; 39322463}. Transmural invasion is seen in 10%, and perforation/extension to adjacent organs may occur {16246400}.

Histopathology

The tumour has an infiltrative growth pattern consisting of aggregates or sheets of large, polyhedral to round, predominantly mononucleated implantation site–type intermediate trophoblast. Scattered multinucleated tumour cells are common. At the periphery, the tumour cells typically infiltrate the myometrium singly or in cords and nests, separating the smooth muscle fibres. Cytologically, the cells have abundant amphophilic (and occasionally eosinophilic or clear) cytoplasm with occasional vacuoles. Nuclear atypia is generally pronounced, with frequent, large, convoluted nuclei and marked hyperchromasia. Chorionic villi are almost never identified. Most PSTTs have a low mitotic count, with 1–2 mitoses/mm2 (equating to 2–4 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.5 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area) {16246400; 19552948}. Characteristic vascular invasion is often present, with tumour cells replacing the vascular wall. Tumour cells diffusely express hPL, MUC4, HSD3B1, HLA-G, GATA3, and MCAM (CD146). Expression of hCG, inhibin, and SALL4 is limited {39876093}. Ki-67 is expressed in 10–30% of cells. PDL1 is highly expressed in PSTT {30339966; 31013360}.

The differential diagnoses include exaggerated placental site, other trophoblastic tumours (epithelioid trophoblastic tumour and choriocarcinoma), squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, epithelioid leiomyoma, and leiomyosarcoma. Rare non-gestational PSTTs of germ cell origin typically arise in the ovary and affect children and young adults (mean age: 22 years; range 2.5–43 years), younger than those with gestational PSTT. Presenting symptoms include abdominal pain, pelvic mass, isosexual precocious puberty, elevated testosterone, and/or a positive pregnancy test. These tumours appear as solid and cystic haemorrhagic masses and frequently coexist with ovarian teratomas containing diverse tissue types. Histologically, they resemble uterine gestational PSTTs {18671454; 31688005; 38172961; 40119593}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

DNA genotyping provides a definitive diagnostic separation of PSTT from other somatic neoplasms, assesses its relationship with antecedent pregnancy for prognostic scoring, and helps determine the tumour origin (gestational vs non-gestational) {18820674; 26980014; 31688005; 34088998; 38172961; 39801212}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an invasive, mass-forming neoplasm with confirmed expression of trophoblastic marker(s).

Staging

The FIGO and Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classifications are used for staging.

Prognosis and prediction

Simple hysterectomy is curative in most cases. Long-term survival for stage I tumour with low-risk disease is approaching 90% at 10 years {19552948; 27789086; 31047719; 39322463}. Approximately 25–30% of patients may develop recurrent disease or metastasis, and about half of them die of the tumour {11520134; 12092014; 35201361}, leading to an overall mortality rate of 6.5–27% {31047719}. The most common metastatic site is lung. Advanced stage, ≥ 48 months since antecedent pregnancy, age > 40 years, and the presence of tumour cells with clear cytoplasm are independent predictors for worse prognosis {16246400; 19552948; 27789086; 31047719; 33139212; 36804610; 39322463}. Fertility-saving surgery has been reported in low-risk young patients {36804610}. Although PSTT is relatively resistant to chemotherapy, anti-PDL1 checkpoint immunotherapy may be an effective salvage therapeutic option {39322463; 40031424}.
