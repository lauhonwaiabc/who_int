5.1 Epithelial tumours of the uterine corpus: Introduction

This chapter includes changes from the Tumours of the uterine corpus chapter in the fifth-edition Female genital tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Female genital tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 4). https://publications.iarc.who.int/592.]], the most important of which is the exclusive focus on epithelial neoplasia and hence the title change. Sarcomas, including adenosarcoma, and endometrial polyps are now covered in section Mesenchymal tumours. Endometrial metaplasia and Arias-Stella reaction are no longer included. Mesonephric-like carcinoma has its own entry that is separate from other endometrial carcinomas.

In 2021, it was estimated that there were 360 253 patients diagnosed with endometrial carcinoma globally, with 84 630 deaths, a significant increase since 1990 {40066179}. For example, there has been a 1.3% annual increase in incidence in the United States {38230766}, 3.5% in the Republic of Korea {30574686}, and 8.3% in Sri Lanka {39228624}. There has been an eightfold increase in incidence between 1980 and 2019 in Japan, and mortality has tripled {38381162}. In the United States, mortality rates have increased among White, Hispanic, Asian American / Pacific Islander, and Black population, but the largest increases were in Black Americans {38230766}.

Endometrial carcinomas have the highest incidence in countries with a very high Human Development Index and the highest obesity rates (see #51028Fig. 5.XXX) {38274134; 37029916}. Approximately 75% of endometrial carcinomas are attributed to obesity, and this affects the incidence and mortality of endometrial carcinoma to a greater degree than those of any other carcinoma {29160902}. Unopposed estrogen and related conditions, such as polycystic ovarian syndrome {38941743; 36960190}, diabetes mellitus {38684955}, and metabolic syndrome {37139668}, are major risks. Oral contraceptives are protective {39981760; 39173088}. A model that predicts the risk of endometrial cancer in asymptomatic women includes age; body mass index; waist circumference; age at menarche, last birth, and menopause; hormone replacement therapy; tamoxifen; oral contraceptives; type 2 diabetes; smoking; and family history of colon cancer {38073256}. A large share of endometrioid carcinomas therefore correspond to Bokhman’s historical type I carcinomas {6822361}. Most large epidemiological studies do not account for histotypes, but it is reasonable to assume that these risks are most important for the development of endometrioid carcinomas.

Endometrioid carcinomas are the most common histotype, representing approximately 80% of all endometrial carcinomas. Serous carcinomas constitute approximately 10%, and the remaining tumours, including carcinosarcomas, are rare. Histology can largely distinguish types of endometrial carcinomas, but this is imperfect {23629444} and may not always be clinically important. Part of this is because of the subjective guidelines for histotyping, but most of it is because something else is at play: the complex pathogenetic landscape of endometrial carcinoma {26166718; 28079598}.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), integrating genomic characterization {23636398} of endometrioid and serous carcinomas, identified four groups of carcinomas: POLE mutation (a hotspot mutation in the exonuclease domain, specifically) {31829442} with an ultramutated phenotype, associated with a good prognosis; microsatellite instability (MSI)–high with a hypermutated phenotype, associated with an intermediate prognosis; and copy-number–high (CN-H; most with TP53 mutations), associated with a poor prognosis. All remaining endometrial carcinomas, referred to as copy-number–low (CN-L), are characterized by low numbers of somatic copy-number alterations and are associated with an intermediate prognosis. Genomic categorization can be applied to all histotypes of endometrial carcinoma. The clinical outcomes linked to each genomic cluster remain relevant irrespective of tumour histotype, i.e. not just in endometrioid carcinoma. POLE mutations, for example, predict an outstanding prognosis regardless of histotype {38780000}. The section section Endometrioid carcinoma provides information about morphological features that roughly correlate with molecular categories, but this is not emphasized in other sections because of insufficient evidence.

It is now recognized that these four molecular subtypes can be refined further. For example, MSI carcinomas with MLH1 promoter methylation are more clinically aggressive than those caused by DNA mismatch repair gene mutations {28709704}; ER-negative no specific molecular profile (NSMP) carcinomas are substantially more aggressive than their ER-positive counterparts {36690721}; and SMARCA4 mutations {33125840}, among others, can be deadly.

Less than 10% of endometrial carcinomas/carcinosarcomas have pathogenic POLE mutations, 25–30% are MSI, 15–20% are CN-H, and the remainder are NSMP. POLE-mutant and MSI tumours are nearly all endometrioid and are fairly well distributed among the FIGO grades. All histotypes are distributed among the molecular subtypes except mesonephric-like carcinomas, which are all CN-L, and (possibly) serous carcinomas, which are essentially all CN-H. CN-H tumours are histologically high-grade, with only a rare exception, discussed in the endometrioid section (section Endometrioid carcinoma). Serous or histologically ambiguous carcinomas and carcinosarcomas account for most CN-H tumours. Occasional endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas belong to this group, as do other rare types. NSMP tumours can be of any histotype again, except serous carcinoma (essentially all CN-H). Endometrioid carcinomas constitute the largest group of NSMP tumours, and 75–90% of them are low-grade (FIGO grade 1 or 2) {23636398; 36241860}.

A limited panel of immunohistochemistry (p53 and the DNA MMR proteins MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2) and POLE mutation analysis, as employed by ProMisE (Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for Endometrial Cancer) {26172027}, can be used as a TCGA surrogate. Since the methodology differs from that of TCGA, the nomenclature differs as well: “POLEmut”, “dMMR” (mismatch repair deficiency; substituting for “MSI”), “p53abn” (substituting for “CN-H”), and “NSMP” (substituting for “CN-L”). These terms, specifically, will be used in this chapter.

Although immunohistochemistry and POLE mutation analysis are currently the most pragmatic ways to classify endometrial carcinomas, there are some issues to be aware of. Almost all MSI carcinomas are dMMR, but not all dMMR carcinomas are MSI. p53abn tumours represent only a subset of CN-H carcinomas, but it is a very large subset – 85–90% of CN-H carcinomas {23636398}. Tumours that do not fall into the POLEmut, dMMR, or p53abn categories are referred to as NSMP.

Approximately 98% of TP53 mutations result in an abnormal p53 immunohistochemical staining pattern (mutation-type pattern), the most frequent of which is the diffuse pattern (intense nuclear staining in > 80% of tumour cell nuclei), followed in frequency by the null pattern (no tumour cell nuclear staining with an intact internal control) and the rare cytoplasmic pattern (a diffuse cytoplasmic blush with some nuclear staining) {29517499; 31829441} [insert figures]. Most abnormalities in DNA MMR protein expression are present diffusely and can be recognized when there is no tumour cell nuclear staining with a positive internal control. The most common pattern, which is due to MLH1 promoter methylation, is loss of MLH1 and PMS2 expression. Less common patterns involve loss of MSH2 and MSH6, MSH6 alone, PMS2 alone, and abnormalities in expression of more than two markers. Occasionally, there is focal (i.e. < 5% of tumour cell nuclei) and weak retained nuclear expression in a background of negative tumour cell nuclei. This can be scored as abnormal if the internal control is intensely stained and as equivocal if it is not. Subclonal staining patterns have also been described (i.e. loss of tumour cell nuclear staining in a geographically defined subset of cells) {27186853; 36866757}, due to focal MLH1 promoter methylation or biallelic somatic mutations that can be seen in the setting of exonuclease domain hotspot POLE mutations. Note that tumours in the POLEmut group may have abnormalities in p53 and/or DNA MMR protein expression, and, similarly, carcinomas in the dMMR group may have p53 abnormalities. These tumours are referred to as multiple-classifier endometrial carcinomas {31829447}. The secondary events, p53 and DNA MMR abnormalities in the POLEmut group, and p53 abnormalities in the dMMR group, are not currently thought to have prognostic significance.

Constructs that account for anatomical stage, grade, histotype, molecular subtype, and (to a lesser extent) ER expression and lymphovascular invasion are increasingly used for clinical risk stratification and treatment {40744042}. They represent a significant advance in clinical care. The 2023 iteration of the FIGO staging of cancer of the endometrium {37337978} was one of several similar attempts {33397713} to codify this approach. The obvious shortcoming is that 2023 FIGO staging, for example, can be used only where the required technology can be accessed. When it is unavailable, POLE sequencing can be safely omitted when the carcinoma is confined to the uterine corpus and is endometrioid and low-grade {36731023}. POLE sequencing is most clinically significant in high-grade tumours that are uterus confined. If immunohistochemistry and molecular testing are not available, it is important to note that the most important data points are anatomical stage, grade, and distinction between endometrioid and non-endometrioid histotypes.
