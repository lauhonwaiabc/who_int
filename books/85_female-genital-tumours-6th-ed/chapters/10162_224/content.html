10.1.6.2 Angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is a malignant vascular neoplasm that variably recapitulates the morphological and immunohistochemical features of endothelial cells.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: haemangiosarcoma; lymphangiosarcoma; malignant haemangioendothelioma; malignant angioendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Angiosarcoma is more common in the uterus and ovary; less commonly, it arises in the vulva and vagina {24257655; 28595254; 19396453; 9591733}.

Clinical features

Nonspecific abdominal/gastrointestinal symptoms, weight loss, pain, and anaemia are presenting symptoms/signs for patients with angiosarcoma at all sites {24257655; 34723849}. Patients with uterine tumours most commonly present with vaginal bleeding. Vulvar tumours may manifest as white papules {26704338}.

Epidemiology

This is an extremely rare neoplasm that accounts for about 1–2% of all soft tissue sarcomas in adults. Its localization in the female genital tract is very uncommon but can occur; in such cases most lesions will involve the ovaries (54%), followed by the uterus (34.4%), vagina (6%), and vulva (5%) {37228354}. Available information is limited to a few case reports published up to this day, and no population level data exist to describe the epidemiology. Based on the case reports, the median patient age at presentation across all sites is 47 years (range: 11–87 years) {24257655; 30179666}. Primary ovarian angiosarcomas usually present in young adults with a median age between 26 and 31 years {9421078; 9591733}. Uterine angiosarcomas present in older adults with a median age of 61.5 years {34723849; 31193499; 32988901}.

Etiology

Radiation therapy and chronic lymphoedema are known predisposing factors for angiosarcoma, including within the female genital tract {28795130; 17519906; 17581409; 16343203; 35540025; 31193499; 32988901}. Several vulvar and vaginal angiosarcomas have been reported to arise in previously irradiated fields {35540025}. In contrast, most ovarian angiosarcomas appear to arise sporadically {38811255}. Rarely, ovarian angiosarcoma has been reported in association with a mature cystic teratoma or, less commonly, with a mucinous tumour/cystadenoma {16731607; 19696615; 21500201}.

Pathogenesis

Angiosarcomas are genetically heterogeneous. Most harbour complex karyotypes, without recurrent chromosomal changes {20217954; 25231439}. High-level MYC gene amplifications (at 8q24) occur in almost all postradiation and chronic lymphoedema–associated secondary angiosarcomas, and rarely in primary angiosarcomas {20949568; 20008140; 24329959; 22121953}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most tumours form a large, friable haemorrhagic mass with poorly defined margins and an ulcerated surface, but flat plaque-like tumours have been reported in the cervix {31815894}.

Histopathology

Most angiosarcomas have a lobulated architecture with closely packed, anastomosing abortive vascular channels, cystic spaces, and solid sheets of spindled to epithelioid cells with haemorrhage and necrosis. Tumour cells are pleomorphic and contain large, hyperchromatic nuclei. Epithelioid angiosarcoma has tumour cells with relatively abundant amphophilic cytoplasm. Mitoses are usually numerous. Prominent stromal hyalinization may be present. Immunohistochemically, the tumour cells stain for CD31, CD34, ERG, and variably for D2-40. Some angiosarcomas, typically epithelioid angiosarcomas, may express cytokeratins and EMA. ER and PR are negative {24257655}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of MYC amplification may be helpful in the setting of radiation-associated or lymphoedema-associated angiosarcoma {22121953; 21909081; 20949568}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: architecturally complex vascular structures lined by atypical endothelial cells; solid patterns and epithelioid morphology may be seen.

Desirable: immunoreactivity for endothelial markers.

Staging

Formal staging of angiosarcoma according to the standard TNM systems (Union for International Cancer Control [UICC] or American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC]) is of limited utility, as the neoplasm’s typically aggressive natural history is often not consistent with soft tissue staging systems.

Prognosis and prediction

Despite the limitations of formal staging systems, prognosis depends on stage (extent of disease) at presentation. Higher-stage tumours are associated with a mean overall survival time of < 30 months {24257655}. Much of the evidence is from uterine and cervical cases. Factors such as epithelioid change, necrosis, and margin status, which have been shown to be prognostically significant for angiosarcomas in general, require specific validation at gynaecological sites {20485141; 17974557}. Older patient age, retroperitoneal site, and large tumour size are associated with poor outcome {9630175; 17974557; 20485141}. The most frequent sites of distant metastasis are the lungs, followed by lymph nodes, soft tissues, bone, liver, and other sites (including the brain) {9630175}.
