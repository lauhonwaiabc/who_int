10.1.3.1 Endometrial polyp

Definition

Endometrial polyp is a benign, localized, polypoid lesion characterized by disorganized endometrial glands embedded in an altered, often fibrotic stroma, frequently containing prominent thick-walled vessels.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Polyps may occur in the fundus or lower uterine segment.

Clinical features

Clinical presentations include abnormal uterine bleeding and infertility, although endometrial polyps may also be asymptomatic. Recurrent endometrial polyp is seen among as many as 13% reported cases {24862037}.

Epidemiology

Endometrial polyps are found in as many as 51% of women in the general population {19632664; 19115236}. Most affected women are perimenopausal or postmenopausal, with risk factors including advanced age, body mass index, endometrial hyperplasia, and history of large endometrial polyps {19632664; 31009859; 15703068; 16963204; 29525689}. They are particularly common in patients undergoing tamoxifen therapy for breast cancer {10202670; 15297160}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The stromal component is clonal {1384681} with recurrent chromosomal rearrangements, most commonly involving HMGA1 (6p21–22) and HMGA2 (12q13–15) {7882366; 7805036; 41137179}. Unlike normal endometrial glands, the epithelial compartment of endometrial polyps is not usually subject to cyclical shedding and harbours endometrial cancer driver mutations at low allelic frequencies {35798968}.

Macroscopic appearance

Endometrial polyps may be sessile or pedunculated, solitary or multiple with size, ranging from < 1 cm to occupying the entire endometrial cavity. The lesion has a narrow or broad-based stalk and a fibrotic cut surface. Tamoxifen-associated polyps tend to be large, frequently multiple, and grossly fibrotic.

Histopathology

Endometrial polyps typically show a polypoid architecture with an altered, often fibrotic stroma, containing scattered thick-walled blood vessels and embedded disorganized endometrial glands. The glands may appear proliferative, atrophic, or secretory. Rarely, degenerated stromal cells with bizarre features may be present. In postmenopausal patients, the stroma is often fibrotic with a diminished glandular component. Metaplastic changes of the glandular epithelium, particularly endocervical-type mucinous metaplasia and ciliated/tubal metaplasia, are common. Torsion of polyp results in haemorrhagic infarction, epithelial metaplasia, and reactive atypia. In patients on hormones, there may be coexisting papillary epithelial proliferations {23211295}. Unlike Müllerian adenosarcoma {2156771}, endometrial polyps lack periglandular cuffing by atypical stromal cells with mitotic activity and typically do not exhibit leaf-like projections into cystically dilated glands. When endometrial polyps display features overlapping with those of low-grade Müllerian adenosarcoma but do not meet the full diagnostic criteria, they are referred to as polyps with unusual stromal features. These lesions are benign if completely excised {25118811; 34675347}. In tamoxifen-associated polyps, glands are often staghorn-shaped and surrounded by a cuff of cytologically bland stromal cells lacking mitotic activity {25118811; 11914630; 34675347}. When stromal smooth muscle is prominent, the polyp is considered adenomyomatous polyp {23926192}.

Concurrent atypical hyperplasia and carcinoma, particularly serous carcinoma, can occasionally occur {20966706}. See section Serous carcinoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: overall polypoid configuration with irregular glands (branched or cystically dilated of variable sizes) in a fibromyomatous and/or endometrial-type stroma including thick-walled blood vessels.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Endometrial polyps are benign.
