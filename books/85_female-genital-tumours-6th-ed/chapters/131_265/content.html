13.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasia: Introduction

The main discussion of neuroendocrine tumours and carcinomas is now found in the sections on section neuroendocrine neoplasms. This change is meant to highlight that the lower genital tract, particularly cervix {29248196} and less commonly vulva {32826525} and vagina {15270619}, is the most common gynaecological site where these tumours occur. This also emphasizes that gynaecological neuroendocrine carcinomas are largely associated with high-risk HPV {29248196; 32826525}. Their etiology and genomic abnormalities {32544169} are more similar to other high-risk HPV-associated genital carcinomas than to small cell carcinomas of lung despite the morphological similarities. The ovarian neuroendocrine tumours of germ cell origin are discussed in the sections on section germ cell tumours, and are unrelated to neuroendocrine tumours of cervix.

The occasional gynaecological small cell carcinomas that are high-risk HPV-independent resemble non-gynaecological small cell carcinomas from a morphological and pathogenetic perspective {32773531}. Endometrial small cell carcinomas are represented in each of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) genomic categories {32773531} (see section Epithelial tumours of the uterine corpus: Introduction), but only the p53abn examples share that feature with pulmonary small cell carcinomas. High-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas can occur as components of mixed carcinomas, such as mixed endometrioid and small cell carcinoma {26945341}. Uncommon cervical small cell carcinomas are high-risk HPV-independent and p53abn {29505425; 38625939}. Merkel cell carcinoma of vulva also rarely occurs {32826525}.

Occasional low and high-grade gynaecological carcinomas show varying degrees of neuroendocrine marker expression. These do not have the characteristic morphology of neuroendocrine carcinoma, notably undifferentiated/dedifferentiated carcinomas {31040050}, and should not be diagnosed as such.
