8.4.2.1 Endometrioid borderline tumour

Definition

Endometrioid borderline tumour is an epithelial tumour composed of crowded endometrioid glands lacking confluent or destructive invasion.

ICD-O coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

ICD-11 coding

Classification in progress, Data not available in this Beta

Related terminology

Not recommended: atypical proliferative endometrioid tumour; endometrioid tumour of low malignant potential.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Ovary

Clinical features

Patients may present with symptoms related to a pelvic mass, or the tumour may be an incidental finding. Coexisting endometrial hyperplasia and/or endometrial endometrioid carcinoma is common (8–39%) {12960810; 3993832; 3414893; 11075848; 29673382}.

Epidemiology

Endometrioid borderline tumours are rare {12436446}. The mean patient age is 46–55 years {11075848; 12960810}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

These tumours are commonly associated with endometriosis and endometrioid adenofibromas. In one study, CTNNB1 mutations were identified in 88% (7/8) cases {16429393}.

Macroscopic appearance

Endometrioid borderline tumours are usually unilateral (~3–10% are bilateral) {11075848; 12960810}, and they typically have a smooth outer surface. The tumours are often large (mean size: 9 cm) {11075848; 12960810} and have a solid cut surface, but they may be focally or even predominantly cystic, with friable haemorrhagic contents.

Histopathology

Endometrioid borderline tumours exhibit two growth patterns: intracystic and adenofibromatous, with the latter being more common {11075848; 12960810; 30098603}. In intracystic tumours, the epithelial proliferation typically protrudes into an endometriotic cyst and has a glandular or simple papillary architecture comprising variably oedematous cores lined by columnar cells with pseudostratified nuclei. In the adenofibromatous pattern, variably sized crowded glands with irregular contours are set in a fibromatous stroma. The glands resemble atypical hyperplasia of the endometrium but display a lobular architecture. The nuclei exhibit mild or moderate cytological atypia, usually with low mitotic activity. Morular or squamous metaplasia is common; mucinous metaplasia may also occur. Confluent invasion (back-to-back glandular architecture with minimal to no intervening stroma) or destructive invasion should be absent; however, confluent morular metaplasia does not warrant a diagnosis of carcinoma. These tumours should be extensively sampled to exclude endometrioid carcinoma since they only very rarely occur without this association.

Endometrioid borderline tumours are positive for PAX8, CK7, ER, and PR, and variably positive for WT1. Morules typically express nuclear β-catenin.

Cytology

Peritoneal washings are usually negative for malignancy or may show endometrial-type epithelium due to associated endometriosis {29316321}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: low-grade endometrioid neoplasm with crowded glands or simple papillae lacking confluent/expansile growth or infiltrative/destructive stromal invasion.

Staging

Endometrioid borderline tumour is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification (see TNM staging of ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]) and the FIGO staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is excellent, and recurrences are rare {22418038}. Endometrial sampling is recommended in women treated conservatively to exclude coexisting endometrial carcinoma or hyperplasia, especially in those who are younger, nulliparous, and have experienced vaginal bleeding {29673382}.
