6.0.0.7 Muir-Torre syndrome

Definition

Muir–Torre syndrome (MTS) is an autosomal dominant tumour predisposition disorder characterized by cutaneous neoplasia (sebaceous tumours and keratoacanthomas) and visceral malignancies. MTS is a subset of Lynch syndrome, due to mutations affecting the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. A similar phenotype can also be seen in patients with inherited MUTYH pathogenic mutations.

Localization

MTS predisposes individuals to skin and visceral malignancies (see below).

Clinical features

Patients with MTS usually develop cutaneous neoplasms and visceral malignancies {7601953}. MTS-associated cutaneous tumours include sebaceous adenoma, sebaceoma, and sebaceous carcinoma, but not sebaceous hyperplasia {3728878; 26892655; 10535567; 12708909; 19951647}. The strongest association is with sebaceous adenoma {16321766}. Multiple sebaceous neoplasms and sebaceous neoplasms outside the head and neck region are strong indicators {18551751; 33201015}. Cystic keratoacanthomas (sometimes multiple and usually showing sebaceous differentiation) in younger patients, at sites without chronic sun exposure, can also be a feature, but these have been less frequently reported.

The visceral malignancies of MTS include tumours of the colorectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, ovary, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, urinary tract (renal pelvis, ureter, and bladder), kidney, brain, and prostate; in particular, colonic adenocarcinomas occur in about 50% of patients with MTS {17409122; 18398828; 7593809; 2029018}. Colonic adenomas are found in about one quarter of patients with MTS.

Epidemiology

MTS has been identified in at least 9% of Lynch syndrome cases {16826164; 18270343}.

Etiology

MTS is caused by a constitutional pathogenic mutation affecting a DNA MMR gene (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2) {24362816}. The risk of tumour formation (for visceral malignancies) varies depending on the affected MMR gene, and these risks are set out in the Prospective Lynch Syndrome Database, available from https://plsd.eu/ {26657901; 27261338; 28754778; 29046738}.

MSH2 mutations are most commonly associated with the MTS phenotype {16908935; 18398828}. MLH1, MSH6, and PMS2 mutations confer a considerably lower risk of cutaneous sebaceous neoplasms {19900449; 28323777; 17409122}.

Pathogenesis

Cells lose MMR function when both alleles of a given MMR gene are inactivated. A normal cell in a patient with MTS carries one inherited mutated MMR gene and acquires a second somatic mutation to the corresponding normal MMR allele, rendering that cell deficient for MMR. This deficiency has several important consequences: it leads to an increase in the point mutation rate of MMR-deficient cells, escape from the normal control of apoptosis (conferring gain of a relative growth advantage), increased mutations affecting repetitive DNA sequences (microsatellites) – resulting in microsatellite instability, and abnormal MMR protein expression {20102395}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The integrity of the expression of MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6) can be assessed by immunohistochemistry. Preserved expression of all four proteins strongly suggests microsatellite stability, whereas loss of nuclear staining for any of the proteins indicates deficient MMR, suggests the candidate gene, and indicates the need for further testing. Loss of MSH2 alone (or MSH2 and MSH6) suggests a mutation in MSH2. Similarly, loss of MLH1 alone (or MLH1 and PMS2) suggests a mutation or acquired promoter methylation in MLH1. Concomitant loss of MSH2 and MSH6 (or MLH1 and PMS2) reflects the heterodimeric binding and stability relationships in MMR complexes {18157157}. Approximately 3–10% of tumours with MMR deficiency show preserved protein expression due to mutations that disable protein function but not protein stability {22086678}. By immunohistochemistry, approximately one half to two thirds of unselected sebaceous neoplasms show deficient DNA MMR protein expression {25006859; 26826402; 18551751}. However, most are sporadic cases, and MMR immunohistochemistry is not routinely used unless there is a history of a previous visceral neoplasm {23212176; 24603434}. Rates of MMR protein deficiency vary according to the anatomical site (> 80% outside the head and neck area vs 30–40% in the head and neck) and tumour type (sebaceous adenomas show loss more commonly than sebaceous carcinomas).

PCR amplification of discrete microsatellite loci in tumour-extracted DNA is a functional assessment of microsatellite instability and strongly correlates with immunohistochemistry {12713593; 11859205}. As many as a third of MTS cases display an apparent microsatellite-stable phenotype on DNA microsatellite instability testing (microsatellite markers developed for colorectal cancer are less sensitive for detecting microsatellite instability in sebaceous neoplasms).

Genetic testing and clinical correlation are critical {24603434}. The positive predictive values for immunohistochemistry alone in the detection of germline DNA MMR defects range from 22% to 37%, although the negative predictive values are as high as 95%, emphasizing a critical need to integrate clinical features (age, family history and personal history of MTS-related cancers, and number of sebaceous tumours) before further testing {25006859; 23212176}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

MTS is characterized by multiple skin sebaceous neoplasms together with one or more visceral cancers, with these neoplasms showing evidence of MMR deficiency (by MMR immunohistochemistry or microsatellite instability testing), but the definitive MTS / Lynch syndrome diagnosis involves identification of a constitutional/germline MMR gene pathogenic mutation.

Staging

Tumours are staged according to the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system, depending on the tumour type.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is consistent with the type and stage of the tumours at the time of their appearance, similar to for sporadic types.
