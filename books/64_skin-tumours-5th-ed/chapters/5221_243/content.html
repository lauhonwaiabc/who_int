5.2.2.1 Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans

Definition

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a superficial fibroblastic mesenchymal tumour with storiform architecture. The majority of cases harbour a COL1A1::PDGFB translocation.

Related terminology

Not recommended: Bednar tumour.

Subtype(s)

Classic dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans; giant cell fibroblastoma; pigmented dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans; dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans with fibrosarcomatous transformation; myxoid dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans; dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans with myoid differentiation; plaque-like dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans

Localization

DFSP is most common on the trunk, followed by the proximal extremities and the head and neck region {10870053; 27806849}. Rare cases involving the distal extremities and acral sites have been reported {29240584}.

Clinical features

Patients with DFSP typically present with a painless, firm, slow-growing plaque or nodule. Discolouration (brown, reddish-blue, or violaceous) may be seen. Longstanding lesions may develop a multinodular or protuberant appearance. Rapid growth in a longstanding lesion may indicate fibrosarcomatous transformation. Rarely, cases can be subcutaneous; in such cases there is a possible association with PDGFD rearrangement or with head and neck locations {35034038; 28420485}. PDGFD-rearranged DFSPs have also been described in tumours in the breast {30014607}.

Epidemiology

DFSP occurs most often in young to middle-aged adults, but it can arise at any age and can even be congenital {32558231; 21429521; 17310000; 12502925}.

Etiology

Most cases are sporadic. Rarely, multicentric atrophic DFSP is seen in association with adenosine deaminase–deficient severe combined immunodeficiency {27176810; 22153773}.

Pathogenesis

The tumour is considered to be fibroblastic in origin. Supernumerary ring chromosomes containing the centromere from chromosome 22 and material from chromosomes 22 and 17 is the characteristic cytogenetic finding {2208065; 8076341; 7757993}. Another alteration, unbalanced t(17;22)(q21.3;q13.1), is more common in paediatric cases. Both aberrations bring the PDGFB gene under the control of the COL1A1 promoter {8988177}. There are numerous breakpoints in the COL1A1 gene (which consists of 52 exons), whereas the PDGFB breakpoint is restricted and always allows expression of PDGFB exon 2 {9739023; 17950782}. After proteolytic processing that removes the COL1A1 protein leader sequence, there is massive overexpression of normal PDGFB ligand, which ignites downstream pathways, probably in an autocrine fashion. Gains in genomic copies of COL1A1::PDGFB and alterations in the mTOR signalling pathway can be seen in fibrosarcomatous transformation {16980946; 28711648}. Rare cases can have cryptic COL1A1::PDGFB or alternative PDGFD rearrangements with COL6A3, EMILIN2, or TNC {34628665; 29955147; 30014607; 35034038; 34256767}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most cases are dermal-based with poorly defined infiltration into the subcutis. A few cases are subcutaneous and have circumscribed borders. The tumours have a grey-white, firm cut surface.

Histopathology

Classic DFSP

The classic form is characterized by a proliferation of uniform, medium-sized spindle cells with a storiform or cartwheel pattern of growth centred in the dermis. It is separated from the epidermis by a grenz zone and infiltrates the subcutaneous adipose tissue in a lace-like or honeycomb pattern. It lacks the overlying epidermal hyperplasia of a dermatofibroma. The periphery is poorly defined and the tumour can show subtle tentacle-like extension along tissue planes and around lobules of subcutaneous adipose tissue. Less commonly, it can be centred in the subcutis, with minimal or no dermal involvement. Mitotic figures are infrequent. There is minimal atypia. Diffuse strong staining for CD34 is characteristic.

Giant cell fibroblastoma

This subtype is most commonly seen in childhood (and is considered a paediatric subtype of DFSP). Giant cell fibroblastoma has a similar localization to the classic form and shows storiform spindle cell morphology, but it also shows additional features including numerous multinucleated giant cells with a wreath-like arrangement of nuclei around pseudovascular spaces and prominent myxoid change {17349565; 12502925}.

Myxoid DFSP

This uncommon subtype is defined as DFSP with > 50% myxoid change. The tumours show multinodular growth with abundant pale myxoid stroma; frequently branching, small, thin-walled vessels; and scattered mast cells {17890911; 17721193}. This subtype is diagnostically challenging, because the storiform pattern can be obscured by myxoid stroma.

Pigmented DFSP

This subtype contains numerous dendritic cells containing melanin pigment. The pigmented cells can show positive staining for S100.

Myoid DFSP

This subtype is characterized by pale nodules of spindle-shaped cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm. It has well-defined margins and is often centred on a blood vessel and associated with stromal hyalinization. The nodules are positive for SMA and negative for CD34 and desmin {26076821}.

Plaque-like DFSP

This subtype is characterized by a poorly circumscribed cellular dermal spindle cell tumour with wavy nuclei, a horizontal to fascicular arrangement of cells, and a myxoid stroma {20061935}.

Other morphological patterns

Granular cell, sclerosing, and atrophic tumours have also been described; hybrids of these and all of the above-mentioned forms can also be encountered.

DFSP with fibrosarcomatous transformation

Fibrosarcomatous transformation, which is seen in approximately 10% of DFSP cases, is characterized by (often abrupt) transition to a herringbone or fascicular growth pattern with increased cellularity, increased atypia, and increased mitotic activity. This subtype may form a discrete nodule in a background of DFSP and often shows diminished or lost CD34 expression. Fibrosarcomatous transformation can occur de novo or in recurrent tumour {9591728}. DFSP shows strong diffuse staining for CD34 in most cases (80–90%). CD34 positivity can be lost in fibrosarcomatous areas. Rarely, these tumours can have pleomorphic to even round cell morphologies {21478726}.

Differential diagnosis

The distinction from dermatofibroma can be difficult on a small or superficial biopsy. Dermatofibroma has less-uniform cytology, with variability of the spindle cell morphology and admixture with other cell types, including macrophages, lymphocytes, and multinucleated giant cells. Dermatofibroma is usually associated with overlying epidermal hyperplasia. Entrapment of collagen is seen peripherally; it is usually CD34-negative. Tumours in the differential diagnosis of DFSP lack rearrangement of PDGFB, but morphology and immunohistochemistry are most often sufficient {17950782; 21484928}.

Cytology

FNAB shows homogeneous spindle cells and tissue fragments with a storiform pattern, fibrillary stroma, naked nuclei, and mild to moderate cytological atypia {15048962}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The majority of cases will harbour a COL1A1::PDGFB rearrangement, sometimes cryptic by FISH, with rare cases harbouring alternative rearrangements with PDGFD and partners such as COL6A3, EMILIN2, or TNC.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a cellular, predominantly dermal-based tumour with monotonous, short spindle cells with a storiform pattern, typically with infiltrative growth and honeycomb subcutis involvement; diffuse CD34 reactivity; the fibrosarcomatous subtype has a fascicular growth pattern with increased mitoses, sometimes with decreased CD34 labelling.

Desirable: rearrangements involving COL1A1::PDGFB (common) or PDGFD (rare).

Staging

The eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system applies to fibrosarcomatous DFSP only.

Prognosis and prediction

DFSP has a substantial risk of local recurrence (which occurs in 20–50% of cases), in particular when incompletely excised {27806849}. Fibrosarcomatous DFSP has an increased risk of local recurrence and additionally has metastatic potential; metastasis occurs in 10–15% of cases, usually after multiple local recurrences, most commonly to lung {16625088; 10870053; 25582537; 27806849}. Because the overexpression of PDGFB leads to activation of the PDGF receptors, tyrosine kinase inhibitors can be used to interfere with the phosphorylation and activation of PDGFB when clinically appropriate {22510939; 15681532; 20194851}.
