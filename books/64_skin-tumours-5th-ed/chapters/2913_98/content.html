2.9.1.3 Melanoma metastatic to the skin

Definition

Metastatic melanoma in the skin is melanoma that has spread from a primary melanoma (cutaneous or extracutaneous) to the skin.

ICD-O coding

8720/6 Melanoma metastatic to the skin

ICD-11 coding

2E08 & XH4846 Metastatic malignant neoplasm involving skin & Malignant melanoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Metastatic melanoma can involve the entire skin. Common sites of involvement are the leg, scalp, arm, and face {20010406}.

Clinical features

Metastatic melanoma manifests as one or several pigmented or amelanotic nodules or plaques, sometimes in a zosteriform or erysipelas-like formation {20010406; 17515511; 29217081; 10078917}. Metastases within a 20 mm radius of a primary melanoma are termed satellite metastases, and those farther away but between the regional lymph node basin are called in-transit metastases {9060544}. Metastatic disease may occur without prior history of melanoma {27213536}.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology is similar to that of the respective primary melanoma.

Etiology

The risk of metastatic disease increases with thickness of the primary {29028110}.

Pathogenesis

Cutaneous melanoma metastasis develops from lymphatic, haematogenous, or perineural spread of the primary lesion, resulting in local, regional, or distant metastases {12230518; 15370120}. In addition, melanoma can spread without entering and exiting the circulation, by engaging in angiotropic extravascular migratory metastasis {15370120; 24572365; 18670348; 27991909; 31720876}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

There is a well-circumscribed nodule in the dermis and/or subcutis, with sheets of atypical melanocytes with mitoses. Epidermal involvement (epidermotropism) is seen in as many as 5% of cases and can simulate a primary melanoma, and it is usually narrower than the underlying nodule {9711667}. Tumour cells are present within adjacent vessels in 5% of cases. Clinicopathological correlation is essential to correctly identify metastatic melanoma. A history of multiple lesions developing in the same locoregional distribution as the primary melanoma is suggestive.

Microsatellitosis is a histologically defined pattern of cutaneous metastasis characterized by microscopic foci of metastatic tumour, often angiotropic, in the skin or subcutis, clearly discontinuous from the primary tumour {31758078}.

Immunohistochemistry

Neoplastic melanocytes express melanocytic markers, including melan-A, SOX10, HMB45, and S100. If the primary melanoma was PRAME-positive, the metastasis usually is too. Dedifferentiated metastatic melanomas may lose melanocytic markers and aberrantly express cytokeratin and/or muscle markers {33428337}.

Differential diagnosis

The histological differential diagnosis of metastatic melanoma in the skin includes primary melanoma, primary dermal melanoma, and atypical naevus. Blue naevus–like metastases are difficult to distinguish from common or epithelioid blue naevus. The presence of cytologically atypical epithelioid melanocytes and mitotic figures can mimic blue naevus–like melanoma {10078917}.

Cytology

FNA is uncommonly used in the skin. The cytological features are similar to those of metastases in other sites.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Driver mutations can be helpful in making conclusions about the primary tumour and its localization, if not known {28467829; 27213536; 32619863}. For example, metastases arising from acral or mucosal melanoma have distinctive copy-number changes, whereas GNAQ/GNA11 mutations are typically drivers in melanoma arising in blue naevus or uveal melanoma {26403583; 19078957}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an intradermal or subcutaneous nodule of malignant neoplastic melanocytes.

Desirable: a history of melanoma; immunohistochemical and/or molecular confirmation in challenging cases; cytological, immunohistochemical, and genetic features similar to those of primary melanoma.

Staging

Staging is performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM criteria {29028110}. The presence of microsatellite, satellite, or in-transit (non-nodal regional) metastases is associated with a worse prognosis and represents an N-category criterion and stage III disease. Cases with distant cutaneous metastases are categorized as M1 and stage IV disease {31758078}.
