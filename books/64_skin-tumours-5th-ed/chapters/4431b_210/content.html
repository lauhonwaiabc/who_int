4.4.3.1B Lymphomatoid granulomatosis

Definition

Lymphomatoid granulomatosis (LyG) is an Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated angiocentric and angiodestructive B-cell lymphoid proliferation involving extranodal sites. LyG is composed of atypical large B cells usually admixed with a large number of T cells. It occurs in patients without evidence of inborn or acquired immunodeficiency other than immunosenescence.

ICD-O coding

9766/1 Secondary lymphomatoid granulomatosis

9766/1 Lymphomatoid granulomatosis, grade 1 and 2

9766/3 Lymphomatoid granulomatosis, grade 3

ICD-11 coding

2A81.3 Lymphomatoid granulomatosis

Related terminology

Acceptable: EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (for grade 3 LyG only).

Not recommended: angiocentric lymphoproliferative lesion.

Subtype(s)

Lymphomatoid granulomatosis, grade 1 and 2; lymphomatoid granulomatosis, grade 3

Localization

LyG always involves the lung. The CNS (40%) and the skin (34%) are the next most frequently involved sites {25321327; 27521314; 21107080}. Skin lesions are frequently disseminated and are less commonly confined to the extremities, trunk, or head and neck {11688570; 7212740}. Lymph node and/or bone marrow involvement is rare {3785978}.

Clinical features

Despite universal lung involvement, only 30–60% of patients present with overt respiratory symptoms {27521314}. About 30% of the patients present with cutaneous lesions, but the majority develop skin lesion during the course of the disease {25321327; 21107080; 761171; 11688570}. Cutaneous lesions can occur at any stage of the disease process, with approximately one third of patients having lesions at initial presentation, but rarely precede pulmonary manifestations {761171; 11688570; 21107080; 25321327}.

Skin lesions consist of multiple erythematous dermal papules and/or subcutaneous nodules with or without ulceration, or less erythematous to whitish plaque-like lesions. Necrosis and ulceration are usually associated with larger nodules. Indurated plaques, lichen sclerosus–like lesions, and alopecia are less commonly seen {11688570; 7212740}.

Epidemiology

The disease is rare, and exact data on its prevalence do not exist. LyG is more common in North America and rare in Asian populations {32107539; 21107080}. The median age is between the fourth and sixth decades of life. The M:F ratio is 2:1 {25321327}.

Etiology

LyG is an EBV-associated B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder with a prominent T-cell infiltrate.

Diminished immunity is thought to be an important factor in the development of LyG.

Pathogenesis

Current hypotheses include defective immunosurveillance of EBV-infected B cells and an abnormal CD8-positive cytotoxic T-cell immune response to EBV, as previously healthy subjects with LyG have shown various defects in cell-mediated and/or humoral immunity {6978760; 8639820; 9354680}. Therefore, despite the fact that evidence of overt inborn or acquired immunodeficiency excludes the diagnosis, immune dysregulation is highly suggestive in the majority of cases. No specific genetic abnormalities have been defined.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

LyG is characterized by an angiocentric and angiodestructive, polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate with involvement of small- to large-calibre vessels. Small lymphocytes predominate and are admixed with variable numbers of histiocytes, plasma cells, and large atypical B cells. The large atypical B cells may resemble immunoblasts or have a more pleomorphic appearance resembling HRS cells. The background T cells may show some atypia or nuclear irregularities but do not appear overtly neoplastic. Neutrophils and eosinophils are usually inconspicuous. Central necrosis is often (although variably) present. Well-formed granulomas are typically absent. In the skin, lymphohistiocytic infiltration can be seen in the subcutaneous fat and is generally associated with fat necrosis.

Immunohistochemistry

The atypical large B cells express pan–B-cell markers, are variably positive for CD30, and are negative for CD15. LyG is an EBV-associated proliferation. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER)-positive cells usually show a spectrum of cell sizes, with large atypical cells but also numerous small B cells being positive. The majority of lymphoid cells in the background are CD3-positive T cells, with CD4-positive cells outnumbering CD8-positive cells {25321327}.

Grading is usually performed at the primary site of presentation (nearly always the lung), based on the proportion of EBV-positive B cells [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes other forms of EBV-positive B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders and can be challenging, particularly when extranodal sites are involved (see the relevant section in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume for more details [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {32107539}).

Although previously the incidence of LyG was reported to be higher in patients with immunodeficiencies (e.g. Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome, HIV infection, or high-dose chemotherapy) {9578085; 12835306; 28293550}, according to the current definition, a history of inborn or acquired immune deficiency and dysregulation (IDD) other than immunosenescence excludes the diagnosis of LyG. In such cases, the diagnosis should follow the guidelines outlined in the sections on IDD and can be designated as “polymorphic lymphoproliferative disorder, LyG-type, EBV-positive, specified IDD setting (e.g. HIV, iatrogenic/MTX)” and, in the case of grade 3–type lesions, as “diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, LyG-type, EBV-positive, specified IDD setting”.

Cytology

The use of FNAB alone is not sufficient for definite diagnosis, and histological biopsy is recommended.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Monoclonal IG gene rearrangements may be identified in most grade 2 and grade 3 lesions. B-cell clonality may not be detected in grade 1 lesions, most likely due to the paucity of EBV-infected cells. Clonal TR gene rearrangements are usually not present, although restricted patterns may be observed {25321327}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: extranodal disease; a polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate with striking angiocentricity; transmural involvement of small to medium-sized vessels by variable numbers of EBV-positive large B cells admixed with abundant small T lymphocytes; exclusion of immune deficiency other than immunosenescence.

Staging

The disease is usually extranodal, so conventional staging (i.e. the Lugano classification, a modification of the Ann Arbor system {25113753}) is not informative.

Prognosis and prediction

The clinical behaviour of LyG varies widely from an indolent process to an aggressive B-cell lymphoma. Accurate histological grading is essential in predicting the disease course and guiding therapy. Accordingly, grade 1 and grade 2 lesions are commonly treated by immune modulation (e.g. interferon) and grade 3 lesions are treated like aggressive lymphoma, with chemotherapy. Overall, in approximately 60% of patients, the disease is fatal, with a median overall survival of 14 months. However, 30% undergo spontaneous remission without treatment. The cause of death is related to the destruction of involved organs, most frequently the lungs {32107539}.
