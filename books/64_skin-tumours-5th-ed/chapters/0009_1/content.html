0.0.0.9 Chapter 1: Introduction to skin tumours

This edition of the Skin tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series builds on the success of the fourth edition while meeting the needs of the series’ fifth edition in terms of its hierarchical taxonomy and improved format. The organization of this volume is similar to that of others in the series, generally following the anatomical structure of the skin and collecting haematolymphoid and soft tissue tumours into separate chapters, with a final chapter on genetic tumour syndromes associated with skin malignancies.

There are few major changes in this edition, but the following are noteworthy:

In the epidermal tumours category, keratoacanthoma remains the subject of debate regarding its origin and nature, as highlighted by a number of recent studies. In this edition, we have elected to classify it separately from squamous cell carcinoma given keratoacanthoma’s benign behaviour, while recognizing that it may represent a well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma with a propensity for spontaneous regression. In contrast, Merkel cell carcinoma is recognized as a neuroendocrine carcinoma of the skin. The Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV) is present in approximately 80% of cases in regions of the world with low sun exposure, whereas cases without evidence of viral etiology form the majority of tumours in countries with higher incidences of Merkel cell carcinoma and have the typical TP53 and RB1 mutations seen in other neuroendocrine carcinomas, with an ultraviolet (UV) radiation–associated etiology.

The classification of melanocyte-derived tumours that was completely revised in the fourth edition has been retained, with some finetuning of terms where further molecular characterization has improved understanding. Naevi are now recognized as lesions with only a single mutation and no other pathogenic alterations, with benign behaviour. The progression of these lesions towards malignancy is evidenced in neoplasms intermediate between naevi and melanomas, now termed “melanocytomas”, as was first introduced in the fourth edition, to define neoplasms that have more than one mutation on the way towards malignancy and often an atypical histological appearance or clinical behaviour. These mutations tend to be in specific pathways, which further define the tumour type of the resulting lesion (e.g. WNT-activated, BAP1-inactivated) {21366452; 24460190; 32057276}. The classification of all melanomas is presented in this volume, including both CNS and ocular melanomas as well as the different types affecting the skin.

Adnexal tumours of the skin come in many forms, but a remarkable proportion of cases occur in association with genetic tumour syndromes, some of which are covered in more depth in the last chapter of this volume. Novel molecular alterations increasingly recognized in sporadic adnexal tumours have also been highlighted, and new provisional entities such as primary adnexal NUT carcinoma have been added to the classification. And a previous omission from the classification has been corrected: nail bed tumours have now been included and assigned their own category.

There have been some major updates to the classification of haematolymphoid tumours affecting the skin in accordance with the fifth-edition Haematolymphoid tumours volume, reflecting increased understanding of the molecular pathology of these tumours and their response to targeted therapy, particularly for the histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms, where ALK and BRAF mutations are particularly important drivers. The members of the cutaneous peripheral T-cell lymphoma family are now listed as separate entities, with “NOS” used for those very rare cases that do not fit with a specific type. A new section within the category of cutaneous lymphoproliferative disorders and lymphomas associated with immunodeficiency and dysregulation has been dedicated to cutaneous CD8-positive T-cell–rich and granulomatous infiltrates associated with inborn or acquired immunodeficiencies and not associated with EBV infection. Lastly, because of the importance of distinguishing reactive lymphoid hyperplasias from neoplasms, we have again included sections specifically dedicated to these proliferations, which were last covered in the third edition. The harmonization of these entities across multiple volumes has been possible throughout most the fifth edition, with authors shared between them.

There are two sections on metastases to skin: one on skin metastases of known primary site and the other a general section on carcinoma of unknown primary site.
