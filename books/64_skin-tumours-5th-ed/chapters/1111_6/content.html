1.1.1.1 Actinic keratosis

Definition

Actinic keratoses (AKs) are very common neoplastic intraepidermal squamous proliferative lesions associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure and characterized by squamous dysplasia. AKs may remain stable, regress spontaneously, or progress to invasive squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

Related terminology

Acceptable: solar keratosis.

Not recommended: keratinocytic intraepidermal neoplasia; bowenoid solar keratosis; bowenoid actinic keratosis; senile keratosis.

Subtype(s)

Proliferative actinic keratosis; hypertrophic actinic keratosis; lichenoid actinic keratosis; pigmented actinic keratosis

Localization

AKs occur on sun-exposed skin of the face, lip (actinic cheilitis), ears, scalp, neck, forearms, and dorsal aspect of the hands {12533168; 17822489; 18410618}.

Clinical features

A presentation as scaly erythematous patches and plaques measuring 2–10 mm on a background of sun-damaged skin is typical. The lesions are frequently multiple and may coalesce {12533168; 17822489; 12079221}.

Epidemiology

AKs are very common. There is an increasing incidence with age, and a predilection for fair-skinned individuals residing in areas of high UV radiation exposure {12533168; 22881586; 24314380; 16908356}.

Etiology

Cumulative exposure to UVB light and chronic immunosuppression are the main risk factors for the development of AKs {17822489}. Male sex, lighter hair and eye colours, and baldness are also risk factors {37169068}.

Pathogenesis

Similar to SCC, AKs show a UV radiation signature, high mutation burden, and somatic copy-number alterations. Frequently mutated genes include TP53, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, and FAT1 {33482222; 12533168; 7923098; 18410618; 7611795; 24627245; 11804749; 8700506}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

AKs are characterized by squamous dysplasia affecting the basal to mid-spinous layer keratinocytes. The dysplastic keratinocytes show enlarged and irregular hyperchromatic or vesicular nuclei. Mitotic figures, including atypical forms, are common. The granular cell layer is typically absent and there is overlying parakeratosis. The acrosyringeal units and follicular ostia are spared, but dysplastic keratinocytes may extend along pre-existing adnexal structures. By definition, squamous dysplasia does not affect the full-thickness epidermis. The superficial dermis shows evidence of solar elastosis, and a chronic inflammatory infiltrate is often present. The lichenoid subtype is characterized by a dense, band-like chronic inflammatory infiltrate with vacuolar change affecting basal cell layer keratinocytes. While the epidermis is typically thinned and flattened, epidermal hyperplasia with marked parakeratosis and lichen simplex chronicus–like changes are seen in the hypertrophic subtype. Budding of dysplastic epidermis is noted in the proliferative subtype, while pronounced epidermal atrophy is seen in the atrophic pattern. Occasionally, acantholysis of the dysplastic keratinocytes (acantholytic AK) is seen, and there may be increased melanin pigmentation of the dysplastic basal cell layer keratinocytes (pigmented AK). In the pigmented subtype, the overlying parakeratosis may be absent. AKs may develop in a background of solar lentigines or seborrhoeic keratoses.

Bowenoid AK has been described as AK with full-thickness squamous atypia and an increased risk for progression to SCC {32455470}; as such, it is best considered to represent SCC in situ.

Prognosis and prediction

AKs may remain stable, regress, or progress to invasive SCC. Although 65% of all primary SCCs were found to arise in a background of AK, the risk of progression was estimated at only 0.6% after 1 year and 2.57% at 4 years; 55% of clinically detected AKs had regressed after 1 year of follow-up, and 70% after 5 years {19382202}.
