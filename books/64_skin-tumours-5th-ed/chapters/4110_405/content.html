4.1.1.0 Mastocytosis

Definition

Mastocytosis is a myeloid neoplasm characterized by a clonal proliferation of mast cells. Cutaneous mastocytosis (CM) is a form of mastocytosis primarily affecting the skin. Systemic mastocytosis (SM) involves at least one extracutaneous organ system, with or without evidence of skin lesions.

Related terminology

Not recommended: urticaria pigmentosa; telangiectasia macularis eruptive perstans.

Subtype(s)

Cutaneous mastocytosis: maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (MPCM); diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis (DCM); mastocytoma.

Systemic mastocytosis (clinical subtypes): bone marrow mastocytosis; indolent systemic mastocytosis; smouldering systemic mastocytosis; aggressive systemic mastocytosis; systemic mastocytosis with an associated haematological neoplasm (SM-AHN); mast cell leukaemia.

Localization

MPCM favours the trunk and proximal extremities, but other areas may be involved. The palms, soles, and central face are usually spared. DCM involves the whole skin surface in the absence of individual skin lesions. Mastocytomas may appear anywhere, more frequently on the trunk and scalp {26476479; 26152315}.

The bone marrow is nearly always involved in SM. It is therefore necessary to undertake morphological and molecular analyses of a bone marrow / aspirate specimen in adults to confirm the diagnosis {24836395}. Rarely, circulating mast cells are detected in the peripheral blood {27118407; 34496018}. Other organs (spleen, lymph nodes, liver, gastrointestinal tract) may be involved in SM {1452127; 1617595; 11377686}. Skin lesions are more frequent in indolent SM and smouldering SM than in advanced SM (which includes aggressive SM, SM-AHN, and mast cell leukaemia) {11377686; 33581142}. In bone marrow mastocytosis, skin lesions are absent.

Clinical features

Adults with cutaneous lesions of mastocytosis have SM in > 90% of cases {23807778}. The clinical presentation of cutaneous lesions in patients with SM is indistinguishable from cases of CM.

The classification of mastocytosis in the skin is based on the number of lesions and morphological features (see #38181Box 6.01). Swelling, reddening, pruritus, or blistering can occur when mast cells are activated, usually by stroking (Darier sign) or temperature changes.

Systemic symptoms originating from mast cell–mediator release can occur in some cases and include flushing, headache, diarrhoea, abdominal pain or cramping, and even anaphylaxis in severe cases. These are more frequently seen in children with extensive skin involvement and increased tryptase levels {22458675}. Unlike in adults, elevated tryptase is not a reliable marker for SM in children, as most children with elevated tryptase levels have disease limited to the skin {26044856}.

Epidemiology

The true incidence and prevalence of CM and SM are unknown. Patients with childhood-onset mastocytosis usually have CM, whereas most patients with adult-onset disease have SM {25662299; 17537151}. In most paediatric patients, onset occurs during the first 6 months of life {26152315}, and congenital cases have been reported. Adult-onset disease typically develops in patients younger than 50 years of age.

SM is most common in adults (generally diagnosed after the second decade of life); the M:F ratio is 1:1 to 1:1.5 {16799601; 2582403}. Well-differentiated SM (see below) predominantly affects women, with onset in childhood and frequent familial aggregation {26100086}.

Etiology

The etiology of sporadic cases is unknown. There have been rare reports of hereditary familial SM, frequently among patients with well-differentiated SM {33754335; 26100086}.

Pathogenesis

Mastocytosis is caused by somatic activating mutations involving KIT {9990072}. The KIT p.D816V mutation in exon 17 is present in > 90% of adult cases and in 35–40% of paediatric cases {16741248; 12598308; 25662299}. KIT mutations in exons 8–11 are observed in as many as 40% of the paediatric cases, and 15–20% of the paediatric cases are KIT wildtype {19865100}. Germline KIT mutations have been reported in familial forms of mastocytosis {16183119; 28978170}.

KIT mutations cause constitutive, ligand-independent hyperactivation of the receptor tyrosine kinase KIT {26158763}. Downstream mediators of oncogenic KIT signalling include PI3K, NF-κB, mTORC2, and PKCδ; they induce proliferation and promote growth and survival of neoplastic mast cells, leading to their pathological accumulation in organs and tissues.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Diagnosis and subtyping of SM is usually based on the histopathological investigation of a bone marrow trephine biopsy specimen. Skin histology does not allow the distinction between patients with or without systemic involvement.

Skin biopsies show a highly variable increase in dermal mast cells with Giemsa, toluidine blue, mast cell tryptase, or KIT (CD117) staining {10224380; 3170991; 26476479; 35412687}. In MPCM, mast cells are often loosely scattered, with a tendency to aggregate around blood vessels and adnexa or form small or even band-like compact infiltrates in the upper dermis {11377676; 26476479}. Mast cells are usually markedly increased in DCM and form nodular infiltrates in mastocytomas {11377676; 26476479}. Mast cells appear as unremarkable histiocytoid cells on H&E {11377676; 30252695}. In Giemsa stains, mast cells can range from spindle-shaped to round with hypergranulated cytoplasm (well-differentiated morphology) {26476479; 11377676; 26100086}. Additional features that can be seen in some cases include vascular ectasia, eosinophils, oedema of the papillary dermis, and subepidermal vesiculation.

Immunohistochemistry

Mast cells always coexpress tryptase and KIT (CD117). Expression of CD2, CD25, and/or CD30 supports their neoplastic nature, but these immunophenotypic aberrations are not always demonstrable in skin biopsies, particularly in adults {18162781; 23807778; 21186345; 25402852; 29794740; 35412687; 35596520}. Well-differentiated SM cases typically lack CD25 and CD2 in bone marrow mast cells, but they express CD30 in most cases {26100086; 27741105; 22370312}.

Differential diagnosis

Basal hyperpigmentation in White patients, an interstitial distribution, and mast cell clusters of three or more favour MPCM over reactive conditions with increased mast cell counts {35412687}. Increased numbers of mast cells showing striking nuclear pleomorphism with bilobed and multilobed nuclei (promastocytes) may be seen in some cases, particularly in those with well-differentiated features, but such a finding does not seem to represent a malignant phenotype in children with CM {30252695; 26100086}.

Cytology

Cytology is not relevant for skin lesions of mastocytosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of KIT mutations in skin biopsies supports the diagnosis of mastocytosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: skin lesions typical of mastocytosis; skin biopsy demonstrating increased numbers of mast cells, preferably confirmed by immunoreactivity for KIT (CD117) and tryptase; for CM: absence of clinical signs of SM, and bone marrow evaluation (when indicated) does not meet the criteria for SM; for SM: see #38180Box 6.02 .

Desirable: aberrant immunophenotype (CD2+, CD25+, CD30+) of dermal mast cells; detection of KIT mutations in lesional skin.

Staging

In children with mastocytosis skin involvement and normal serum tryptase levels, the diagnosis of CM is assumed without need for further investigations. A complete bone marrow study is recommended in all adults regardless of tryptase levels {34901755}.

Prognosis and prediction

Most cases of CM in children tend to resolve around puberty, although reliable factors to predict the prognosis of childhood-onset mastocytosis are lacking. An increased risk of systemic disease in paediatric patients is seen in those who present with the disease after 2 years of age, those with mast cell activation symptoms or persistently increased tryptase levels beyond adolescence, or those with abnormal blood counts. DCM may be potentially fatal, but most cases involute in childhood, leaving a wrinkled and thickened area of skin. In adult-onset CM the skin lesions are usually lifelong, with a variable degree of mast cell activation symptoms, but the disease does not have an impact on life expectancy {26476479}. In patients with aberrant/clonal bone marrow mast cells who do not meet enough criteria for SM, close follow-up is recommended to detect eventual progression to SM.

Adult onset of mastocytosis in the skin is closely associated with systemic involvement {23807778}. Most patients with indolent SM have a similar life expectancy to that of the general population; however, a small subset of cases can progress to advanced SM (aggressive SM, SM-AHN, or mast cell leukaemia), usually after a prolonged phase of indolent SM {19541349}. The risk of progression strongly relies on the extent of involvement of haematopoiesis by the KIT p.D816V mutation {31151985}. Moreover, KIT p.D816V mutation burden also predicts survival in patients with SM {24750133; 31018976}. The clinical course of smouldering SM is often stable for many years, but progression to advanced SM can occur. Based on survival rates, advanced forms of mastocytosis, especially mast cell leukaemia, have a poor prognosis {19890907; 31676322}.

Major criterion:

·    Multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥ 15 mast cells in aggregates) detected in sections of bone marrow and/or other extracutaneous organ(s)

Minor criteria:

·    > 25% of all mast cells are atypical cells (type I or type II) on bone marrow smears or are spindle-shaped in dense and diffuse mast cell infiltrates in sections of bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ(s)a

·    Activating KIT point mutation(s) at codon 816 or in other critical regions of KITb in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ(s)

·    Mast cells in bone marrow, blood, or other extracutaneous organ(s) aberrantly express one or more of the following antigens: CD2, CD25, CD30c

·    Baseline serum tryptase concentration > 20 ng/mL in the absence of a myeloid AHN;d in the case of a known HαT, the tryptase level could be adjustede

The diagnosis is SM if the major criterion and at least one minor criterion are fulfilled, or if at least three minor criteria are fulfilled.

AHN, associated haematological neoplasm; HαT, hereditary alpha-tryptasaemia.

aIn tissue sections, an abnormal mast cell morphology counts in both a dense infiltrate and a diffuse mast cell infiltrate. In the bone marrow smear, an atypical morphology of mast cells does not count as an SM criterion when the mast cells are located in or adjacent to bone marrow particles. Morphological criteria of atypical mast cells have been described {11377686}. bAny type of KIT mutation counts as a minor SM criterion when published solid evidence for its transforming behaviour is available (an overview of potentially activating KIT mutations is provided in the supplementary material of {34901755}). cExpression must be confirmed by either flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. dMyeloid neoplasms can lead to increased serum tryptase levels. Therefore, this criterion does not count in cases of SM with an AHN. eA possible mode for adjustment has been proposed in {34901755}. The basal tryptase level may be divided by 1 plus the number of extra copies of the α-tryptase gene (TPSAB1). For example, if the tryptase level is 30 ng/mL and two extra copies of the α-tryptase gene are found in a patient with HαT, the HαT-corrected tryptase level is 10 ng/mL (30/3 = 10), thereby not meeting the level of a minor SM criterion.
