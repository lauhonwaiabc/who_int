7.1.1.2 Cancer of unknown primary site

Definition

Cancer of unknown primary (CUP) is a primary metastatic, malignant epithelial or undifferentiated neoplasm for which a standardized diagnostic workup including clinical, histopathological, radiological and (sometimes) endoscopic examination fails to identify a site of origin.

ICD-O coding

Code according to histology of metastasis.

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Acceptable: CUP syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Adenocarcinoma; squamous cell carcinoma; undifferentiated carcinoma; undifferentiated neoplasm

Localization

Metastases are found most frequently in the liver, lung, lymph nodes, brain, and bones, but they can involve any organ system and localization {22414598}.

Clinical features

CUP can be asymptomatic in the early stages. Because of the potential involvement of virtually any organ system, at advanced stages symptoms can be diverse and include asthenia, weight loss, pain, anaemia, swollen lymph nodes, dyspnoea, cough, indigestion, loss of appetite, jaundice, seizures, neurological abnormalities, and abdominal distension {26314775; 22048624; 22414598}. For single-site/oligometastatic CUP, PET-CT is used for staging, and ablative surgery plus irradiation is used for treatment {34521064}. For disseminated, unfavourable CUP, staging is performed using CT or MRI {33687747}.

Epidemiology

The age-adjusted incidence of CUP was 13.3 and 15.9 cases per 100 000 person-years in women and men, respectively, in England in 2015. The global incidence has decreased over the past 10–20 years as diagnostic methods have improved, and it currently accounts for 1–2% of all cancers {31254795}. Accordingly, in Switzerland, age-standardized incidence rates of CUP increased from 10.3 to 17.6 cases per 100 000 person-years between 1981 and 1997 and decreased to 5.8 cases per 100 000 person-years in 2014 {30019510}. The incidence increases with age. Approximately 50% of CUP cases can be categorized as well-differentiated to moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas, about 30% as poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas or undifferentiated carcinomas, about 15% as squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs), and about 5% as undifferentiated neoplasms {22387673; 30019510; 12957453}.

Etiology

The etiology of CUP remains unknown. The risk of developing CUP is associated with smoking, obesity, type 2 diabetes {26011105; 24692151; 35026689}, and autoimmune disorders {26103152}. Familial predisposition is another established risk factor {21189391}. Alcohol consumption, body mass index, waist circumference, socioeconomic status, and ethnic background may be additional risk factors {24726751; 23860528; 24692151}.

Pathogenesis

CUP is characterized by a wide range of mutations, which are likely to be dependent upon tissue of origin. Common mutations involve TP53, KRAS, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and STK11 {27322425; 33219618; 26182302; 31970771}. The most important mutations from a clinical perspective are those in driver oncogenes that are often studied with next-generation sequencing (NGS) to detect mutations that may be associated with oncoproteins targetable by inhibitors as part of treatment {33687747; 33219618; 27900363}.

It currently remains unclear whether CUP represents a heterogeneous group of metastatic cancers where the primary cannot be detected or a unique cancer type with distinct genetic and biological features {32511869; 33941777; 17470684}. Theories explaining the absence of a primary tumour in CUP include (1) extremely small primaries evading detection, (2) prior removal of presumed non-invasive primaries (e.g. adenomas, cervical dysplasias) that in fact had already metastasized before removal, (3) immune-mediated or otherwise spontaneous tumour regression, and (4) on-site transformation of epithelial/undifferentiated cells dispersed during embryonic development (stem cell theory).

Macroscopic appearance

In CUP, cancer cells often appear as multiple nodules, which do not display morphology or properties referable to the tissue or organ in which they arose and therefore cannot be related to any suspected distant primary site. Usually, multiple biopsy specimens are submitted for the diagnostic workup. It is recommended to distribute the biopsies into multiple formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded blocks in order to have enough material available for histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular workup.

Histopathology

After the confirmation of a carcinoma, four main histological subtypes of CUP are recognized: adenocarcinoma, SCC, undifferentiated carcinoma, and neuroendocrine carcinoma. Several immunohistochemistry panels for exclusion of metastases of known origin and non-epithelial neoplasms have been published {26314775; 33687747; 19179185; 7636553}. See #33551Table 8.01 .

For lineage classification, lymphoma should be excluded by immunostaining for CD45 (be aware of downregulation of CD45 expression in immature B-cell neoplasms); melanoma by SOX10, S100, melan-A, and HMB45; and mesothelioma by calretinin, CK5/6, WT1, and D2-40. In case of a triple-negative screen (no carcinoma, no melanoma, no lymphoma), a mesenchymal origin has to be considered. Sex cord stromal and adrenocortical tumours can be screened for by staining with SF1, inhibin, melan-A, and calretinin.

If clinically, radiologically, endoscopically, or histomorphologically suspected, in addition to basic CK7/CK20 immunostaining, specific immunohistochemistry panels should be considered for exclusion of a respective primary: hepatocellular carcinoma (arginase-1 [ARG1], HepPar1), urothelial carcinoma (GATA3, p63), upper gastrointestinal / pancreaticobiliary carcinoma (CDX2, CK19, SMAD4), Merkel cell carcinoma (synaptophysin, INSM1) {22271206; 33128463}, endometrial and cervical carcinoma (PAX8, ER, PR, p40), mammary carcinoma (GATA3, TRPS1 {38647255}, mammaglobin, GCDFP-15 [BRST2], ER, PR, ERBB2), triple-negative breast cancer (SOX10), salivary gland carcinoma (EMA, p63, S100, SMA, GATA3, AR, ERBB2) {24824612}, and non-small cell lung and thyroid carcinoma (thyroid transcription factor 1 [TTF1], napsin A, thyroglobulin, and sometimes loss of SMARCA4 [BRG1]) {33687747}. Cases in men with osteoblastic metastases or elevated serum PSA have to be screened with PSMA, PSA, and/or NKX3-1. With SCC morphology, CK5/6, high-molecular-weight cytokeratin (34βE12), and p40 should be considered, along with p16 (block-type positivity) as a surrogate marker for HPV infection. The following immunohistochemistry panels should be used to diagnose favourable CUP subtypes: metastases for a renal cell carcinoma profile (PAX8, PAX2, KIT, RCCm, CD10, AMACR [racemase], TFE3, TFEB, CAIX), women with peritoneal carcinomatosis for serous adenocarcinoma (PAX8, WT1, p53, ER), and neuroendocrine tumours (NET G1–G3) (INSM1, synaptophysin, chromogranin). Poorly differentiated colorectal carcinoma often has preserved staining of SATB2. Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma can be challenging; a loss of expression of BAP1 and SMAD4 and a lack of expression of ARG1 can support the diagnosis together with the radiology. For an undifferentiated carcinoma with a midline distribution, a germ cell tumour needs to be considered (SALL4, OCT3/4, CD30, KIT, AFP, β-hCG) {33687747}, or a highly aggressive NUT midline carcinoma (positive for NUTM1, pancytokeratin markers, p40/p63, and synaptophysin in occasional cases).

As high-level PDL1 expression has been shown to predict improved response to and prognosis with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment across different cancer entities including CUP {27718847; 31056702; 34843940}, immunostaining for PDL1 should be included, and both tumour proportion score (TPS) and combined positive score (CPS) should be reported.

Cytology

Initial evaluation of patients presenting with a suspected malignancy without a clear clinical or radiological impression of a primary site frequently includes FNA, to help establish a diagnosis and/or to procure tissue for additional studies (e.g. flow cytometry, molecular testing). Key morphological patterns, such as epithelioid, small blue round cell, and spindle cell, should guide the selection of a few markers, ideally performed on cell block material, to confirm the diagnosis. In difficult cases, such as CUP, in which multiple markers are necessary, it is recommended to convey the situation to the treating physician and obtain biopsy material for further histopathological analysis and molecular testing {30190140; 30156769}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The mutation profile of unfavourable CUP has been assessed in numerous panel NGS studies to identify targets for molecular therapies {31970771; 24037760; 26182302; 33219618; 25415047}. Given potential treatment options with targeted therapies and ICIs, panel or (ideally) comprehensive NGS should be performed using a pan-cancer panel covering molecular targets across different entities {33048619; 33434511}. In some cases, the mutation profile also provides hints towards the putative primary, such as when ALK, ROS1 (non-small cell lung carcinoma), TMPRSS2 (prostate cancer), or NUTM1 (NUT carcinoma) rearrangements are detected. It is best practice for NGS findings in CUP to be considered a molecular tumour board. Tumour-agnostic therapy should also be considered for cases with NTRK fusions and with BRAF, FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR3 mutations {38834388}.

As the responsiveness of tumours with high microsatellite instability (MSI) / mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency {26028255; 31056702; 31682550} and cancers with a high tumour mutation burden (TMB) (≥ 10 mutations/Mb) {29658845; 30395155; 31056702} to ICIs has been shown across different tumour entities, some PD1 and PDL1 inhibitors have been granted entity-agnostic (including CUP) approval for the treatment of high-MSI/MMR-deficient and high-TMB cancers {30787022}. Response to ICI treatment has been reported in cases of unfavourable CUP that relapsed or were refractory to chemotherapy {34843940}. As a result, MSI and TMB testing are often performed to guide therapy.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential (true CUP): disseminated malignancy with no primary site identified after standardized diagnostic workup including clinical, histopathological, radiological, and (sometimes) endoscopic examination.

Desirable (if test is available): molecular investigation, often by NGS, including MSI/MMR and TMB analysis, to identify personalized therapeutic approaches.

Staging

CUP is stage IV by definition per the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system. It is staged according to the original tumour type, if this can be identified.

Prognosis and prediction

Although CUP represents only 1–2% of all malignancies it remains a common cause of cancer-related deaths, with a mean survival time of < 1 year {26314775; 22048624; 22414598}. Only a minority (20%) of patients with CUP have a favourable prognosis, including those with single-site/oligometastatic CUP if amenable to ablative surgery and/or irradiation {34521064} and patients with CUP subtypes. This latter group of patients includes women with isolated axillary lymph node metastases (breast-like CUP), women with peritoneal serous carcinoma (ovary-like CUP), patients with SCC restricted to non-supraclavicular cervical lymph nodes (head/neck CUP), men with osteoblastic metastases and/or with CUP showing immunohistochemical/serum PSA expression (prostate-like CUP), patients with adenocarcinoma with a lower gastrointestinal immunohistochemistry profile (CK7−, CK20+, CDX2+; colon-like CUP), and patients with metastases with a renal cell carcinoma–like histological and immunohistochemical profile {29610002; 30268423}.

Although tissue-of-origin identification by gene expression or DNA methylation profiling seems feasible, two recent randomized trials failed to demonstrate the superiority of gene expression profiling–based site-specific therapy over standard empirical chemotherapy {30653423}. Consequently, as of now, no recommendation for the use of gene expression / DNA methylation profiling–based tissue-of-origin identification can be given.
