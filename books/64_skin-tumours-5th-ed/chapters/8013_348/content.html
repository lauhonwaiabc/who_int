8.0.1.3 Subungual keratoacanthoma

Definition

Subungual keratoacanthoma is a rapidly growing squamoproliferative nail bed tumour with histological overlap with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

ICD-O coding

8071/1 Subungual keratoacanthoma

8071/1 Subungual tumour of incontinentia pigmenti

ICD-11 coding

2C31.1 Keratoacanthoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Subungual tumour of incontinentia pigmenti (STIP)

Localization

Subungual keratoacanthoma and STIP can occur on any digit, predominantly the fingers.

Clinical features

These painful tumours may affect fingers and, less commonly, toes. They arise in the distal nail bed or occasionally in the proximal nail fold; in the latter, they present as paronychia. Solitary, multiple, eruptive, and familial forms occur. Patients often present with a painful nodule with subungual hyperkeratosis and/or nail spicule. Unlike keratoacanthoma at other sites, they may have an earlier onset, rarely resolve spontaneously, and often require surgery. Bone erosion or osteolysis may occur. Subungual keratoacanthoma and STIP are indistinguishable histologically {9767302; 8341755; 7982683}, although their occurrence in young women should trigger a search for possible incontinentia pigmenti. STIPs correlate with late-stage, verrucous disease and typically arise between the ages of 15 and 35 years {20514792}; such lesions may present more subtly, with nail dystrophy or destruction or paronychia, which may be the sole manifestation of the disease {14674911; 8747582; 20514792}. Subungual hyperkeratosis may precede tumours {8747582}. Although some lesions involute, the extreme pain associated with these tumours has driven patients to amputation as treatment, although retinoids have been used successfully more recently {9580828}.

Epidemiology

Subungual keratoacanthoma is a rare tumour that occurs most commonly in middle-aged, light-skinned individuals {8033384; 7982683}, in a slightly younger age group than keratoacanthoma elsewhere. STIPs occur in women on average aged between 15 and 35 years {20514792}.

Etiology

HPV15 has been implicated in a small series of STIPs {19213657; 32257968}.

Pathogenesis

STIPs develop in the late verrucous phase of incontinentia pigmenti.

Macroscopic appearance

Both tumours may appear as nodular or nodulocystic lesions.

Histopathology

The features overlap with those of cutaneous keratoacanthoma, with a lobular proliferation of eosinophilic keratinocytes and a crateriform to cystic contour, with adjacent and central hyperkeratosis. A cystic configuration may be evident even in lesions that do not regress. However, distinguishing features include a more vertical orientation, multiple and often clustered dyskeratotic keratinocytes (often numerous in areas of hyperkeratosis), and fewer eosinophils and neutrophils. They may lack fibrosis at their bases {7258561; 4067031; 8033384; 7982683}. Rarely, calcifying keratinocytes and corneocytes may be observed {24056180}. The higher expression of p53 and Ki-67 in subungual SCC can be useful in distinguishing it from subungual keratoacanthoma {18616725; 20456385}. In STIP, the tumour may displace the nail plate, leading to dystrophy. The differential diagnosis includes conventional SCC, subungual epidermoid inclusions, and onycholemmal carcinoma.

Cytology

Not applicable

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Incontinentia pigmenti is caused by an X-linked mutation in IKBKG, the gene encoding NEMO, required for activation of NF-κB. One report of a small number of cases studied via multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification analysis demonstrated that amplification of the genes encoding NF-κB1 (NFKB1) and cortactin (CTTN, previously EMS1) occurs in subungual keratoacanthoma but not in digital/subungual SCC. This amplification of NF-κB1 was confirmed by immunohistochemistry {20456385}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a nodular or nodulocystic lesion; a lobular to cystic proliferation of eosinophilic keratinocytes, with many dyskeratotic keratinocytes present, forming clusters, and associated hyperkeratosis.

Desirable: calcification of individual keratinocytes and corneocytes.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

These tumours do not have a tendency to resolve spontaneously like other cutaneous keratoacanthomas, and they are usually removed surgically. Retinoids have been used in treatment of STIP {9580828}.
