2.1.2.1 Low-CSD melanoma (including superficial spreading melanoma)

Definition

Melanoma in skin with a low degree of cumulative sun damage (low-CSD melanoma) occurs in skin with mild to moderate solar elastosis (individual elastotic fibres still identifiable) in the surrounding dermis. Melanomas that arise in low-CSD skin via other pathways (e.g. Spitz melanoma) are described in their respective sections.

Related terminology

Not recommended: pagetoid melanoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Low-CSD melanoma may occur at any cutaneous site, favouring locations with intermittent sun exposure; men’s backs and shoulders and women’s legs are the most typical localizations {8000999}.

Clinical features

Low-CSD melanoma of the superficial spreading melanoma (SSM) pattern arises as an irregularly outlined and pigmented macule that will become focally raised or nodular and eventually ulcerated as it progresses. Dermoscopic features of melanoma include asymmetry, multiple structures and colours, atypical reticular pattern, and blue veils. Low-CSD melanoma of the nodular melanoma pattern presents as a rapidly growing nodule without an adjacent macule. Occasionally, low-CSD melanoma may be amelanotic and mimic a non-melanocytic neoplasm. Rarely, satellite metastases occur close to the primary tumour. Low-CSD melanomas are usually asymptomatic, but itching and ulceration with oozing, crusting, and bleeding may be symptoms of melanoma {32057276}.

Epidemiology

Low-CSD melanoma accounts for nearly two thirds of cases in light-skinned people (Fitzpatrick skin phototypes I–III); it is less common in populations with more pigmented skin. Both sexes are affected equally. The incidence rises with age until approximately 55 years, and then gradually drops {18408748; 14679157}.

Etiology

Low-CSD melanoma is epidemiologically linked to sun exposure; genomic analyses reveal a high mutation burden with an ultraviolet (UV) radiation signature {20016485; 26091043; 28467829}. Repeated sunburn in childhood and intermittent sun exposure throughout life are associated with an increased risk of developing low-CSD melanoma. Tanning bed use has been linked to an increased rate of melanoma in young women {26818409}. Melanoma susceptibility genes include CDKN2A, CDK4 {26827760; 17047042}, and several genes that play a role in telomere maintenance.

Pathogenesis

A benign naevus is found in approximately 50% of cases {25239415}. BRAF p.V600E is present in > 50% of low-CSD melanomas {14679157; 26091043; 22842228} and arises early during progression. Mutation of CDKN2A (encoding p16) is common, and biallelic inactivation of CDKN2A occurs at the transition to invasive melanomas {22108608}. TERT promoter mutations are found in most low-CSD melanomas and are rare in naevi in low-CSD skin {30211730}. PTEN and TP53 mutations occur in advanced primary melanomas. The point-mutation burden increases with tumour progression from naevus to melanoma; a UV radiation signature is detectable at all stages {26559571}. See section Genomic landscape of melanoma for additional details.

Histopathology

In the pagetoid pattern of low-CSD melanoma (SSM), the in situ component is characterized by an intraepidermal proliferation of atypical melanocytes at many levels of the epidermis (pagetoid scatter) and variably sized nests along the dermal–epidermal junction. This portion typically extends laterally from any invasive component. The neoplastic melanocytes have large nuclei, irregularly clumped or dense chromatin, variably prominent nucleoli, and eosinophilic or lightly pigmented cytoplasm. A confluence of intraepidermal nests with scalloping of the surrounding epidermis and a prominent buckshot-like pattern of pagetoid scatter are often seen. Tumour cells may extend along hair follicles or eccrine ducts {25769708}.

The lentiginous pattern of low-CSD melanoma is characterized by a confluent proliferation of atypical melanocytes along the base of the epidermis, with fewer nests and less prominent pagetoid scatter. The atypical lentiginous melanocytes have enlarged, densely chromatic nuclei, usually smaller than those of neighbouring keratinocytes; inconspicuous nucleoli; and scant amphophilic or delicately melanized cytoplasm.

Low-CSD melanoma (SSM) may invade as only a few scattered cells into the superficial papillary dermis without mitotic activity (invasive radial growth phase), or it may form a dermal vertical growth phase (VGP) of expansile nests larger than those observed in the epidermis, often with mitoses. Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) may be present along the advancing margin of the VGP or throughout the VGP (brisk TILs), patchy infiltration of the VGP (non-brisk TILs), or no lymphocytic infiltration of the VGP (absent TILs). Regression may occur in the radial growth phase as a focal absence of tumour cells in the epidermis, with subjacent increased fibrosis, vascularity, and sometimes pigment-laden macrophages.

The dermis shows varying degrees of solar elastosis but no coalescence of elastotic fibres into amorphous aggregates characteristic of high-CSD melanoma {16809487}.

Naevoid melanoma and nodular melanoma are covered in section Naevoid melanoma and section Nodular melanoma .

Immunohistochemistry

Neoplastic melanocytes are positive for SOX10, MITF, HMB45, melan-A (MART1), and S100, but lineage markers can be lost as the tumour progresses. A combined Ki-67/melan-A stain can help identify proliferative areas of dermal tumour in inflamed tumours. Most tumours are positive for PRAME. There is often loss of staining for p16, especially in the VGP {32330325}. Immunohistochemistry for BRAF p.V600E can assist in the differential diagnosis with Spitz tumours.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of the pagetoid pattern of low-CSD melanoma (SSM) in situ includes high-grade dysplastic naevi, Paget disease, carcinoma in situ, and other tumours that display a pagetoid intraepidermal growth pattern. The dermal component typically displays no reduction in nuclear or cell size with descent into the dermis, distinct from the maturation observed in the dermal component of naevi. Mitoses are rarely observed in naevi. The lentiginous pattern of low-CSD melanoma is distinguished from lentigo maligna melanoma by the absence of marked solar elastosis and epidermal atrophy.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are not usually necessary for characteristic cases. Chromosomal abnormalities are common and most often involve losses at chromosomes 9, 10, 6q, and 20, and gains of chromosomes 1q, 6p, 7, 8q, 17q, and 20q {14578177}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a cytologically or architecturally atypical melanocytic proliferation on skin with low to moderate solar elastosis.

Desirable: intraepidermal pagetoid scatter or a confluent lentiginous proliferation.

Staging

Low-CSD melanoma is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of low-CSD melanoma (SSM) worsens with increasing tumour thickness or mitotic count, or with the presence of ulceration, lymphovascular invasion, or microscopic satellites.
