1.1.3.1 Seborrhoeic keratosis

Definition

Seborrhoeic keratoses are benign intraepithelial neoplastic squamous proliferations encountered on ageing skin.

ICD-O coding

8052/0 Seborrhoeic keratosis

8052/0 Irritated seborrhoeic keratosis

8052/0 Stucco keratosis

ICD-11 coding

2F21.0 Seborrhoeic keratosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: senile wart; seborrhoeic wart; basal cell papilloma.

Subtype(s)

Irritated seborrhoeic keratosis; stucco keratosis

Localization

Seborrhoeic keratoses show a wide anatomical distribution, with a predilection for the trunk and the head and neck region. They often cluster in the skin folds of the axillae and groins. The palms, soles, and mucosal surfaces are spared.

Clinical features

Seborrhoeic keratoses may be solitary or multiple and manifest as flat-based warty papules and plaques with tan to brown or black discolouration and a stuck-on appearance. Stucco keratoses are small and confined to the lower extremities. Dermoscopy reveals multiple brown or white clods, representing plugs of keratin, and thick ridges impart a cerebriform pattern {18613881}. The abrupt onset of numerous keratoses has been referred to as the Leser–Trélat sign and may be an indicator of an underlying visceral malignancy.

Epidemiology

Seborrhoeic keratoses are very common and show an increasing incidence with age, typically arising in middle-aged and elderly people. There is no significant sex predilection.

Etiology

The development of seborrhoeic keratoses is related to the interplay between increasing age and sun exposure; an inflammatory milieu may also predispose to lesions {34311463}.

Pathogenesis

Paradoxically, oncogenic mutations are noted, including somatic mutations in FGFR3, which may result in increased keratinocyte proliferation and reduced apoptosis {15772091; 16338952; 16778799; 20420824}. Other mutated genes include PIK3CA, KRAS, HRAS, EGFR, and AKT1, as well as TERT and DPH3 promoter mutations {19845664; 23739246; 28410231}. The mechanism by which these mutations fail to lead to malignancy in seborrhoeic keratosis is unknown {34311463}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Seborrhoeic keratoses are intraepidermal proliferations of basaloid keratinocytes. They are well circumscribed, with papillomatous outlines and a flat base. There is overlying hyperkeratosis, and tangential sectioning of the squamous invaginations gives rise to so-called horn cysts. The keratinocytes are small and bland-appearing, without cytological atypia or nuclear pleomorphism. Increased cytoplasmic melanin pigmentation may be seen. Different growth patterns and subtypes may be encountered. The acanthotic pattern is most commonly encountered and shows broad, regular columns of neoplastic keratinocytes, whereas the keratotic or papillomatous subtype shows a florid papillomatous architecture with abundant hyperkeratosis. The adenoid or reticulated pattern is composed of interconnecting elongated and delicate rete with small horn cyst formation. The clonal pattern is characterized by intraepidermal nests of neoplastic keratinocytes (Borst–Jadassohn pattern). The irritated subtype shows associated chronic inflammatory changes with areas of squamatization and squamous eddy formation. Tumours containing prominent intracytoplasmic melanin pigment are often also associated with pigment incontinence in the superficial dermis and are regarded as the pigmented subtype. Stucco keratosis shows papillomatosis and hyperkeratosis with a church-spire appearance.

Differential diagnosis

The most important differential diagnoses include actinic keratosis and squamous cell carcinoma in situ, which are characterized by squamous dysplasia not encountered in seborrhoeic keratoses. Squamatization and reactive atypia in the irritated subtype may be mistaken for squamous dysplasia. It is also important to understand that squamous dysplasia may arise in a background of a seborrhoeic keratosis. Verrucae can be separated by the presence of viral cytopathic change. The distinction between seborrhoeic keratosis and viral wart is often difficult. The presence of viral cytopathic change and dilated papillary dermal vessels favours the latter. HPV DNA is found in as many as 50% of seborrhoeic keratoses, especially in genital regions. Lesions with overlapping features may be termed verrucous keratosis.
