2.9.1.5 Melanoma metastatic to other organs

Definition

Melanoma metastatic to other organs is the spread from a primary melanoma to sites beyond the skin and subcutaneous fat.

ICD-O coding

8720/6 Melanoma metastatic to other organs

8720/6 Regional and distant lymph node metastases

8720/6 Visceral metastases

ICD-11 coding

2E0Y & XH4846 Malignant neoplasm metastasis, unspecified in other specified sites & Malignant melanoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Regional and distant lymph node metastases; visceral metastases

Localization

Melanoma metastases may involve any site, most commonly regional lymph nodes (in 60–80% of patients with metastatic melanoma) {14485771; 29028110} [[Meyers ML, Balch CM. Diagnosis and treatment of metastatic melanoma. In: Balch CM, Houghton AN, Sober AJ, et al., editors. Cutaneous melanoma. 3rd ed. St. Louis (MO): Quality Medical Publishing; 1998. pp 325–72.]], followed by distant nodes, lungs (18–36%), liver (14–20%), brain (12–20%), bone (11–17%), and intestine (1–7%) {6769552; 2346132}. Uveal melanomas, melanomas arising in blue naevi, and leptomeningeal melanomas have a striking propensity to metastasize to the liver {28229253; 11224601; 26645730; 342085}.

Clinical features

The most common presentation of melanoma metastases is regional lymph node spread with sentinel or palpable clinical nodal involvement in patients with prior primary cutaneous melanoma {29028110}. Much less commonly, patients may present with solitary or widespread distant metastatic disease with known or unknown primary melanomas [[Meyers ML, Balch CM. Diagnosis and treatment of metastatic melanoma. In: Balch CM, Houghton AN, Sober AJ, et al., editors. Cutaneous melanoma. 3rd ed. St. Louis (MO): Quality Medical Publishing; 1998. pp 325–72.]]. A detailed clinical history and comprehensive review of any pertinent antecedent melanocytic lesions or other suspicious tumours may be critical in establishing a correct diagnosis.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology corresponds to the type of the primary melanoma.

Etiology

Melanoma metastases can develop in any individual with melanoma, with a risk that increases with the thickness of the primary melanoma {29028110; 34066842}.

Pathogenesis

Melanoma metastases arise from lymphatic, haematogenous, perivascular, or perineural spread of neoplastic melanocytes from the primary tumour {12230518; 15370120}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Lymph node metastases range in cellularity from single cells to bulky tumour aggregates associated with effacement of the nodal architecture. Characteristically, nodal metastases are often first observed in the subcapsular sinus of the node, but both parenchymal and fibrous capsular and trabecular involvement may be seen [[Scolyer RA, Busam KJ, Barnhill RL. Metastatic melanoma. In: Barnhill RL, Piepkorn M, Busam KJ, editors. Pathology of melanocytic nevi and melanoma. 3rd ed. Berlin (Germany): Springer; 2014. pp. 529–67.]].

Visceral metastases are often solitary or multifocal nodules with angiotropic infiltrating features at the invasive front. Tumour cells can infiltrate (and replace) the surrounding tissue in a non-disruptive pattern in association with vascular co-option and angiotropism of tumour cells in a pericytic location, which is termed pericytic mimicry {31720876}. Otherwise, metastases are expansile nodules with or without a collagenous desmoplastic rim. Diffuse microscopic involvement of an organ such as the liver by micrometastases of the replacement (or infiltrating) type is termed miliary metastasis {9591028; 19329272} and has been well documented in uveal melanoma. Very advanced, late-stage, and terminal metastatic disease occurs with the diffuse dissemination of melanoma cells in the blood and in many or all organs {35650276; 15578887; 9934768}. At the microscopic level, melanoma cells may diffusely infiltrate organs, often resulting in engorgement of tissues. In the liver, this is termed the sinusoidal growth pattern {35650276; 15578887; 9934768}.

CNS melanoma metastases exhibit expansile nodular patterns without desmoplasia and with a diffusely infiltrating angiotropic pattern {19318812; 30927294}.

The cytomorphology of most melanoma metastases resembles that of the primary tumour, with the most common cell types being pigmented or amelanotic epithelioid cells, spindle cells, and naevoid cells; however, cytological features may be heterogeneous within metastases. Rhabdoid, clear cell, small cell, and anaplastic phenotypes occur {20010406; 33428337}.

Differential diagnosis

The histological differential diagnosis of metastatic melanoma includes primary melanoma and a wide range of poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumours, including sarcomas, carcinomas, and lymphomas [[Scolyer RA, Busam KJ, Barnhill RL. Metastatic melanoma. In: Barnhill RL, Piepkorn M, Busam KJ, editors. Pathology of melanocytic nevi and melanoma. 3rd ed. Berlin (Germany): Springer; 2014. pp. 529–67.]]. A detailed clinical history concerning a previous primary or metastatic melanoma, an antecedent melanocytic lesion previously biopsied or regressing, or other tumours may be crucial to establishing the correct diagnosis. In lymph nodes, conventional nodal naevi and blue naevi must be considered. Naevi are most frequently localized to the fibrous capsular and trabecular areas but may involve the lymph node parenchyma. Attention to cytomorphological features and the use of immunohistochemical markers may aid in this distinction.

Cytology

Tumour cells are large pleomorphic epithelioid cells with large nucleoli and often with intracytoplasmic melanin pigment.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

See section Melanoma metastatic to the skin .

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: proliferation of malignant neoplastic melanocytes at an extracutaneous site; exclusion of a primary melanoma.

Staging

Staging is performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM criteria {29028110}. The presence of nodal regional metastases is associated with a worse prognosis and represents an N-category criterion and stage III disease. Cases with distant cutaneous metastases are categorized as M1 and stage IV disease {31758078}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for patients with melanoma metastases varies depending on stage, number of distant metastatic sites, and extent of distant metastatic disease burden {6668496; 11078491; 9191522; 29028110; 29917326; 32304183}. The 5-year survival rates for stage III and IV disease are 77% and 27%, respectively {29028110}.
