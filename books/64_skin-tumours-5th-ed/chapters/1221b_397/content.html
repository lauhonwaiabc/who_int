1.2.2.1B Acantholytic squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Acantholytic squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a histological type of invasive SCC characterized by impaired intercellular adhesion that may result in the formation of pseudoglandular spaces.

ICD-O coding

8075/3 Acantholytic squamous cell carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C31.Z & XH7LH0 Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma & Squamous cell carcinoma, adenoid

Related terminology

Not recommended: pseudoglandular squamous cell carcinoma; pseudovascular squamous cell carcinoma; adenoid squamous cell carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The lesions often involve skin of the head, neck, face, and ears. Other sun-exposed sites can also be involved. In one review, about 60% of lesions involved the head and neck, and 40% involved the trunk and extremities {27991679}.

Clinical features

The clinical appearance of acantholytic invasive SCC is the same as that of its non-acantholytic counterpart (see section Squamous cell carcinomas).

Epidemiology

Acantholytic SCC accounts for < 5% of all SCCs, although the criteria for this designation (focal vs widespread acantholysis) are not well established. Older men are most frequently affected {23525614}, and an increased incidence has been reported in organ transplant recipients {15061848}.

Etiology

Given that most acantholytic SCCs occur in chronically sun-exposed sites, ultraviolet (UV) radiation–induced damage is probably a causative factor. The known association with chronic immunosuppression {15061848} implies a role for defective immunosurveillance.

Pathogenesis

The histogenetic factors are most often the same genomic, molecular, and causative factors that are applicable to ordinary invasive SCC. Compared with non-acantholytic SCCs, acantholytic SCC has been shown to have decreased expression of desmoglein-3 and E-cadherin {23525614}. Defective desmosomal protein expression has been confirmed {25264142} and is probably largely responsible for the acantholytic pattern observed histologically. The genetic profile of and potential susceptibility loci for acantholytic SCC are not yet understood.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

At scanning magnification, the overall architecture of invasive acantholytic SCC does not differ substantially from that of ordinary non-acantholytic SCC types. The overlying epidermis is often thickened and hyperkeratotic or ulcerated, and full-thickness carcinoma in situ may be absent (a factor to consider when evaluating superficial shave biopsies). The hallmark of this type is acantholysis resulting in variably sized spaces within the invasive tumour lobules, which result from cell separation due to acantholysis of tumour cells, often containing floating individual tumour cells, which are rounded in contour and may exhibit premature keratinization (dyskeratosis) or necrosis. When these spaces are bordered by an intact layer of tumour cells, a pseudoglandular pattern may develop, reminiscent of glandular or vascular spaces (pseudovascularization). Immunohistochemically, acantholytic SCC shows reactivity typical of ordinary invasive SCC: pseudoglandular spaces are negative for mucin, CK7, and CEA {25474451}, and pseudovascular regions are negative for CD31 and ERG.

Differential diagnosis

Lesions with extensive acantholysis can mimic either primary cutaneous (adnexal) or metastatic adenocarcinoma, and pseudoglandular spaces must be differentiated from the expression of true glands, as seen in adenosquamous carcinoma. Pseudovascular regions may superficially resemble angiosarcoma. Generally, acantholytic SCCs are not sufficiently well differentiated to elicit consideration of acantholytic benign keratosis (e.g. warty dyskeratoma).

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a clinical appearance and architecture similar to those of other SCC types; evidence of invasion; thickening of the epidermis with acantholysis (cell separation), producing variably sized spaces within the invasive tumour lobules.

Desirable: a pseudoglandular pattern.

Staging

Depending on the site, the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging system applies.

Prognosis and prediction

It has been suggested that acantholytic SCC is more aggressive than other SCC types {23525614}, with a mortality rate at follow-up of 19% {2768593}, although more recent data conflict with this interpretation {27991679}. Histological attributes that may confer aggressive behaviour have recently been comparatively evaluated for acantholytic versus non-acantholytic SCC {27991679}; these included microscopic tumour diameter, depth of invasion, degree of differentiation, and presence of perineural invasion. No significant differences between acantholytic and non-acantholytic ordinary SCCs were identified; overall, the notion that acantholytic SCC is a biologically and clinically more aggressive type of SCC has not been substantiated {21410819}.
