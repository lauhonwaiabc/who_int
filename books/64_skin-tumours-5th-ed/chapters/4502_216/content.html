4.5.0.2 Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma

Definition

Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma (ATLL) is a mature T-cell neoplasm caused by human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1), which commonly involves the skin.

Related terminology

Acceptable: adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma, HTLV-1 associated; cutaneous adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

Smouldering adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma; chronic adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma; lymphomatous adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma; acute adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma

Localization

Approximately 50% of patients with ATLL present with skin lesions {10659498}. Peripheral blood, bone marrow, lymph node, skin, spleen, liver, gastrointestinal tract, bone, lung, CNS, and other organs may be involved {6602943}.

Clinical features

On the basis of organ involvement and severity, ATLL is divided into four clinical subtypes: acute, chronic, lymphoma type, and smouldering {1751370}.

The cutaneous lesions are varied, with nodulotumoural (38.7%), plaque type (26.9%), and multipapular (19.3%) being the most common manifestations {21325600}. They may also include patch, erythrodermic, and purpuric morphologies.

Other cutaneous manifestations of HTLV-1 infection include childhood infective dermatitis {23592987; 1978165} and lichenoid dermatitis {26077665}.

No preferential site of skin involvement has been described {21325600; 18477226}. The cutaneous form of ATLL with minimum haematological abnormalities usually belongs to the smouldering type {21325600}. Patch/plaque lesions and erythroderma may be indistinguishable from the lesions of mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome, respectively {21325600}.

Epidemiology

ATLL occurs frequently in areas of HTLV-1 endemicity: southwestern Japan, the Caribbean, intertropical Africa, the Middle East, South America, and Papua New Guinea [[WHO. Human T-lymphotropic virus type 1: technical report, February 2020. Geneva (Switzerland): WHO; 2021. Available from: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240020221. Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO.]]. It may be encountered elsewhere via migration of HTLV-1 carriers. Three major routes of HTLV-1 infection include breastfeeding, sexual intercourse, and blood transfusion.

Only a small fraction (3–5%) of patients with HTLV-1 infection develop ATLL, and the estimated incidence is 46–710 cases per 100 000 person-years, with a higher incidence in endemic regions. A variety of risk factors and determinants for developing ATLL among people with HTLV-1 infection have been reported, including male sex, older age, higher HTLV-1 proviral load (≥ 40 copies per 1000 peripheral blood mononuclear cells), longer duration of infection (> 20 years), and younger age at acquisition (infancy or childhood) [[WHO. Human T-lymphotropic virus type 1: technical report, February 2020. Geneva (Switzerland): WHO; 2021. Available from: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240020221. Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO.]].

Etiology

Although chronic infection with HTLV-1 is the cause of ATLL, the full transformation of infected clones probably requires additional genetic and/or epigenetic events.

Pathogenesis

The HTLV-1 genome encodes two crucial proteins, Tax and HBZ, that regulate the growth and survival of infected cells {28893939}. Frequent alterations are observed in genes associated with T-cell receptor / NF-κB signalling and immune evasion {26437031; 34695199; 27281199}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Tumour cells often form Pautrier-like microabscesses in the epidermis, with a dermal perivascular infiltrate. Nodulotumoural lesions may demonstrate extension to subcutaneous fat {17388788}. The neoplastic lymphoid cells are generally medium-sized to large with nuclear pleomorphism {17388788}, and they occasionally show mitoses. Nuclear irregularity may be marked, with polylobate flower cells often seen in the blood and tissues. Eosinophils may be intermingled with lymphocytes.

Immunophenotype

Th tumour cells are usually positive for CD3, CD4, CD25, and CCR4, and often positive for FOXP3, suggesting a T regulatory (Treg) cell–like immunophenotype {7665144; 15198736; 16193085}, but they are negative for CD8, CD19, and CD20. In the advanced stage, the neoplastic cells express CADM1 with loss of CD7 {24727323}, and the large transformed cells may be positive for CD30.

Differential diagnosis

Most cases show epidermotropism with Pautrier microabscesses of the malignant T cells, which may be indistinguishable from those of mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome (see Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria, below).

Cytology

Circulating ATLL cells are agranular, medium-sized to large lymphoid cells with cerebriform or flower-like nuclei.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Monoclonal integration of HTLV-1 can be identified from the neoplastic T cells by using peripheral blood samples, fresh tissue samples, or fixed tissue specimens {32801340; 32973330}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a neoplastic lymphoid cell proliferation with a mature T-cell phenotype; occurrence in an HTLV-1 carrier.

Desirable: lymphoid cells with prominent convolutions and lobulation; detection of monoclonal integration of HTLV-1 provirus or related transcripts (including HBZ) to exclude mycosis fungoides or Sézary syndrome arising in an HTLV-1 carrier.

Staging

The Lugano classification (a modification of the Ann Arbor system) can be used.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with the indolent types are usually observed without therapeutic intervention, but approximately a half of the patients may progress to the aggressive form within 5 years {28515095}. The erythrodermic and nodulotumoural types show a poor prognosis, comparable to that of the lymphomatous type {21325600}. PD1 inhibitor therapy might induce rapid progression of ATLL {29768155}.
