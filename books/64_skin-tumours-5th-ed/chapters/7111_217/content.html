7.1.1.1 Metastases to skin

Definition

Metastases to skin are usually epithelial, melanocytic, or mesenchymal malignant lesions that metastasize to involve the skin (epidermis to subcutaneous tissue). Involvement of the skin by systemic haematolymphoid lesions is not considered metastasis.

ICD-O coding

Multiple codes ending in /6, depending on the primary source

ICD-11 coding

2E08 Metastatic malignant neoplasm involving skin

Related terminology

Acceptable: cutaneous metastasis; secondary involvement of the skin.

Subtype(s)

The tumour type will depend on the primary site of the tumour. A strategy for the workup of epithelioid tumours is given in #42077Fig. XXX .

Localization

Cutaneous metastases usually involve the areas of the skin overlying the primary lesion. Thus, primaries in the abdominal region typically metastasize to the skin of the abdomen (e.g. Sister Mary Joseph nodule for those involving the periumbilical region), while thoracic tumours typically involve the skin of the chest (e.g. mammary Paget disease involving the nipple and areola) {19922483}. Many primaries may involve the scalp {8335743}, probably because of the high vascularity of the skin of the scalp.

Clinical features

Cutaneous metastases are more common in elderly individuals, although young children may also be affected {11100807}. In approximately 1–12% of the cases the cutaneous metastasis is the first symptom of the malignancy {8335743; 17515511}. Overall, the most common origin is breast cancer in women and melanoma in men, followed by lung, colon, head and neck, and ovarian cancers, and others {8335743; 23432658; 30497674}. Cutaneous metastases commonly manifest as isolated nodules or papules. Less common are large plaques and grouped papules in a zosteriform pattern. Secondary extramammary Paget disease (epidermotropic metastatic carcinoma) may arise as eczematous plaques, most commonly in the genital region (see also section Extramammary Paget disease). Rare cases of breast and salivary gland carcinomas present extensive vascular invasion with an en-cuirasse morphology {23050032}. Metastases from primary cutaneous lesions may be satellites (within 50 mm of the primary lesion), in transit (between the primary lesion and the regional lymphatic basin), or distant (all others).

Epidemiology

The incidence of cutaneous metastasis is 1–10% in autopsies {17866382; 15405683} and ranges from 1.2% to 10% in surgical and biopsy specimens {19056145; 8335743; 12630642}. Some of these studies have indicated that cutaneous metastasis is more common in White people than in Asians, although more studies are needed.

Etiology

The cause is that of the primary tumour type.

Pathogenesis

Most cutaneous metastases appear to correspond to haematogenous spread. There is also lymphatic and angiotropic extravascular spread and, rarely, iatrogenic implantation, such as transfer of squamous carcinoma cells through a needle administering an analgesic used both in the tumour and the grafting area {31967105; 15370120; 22716270}. The mechanisms by which some tumours more frequently metastasize to the skin than to other organs is not fully determined. However, as proposed by Dr Paget in 1889 with his “seed and soil” theory {2673568}, there is a likely exchange of information between the metastatic tumour cells (seed) and the microenvironment of the recipient tissue (soil).

Macroscopic appearance

Metastases to skin are mostly nodular or erythematous lesions.

Histopathology

The morphology will depend on the primary lesion {22617133}. In general, metastases present as relatively well-circumscribed lesions involving either the dermis or the subcutaneous tissue. Vascular invasion is usually more frequent than in primary cutaneous lesions.

Differential diagnosis

The lesions tend to have the same morphology as the primary {30605024}. Breast carcinoma has a ductular or (less commonly) lobular pattern. Some breast lesions may have prominent vascular invasion (inflammatory carcinoma). Occasionally the stroma is markedly desmoplastic and may resemble a dermatofibroma. Most metastases tend to resemble their primary lesions (e.g. lung adenocarcinoma shows similar a glandular arrangement both in the skin and in the lung, and colon adenocarcinoma metastatic to the skin may show dirty necrosis). However, in some cases the metastases may mimic a primary lesion (e.g. clear cell renal carcinoma metastatic to the skin may be confused with cutaneous xanthomas). Similarly, some metastatic melanomas have a prominent intraepidermal component that even extends beyond the dermal component or appears to be just in situ melanoma, thus mimicking a primary lesion. Immunohistochemistry may be helpful. Metastatic adenocarcinomas to the skin tend to be negative for p63, calretinin, and D2-40 {17518775; 20190734; 20377670}. Several authors have proposed algorithms to try to determine the primary tumour in metastasis of unknown origin {30605024}. Molecular analysis has been proposed to try to detect a genetic signature compatible with an internal malignancy or to provide possible treatments even if the primary origin is unknown {32010631}.

Cytology

The morphological features will depend on the histological type of the metastasis {33128312}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnostic molecular pathology is related to the primary lesion.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Metastases to the skin are usually dermal or subcutaneous nodules, rarely connected with the overlying epidermis.

Staging

Staging is related to the primary lesion.

Prognosis and prediction

Detection of metastasis to the skin conveys a poor prognosis {11908133; 18312322}. When the lesions are discovered at the same time as the primary tumours, they are considered poor prognostic indicators {15186430}.
