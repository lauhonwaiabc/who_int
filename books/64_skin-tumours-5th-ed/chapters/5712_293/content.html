5.7.1.2 Atypical fibroxanthoma

Definition

Atypical fibroxanthoma (AFX) is a dermally based neoplasm of uncertain histogenesis that generally behaves in a low-grade fashion when strict diagnostic criteria are used. Most cases arise on sun-damaged sites in elderly individuals. The diagnosis requires the evaluation of multiple immunostains.

ICD-O coding

8830/1 Atypical fibroxanthoma

ICD-11 coding

2F72.Y & XH1RM7 Other specified neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of skin & Atypical fibrous histiocytoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: superficial malignant fibrous histiocytoma; dermal undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Tumours usually develop on sun-exposed sites, especially the head and neck. The ear is a particularly common location. Purported lesions developing on the limbs in the young are likely to represent other entities, such as atypical fibrous histiocytoma.

Clinical features

AFX often manifests as a solitary, localized nodule up to 20 mm in diameter, on sun-damaged skin {20526171; 19807823; 16197421; 22709412}. Ulceration is common, often with a short history of rapid growth. Although there are no specific clinical features, the diagnosis can sometimes be suspected from the context and lack of pigmentation or features pointing to a diagnosis of basal or squamous cell carcinoma.

Epidemiology

The tumours are rare, mostly occurring in sun-damaged skin of elderly White patients, especially those living in areas of high solar radiation {20526171; 19807823; 21269345}. Men are more often affected than women. AFXs have been reported in young patients with Li–Fraumeni syndrome with germline mutation of TP53 {24299451} and xeroderma pigmentosum {12485439; 17378688}.

Etiology

A role for ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure is supported by clinical factors and by the observed associations with Li–Fraumeni syndrome and xeroderma pigmentosum {11406660; 8030743; 12485439; 17378688}. Some cases have occurred in the field of radiotherapy {21269345; 27028396} and in the context of immunosuppression and organ transplantation {22129349}. Associated trauma has been rarely reported.

A role for UV radiation exposure is also supported by evidence of multiple UV radiation–related genetic changes, including UV radiation signature mutations in TP53 {8030743; 29141020; 29099504; 32079866; 24030750}. Studies, including comparative genomic hybridization, show genomic changes including 9p and 13q deletions, TERT mutations, and many abnormalities in common with pleomorphic dermal sarcoma {15015571; 29141020; 29099504; 30809375; 32540221; 34202213}.

AFXs have been seen in Li–Fraumeni syndrome (with germline TP53 mutation) {24299451} and xeroderma pigmentosum {27046537; 3631985; 17378688}. One patient with xeroderma pigmentosum showed TP53 mutation and germline mutation of CDKN2A (p16INK4a/p14ARF) {12485439}.

Pathogenesis

The origin of AFX remains unclear, but electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry suggest a histogenesis from undifferentiated dermal mesenchymal cells {907965; 196742}.

Macroscopic appearance

An often ulcerated, circumscribed tumour as large as 20 mm in diameter is present in the dermis, with a firm cut surface and sometimes with haemorrhage.

Histopathology

A circumscribed dermal tumour is present with no involvement of the subcutis. The neoplasm is typically highly cellular, with striking nuclear enlargement, pleomorphism, and atypia. Mitoses are frequent, with atypical forms. Enlarged spindle or epithelioid cells are seen, some with histiocyte-like appearances. Irregular fascicles may be present with scattered inflammatory cells and occasional haemorrhage. Ulceration is common, sometimes with an epidermal collarette or atrophy. Necrosis is absent in AFX. Plaque-like growth is occasionally seen with a band of cells parallel to the epidermis. These plaque-like lesions may be less well circumscribed {20526171}.

AFX with a spindle cell pattern shows exclusively spindle-shaped cells in fascicles with eosinophilic cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei, and prominent nucleoli {26098709}. Sometimes the cytological features of this pattern are relatively bland. AFX with a clear cell pattern exhibits cells with clear to foamy cytoplasm and hyperchromatic, pleomorphic nuclei {26848640}. Glycogen is of limited extent or absent. Further patterns include granular cell, keloidal, myxoid, and sclerotic forms. Regression is sometimes seen, which can be extensive, making diagnosis on a partial biopsy challenging. Pigmented AFX show haemosiderin deposition and haemorrhage {9870148; 24080496}. Some AFXs contain osteoclast-like giant cells, which may be multinucleated tumour cells (demonstrating nuclear atypia) or background histiocytes (CD163-positive with bland nuclei).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry is vital to exclude other malignancies, especially melanoma, leiomyosarcoma, and poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. A metastasis should always be considered. A broad panel of immunostains is essential. In particular, melanocyte markers (e.g. SOX10, S100) and keratinocyte markers (e.g. MNF116, AE1/AE3, 34βE12, p63, p40) should be negative {20526171; 19807823; 19476522; 25029117}. Included S100-positive Langerhans cells can be numerous and confirmed with CD207 (langerin) or CD1a. Many AFXs are MITF-positive, and occasionally there may be focal expression of melan-A {25238448; 22050092}. In some studies, most tumours stain with SMA, but allied desmin or h-caldesmon staining would suggest leiomyosarcoma {26098709}. Desmin positivity has been reported in AFX, but these tumours may represent leiomyosarcoma, particularly if staining is prominent. CD10 is almost invariably strongly and diffusely expressed in AFX and may be the only positive stain. Although this is nonspecific and can be seen in some melanomas and squamous cell carcinomas, for example, AFX should be diagnosed with great caution if CD10 is absent. CD68 and CD163 are often positive but are of limited diagnostic value and also stain included macrophages. Multinucleated tumour giant cells may non-specifically uptake immunostains including HMB45 and calponin. In vascular-appearing tumours, negative staining for CD34 and ERG may be required to exclude angiosarcoma {24080496}. Some AFXs express EMA (without evidence of other epithelial marker positivity) and CD31 {24080496}.

Differential diagnosis

See #34497Table 7.03 .

Cytology

Malignant cells of epithelioid or spindly appearance are seen, but histopathology and immunostaining are essential for definitive diagnosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in most cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: usually pleomorphic (but variable) morphology; strict confinement to the dermis; immunonegativity for at least two epithelial and melanocytic markers (e.g. keratins, p63, S100, HMB45, melan-A, and SOX10).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Most AFXs behave in a benign fashion if stringent diagnostic criteria are used. Recent studies suggest that any permeation of the subcutis should be regarded as pleomorphic dermal sarcoma rather than AFX {25590287; 35276548}. As many as 5% recur, usually due to incomplete excision. Metastases rarely occur, with occasional fatalities {25590287; 29981390}.
