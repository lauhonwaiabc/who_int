4.4.1.1 Primary cutaneous CD4-positive small or medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder

Definition

Primary cutaneous CD4-positive small or medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder (PCSM-LPD) is characterized by a predominance of small to medium-sized CD4-positive pleomorphic T cells, and by a solitary skin lesion without evidence of the plaques typical of mycosis fungoides.

ICD-O coding

9709/1 Primary cutaneous CD4-positive small or medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder

ICD-11 coding

2B0Y & XH3QE7 Other specified primary cutaneous mature T-cell or NK-cell lymphomas and lymphoproliferative disorders & Primary cutaneous CD4-positive small/medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder

Related terminology

Not recommended: primary cutaneous CD4+ small/medium pleomorphic T-cell lymphoma; pseudolymphomatous folliculitis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

PCSM-LPD occurs most often in the head and neck (50%), followed by the trunk and the upper and lower extremities.

Clinical features

Because PCSM-LPD cases have the same benign clinical course as cutaneous reactive T-cell–rich lymphoid proliferations (see section Reactive T-cell–rich lymphoid proliferations), the term “primary cutaneous CD4-positive small or medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder” rather than “lymphoma” is preferred.

PCSM-LPD affects adults in the majority of cases, but paediatric cases have been described {18311111; 19461234; 32271188; 21641676; 24996443; 27550169}. The most frequent clinical presentation is an asymptomatic erythematous solitary nodule, followed by tumour, papule, macule, or plaque. The size ranges from 4 mm to 60 mm. In patients with multifocal lesions, a diagnosis of peripheral T-cell lymphoma NOS must be ruled out.

Epidemiology

This is the second most common cutaneous T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder following mycosis fungoides, accounting for 6% {30635287} to 7.5% of primary cutaneous lymphomas {33131055}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

PCSM-LPD may reflect a response to an antigen stimulus, with both B-cell and T-cell recruitment, including a signifying T follicular helper (TFH) cell population {34231494}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

There are two common architectural patterns (see #38258Table 6.13): mostly (78% of cases) a nodular and/or diffuse lymphoproliferation infiltrating the entire dermis and sometimes expanding into the subcutis; and less frequently, a subepidermal band-like infiltrate filling the superficial dermis (resembling mycosis fungoides / Sézary syndrome), often associated with a periadnexal distribution {32271188; 21989349}. Epidermotropism may be present focally, but if epidermotropism is conspicuous, the diagnosis of mycosis fungoides should be considered. Adnexotropism (with follicular destruction) is very characteristic {19461234; 32271188; 31313694}.

A predominance of small to medium-sized pleomorphic T cells with no more than 40% of large cells is characteristic {33989212; 32271188; 28965629}.

Immunohistochemistry

PCSM-LPD has a CD3+, CD4+, CD8−, CD30− phenotype but includes an admixed B-cell component (the latter can account for up to 60% of cells, sometimes with large immunoblasts) {33989212; 28965629}. Small reactive CD8+ T cells as a minority admixed population are almost ubiquitous (arranged in a starry-sky pattern {32271188}), and sparse CD30+ T or B cells {33989212; 18987541; 28965629}, plasma cells, eosinophils, and histiocytes (including multinucleated giant cells) may be present {19461234; 32271188; 21989349}. Lymphoid follicles are uncommon. Plasma cells are sometimes light chain–restricted. The loss of pan–T-cell antigens (except for CD7) is uncommon. Cytotoxic proteins are not expressed {18311111; 19461234; 21989349; 18987541}. Atypical CD4+ T cells express TFH markers: PD1 (by medium-sized to large cells forming clusters or rosettes) {18987541}, ICOS, CXCL13 (few positive cells), and BCL6 (sparse medium-sized to large lymphocytes), but not CD10 {32271188}. The proliferative rate is often low (Ki-67 index: ~25%), but it can (rarely) reach 40% {33624292}.

Differential diagnosis

The major differential diagnosis is marginal zone B-cell lymphoma {34231494}. The differential diagnosis also includes cutaneous reactive T-cell–rich lymphoid proliferation (but reactive T-cell–rich lymphoid proliferation and PCSM-LPD belong to a common spectrum of diseases), mycosis fungoides, and lymphomatoid papulosis (in case of multiple skin lesions). Rare cases presenting with widespread skin lesions, > 40% large pleomorphic T cells, and/or a high proliferative fraction do not belong to this group and are better classified as peripheral T-cell lymphoma NOS.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

TR genes are clonally rearranged in most cases (70%) {21989349; 18987541; 33915243}, with no impact on prognosis {32271188}. A coexisting B-cell clone may occur (26%) {33989212; 32271188}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an asymptomatic solitary lesion; one of the two architectural patterns (see #38258Table 6.13); a predominance of CD4+ small to medium-sized pleomorphic T cells admixed with a B-cell component; TFH phenotype (strong PD1 expression by atypical cells +/− ICOS, BCL6, CXCL13, but CD10 is negative).

Desirable: localization in the head and neck > trunk > extremities; adnexotropism; absence of lymphoid follicles; scattered reactive cells (CD8+ T cells, CD30+, plasma cells, eosinophils, and histiocytes).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients have an excellent prognosis; local or distant recurrences are uncommon, and there is no long-term risk of secondary lymphoma {33326960; 33915243}. Intralesional steroids, surgical excision, and radiotherapy are the preferred modes of treatment. Spontaneous regression after biopsy is frequent (reported in 32% of cases) {18311111; 32271188; 24996443; 21989349}.
