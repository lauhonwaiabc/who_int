2.0.0.2 Genomic landscape of melanoma

Like other cancers, melanomas arise through the accumulation of mutations that constitutively activate growth-promoting signalling pathways and ablate tumour suppressive mechanisms. The mutational mechanisms causing these genetic alterations vary considerably, depending on the context. Melanomas arising in skin with some degree of cumulative sun damage (CSD), which is reflected in the degree of perilesional solar elastosis, typically harbour a high number of point mutations, mostly cytosine to thymidine (C>T) transitions at dipyrimidine sites, a hallmark of ultraviolet (UV) radiation–induced mutations {26091043}. High-CSD melanomas carry the highest mutation burden, ranking among the most highly mutated cancers overall, with an average of 30 mutations per million base pairs of DNA {26214590}. This rate is exceeded only in desmoplastic melanomas on sun-damaged skin, which have a median of 62 mutations per million base pairs {26343386}. Low-CSD melanomas have about 15 mutations per million base pairs. In contrast, melanomas arising in the glabrous skin, nail apparatus, or mucous membranes have low mutation burdens, typically without a UV radiation signature. Instead, these melanomas are characterized by abundant copy-number changes, including multiple high-level amplifications {16291983}. Unlike in other solid tumours, these complex aberrations arise early in the tumours’ progression and appear to be the dominant force shaping their evolution {37904969; 39034322}. Uveal melanomas, as well as the closely biologically related melanomas arising in blue naevus and primary melanomas of the CNS, also have very low mutation burdens, but they lack the highly rearranged genomes of acral and mucosal melanomas (see #32386Table 3.03) {24460190} and thus appear to lack any extrinsic or intrinsic factors increasing their mutation rate.

In cutaneous melanomas, genetic alterations disrupt a common set of key signalling pathways. These include the MAPK pathway, which is activated by mutations affecting the upstream receptor at the level of KIT, RAS family members acting immediately downstream of receptors, negative regulators of RAS (e.g. neurofibromin [NF1], RASA2, and SPRED1), and RAS effectors acting further downstream (e.g. BRAF, MEK, and cyclin D1). Alterations in the genes encoding these proteins tend to be mutually exclusive of each other in untreated melanomas and arise early in their progression, often giving rise to melanocytic naevi first. Naevi constitute clonal proliferations of partially transformed melanocytes that are kept in check by means of intact tumour suppressor mechanisms such as oncogene-induced and replicative senescence. These barriers can be overrun by inactivation of CDKN2A (encoding p16) by deletion or mutation, which is very common in cutaneous melanomas; by amplification or mutation of CDK4 (which encodes p16’s target kinase); and by loss of RB1. CDKN2A and CDK4 mutations are also seen in the form of germline alterations, which predispose individuals to melanoma with high penetrance {26488006}. Replicative senescence is overcome by mutations of the TERT promoter {23348506}, which constitute the most common mutations in cutaneous melanomas. The mutations cause increased expression of telomerase, resulting in cell immortalization by extension of the replicative lifespan of cells, allowing them to avoid replicative senescence in response to telomere shortening. Germline mutations of the TERT promoter and other genes encoding proteins involved in telomere maintenance (e.g. POT1, ACD, and TERF2IP) result in longer telomeres and are another cause of melanoma predisposition {26488006}.

Other signalling pathways recurrently mutated in melanomas include pathways involved in chromatin modifications, such as those related to the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex {26091043; 22817889; 23355908} (mainly affecting genes such as ARID2, ARID1A, and EZH2), and inactivation of the histone modifier BAP1, which is the major tumour suppressor gene in uveal melanoma {21051595}.

Although the melanoma types share alterations in these pathways, the way the pathways are deregulated varies among the types (see #32387Table 3.04). In low-CSD melanomas, BRAF p.V600E mutations account for the majority of MAPK pathway–activating mutations. In contrast, high-CSD melanomas rarely harbour BRAF p.V600E mutations but instead have other BRAF mutations or (more frequently) loss-of-function mutations in NF1 or activating mutations of NRAS or occasionally KIT. These differences in genetic alterations, along with the different age distributions (low-CSD melanomas tend to arise in younger individuals) and the associations with different precursors (low-CSD melanomas often arise in naevi; high-CSD melanomas do not), gave rise to the dual pathway hypothesis and the proposal to classify these melanoma types into two separate categories, broadly corresponding to the traditional subtypes of superficial spreading melanoma and lentigo maligna melanoma {14679157; 12783935}.

Uveal melanoma stands out in that it has Gqα signalling pathway mutations, most (~90%) of which affect two closely related genes, GNAQ and GNA11, which encode the alpha subunits of heterotrimeric G proteins. Less commonly, mutations affect the G protein–coupled receptor CYSLTR2 (which signals through Gqα) or PLCB4 (which encodes the Gqα effector phospholipase Cβ4). Subsequent mutations that demarcate malignant progression to melanoma are inactivating mutations in the histone deubiquitinase BAP1 {19078957} or mutations that change the function of the splicing factor SF3B1 {31597964} or the translation elongation factor EIF1AX. Mutations in GNAQ or GNA11, followed by progression mutations in BAP1, EIF1AX, or SF3B1 {31253977}, are also found in melanomas arising in blue naevus, as well as in primary leptomeningeal melanomas and melanocytomas {26769193; 22307269; 19078957; 21083380}. The similarities between the genetic alterations found in uveal melanoma, melanoma arising in blue naevus, and leptomeningeal melanomas suggest that these tumours are closely biologically related entities that originate from a developmentally distinct type of melanocyte that does not reside within an epithelial structure {24460190}.

The multiple pathogenic mutations found in fully evolved melanomas may occur in variable sequences. Benign naevi typically harbour a single MAPK pathway mutation – such as a BRAF p.V600E mutation (in common acquired naevi) {12447372}; an NRAS mutation (in congenital and some acquired naevi) {8043423}; an HRAS mutation {10980135} or a kinase fusion of ALK, BRAF, ROS1, NTRK1, NTRK3, MET, MAP3K8, or RET {23890088; 24445538; 26013381; 27477320; 30833747} or fusions involving the RAS-exchange factors RASGRF1 and RASGRF2 (in Spitz naevi); or a Gqα pathway mutation (in blue naevi) – but no additional mutations. Therefore, these mutations appear to be sufficient for naevus initiation {26559571}, but the proliferation of their constituent cells is limited by several mechanisms subsumed under the term “senescence”. The clonal expansion of these partially transformed melanocytes increases the likelihood that one of them acquires an additional pathogenic mutation that overrides mechanisms that keep their proliferation in check. Such a mutation then leads to a second clonal expansion of the affected cell, increasing the risk of additional mutations and progression to melanoma {26559571}. Melanocytomas are intermediate melanocytic neoplasms captured during this second clonal expansion. Their histomorphology varies depending on the nature of the second mutation. Examples of melanocytomas include BAP1-inactivated melanocytoma, pigmented epithelioid melanocytoma, and WNT-activated deep penetrating melanocytoma, which arise from common acquired naevi, typically harbouring BRAF p.V600E mutations, by biallelic inactivation of BAP1 (in the case of BAP1-inactivated melanocytoma), inactivation of PRKAR1A (in pigmented epithelioid melanocytoma), or activation of β-catenin or ID1 (in WNT-activated deep penetrating melanocytoma) {22367297; 18059235; 28935960; 32732488}.

Naevi are thus facultative melanoma precursors because they are composed of partially transformed melanocytes harbouring one of the multiple mutations required for melanoma formation. The numerical expansion of the original melanocyte that first acquired the oncogenic mutation into hundreds of thousands of daughter cells carrying the same mutation, forming the naevus, thus increases the probability of one of those cells acquiring additional pathogenic mutations. This probability is very low in naevi and low in melanocytomas, corresponding to the concept of “benign” or “low-risk” tumours.

In addition, steps can be skipped and there is a substantial proportion of melanomas in which no precursor lesion is identified (i.e. de novo melanoma).
