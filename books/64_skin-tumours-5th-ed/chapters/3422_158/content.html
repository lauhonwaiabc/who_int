3.4.2.2 Extramammary Paget disease

Definition

Extramammary Paget disease (EMPD) is a rare adenocarcinoma characterized by a predominant intraepithelial growth of neoplastic cells originating in the skin (primary EMPD) or representing intraepithelial spread of an underlying visceral carcinoma (secondary EMPD).

Related terminology

Acceptable: Paget disease.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The labium majus of the vulva is the most common site, followed by the labium minus and clitoris. The perineum, perianal area, scrotum, penis, lower abdomen, and inguinal folds can also be affected. Outside the anogenital region, EMPD can very rarely affect non-genital regions {19674203; 30807292}.

Clinical features

The most common presentation is a relatively well-demarcated flat or slightly elevated, scaly, oozing, erythematous patch or plaque accompanied by pruritus or burning; thus, these lesions often clinically resemble eczema. They generally cover a large area (median: 20.5 cm2) {31650976}. Rare forms include lesions with hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation and multicentric lesions (e.g. genital lesions associated with unilateral or bilateral axillary involvement, occurring synchronously or metachronously) {9391560}.

Epidemiology

EMPD accounts for about 1% of all neoplasms in the anogenital area. It mostly occurs in White postmenopausal women, with a median age of about 70–75 years. Men are affected less often.

Etiology

The inciting factors for primary EMPD are unknown, but it may represent colonization of the epidermis by carcinoma cells originating in the underlying glandular structures. It has been suggested that Toker cells might be a source for EMPD {31046904; 18462365}. Secondary EMPD represents skin involvement from an underlying carcinoma, most commonly originating from the lower gastrointestinal tract (distal colon or rectum) or urinary tract (urinary bladder or prostate) {34436026}.

Pathogenesis

The histogenesis of primary EMPD is unknown. ERBB2 amplification is rare, but ERBB2 (HER2) expression can be seen in a third of cases {16932067; 19519604}. Mutations in genes encoding the PI3K/AKT cascade have been found to significantly correlate with CDH1 hypermethylation {22821211; 28291131}. A study using comparative genomic hybridization revealed amplification at chromosomes Xcent-q21 and 19, and loss at 10q24-qter {16086738}.

Macroscopic appearance

Patients present with a pruritic, erythematous, eczematous lesion mimicking a vulvar dermatosis or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (VIN 3).

Histopathology

The intraepithelial neoplastic cells (Paget cells) have vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli and abundant basophilic to amphophilic cytoplasm. They occur as solitary cells or well-demarcated solid nests or gland-like structures. Many cases also involve underlying skin adnexa, especially hair follicles and eccrine ducts {26863064}. Occasionally, there is focal dermal invasion by single cells, glands, or small groups of cells (invasive Paget disease). Less common morphological changes include epidermal hyperplasia resembling syringocystadenocarcinoma papilliferum, syringoma-like structures, melanin pigment within the cytoplasm of the neoplastic cells simulating a melanocytic lesion, signet-ring cell morphology, and association with other vulvar lesions {21169735; 26863060; 26863065; 19384061}. Paget cells usually stain positively with mucicarmine, Alcian blue, and PAS reaction (diastase-resistant).

Immunohistochemistry

Primary EMPD is consistently positive for CK7 and GATA3 and with CAM5.2, and it often expresses CEA, GCDFP-15, ERBB2, CA125, and AR {28418164}. ER and PR may be positive or negative {31297606; 10761460}.

Differential diagnosis

The most important issue is the distinction between primary and secondary EMPD. Coexpression of CK20 and CDX2 by the Paget cells and dirty necrosis (although the latter is unusual) support a colorectal origin. Uroplakin-3 expression is typical for urothelial carcinoma, whereas coexpression of PSA and NKX3-1 suggests an underlying prostatic carcinoma.

Potential mimics of EMPD are vulvar Toker cells, vulvar and penile analogues of cervical stratified mucin-producing intraepithelial lesion {19238078; 27097242}, high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion, melanoma, pagetoid dyskeratosis, and clear cell papulosis. In addition to immunohistochemistry, clinicopathological correlation is essential for correct diagnosis.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: presence of atypical epithelial cells involving the epidermis; confirmation by immunohistochemistry (low-molecular-weight cytokeratin, CK7, CEA).

Desirable: clinical/imaging evaluation to help distinguish primary from secondary; additional immunohistochemistry (uroplakin, CK20, p63, CDX2, NKX3-1, ERBB2).

Staging

Staging depends on the subtype, primary versus secondary EMPD, and the location; for example, invasive vulval Paget disease is staged the same way as other vulval cancers. There is a proposal for TNM staging {27329007}.

Prognosis and prediction

The recurrence rate of primary EMPD is associated with positive surgical margins, multicentricity, and skip areas, but the overall mortality is similar to the general matched population {34436026}. Primary EMPD with an invasive component is significantly associated with both lymph node metastasis and a worse prognosis {26508372}. Cases with mutant PIK3CA and AKT1 are often invasive {22821211; 28291131}. Expression of CXCR4 and ACKR3 (CXCR7) appears to be associated with increased aggressiveness {28900484}. There have been several studies evaluating the possible prognostic value of sentinel lymph nodes {34436026}. The prognosis of secondary EMPD depends on the underlying malignancy.
