5.3.4.2 Cutaneous angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is an aggressive malignant vascular neoplasm that variably recapitulates the morphological and immunohistochemical features of endothelial cells.

ICD-O coding

9120/3 Cutaneous angiosarcoma

9120/3 Primary cutaneous angiosarcoma

9120/3 Postradiation angiosarcoma

9120/3 Lymphoedema-associated angiosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B56.Y & XH6264 Angiosarcoma, other specified primary site & Haemangiosarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: haemangiosarcoma; lymphangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Primary cutaneous angiosarcoma; postradiation angiosarcoma; lymphoedema-associated angiosarcoma

Localization

Primary cutaneous angiosarcoma usually occurs in elderly patients on sun-damaged skin of the head and neck {21190880; 26276214; 28916596; 32829071}. Secondary (radiation-induced) angiosarcoma most commonly involves the breast or chest wall following breast-conserving surgery and radiation for carcinoma {15166670; 16006792; 31448482}. Lymphoedema-associated angiosarcoma/lymphangiosarcoma may develop in the ipsilateral arm after radical mastectomy or in other settings of longstanding lymphoedema {22682884; 21518378; 32829071; 23024552}.

Clinical features

Early lesions can resemble ecchymoses/haemangiomas, non-vascular tumours, or inflammatory diseases. With progression they often develop into erythematous to violaceous plaques/nodules {14534889; 32829071}. Radiation-associated angiosarcomas have an average latency of approximately 5–8 years {15166670; 16006792; 32043616}. Lymphoedema-associated lesions have an average latency of > 10 years {26617830; 22682884; 26312725}.

Epidemiology

Cutaneous angiosarcoma usually arises in elderly patients on sun-damaged skin of the head and neck {21190880; 26276214; 28916596; 32829071}. It also occurs after surgery and radiation therapy, usually for breast cancer, and in the setting of chronic lymphoedema {15166670; 16006792; 22682884}.

Etiology

Most primary cutaneous angiosarcomas are probably related to chronic sun damage, whereas secondary angiosarcomas are most commonly related to radiation or chronic lymphoedema {32829071; 15166670; 16006792; 22682884}.

Pathogenesis

Angiosarcomas are genetically heterogeneous. Most (primary and secondary) cases harbour complex genetic profiles, lacking recurrent chromosomal changes and containing copy-number alterations and mutations encoding angiogenic factors or endothelial receptors {20217954; 25231439; 19723655}. There is also upregulation of genes related to angiogenesis and vascular-specific receptor tyrosine kinases (including TIE1, TEK, KDR [encoding VEGFR2], and FLT4 [encoding VEGFR3]) compared with other sarcomas {19723655; 18041747}. Some alterations are more frequent in radiation-associated angiosarcomas, but there is some overlap with primary neoplasms {19723655; 20949568}. Angiosarcoma of the head, neck, face, and scalp is associated with a high tumour mutation burden and a dominant ultraviolet (UV) radiation damage mutation signature, which suggests that for the subset of patients in this setting, UV radiation damage may be a causative factor and that immune checkpoint inhibition may be beneficial {32042194}. High-level MYC gene amplifications (at 8q24) are present in almost all angiosarcomas associated with irradiation and chronic lymphoedema, and rarely (< 10%) in primary angiosarcomas (usually of the breast and bone) {20949568; 20008140; 24329959; 22121953}; MYC encodes a transcription factor that potently affects cell proliferation and has proangiogenic functions via miR-17–92 cluster activation {16878133; 34958509}. Less frequently, secondary angiosarcomas are associated with coamplification of FLT4 (encoding VEGFR3) at 5q35 {20949568} and MAML1 (a NOTCH pathway effector); these coexist with MYC amplifications {22383169; 34958509}. Recurrent somatic mutations involving angiogenic signalling pathways (e.g. in KDR, PTPRB, and PLCG1) have been described in approximately 40% {10815805; 19451274; 24633157}. Rare mutations also occur in RAS genes, PIK3CA, TP53, FLT4, and TIE1 {19723655; 22648906; 24633157; 34958509}. A small subset of angiosarcomas are associated with CIC gene abnormalities; these typically occur in younger patients and have inferior disease-free survival {26735859}. Recurrent gene fusions are extremely rare in angiosarcoma, with primary CIC-rearranged angiosarcoma documented in 3 young adults, with tumours showing epithelioid to rhabdoid morphology and lacking vasoformation {26735859}; missense CIC mutations can occur with or without CIC fusions, typically arising in primary neoplasms {26735859; 34958509}.

Macroscopic appearance

Angiosarcomas are usually haemorrhagic masses and may be multinodular, with a variably well-circumscribed to infiltrative appearance {9630175}. Poorly differentiated tumours may have a fleshy appearance. The extent of the tumour is often difficult to appreciate on gross examination.

Histopathology

There is a wide range of histopathological features, and a given tumour may have a combination of different patterns {15166670; 16006792; 25330326; 18162773; 21164288; 32829071; 20175826; 7962828}. Classically, angiosarcoma is composed of architecturally complex vessels lined by hyperchromatic endothelial cells with multilayering of tumour cells in the vascular lumina. Tumours may also have back-to-back sieve-like vessels, a solid spindle cell pattern, epithelioid morphology, granular cell morphology, and a clear/foam cell appearance. In postradiation angiosarcoma, distinctive capillary lobule–like and radiation dermatitis–like patterns have been described {16121049; 28169467}.

Immunohistochemically, angiosarcoma is positive for CD31, CD34, FLI1, and ERG, and most are positive for podoplanin (D2-40) {7512718; 23518636; 21317715; 22716285}. Radiation- and lymphoedema-associated angiosarcomas are almost always positive for MYC, but can be occasionally negative {22121953; 21909081; 27780597}. Primary angiosarcomas are rarely positive for MYC {24091875} Radiation-induced angiosarcomas also have frequent loss of H3 p.K27me3 expression {28983701}. Epithelioid angiosarcomas may be positive for cytokeratin and EMA {21164288}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Radiation- and lymphoedema-associated angiosarcomas have amplification of MYC in > 90% of cases; FISH is a useful diagnostic technique {20949568; 21909081; 22121953; 24457083; 33128474}. A subset of radiation-induced angiosarcomas have FLT4 amplification {20949568}. Primary cutaneous angiosarcoma rarely has MYC amplification, and molecular testing plays no role in this setting {24091875}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: vasoformative channels with multilayering of atypical endothelial cells or sheet-like growth of atypical endothelial cells; nuclear atypia and mitotic activity; CD31 and ERG expression by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

All angiosarcomas are considered aggressive neoplasms, and current staging systems are not applicable.

Prognosis and prediction

Angiosarcomas are aggressive, with a high rate of local recurrence and metastasis (most commonly to the lungs, but also to lymph nodes). Radiation-induced angiosarcoma also has a risk of metastasis to the contralateral breast {15166670}. The prognosis of angiosarcoma is poor, with a 5-year survival rate of < 30% {28118230; 8635113}. Some studies have shown a worse prognosis with radiation-induced angiosarcoma {30718086}, while others have shown that its overall survival is superior to that of primary cutaneous angiosarcoma {31773514}. Features associated with adverse prognosis include age > 70 years, large tumour size, necrosis, greater tumour depth, and epithelioid morphology {18162773}.
