2.8.0.5 Uveal melanoma

Definition

Uveal melanomas are malignant melanocytic tumours arising in the uveal tract.

Related terminology

Not recommended: ocular melanoma.

Subtype(s)

Mixed epithelioid and spindle cell uveal melanoma; epithelioid cell uveal melanoma; spindle cell uveal melanoma, NOS; spindle cell uveal melanoma, type A; spindle cell uveal melanoma, type B; iris melanoma; ciliary body melanoma; choroidal melanoma

Localization

Uveal melanomas most commonly occur in the choroid, followed by the ciliary body and then the iris. They can infiltrate the anterior chamber angle, retina, vitreous, optic nerve, and extraocular tissues {18554722; 28240745; 32273508}.

Clinical features

For detailed descriptions of clinical features and investigations, see the relevant sections in the Eye and orbit tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Eye and orbit tumours [Internet; beta version ahead of print]. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2023. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 13). https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int/chapters/65.]].

Approximately 25% of uveal melanoma cases are asymptomatic and diagnosed during routine eye examination {25704938; 22503229}. Common symptoms of choroidal and ciliary body melanoma include photopsia (perceived flashes of light), floaters, and loss of vision {19606159; 32273508}. Occasionally, large lesions result in pain, redness of the eye, and secondary glaucoma {25704938}. Iris melanomas may present with a pigmented spot/nodule on the iris surface or diffuse colour change. They are also associated with displacement of the pupil, hyphaema, glaucoma due to angle infiltration, and (occasionally) extraocular extension {22370659}.

Multifocal uveal melanomas have been reported {29344499; 30675471}. Bilateral uveal melanoma occurs rarely {12855969; 17952820}.

Epidemiology

The worldwide incidence is estimated to be about 7000 cases per year [[Kivelä T. Incidence, prevalence and epidemiology of ocular melanoma. In: Murray TG, Boldt HC, editors. Ocular melanoma: advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. London (UK): Future Medicine; 2014. pp. 20–38.]], accounting for 5% of all primary melanomas {3279559}.

Uveal melanomas are most common in White populations (in which 65% of all cases are reported) and rare in individuals of Asian or African ancestry {3279559; 18191091; 8459526; 21704381}. They show no sex predilection {22079972}.

Etiology

Most uveal melanomas appear to develop de novo. Others develop from melanocytes in uveal naevi {11864894; 16650691; 16154197} or in naevus of Ota, all with a lifetime risk of 1 in 400 {9442799}. Dysplastic naevus syndrome {15763193; 19167086} and BAP1-related tumour predisposition syndrome {22935333; 9442799; 21874003} are associated with an increased risk of uveal melanoma. Other risk factors include light skin pigmentation, self-described inability to tan, and light-coloured irises {16401785}.

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure shows an association with iris melanomas {32415113; 33824477} but not with choroidal or ciliary body melanomas. Inherited mutations in the DNA-repair gene MBD4 increase the risk of uveal melanoma by increasing the mutation rate {29760383}.

Pathogenesis

Uveal melanomas arise from uveal melanocytes through mutations in the Gqα signalling pathway, affecting GNAQ and GNA11 in > 90% of cases, with the remaining tumours having mutation of the Gqα-coupled receptor CYSLTR2 or the downstream effector of Gqα/G11α phospholipase Cβ4 (encoded by PLCB4) {27123562; 21083380; 26683228}. Pathogenic GNAQ and GNA11 variants affect codons p.Q209 and (less commonly) p.R183; these lead to constitutive activation of the MAPK pathway and relay signals via PKC and the GTP-exchange factor RasGRP3, which is highly overexpressed in uveal melanoma {28486107} (see #36139Box 3.03).

Transformation to uveal melanoma is caused by additional genetic alterations in one of three genes: (1) biallelic inactivation of the ubiquitin carboxy-terminal hydrolase BAP1 on chromosome 3p21, typically by a combination of an inactivating mutation and monosomy 3, is associated with the worst prognosis {31563544}; (2) hotspot mutations in the splice factor SF3B1, typically at codon 625 {23313955}, result in mis-splicing of genes including BRD9, a member of the non-canonical BAF complex involved in epigenetic regulation {31597964}; (3) EIF1AX encodes a translation initiation factor {23793026}, and mutations cluster near the 5′ part of the gene.

Less frequently mutated genes of relevance include TP53 and RPL5, encoding an inhibitor of MDM2, which degrades p53 {32415113}. Metastases often show additional pathogenic variants in genes involved in the PI3K/AKT pathway, inactivation of CDKN2A, and activation of PRC2 {31253977} (see #36139Box 3.03).

Macroscopic appearance

Uveal melanomas are dome- or ring-shaped tumours. Choroidal melanomas may exhibit a flat to dome-shaped appearance when confined by Bruch membrane, or a collar-button or mushroom-like shape when they break through Bruch membrane.

Histopathology

Uveal melanomas appear as tumours comprising spindle A (naevus-like) cells, spindle B cells, epithelioid cells, or mixed (spindle B and epithelioid) cells {6624832}, with varying pigmentation. Epithelioid cell melanomas are associated with the worst prognosis, and spindle cell melanomas are associated with the best prognosis {15327098}. Other parameters are summarized in #36139Box 3.03 .

When reporting uveal melanoma, it is important to include the extent of the disease within and outside the eye, highlighting which structures are affected.

Immunohistochemistry

Uveal melanomas are typically immunoreactive for HMB45, S100, melan-A, MITF, tyrosinase, and SOX10 {17445277; 12742347; 11511840; 1762354}. Proliferative activity can be assessed using Ki-67 and phosphohistone H3 {8664054; 19900200}. A lack of nuclear immunostaining for BAP1 in uveal melanoma indicates a high risk of metastasis {25058347; 24633195; 27015033}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses include uveal tract naevi, bilateral diffuse melanocytic proliferation, choroidal metastases (predominantly carcinomas), haemangiomas, and rare pigment epithelial tumours.

Cytology

Transvitreal or transscleral FNAB may be performed to confirm the diagnosis, but it is usually undertaken for genetic prognostication {28596284; 27324683; 32273508}. The cytological specimen may contain cells with spindle-shaped cytoplasm or round cells with variable amounts of cytoplasm. Intracytoplasmic melanin pigment granules are often present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology mainly serves prognostic rather than diagnostic purposes. Copy-number and gene expression changes, as well as mutation status, can be useful in assessing the risk of metastasis {28399342; 35008260}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a melanocytic tumour arising in the uveal tract containing atypical spindle or epithelioid cells with round to oval nuclei and prominent nucleoli.

Desirable: positivity for at least one melanocytic marker.

Staging

Uveal melanomas are staged according to the latest Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification, which has separate staging for ciliary body and choroidal melanomas as distinct from iris melanomas [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]]. According to this system, choroidal and ciliary body melanomas are grouped by size as small (T1), medium (T2), large (T3), or very large (T4), and they are staged (stage I–IV) according to size, ciliary body involvement, extraocular extension, and presence of metastasis {23816968}. Iris melanomas are grouped as T1–T4 according to their invasion to adjacent tissues. Staging systems also exist for newly diagnosed metastatic uveal melanoma {27296487; 25780931; 31234340; 34999237}.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognostic clinical, anatomical, histomorphological, and genetic parameters for uveal melanoma are summarized in #36139Box 3.03 {18342942; 28240745; 28828481; 30278339}.

As many as 50% of patients with uveal melanoma ultimately die from haematogenous metastasis, most commonly (in 90% of cases) to the liver {11346394; 14578381}. The median survival time after detection of metastasis is 10–12 months, and the survival rate decreases from 52% at 1 year to 25% at 2 years and 13% at 3 years {30664106; 31150059; 34999237}.
