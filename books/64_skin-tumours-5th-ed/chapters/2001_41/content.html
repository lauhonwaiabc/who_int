2.0.0.1 Melanocytic neoplasms: Introduction

Primary melanomas on the skin have traditionally been classified by histopathological and clinical features based on contemporaneous proposals by McGovern (1970) {5520514} and Clark et al. (1969) {5773814}. Recent advances in cataloguing genetic alterations in melanoma have confirmed the existence of biologically distinct melanoma subtypes and added nuance to their distinguishing features. An abundance of somatic mutations with tell-tale signatures have identified ultraviolet (UV) radiation from natural and artificial sources as the main mutagen for most melanomas arising on sun-exposed skin. In contrast, some melanomas, mainly those arising on sun-shielded sites such as the palms, soles, nail apparatus (acral melanoma), and uveal tract of the eye (uveal melanoma), as well as those on mucosal membranes (mucosal melanoma), lack molecular signatures of UV radiation. Acral and mucosal melanomas stand out in that they instead have clustered copy-number changes with high-level amplifications and structural rearrangements, which arise early in their evolution. This points to intrinsic mutational mechanisms related to telomere dysfunction or defective double-stranded DNA repair {16291983; 28467829; 32025007} as underlying mechanisms. Finally, uveal melanomas have a very low burden of point mutations and comparatively few copy-number and structural rearrangements. Additional patterns have emerged that point to biologically different melanoma subtypes with distinct sets of driver mutations. Melanomas on the more heavily sun-exposed skin paradoxically develop later in life than those on intermittently sun-exposed skin. However, the two melanoma subgroups differ in the pattern of driver oncogenes, indicating that they represent biologically distinct subtypes. These two subtypes likely arise from different melanocyte sublineages with different vulnerabilities for oncogenic transformation {16291983; 16572428; 14679157; 12783935}.

The differences in the mutational mechanisms and the frequency of alterations of certain genes in particular types of melanomas have helped refine the conceptual basis for the classification of melanocytic neoplasms beyond clinical and histopathological criteria. The integration of new genetic data with existing information has clearly demonstrated that melanoma is not a single disease but a group of distinct subtypes that differ in their genetic features, clinical and histopathological presentation, age of onset, preferential anatomical site, and cell of origin. All melanomas have in common the genetic disruption of multiple signalling pathways caused by combinations of activating mutations in oncogenes and inactivation of tumour suppressor genes, indicating that they develop mostly through stepwise tumour progression.

The evolution of individual melanoma subtypes can be traced backwards to their respective precursor lesions, using shared somatic mutations. Benign naevi already show oncogenic alterations, mainly in the MAPK pathway, defining them as clonal proliferations of partially transformed melanocytes {2682463; 10980135; 12447372; 30772300}. Their MAPK pathway mutations are sufficient for naevus formation and represent initiating mutations. The morphological differences among different naevi in part reflect the differences in the oncogenic signalling caused by these mutations. However, they probably also reflect differences among the cell states and sublineages of the melanocyte of origin, in which the initiating mutation arose. At least two different developmental pathways send melanocyte precursors from the neural crest, via different routes, to their destination tissues, generating stem cell populations residing in different niches that have distinct vulnerabilities for transformation. For example, melanocytic neoplasms in the uveal, blue, and leptomeningeal pathway probably arise from the melanocyte subpopulation derived from the ventromedial development pathway, which also generates Schwann cell precursors {19837037}. This may explain the propensity of these melanocytic neoplasms to harbour Gqα pathway mutations {21083380}. Furthermore, melanocytes on volar skin have gene expression patterns that distinguish them from those in the hair-bearing skin {34475532}.

Genetic studies have revealed that certain melanocytic neoplasms with atypical features often harbour additional pathogenic mutations, indicating that they have evolved beyond the initial stage of tumour development but not reached the malignant state. Many of these are biphasic or asymmetrical, reflecting more than a single clonal expansion. Some of these “intermediate” lesions have characteristic clinicopathological features, such as pigmented epithelioid, WNT-activated, and BAP1-inactivated melanocytomas. All of these examples are genetically characterized as “intermediate” between naevus and melanoma because they carry pathogenic mutations in addition to the initiating mutation, which is the sole alteration found in histopathologically bland-appearing naevi. The secondary alterations found in these intermediate lesions alter specific signalling pathways that vary between the histopathological types and therefore probably contribute to their characteristic clinicopathological features.

Based on these molecular findings, neoplasms that harbour the defining genetic alterations or the characteristic histopathological features associated with these alterations have been renamed as BAP1-inactivated, Spitz, and WNT-activated melanocytomas.

By convention, histopathologically bland naevi, genetically defined as those harbouring only a single mutation, are considered “benign”. This connotes that their risk of progression to melanoma is very low, estimated as 1 in 200 000 {12622618}. The progression risk of the above-described melanocytomas is probably still low but is estimated to be as much as two orders of magnitude higher. To harmonize the nomenclature, the WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board has adopted the term “melanocytoma”, proposed by Zembowicz and Scolyer (2011) {21366452}, to designate genetically intermediate lesions, while restricting the term “naevus” to lesions with features of those demonstrated to have only the initiating mutation and lacking additional pathogenic mutations. We have opted to keep the term “uveal melanocytoma” out of tradition, although this tumour has all the defining features of a naevus. The diagnosis of melanocytoma in the skin should be paired with a classifier that specifies the specific type of melanocytoma, such as WNT-activated melanocytoma. Definitive treatment guidelines to guide excision margins remain to be developed, as do recommendations for re-excision of partially biopsied melanocytoma.

Progress in cataloguing the recurrent pathogenic alterations in various melanoma subtypes and their associated precursor lesions led to a classification schema of melanocytic neoplasms that integrates genetic alterations, clinicopathological characteristics, and epidemiological features to separate melanoma subtypes in one dimension, with their respective precursor lesions ordered by their evolutionary state (e.g. naevus followed by melanocytoma) in the other dimension {24460190}. This schema was adopted as the basis for the 2018 WHO classification of melanocytic neoplasms, integrating the terms and concepts of prior classifications {32057276}. The established terms “superficial spreading melanoma”, “lentigo maligna melanoma”, “acral melanoma”, and “nodular melanoma” were preserved and grouped as best as possible, with the categories supported by molecular features. Clark et al. also introduced the concept that superficial spreading and lentigo maligna melanomas can be further divided into those in the radial (or horizontal) growth phase (RGP) and those in the vertical growth phase (VGP). This distinction has clinical implications, because RGP melanomas have a good prognosis, and transition to the VGP is associated with metastatic potential. Nodular melanoma was defined as a primary melanoma in the VGP, with no identifiable RGP {5773814}. To date, no specific genetic alterations distinguishing VGP-only melanomas from those with an RGP have been identified, and VGP-only melanomas can be found within the various molecularly supported categories defined by molecular criteria. Therefore, nodular melanomas were listed as special clinicopathological presentations in both the low- and high-CSD (cumulative sun damage) categories introduced below, which has been continued in the current edition. Melanoma in situ (i.e. melanoma confined to the epithelium – epidermis or mucosa) is the earliest progression stage for most melanomas and does not constitute a melanoma subtype per se.

The current edition distinguishes nine melanoma types: three with UV radiation as the major mutagen (low-CSD melanoma, high-CSD melanoma, and desmoplastic melanoma) and six without clear relation to UV radiation (acral melanoma, mucosal melanoma, uveal melanoma, melanoma associated with a congenital naevus, melanoma associated with a blue naevus, and Spitz melanoma).

Melanomas caused by UV radiation exposure

Melanomas arising in sun-exposed skin are most common in individuals with light skin tone and poor tanning ability. Clinical, epidemiological, and genomic observations have identified the degree of sun exposure as a determinant of melanoma subtype {16291983; 16572428; 14679157; 12783935}. Melanomas on sun-exposed skin with a low or moderate amount of solar elastosis have genetic, histopathological, and clinical characteristics different from those of melanomas on skin with marked solar elastosis. Solar elastosis probably reflects the total cumulative dose of exposure to UV radiation in the context of the shielding and repair capacity of a given individual’s skin. Two families, low-CSD and high-CSD melanomas, are distinguished based on the degree of solar elastosis.

Low-CSD melanoma (including superficial spreading melanoma and low-CSD nodular melanoma) is defined by low to moderate solar elastosis in the surrounding skin (individual elastotic fibres are still identifiable rather than forming amorphous aggregates). It typically arises on the intermittently sun-exposed skin (back, proximal extremities) of individuals aged < 55 years. Exposure to UV radiation from the sun or from tanning beds is another risk factor {20669232}. Histopathologically, there is nested proliferation and pagetoid scatter of large neoplastic melanocytes with dusty cytoplasmic melanin pigmentation in the RGP. Genomically, BRAF p.V600E mutations predominate {14679157}. Low-CSD melanomas commonly arise from common or dysplastic naevi through the acquisition of additional pathogenic genetic alterations such as inactivation of CDKN2A and promoter mutations of TERT, resulting from UV irradiation of the naevus cell population. A subset of nodular melanomas probably also falls into this category, most likely arising as a pure VGP due to rapid transition through an RGP that is no longer recognizable or through an inverse progression cascade in which usually later-occurring mutations are already present when the proliferation-inducing mutation in the MAPK pathway takes place {27125352}. The existence of such mutations has recently been demonstrated in normal-appearing melanocytes in sun-exposed skin {33029006}.

High-CSD melanoma (including lentigo maligna melanoma and high-CSD nodular melanoma) typically arises on the chronically sun-exposed skin (face, distal extremities) of individuals aged > 55 years. It is defined histopathologically by severe solar elastosis (amorphous aggregates of elastotic material) in the adjacent dermis. Neoplastic melanocytes within the epidermis assume a “lentiginous” (predominantly basilar single-cell) pattern and are smaller, with minimal cytoplasm and less pigment. Patients often have other neoplasms associated with chronic sun exposure as well, such as actinic keratoses and squamous cell carcinoma {16572428}. Pathogenic alterations include BRAF p.V600K and other non-p.V600E mutations, biallelic NF1 inactivation, and NRAS and KIT mutations {16908931; 26214590; 22535154}. These melanomas often originate from melanoma in situ.

Desmoplastic melanoma is a less frequent tumour type that presents as an intradermal sarcomatoid spindle cell proliferation, which may arise in an RGP of lentigo maligna type or de novo. Most lesions occur on severely sun-damaged skin and have a very high mutation burden, with a very strong UV radiation signature. Inactivating NF1 mutations are the most common driver mutations, often combined with diverse other activating mutations in the MAPK pathway {26343386; 26076063}. Rarely, desmoplastic melanomas can arise on skin without severe elastosis, or even in the context of acral or mucosal melanoma. These melanomas are expected to share the genomic features of melanomas in their respective pathways.

Melanomas arising at sun-shielded sites or without known etiological association with UV radiation exposure

Spitz melanoma (malignant Spitz tumour) is the malignant end of a spectrum of biologically related tumours, with Spitz naevi at the benign end and Spitz melanocytoma (atypical Spitz tumour) as an intermediate category. Histologically, the lesions are composed of large spindle and/or epithelioid melanocytes {367281}. Spitz tumours occur in a younger age group than most other melanomas, and Spitz melanomas tend to have a better prognosis than conventional melanomas of similar stage. Solar elastosis is usually absent or minimal, and mutation signatures do not implicate UV radiation exposure as a significant contributor. Spitz tumours have distinctive genetic alterations, which include initiating mutations in HRAS or kinase fusions of ROS1, NTRK1, NTRK3, ALK, BRAF, MET, MAP3K8, and RET {10980135; 24445538; 26013381; 31498175}. They progress to melanomas through genetic inactivation of CDKN2A and acquisition of TERT promoter mutations {26061100; 23388126}. The genetic criteria to delineate Spitz melanocytoma (atypical Spitz tumour) from Spitz melanoma remain to be firmly established, but loss of CDKN2A, typically by homozygous deletion, does not appear to be sufficient for melanoma formation {26061100}. The term “spitzoid” can be applied to tumours that resemble Spitz tumours morphologically but lack the defining molecular characteristics. Many such “spitzoid” tumours harbour BRAF mutations {34753863} and thus do not fall into the Spitz category.

Acral melanoma (including nodular melanoma in acral skin) is defined as melanoma arising on the glabrous skin or nail apparatus. There is no apparent etiological relationship with sun exposure, and chronic trauma has been suggested as a risk factor {24067997; 27305207}. Solar elastosis is typically absent. Acral melanomas have distinctive genetic alterations such as clustered focused high-level amplifications, deletions, and structural rearrangements {16291983; 28467829; 33007263}. They also have a comparatively low point-mutation burden and only infrequently show a UV radiation signature. Recurrent gene alterations include amplifications of CCND1, TERT, CDK4, GAB2, and MDM2; mutations in KIT, NRAS, and NF1; and fusions of ALK, RET, NTRK3, or other kinases {16908931; 16291983; 30657954; 23751074; 27864876}. The earliest progression stages are melanoma in situ (which can be very subtle in its early phases), preceded by histopathologically difficult or impossible to detect fields of partially transformed melanocytes (field cells), which already harbour the characteristic amplifications {10766187; 39034322}. Most acral melanomas have a lentiginous RGP, whereas the VGP may be composed of spindle cells or relatively small ovoid cells and may be desmoplastic. Nodular melanoma in acral skin may share attributes with the VGP of acral melanoma. A subset of acral melanomas (< 20%) show genetic alterations similar to those of low-CSD melanoma (in that they have BRAF p.V600E mutations) and lack the complex structural rearrangements and focused amplifications found in most acral melanomas {16291983}. It is likely that the lesions in this subset represent low-CSD melanomas on acral skin, with some also arising from naevi {33978681}.

Mucosal melanoma has no etiological relationship with sun exposure, and like acral melanoma it shows a low mutation burden with frequent clustered amplifications, deletions, and structural rearrangements {16291983}. It arises in lentiginous proliferations of melanocytes or overt melanoma in situ, or de novo. The VGP may be spindled or ovoid cells and may be desmoplastic. KIT, NRAS, NF1, and SPRED1 mutations have been described in a substantial proportion of tumours (varying by site), and BRAF mutations are uncommon {16908931; 23620124; 20457136; 30385465}.

Melanoma arising in congenital naevus occurs within a pre-existing naevus. Congenital naevi are caused by somatic mutations in the MAPK pathway, typically mutations of NRAS or mutations or fusions of BRAF, which arise in utero or shortly after birth. A small subset can progress to melanoma, often in childhood. Although sun exposure is not involved in naevus formation, it may play a role in the transformation of congenital naevi to melanoma, as has been shown for other naevi {26559571}. NRAS mutation is the predominant somatic event in giant congenital naevi {24129063}, as well as in the melanomas that arise therein {16888631; 25268584}. The genetic profile of small and medium congenital naevi is different, with a higher proportion of cases bearing BRAF mutations {16888631; 24129063}.

Melanoma arising in blue naevus occurs within a pre-existing naevus. Blue naevi are caused by mutations activating the Gqα signalling pathway, mainly mutations in GNAQ and GNA11 {19078957; 21083380}. Transformation occurs through progression mutations similar to those in uveal melanoma, through biallelic loss of BAP1, and/or through change-of-function mutations in SF3B1 and EIF1AX {26645730; 28409567}. The role of UV radiation exposure in initiation and progression is difficult to assess, because comprehensive analyses are lacking.

Uveal melanoma arises from melanocytes in the choroid of the eye. It is caused by activating mutations in the Gqα pathway, initiated by mutations of GNAQ, GNA11, PLCB4, or CYSLTR2 {19078957; 21083380; 26683228; 27089179}. Progression to melanoma occurs through additional mutations affecting BAP1, SF3B1, or EIF1AX {21051595; 23793026}. Uveal melanomas share these genetic features with primary leptomeningeal melanocytic tumours {25315378}, blue naevi, and melanomas associated with blue naevi.

Primary melanoma arising in the CNS belongs to the broader category of primary meningeal melanocytic tumours, which can present as neoplasms originating either from leptomeningeal melanocytes or by colonization of the leptomeninges by partially transformed melanoblasts during embryonic development in the context of giant congenital naevi with neurocutaneous melanosis. Circumscribed primary meningeal melanocytic tumours are caused by mutations in the Gqα pathway, involving GNAQ, GNA11, PLCB4, or CYSLTR2 in a mutually exclusive fashion. Progression to melanoma occurs through mutations of EIF1AX, SF3B1, or BAP1 {27060446; 26744134; 29891723}, similarly to uveal melanoma and melanoma associated with blue naevus.

Meningeal melanocytic neoplasms in children typically arise as diffuse tumours in the context of giant congenital naevi and neurocutaneous melanosis, and they most commonly harbour NRAS mutations, with a minority showing BRAF mutations or fusions {25487565; 28253523; 23392294; 23303902; 25490715}.

Alternative classification schemata

Disease classification can be carried out along different axes, such as histomorphology, genetic features, organ involvement, and so on. The above melanoma subtypes are distinguished based on a combination of clinical characteristics, histopathological features, operant mutational mechanisms, and somatic mutations, creating opportunity for overlap and ambiguity. Two alternative classification schemata also deserve mentioning, with a proposal for how to project them on the schema outlined above:

Paediatric melanomas are a heterogeneous group of different melanoma subtypes, including melanomas arising de novo or associated with congenital naevi, melanomas associated with blue naevi, Spitz melanoma, and (particularly in adolescents) low-CSD melanoma {8625056; 27956274}. Congenital melanomas can be due to metastasis of a maternal melanoma of any type or a melanoma associated with congenital naevi type, or they can arise de novo; there is little information on the molecular alterations underlying the rare de novo type. Prepubertal primary melanomas often originate from congenital naevi or arise de novo. UV radiation exposure plays no major role, and BRAF mutations are uncommon {25268584; 28895292}. In contrast, postpubertal melanomas are often of the low-CSD subtype, with common BRAF p.V600E mutations.

Conjunctival melanomas arise from the bulbar or palpebral conjunctiva of the eye. They do not represent a distinct disease entity but rather a biologically and genetically heterogeneous group. Their genomic features indicate that they comprise the equivalent of low-CSD melanoma (with frequent BRAF p.V600E mutations, often combined with TERT promoter mutations) or high-CSD melanoma (with NF1 and NRAS mutations, often combined with TERT promoter mutations), mucosal melanomas (with mutations in KIT and ATRX), or melanoma arising in blue naevus or uveal melanoma (with mutations in GNAQ or GNA11 combined with mutations in BAP1, SF3B1, or EIF1AX) {35085662}. They can arise from precursor lesions in the respective pathways, including conjunctival naevi, primary acquired melanosis, or blue naevi.

Prognostic classification

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging model includes tumour thickness, ulceration, and microscopic satellites {19917835} [[Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. Other attributes with prognostic relevance include mitotic rate per mm2, tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, lymphovascular invasion (especially lymphatic invasion), neurotropism, regression, and (to a lesser extent) Clark level of invasion and site of the primary. Localized primary melanomas constitute TNM stages I and II; regional metastasis defines stage III, and distant metastasis defines stage IV. Stage 0 constitutes melanoma in situ, which has no capability to metastasize. Invasive primary melanomas are staged from pT1 to pT4 and assigned to the respective pathological stage as shown in #33780Table 3.01 {29923435}.

Diagnostic reproducibility

Despite best efforts to provide clear criteria for diagnosis, pathologists use diagnostic terms differently depending on individual, local, and regional practices {15370112}. The reproducibility of diagnoses between pathologists is poor, especially for intermediate lesions and melanocytic neoplasms of low malignant potential {28659278}. This can result in uncertainty as to the most appropriate management, especially when patients are referred between centres with different practice patterns. The WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board supports the use of descriptive terminology for lesions that are characterized by conflicting criteria. “Superficial atypical melanocytic proliferation of uncertain significance (SAMPUS)” and “intraepidermal atypical melanocytic proliferation of uncertain significance (IAMPUS)” are terms for lesions in which RGP or in situ melanoma, respectively, is in the differential diagnosis. In this context, “of uncertain significance” means with risk primarily of local regrowth and further progression. The term “melanocytic tumour of uncertain malignant potential (MELTUMP)” can be used for thicker lesions whose differential diagnosis includes VGP melanoma as a leading consideration (see the definitions listed in #38251Box 3.01). “Malignant potential” in this context means risk of metastasis and potential death from disease even after complete excision. It is recommended that the above terms for intermediate or indeterminate lesions be used with reference to the respective pathway; for example, “MELTUMP intermediate between blue naevus and melanoma arising in blue naevus”. For clarity, the acronyms specified above should always be spelled out in formal reports. In general, the treatment of these cases should be planned with the differential diagnosis of melanoma taken into consideration, and staging attributes should be supplied {15370112}. In the ICD-O classification, benign neoplasms are coded /0, intermediate neoplasms (including SAMPUS, IAMPUS, and MELTUMP) are coded /1, melanocytic neoplasms of low malignant potential and melanomas in situ are coded /2, and invasive melanomas are coded /3.

Management-based classification

One of the most important uses of a diagnosis is to guide management. The specific diagnosis is particularly important for melanomas, but it also guides the management of intermediate lesions that have been incompletely excised; in general, these lesions should be completely removed to enable full histological evaluation and to minimize the potential for local persistence, recurrence, and progression. The accuracy and reproducibility of the diagnosis of intermediate lesions is improved by the use of molecular markers but remains challenging using current criteria, and there is considerable variation in the use of terminology across institutions {28659278}. The revised Melanocytic Pathology Assessment Tool and Hierarchy for Diagnosis (MPATH-Dx version 2.0) schema {36630138} has been developed in an effort to reduce uncertainty and promote consistent management practices, and the efficacy of the previous version has been evaluated {28659278; 27189823; 24176521}. This schema proposes categorizing each case into one of four classes to guide treatment options (see #38353Table 3.02). These are general guidelines that should be interpreted in accordance with local and national standards, and clinical factors should also be considered. In most countries, there are formal evidence-based national guidelines for classes III and IV (the melanomas) {27059193}, but not for classes I and II. Therefore, these guidelines do not impact the current recommendations for management of melanomas but are intended to guide the management of benign and intermediate tumours.
