2.2.1.2 Desmoplastic melanoma

Definition

Desmoplastic melanoma is composed of spindled neoplastic melanocytes within a desmoplastic or fibrous stroma.

ICD-O coding

8745/3 Desmoplastic melanoma

8745/3 Pure desmoplastic melanoma

8745/3 Mixed (combined) desmoplastic melanoma

ICD-11 coding

2C30.Y & XH1Z15 Other specified melanoma of skin & Desmoplastic melanoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: neurotropic melanoma; desmoplastic neurotropic melanoma.

Subtype(s)

Pure desmoplastic melanoma; mixed (combined) desmoplastic melanoma

Localization

Desmoplastic melanoma most commonly involves chronically sun-exposed skin of the head and neck region. In men, the ears and bald scalp are commonly affected. It can also occur at sites with minimal or no evidence of ultraviolet (UV) radiation–induced damage, such as acral {7812919} and subungual sites {25307258}, and the oral {8697380}, nasal {35257324}, and genital {15840097} mucosa.

Clinical features

Most cases arise as a painless indurated plaque or poorly demarcated area of scar-like skin thickening on the sun-exposed skin of patients aged > 65 years. Almost half of cases lack pigmentation and are often mistaken for a scar, basal cell carcinoma, or dermatofibroma. Lesions arising from lentigo maligna can show superficial pigmentation.

Epidemiology

Desmoplastic melanomas account for 1–4% of melanomas in light-skinned individuals. The M:F ratio is 1.75:1. The median age at diagnosis (~65 years) is slightly older for desmoplastic melanoma than for the most common melanoma subtypes {31186784}.

Etiology

UV irradiation is implicated as the predominant etiological agent by clinical, epidemiological, and genomic findings, including the common localization of desmoplastic melanoma in severely sun-damaged skin, the very high mutation load, and UV radiation mutation signatures {30930085}.

Pathogenesis

Most desmoplastic melanomas have an exceedingly high mutation burden (median: > 60 mutations/Mb) with a strong UV radiation signature, implicating a melanocyte situated in a superficial location as the cell of origin {26343386}. Desmoplastic melanomas typically lack strongly activating mutations in the MAPK pathway, such as BRAF p.V600E and NRAS p.Q61R/K mutations. Instead, 55% of cases harbour biallelic inactivation of NF1 combined with a diverse array of other mutations that activate the MAPK pathway, including amplification of receptor tyrosine kinase genes (e.g. EGFR, MET, and ERBB2) and inactivation of the tumour suppressor gene CBL {26343386; 26076063}. Mutations in the RB1 pathway are also common, primarily (i.e. in 47% of cases) affecting CDKN2A, and 48% of cases have TP53 mutations.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

The dermal-based paucicellular tumours contain spindle-shaped melanocytes (which often resemble fibroblasts and are usually non-pigmented) in and between abundant fine, sclerotic or mature collagen bundles. The stroma often has a fibromyxoid appearance {15370114}. The distribution of spindle cells is usually haphazard, but the cells occasionally form parallel bundles or storiform areas. The spindle cells often extend into the subcutis diffusely or in fibrous bands and may involve fascia or fibrous septa of the subcutis {15731577}. The overlying epidermis is usually thin and atrophic, and the adjacent superficial dermis usually shows severe solar elastosis. There are accompanying aggregates of lymphocytes. The spindle cells are minimally to moderately pleomorphic, with scattered cells with larger or more hyperchromatic nuclei. Paucicellular tumours are easily missed on punch and shave biopsies, and more-cellular tumours may be mistaken for a scar or a neurofibroma. Lentigo maligna or an atypical epidermal melanocytic proliferation may be seen in the overlying epidermis. Neurotropism is present in about 30% of cases {28731051}, with tumour cells surrounding nerves in a circumferential fashion (perineural invasion) or by direct invasion of the endoneurium of nerves (intraneural invasion). Occasionally, desmoplastic melanoma may form nerve-like structures (neural transformation), which is regarded by some as a form of neurotropism. There rarely is an associated common acquired naevus. Vascular invasion is rare. Combined desmoplastic melanomas, defined as desmoplastic melanomas with foci of non-desmoplastic melanoma, have a worse prognosis {15489657; 15827808}.

Immunohistochemistry

The spindle cells are positive for S100, SOX10, and p75-NGFR, although only a few nuclei may be positive. HMB45 and melan-A staining is usually negative, except in the non-desmoplastic areas of combined desmoplastic melanomas. SMA and CD10 may be positive. PRAME is positive in one third of cases {30045064}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes desmoplastic naevus, which can also have perineural extension but lacks asymmetry, mitoses, nuclear atypia, and lymphoid infiltrates, and is usually positive for melan-A. Desmoplastic Spitz naevi may have many HMB45-negative spindle cells but are usually symmetrical, have epidermal thickening, include at least a few plump cells, and have rare or absent mitoses. Sclerosing cellular blue naevi, which are most frequent on the scalp, also lack mitoses and are diffusely HMB45-positive. Desmoplastic change may also occur in other melanoma types; however, they usually express melanocytic markers such as HMB45 and melan-A, at least focally. Immature scars, especially in re-excision specimens, may resemble desmoplastic melanoma and may have some spindle cells that are positive for S100 and SOX10, as well as foci of lymphocytes and mitoses. Other differential diagnoses include dermatofibroma, atypical fibroxanthoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, and leiomyosarcoma {24618614}; these can usually be distinguished by morphology and immunohistochemistry.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are usually not required to establish the diagnosis. Mutations in NF1 and a high mutation burden support the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant neoplasm with spindled melanocytes in a desmoplastic stroma; expression of S100 and SOX10; no expression of melan-A, HMB45, or tyrosinase except in desmoplastic areas; for pure desmoplastic melanoma: > 90% of tumour consists of a diffuse low-density proliferation of spindle cells in a desmoplastic stroma; for mixed desmoplastic melanoma: ≥ 10% of compact aggregates or nodules of melanoma cells without intervening fibrosis.

Desirable: presence of prominent solar elastosis in dermis; presence an associated atypical intraepidermal melanocytic proliferation or melanoma in situ; presence of melanin pigment in a few tumour cells; cytological atypia of melanocytes; evidence of mitotic activity; accompanying aggregates of lymphocytes within and/or at the edge of the tumour; neurotropism; exclusion of other desmoplastic spindle cell neoplasms.

Staging

Desmoplastic melanoma is staged according to the current Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification for cutaneous melanoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Desmoplastic melanomas have a better prognosis than other melanoma subtypes of similar tumour thickness. The prognosis worsens with increased tumour thickness, mitotic rate, and age; male sex is also associated with a poorer prognosis {20564101}. Local recurrences are common, in particular when neurotropism is present {18823042}. Lymph node metastasis is less frequent in pure desmoplastic melanoma than in other subtypes, with lung metastasis as the most common site of metastasis.
