6.0.0.6 BAP1-related tumour predisposition syndrome

Definition

BRCA1-associated deubiquitinase 1 (BAP1)-related tumour predisposition syndrome (BAP1-TPDS) is a hereditary, autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by germline mutations in the BAP1 gene.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

614327 Tumour predisposition syndrome 1; TPDS1

ICD-11 coding

Not available

Related terminology

Acceptable: BAP1 loss familial cancer syndrome; BAP1 hereditary cancer syndrome.

Not recommended: tumour predisposition syndrome; cutaneous/ocular melanoma, atypical melanocytic proliferations, and other internal neoplasms (COMMON) syndrome; BAP1 loss familial cancer syndrome; BAP1 hereditary cancer syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Individuals carrying pathogenic germline variants of BAP1 are at increased risk of developing various tumour types, most commonly BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi/tumours, uveal melanoma, cutaneous melanoma, mesothelioma, renal cell carcinoma, and basal cell carcinoma.

Clinical features

A common manifestation of BAP1-TPDS is multiple BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi/tumours. Beginning in the second decade of life, affected patients frequently develop multiple inconspicuous, skin-coloured to reddish-brown, 4–12 mm, dome-shaped, well-circumscribed papules, predominantly on the sun-exposed skin. Some of these BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi/tumours display small brown areas at their periphery, probably because they evolve from conventional naevi. The number of lesions varies, typically ranging from a few to > 50 {21874003}.

Individuals with pathogenic BAP1 germline mutations have an increased risk of developing uveal melanoma, peritoneal and pleural mesothelioma, cutaneous melanoma, and clear cell renal cell carcinomas {30517737}. In addition to these main tumour types, patients may develop basal cell carcinoma {25080371}, meningioma {21941004}, cholangiocarcinoma {24185509}, and hepatocellular carcinoma {31862487}, and probably other cancer types as well, including leptomeningeal melanoma {25900292}, lung adenocarcinoma {21941004}, thyroid cancer {26774355}, paraganglioma {22889334}, and bladder cancer {32218990}. Compared with the general population, patients with BAP1-TPDS have a younger average age at tumour onset.

Epidemiology

BAP1-TPDS is a rare hereditary cancer syndrome. Approximately 200 affected families, with 140 unique BAP1 germline variants, have been reported in the literature {30517737}.

Etiology

BAP1-TPDS is caused by heterozygous germline mutations in the tumour suppressor gene BAP1, located on chromosome 3p21. Inactivation of BAP1 may occur due to loss-of-function mutations altering the BAP1 nucleotide sequence or due to chromosomal deletions involving the BAP1 locus. The mutations are most commonly deleterious nonsense, frameshift, or splice-site mutations that result in a truncated, non-functional protein (null variants), or deleterious missense mutations that interfere with the ubiquitin hydrolase function of BAP1 {23277170}. Interestingly, BAP1 germline mutations have also been reported in neurodevelopmental disorder (Kury–Isidor syndrome; MIM number: 619762) {35051358}.

Pathogenesis

BAP1 encodes a deubiquitinase and acts as a tumour suppressor in multiprotein complexes. BAP1 is involved in numerous cellular processes, including protein deubiquitination {9528852}, DNA damage repair {24347639}, cell-cycle regulation {21484256}, chromatin remodelling {20436459}, transcriptional regulation {20805357}, and apoptosis {28614305}.

Histopathology

BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi/tumours are predominantly intradermal melanocytic neoplasms, with occasional involvement of the junctional epidermis. The majority of lesions consist of an admixture of small naevoid cells, which are seen in conventional naevi, as well as larger epithelioid cells. The epithelioid melanocytes show varying degrees of atypia, ranging from naevoid, slightly epithelioid melanocytes with minimal atypia to very large, epithelioid cells with well-defined cytoplasmic borders, large amounts of amphophilic cytoplasm, and pleomorphic vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli. Some tumours display marked atypical features, such as nuclear pleomorphism, high cellularity, or increased mitotic activity; cases of metastasis have been described {34857909}. These findings suggest a histological and clinical spectrum ranging from lesions with minimal atypia and benign clinical behaviour (BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevus) to lesions with severe atypia and uncertain clinical behaviour (BAP1-inactivated melanocytoma).

The mitotic activity is usually low. Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes are frequently seen {23277170; 22367297; 34857909}. In most lesions, the wildtype BAP1 allele is inactivated (by various somatic alterations), which usually leads to a lack of nuclear BAP1 expression by immunohistochemistry. Most lesions also harbour mutations activating the MAPK pathway, most commonly BRAF p.V600E mutations, or rarely NRAS mutations or RAF1 fusion {22367297; 34857909}.

Cytology

Cytology is not relevant for most skin tumours. See individual tumour types for details where this is used.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Tumours associated with BAP1-TPDS usually exhibit biallelic loss of BAP1, resulting in loss of nuclear BAP1 expression by immunohistochemistry {23277170}. Somatic inactivation of BAP1 and loss of BAP1 protein expression can also occur in sporadic tumours, and the diagnosis of BAP1-TPDS should always be confirmed by genetic testing of the germline DNA.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Clinical suspicion for BAP1-TPDS is often raised when patients present with multiple BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi/tumours or other associated tumours, particularly when the tumours develop at a young age. The diagnosis of BAP1-TPDS should be confirmed by identifying a loss-of-function BAP1 mutation or a deletion affecting the BAP1 locus in the germline DNA.

Staging

Tumours are staged as appropriate for each type, if malignant.

Prognosis and prediction

The exact risk of an individual patient with BAP1-TPDS of developing one or more of the associated tumours has not yet been determined. In one study, approximately 85% of patients with pathogenic BAP1 germline mutations developed at least one tumour. In the same study, the risks for the main tumour types for carriers with null variants of BAP1, for carriers with missense variants of BAP1, and in the normal population (estimated lifetime risk), respectively, were as follows {30517737}:

Uveal melanoma: 24.7%, 24%, 0.02%; Mesothelioma: 20.4%, 14.0%, 0.04%; Cutaneous melanoma: 17.0%, 36%, 2.2%; Renal cell carcinoma: 10.2%, 12.0%, 1.6%.

However, these data might underestimate the risk, because the tumour development data were collected independently of age, indicating that young individuals with pathogenetic BAP1 germline mutations at the time of data collection may develop tumours later in life. On the other hand, the study might overestimate the risk due to an ascertainment bias, because carriers were identified based on the presence of tumours associated with BAP1-TPDS, indicating that carriers without tumours were less likely to be identified {30517737}.

Additional environmental factors may increase risk; for example, ultraviolet (UV) radiation and asbestos exposure may increase the risk of melanoma and mesothelioma, respectively. Prognosis depends on tumour type and stage of malignancy. BAP1-inactivated melanocytic naevi and tumours of the skin have a good prognosis and rarely metastasize {34857909}.
