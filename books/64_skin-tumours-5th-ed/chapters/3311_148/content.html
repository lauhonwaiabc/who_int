3.3.1.1 Sebaceoma

Definition

Sebaceoma is a benign adnexal neoplasm showing sebaceous differentiation. It is characterized by rounded, cellular lobules and microcystic spaces and consists mostly of immature basaloid cells admixed with occasional mature sebocytes (< 50% of the lesion).

ICD-O coding

8410/0 Sebaceoma

ICD-11 coding

2F22 & XH0QL4 Benign neoplasms of epidermal appendages & Sebaceoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: sebaceous epithelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Sebaceomas arise most commonly on the face and scalp, and rarely on the trunk or extremities {11176054; 8543694; 6703260}.

Clinical features

Sebaceomas are solitary, yellow-tan papules/nodules {6703260}. They may rarely arise in a pre-existing sebaceous naevus {26990478; 27194974}. Lesions associated with Muir–Torre syndrome may be multifocal {10770437}. Mismatch repair (MMR)-deficient sebaceous neoplasms occur more commonly outside of the head and neck area than do MMR-stable sebaceous neoplasms {18551751; 33201015}.

Epidemiology

Sebaceomas may be associated with Muir–Torre syndrome {10770437}, part of Lynch syndrome (see the Digestive System Tumours and Genetic Tumour Syndromes volumes of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579.]] [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Genetic tumour syndromes [Internet; beta version ahead of print]. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2023. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 14). https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int/chapters/67.]]). The mean patient age at diagnosis is 70 years {6703260}, and there is a female predominance {12142607; 6703260}.

Etiology

Sebaceomas may arise through defective MMR, which manifests as a loss of expression of MMR gene products, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2; a subset of these lesions are related to Muir–Torre syndrome {19515040; 25427047; 26892655; 28323777; 21499234; 20055911; 33201015}. Preserved expression of all four proteins suggests microsatellite stability, whereas loss of nuclear staining for any of the proteins indicates deficient MMR, suggests the candidate gene, and indicates the need for further testing (see section Muir–Torre syndrome). However, clinical parameters outperform MMR immunohistochemical studies for identifying patients at risk for Muir–Torre syndrome {24603434; 23212176}. Moreover, a newly described rare autosomal recessive form of Muir–Torre syndrome lacks all aberrations in the expression of MMR gene products {26892655}.

Pathogenesis

Next-generation sequencing studies of sebaceomas have found frequent mutation of RAS family genes (HRAS and KRAS), TP53, CDKN2A, EGFR, and CTNNB1. Abnormalities of TP53 were most common {27311873}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Sebaceoma consists of well-circumscribed cellular lobules composed mostly of small monomorphic immature basaloid cells admixed with fewer mature sebocytes (< 50%), haphazardly distributed throughout the tumour. Mature sebocytes have abundant cytoplasmic vacuoles that produce scalloped nuclei. Duct-like structures and cystic areas may be seen, which often show an eosinophilic cuticle and occasionally contain holocrine secretions {28477886; 27836252; 6703260}. Infundibulocystic structures, squamous metaplasia, and apocrine differentiation can also be observed {26760686}. Mitotic figures may be common in the immature component, but atypical forms and necrosis are not expected. A variety of growth patterns have been described in sebaceoma, sometimes even within the same neoplasm, including reticulated (rippled), cribriform, sinusoidal/labyrinthine, carcinoid-like, and glandular configurations {26990478; 19461240; 27836252; 15900121; 29924747}. EMA and adipophilin are immunohistochemical markers associated with the membranes of the cytoplasmic vacuoles. They provide a “bubbly” appearance in many mature sebocytes and occasional immature basaloid cells of sebaceous lesions {17593083; 20118912; 16556662; 29087003}. MMR-deficient sebaceous neoplasms show cystic change, intratumoural mucin, squamous metaplasia, ulceration, a lymphocytic infiltrate, and intertumoural heterogeneity more often than do MMR-stable sebaceous neoplasms {33201015}.

Differential diagnosis

Sebaceous carcinoma and sebaceous adenoma are the most common differential diagnoses. BerEP4 negativity may help in the distinction from basal cell carcinoma {17593083}. Some other cutaneous neoplasms, such as squamous cell carcinomas and basal cell carcinomas, may show very focal sebaceous differentiation. Many reported cases of so-called “ripple-pattern” trichoblastomas are in fact sebaceoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not relevant for individual tumours. Germline sequencing may be indicated in some cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a solitary, yellow-tan papule/nodule; well-circumscribed cellular lobules composed mostly of small, monomorphic, immature but cytologically benign basaloid cells admixed with fewer mature sebocytes (< 50%), haphazardly distributed throughout the tumour.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Sebaceoma is a benign lesion, and simple excision is curative. However, the subset of patients with this tumour who have Muir–Torre syndrome may experience morbidity and mortality from associated visceral malignancies {26892655}. Rare examples of malignant transformation to sebaceous carcinoma have been reported {26268140; 23550845}.
