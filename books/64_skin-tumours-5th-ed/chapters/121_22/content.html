1.2.1 Basal cell carcinomas

Definition

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a family of carcinomas that arise either from epithelial cells of the epidermis or from hair follicle stem cells. There are numerous morphological types with distinctive clinical and histological features but similar epidemiology and etiology. All types have in common the presence of nests of basaloid cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and minimal cytoplasm.

Localization

BCC is localized in the skin, particularly in the setting of chronic sun damage.

Clinical features

BCC is most common on the head and neck, followed by the trunk and extremities; the various types show different location predilections and clinical appearances. BCC has also been reported in the perianal and genital region, nail unit, palm, and sole. Dermoscopy improves the sensitivity of clinical diagnosis over visual examination from 67% to 85% {30582991}.

Epidemiology

BCC is the most common human malignancy {28966807} and represents about 80% of all cancers of the skin {20231499}. Incidence worldwide is rising, with an estimated 4 million new cases worldwide in 2019. The highest rates are seen in Australia, and the lowest in parts of Africa {22251204}. Over the past decade, the largest increase in incidence occurred in the USA; there were unexpected increases in eastern Asia and tropical Latin America and decreases in Brazil and some African countries {34105887}. Incidence is inversely related to geographical latitude combined with the pigment status of the population {28220485}. Risk increases with age, and prolonged life expectancy has contributed to the rise in cases {26520231}. Although BCC is more common in men overall, a dramatic increase in incidence has been seen in young women in Europe and the USA, resulting in a predominance of women in that age group. This may be due to higher use of indoor tanning and closer attention to skin health with increased medical visits {28220485}.

Etiology

Patients who are older, are male, have sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure due to light skin pigmentation, and are exposed to intense intermittent UV radiation are most likely to develop BCC {28220485}. Chronic arsenic exposure may cause BCC {32759706}. Radiation treatment, especially in youth, is strongly associated with BCC development {17917604}. All patients with naevoid BCC syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) develop BCCs; patients with xeroderma pigmentosum, Bazex–Dupré–Christol syndrome, and Rombo syndrome are also at risk {19557015; 34608840}. Polymorphisms in the MC1R, ASIP, and TYR genes, which regulate melanin production, increase the risk of BCC {22158557; 18488027}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of BCC involves an interplay between genetic susceptibility and sporadic UV radiation–induced mutations; BCC has the most mutations of any human cancer {26950095}. Germline mutations in PTCH1 on chromosome 9q22.3-q316 are the cause of BCC in Gorlin syndrome; see section Naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome). Loss of heterozygosity of PTCH1 is seen in 58–69% of sporadic BCCs; less frequently, SMO and PTCH2 are affected, and most of these are UV radiation signature mutations {32759706}. Mutant PTCH1 and PTCH1 proteins are receptors for the sonic hedgehog protein, and mutant PTCH1 leads to the increased release of transmembrane protein SMO, which leads to downstream activation of the GLI1 transcriptional pathway that is involved in cell renewal, fate, and survival, and in angiogenesis. Inactivating TP53 alterations are seen in 40–65% of sporadic cases; loss of heterozygosity of this gene is mutually exclusive with PTCH1 {29165358}. TP53 alterations cause dysregulation of cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and DNA repair.

Histopathology

All types of BCC are characterized by basaloid tumour cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Apoptotic cells are common within the nests. Stromal changes include fibromyxoid change, retraction of tumour nests from stroma, calcification, and amyloid deposition. Tumour nests may be colonized by melanocytes and contain melanin pigment. Colonization by melanoma (lentigo maligna) cells has been reported {27373211; 33061513}. Mature keratinization is most common in nodular BCC.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes basaloid follicular neoplasms (trichoepithelioma, trichoblastoma, basaloid follicular hamartoma), basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, sebaceous carcinoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma. BCC shows strong expression of p40/p63 and CK5/6, diffuse BCL2 and CD10 positivity, and focal AR expression, and is negative for EMA, CEA, and CK20. The basaloid follicular neoplasms show colonizing CK20-positive Merkel cells, stromal but not tumoural CD10 expression, and peripheral BCL2 positivity. Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma is positive for EMA and negative for BerEP4, and sebaceous carcinoma is strongly positive for EMA, AR, and adipophilin, but negative for BerEP4. Merkel cell carcinoma is diffusely CK20-positive with perinuclear dot-like positivity and diffusely expresses neuroendocrine markers {29072946}. A potential pitfall is that BCC may express neuroendocrine markers: CD56 (NCAM) is positive in 95% of cases, chromogranin A in 28%, and synaptophysin in 18% {23292839; 28548901}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular testing is not required for primary diagnosis, but identification of mutations in the hedgehog pathway along with a high tumour mutation burden may be helpful in diagnosing a rare case of metastatic BCC. Mutations in SMO confer resistance to pharmacological inhibition of the hedgehog pathway by vismodegib {31870915}.

Staging

Staging is according to the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system, as for other cutaneous malignancies.

Prognosis and prediction

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines stratify BCC types by risk of recurrence, on the basis of clinical and pathological parameters (see #34667Table 2.02). A consensus panel has advocated unifying infiltrating, sclerosing/morphoeic, and micronodular BCC into a single “infiltrative BCC” category to improve reproducibility in diagnosis and stratification to the high-risk group {34931722}.
