1.2.2 Squamous cell carcinomas

Definition

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a malignancy of epidermal keratinocytes that exhibits varying histology. It goes through stages of development from dysplasia to in situ lesions to invasive carcinoma.

ICD-O coding

8070/3 Squamous cell carcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C31.Z Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Individual SCC types are described in the sections that follow this overview.

Localization

SCC and its precursor, actinic keratosis, are most common in chronically sun-exposed skin of the head and neck, followed by the trunk and legs (especially in women) {35417326}. Verrucous SCC is almost always on the genitalia, hands, and feet {24969990}. SCC may arise in a site of chronic trauma/inflammation, such as a chronic ulcer, burn scar, or radiation dermatitis, known as a Marjolin ulcer {19881930}. Onycholemmal carcinomas are SCCs arising from the nail bed.

Clinical features

SCC appears as a solitary red scaly plaque, progressing to a hyperkeratotic, often ulcerated nodule. Lesions with perineural involvement produce pain/paraesthesia. Regional lymph node involvement is the first route of metastasis. Dermoscopy shows distinctive vascular patterns: small dotted, glomerular, hairpin/looped, and/or serpentine vessels {29332704}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of SCC is difficult to establish, but data on non-melanoma skin cancer suggest that there were an estimated 701 000 SCC tumours affecting 430 500 people in the USA in 2006 (assuming that 20% of tumours are SCC) {20231499}. SCC is the second most common form of skin cancer (after basal cell carcinoma) and is more common in men than in women. Most cases occur in sun-exposed skin of elderly individuals, most commonly individuals with lighter skin pigmentation.

Etiology

In addition to ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure, other factors that have been implicated in the etiology of SCC include chronic immunosuppression, other forms of radiation, topical carcinogens, burn scars, chronic inflammation, sinus tracts, HPV infection, arsenic, and coal tar. Cutaneous SCC was the first occupational cancer to be identified, described in the scrotal skin of British chimney sweeps in 1775 by Percivall Pott.

Patients with xeroderma pigmentosum, vitiligo, and albinism are at increased risk of SCC. A recent two-stage genome-wide association study of a large number of SCCs revealed 11 susceptibility loci. Of these, 7 are related to pigmentation: MC1R, ASIP, TYR, SLC45A2, OCA2, IRF4, and BNC2. The other 4 are involved in tumour immune evasion, apoptosis inhibition, or other oncogenic pathways: 11q23.3 (CADM1), 2p22.3, 7p21.1 (AHR), and 9q34.3 (SEC16A) {27424798}.

Pathogenesis

Most UV radiation–related cutaneous SCCs appear to develop through a multistep process in actinic keratoses or in fields of squamous dysplasia. Common mutations involve tumour suppressor genes such as TP53, NOTCH1, and NOTCH2 {27193637}. Activating mutations in genes such as HRAS and KRAS are also frequently involved. UV radiation–induced TP53 mutations probably occur early on in the process. Advanced age, immunosuppression, and environmentally determined defects in the epigenome appear to contribute to impaired elimination of precursors of malignant transformation. Cutaneous SCC probably arises from an epidermal or follicular stem cell {25100530}.

Recent genetic expression profile studies suggest that the differentially expressed genes CXCL8 (IL-8), MMP1, HIF1A, ITGA6, and ITGA2 are promising diagnostic and therapeutic targets {26893747}.

Histopathology

Although many SCCs progress from in situ lesions to progressively more deeply invasive tumours, some SCCs have no identifiable in situ precursor. Invasive SCC varies from well to poorly differentiated, with most classified as well to moderately differentiated. Some well-differentiated forms have a histological resemblance to keratoacanthoma, which is now considered to be a separate type of epidermal neoplasm. These are often termed keratoacanthoma-like SCC. Invasive tumour shows polygonal cellular profiles, pleomorphism, and varying amounts of cytoplasmic and extracytoplasmic keratin. Immunohistochemistry is generally not required, except for some poorly differentiated SCCs. Cutaneous SCC expresses p63, p40, CK5/6, MNF116 keratin, and 34βE12 keratin, with patchy EMA positivity. Unlike basal cell carcinoma, BerEP4 is negative.

Differential diagnosis

Paget disease and melanoma in situ may mimic SCC in situ. CK7 or CEA positivity in Paget disease, and SOX10 in melanoma, are diagnostic. Well-differentiated SCC must be distinguished from pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, syringometaplasia, inverted follicular keratosis, and inflamed verruca. Re-excision perineural invasion occurs when squamoid cells surround nerves in scars, and it can mimic SCC. Poorly differentiated or sarcomatoid SCC must be differentiated from melanoma, atypical fibroxanthoma, sarcoma, and lymphoma; a panel of immunohistochemical stains including SOX10, CK5/6, p63, and CD45 (LCA) is recommended. Colonization by melanocytes or melanoma may occur {29641705}.

Cytology

FNAC is used to evaluate enlarged regional lymph nodes. Pap-stained slides show orangeophilic polygonal cells with an increased N:C ratio, hyperchromatic nuclei, and chromatin clumping {10692060}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not recommended

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a solitary plaque, often progressing to a hyperkeratotic or ulcerated nodule; infiltration of dermis by islands of atypical cells with variable keratinization.

Desirable: an in situ component may be present; immunohistochemistry shows expression of p63, p40, CK5/6, MNF116 keratin, and 34βE12 keratin.

Staging

The eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system is applicable to SCCs of head and neck origin only; it uses tumour diameter, invasion of underlying structures such as bone, and perineural invasion in nerves > 0.1 mm diameter for primary tumours and incorporates nodal and systemic involvement.

Prognosis and prediction

Confirmed high-risk histological prognostic features are a tumour thickness of > 2 mm, Clark level IV or V invasion, perineural invasion, primary site on the ear or lip, and poor differentiation. Perineural invasion of nerves ≥ 0.1 mm in diameter correlates with higher disease-specific death rates {19889009}. SCCs arising in organ transplant patients are often multiple and aggressive. Some types of SCC show distinct prognoses. Tumours with desmoplastic histology are often considered worse {32169476}. Lymphoepithelial carcinoma of the skin is considered to have an intermediate metastatic potential, with a 5–10% risk of regional metastasis {16466506}. Acantholytic SCC does not show a worse prognosis than typical SCC {27889291}.
