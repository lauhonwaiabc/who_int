2.7.1.1 Congenital melanocytic naevus

Definition

Congenital melanocytic naevus (CMN) is a benign melanocytic neoplasm present at birth or appearing in the first year of life (late or tardive congenital naevus). Blue naevi can also arise at birth but are discussed in a separate section.

ICD-O coding

8761/0 Congenital melanocytic naevus

ICD-11 coding

2F20.2Z Congenital melanocytic naevus, unspecified

Related terminology

Acceptable: congenital naevocytic naevus; congenital naevus syndrome; neurocutaneous melanosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

CMN can occur anywhere on the body {15692463}.

Clinical features

In adults, CMNs are categorized as small (< 15 mm), intermediate (15–200 mm), or giant (> 200 mm). Small and intermediate CMNs typically present as symmetrical, oval or round, brown to black, well-circumscribed macules or plaques that are homogeneous in texture and often have prominent terminal hairs or a rugose or pebbly surface. Giant naevi may exhibit thickening of the skin and soft tissue, and those affecting the scalp and back can involve the CNS (neurocutaneous melanosis) {22980258}.

Epidemiology

About 1% of newborns have a small CMN. Giant congenital naevi affect 5 newborns per 100 000 births.

Etiology

CMN is a clonal proliferation of melanocytes caused by a postzygotic mutation.

Pathogenesis

The majority (~90%) of large CMNs harbour NRAS mutations {16888631}. BRAF p.V600E mutations are less common (5–10%) {25490715}. KRAS, APC, and MET mutations and ZEB2::ALK and SOX5::RAF1 gene fusions have also been observed {30359577}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

CMNs are larger than common acquired naevi and frequently involve the reticular dermis and subcutis, with splaying of naevus cells between collagen bundles and around dermal appendages and neurovascular bundles {33464753; 20442637; 22771898}. In infants, there may be striking pagetoid scatter and confluence of the intraepidermal nests in the centre of the lesions. Cells in the dermis often change from large epithelioid type A naevus cells in the upper dermis to small lymphocyte-like type B cells to spindled type C cells in the deep reticular dermis. There is no significant atypia or mitotic activity {28078671}.

Immunohistochemistry

CMNs usually express the melanocytic markers melan-A and SOX10 and show a gradual decrease of HMB45 expression with depth.

Differential diagnosis

CMN with an atypical junctional proliferation of melanocytes may simulate melanoma in situ, but such areas are typically restricted to the centre of the lesion. Cases with increased cellularity can be difficult to distinguish from naevoid melanoma. Deep, atypical, or clustered mitotic figures, nuclear enlargement, pleomorphism, hyperchromatism, and sheet-like growth {26602414; 22176022; 15370108} are among the most important features pointing towards melanoma.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are usually unnecessary for the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large melanocytic naevi, usually present at birth or in early life; deep reticular dermal involvement, splaying of collagen, and perivascular and periadnexal distribution of naevus cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The lifetime risk of melanoma arising in a CMN of any size is approximately 1–2%; for large CMNs with satellite naevi or CNS involvement it is as high as 10–15% {29999207; 28078671}.
