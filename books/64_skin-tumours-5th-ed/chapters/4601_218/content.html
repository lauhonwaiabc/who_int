4.6.0.1 Reticulohistiocytosis

Definition

Reticulohistiocytosis (RHC) is a clinically heterogeneous group of disorders of macrophages characterized by large epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. The importance of differentiating RHC from other histiocytoses lies in the rheumatological and neoplastic associations of this group.

Related terminology

Acceptable: reticulohistiocytoma.

Not recommended: giant cell reticulohistiocytosis; lipoid dermatoarthritis.

Subtype(s)

Clinical subtypes: single system / single focal reticulohistiocytosis; multifocal reticulohistiocytosis; multicentric reticulohistiocytosis; reticulohistiocytosis with an associated haematological neoplasm

Localization

RHC may be skin-limited or systemic {16625100; 31955466; 17113966}. RHC with an associated haematological neoplasm (RHC-AHN) may involve the bone marrow {28074480}.

Clinical features

See #34105Table 6.04 .

Solitary cutaneous RHC (SCR) (also known as reticulohistiocytoma) and generalized cutaneous RHC (GCR) are skin-limited diseases (i.e. single system), whereas in multicentric RHC (MR), skin lesions are invariably associated with erosive polyarthritis, and patients with RHC-AHN may present with bone marrow involvement and hepatosplenomegaly (i.e. multisystem) {31955466; 26966089}.

Patients with SCR present with a single cutaneous or mucosal, firm, reddish-brown papule or nodule that may involute {1575538; 16625100}. Patients with GCR present with multiple papulonodular lesions with similar features {16595113}.

In MR, erythematous papular skin lesions may itch and often appear near cartilaginous structures (ears, nose) and above the involved joints. They are multiple and may coalesce into an irregular plaque (cobblestone appearance). An aggressive erosive peripheral symmetrical polyarthritis is the initial symptom in 40% of cases, often associated with fever, weight loss, and weakness. The heart, lungs, and liver are also less frequently involved. About a third of patients with MR develop epithelial malignancies (e.g. pulmonary or gynaecological) or autoimmune disease (e.g. primary biliary cirrhosis, systemic sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome, and dermatomyositis) {17618169; 25900189; 27886798}.

Patients with RHC-AHN present with multiple cutaneous lesions (mimicking GCR) in association with myeloid neoplasms {28074480; 21508571; 33125722}. Patients with RHC lesions may rarely present with clinical features of Erdheim–Chester disease {30710384; 31955466} or present concurrently with Langerhans cell histiocytosis (mixed histiocytosis) {32969291}.

Epidemiology

RHC occurs in adults aged 30–40 years. The M:F ratio varies from 8:1 in RHC-AHN to 1:3 in MR {16625100; 16595113; 27026876; 33125722}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. SCR and GCR may be reactive conditions {1575538}, and MR may represent a systemic (auto)immune or paraneoplastic disorder {25900189}. RHC-AHN may be clonally related to myeloid neoplasms {28074480; 33125722}.

Pathogenesis

MR may have mutant phosphorylated ERK pathway genes (BRAF, MAP2K1), mutant TET2, or translocations involving KIF5B {30710384; 31171640}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Histological findings are similar in all clinical forms and involved tissues. Cutaneous lesions show a dermal infiltrate of large mononuclear (epithelioid), binucleated, or multinucleated giant cells with randomly oriented nuclei and finely granular ground-glass cytoplasm. Early-stage lesions show a mixed infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, and macrophages, with few multinucleated giant cells. Late-stage lesions show a diffuse infiltrate with numerous giant cells. The giant cells sometimes show emperipolesis of neutrophils or lymphocytes. Synovial biopsies show similar infiltrates {17903621; 16625100; 31955466}.

Immunohistochemistry

The macrophages are positive for CD14, CD68 (KP1 and PGM1), CD163, vimentin, lysozyme, and fascin. They are variably positive for factor XIIIa and S100, and negative for CD1a and CD207 (langerin). In MR, electron microscopy typically reveals pleomorphic cytoplasmic inclusions and collagen phagocytosis in 50% of cases {7229425; 15785133; 17903621; 16625100; 31955466}.

Differential diagnosis

The typical manifestation of Erdheim–Chester disease (diabetes insipidus, retroperitoneal, cardiological involvement, metadiaphysial long bone lesions) and other defined histiocytoses (e.g. ALK-positive histiocytosis) should be ruled out {31955466; 34727172}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There is no diagnostic molecular marker for RHC. However, BRAF, MAP2K1, and TET2 mutations have been described in multisystem cases of RHC, as in other histiocytoses {30710384; 31171640; 31955466}, and may help in the diagnosis in difficult cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cutaneous proliferation of CD163+, CD1a− large mononuclear or multinucleated epithelioid cells with ground-glass cytoplasm; MR can be diagnosed only in multisystem cases with arthropathy; for RHC-AHN, an underlying haematological neoplasm must be demonstrated; no clinical evidence of Erdheim–Chester disease; other defined histiocytoses (e.g. ALK-positive histiocytosis) should be ruled out.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

After a diagnosis of RHC, multisystem involvement and rheumatological and neoplastic association should be ruled out {31955466}. As for other histiocytoses, a complete clinical, radiological, and haematological survey is needed for the detection of extracutaneous involvement {32106306}. SCR is cured by excision. GCR is sometimes self-healing {16625100; 16595113}. The prognosis of MR is linked to joint destruction by arthritis and to associated disorders {25900189; 27886798}. In RHC-AHN, the prognosis is dependent on the associated haematological neoplasm {28074480; 21508571; 33125722}.
