3.1.2.3 Porocarcinoma

Definition

Porocarcinoma is the malignant counterpart of poroma. Porocarcinoma may develop de novo or, less commonly, from a pre-existing poroma.

ICD-O coding

8409/3 Porocarcinoma

8409/2 Porocarcinoma in situ

ICD-11 coding

2C33 & XH7WE6 Adnexal carcinoma of skin & Porocarcinoma in situ

2C33 & XH7VK4 Adnexal carcinoma of skin & Porocarcinoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: malignant eccrine poroma.

Not recommended: malignant hidroacanthoma simplex.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most common site is the lower limbs, followed by the head and neck, trunk, and upper limbs {8338322; 2160378; 11395548; 8391881; 28721214}.

Clinical features

Patients with porocarcinoma present with an ulcerated nodule. Rapid growth, ulceration, and bleeding of a longstanding pre-existing poroma may also be observed.

Epidemiology

In one large series, the prevalence of porocarcinoma was 18 cases in 450 000 consecutive skin biopsy specimens (0.004%). In large series, the mean age at presentation has varied from 61.5 to 73 years (range: 12–91 years). Demographic data vary greatly in the literature, and there is no sex predilection {11395548; 1325487; 28721214}.

Etiology

The etiology is poorly understood. Molecular evidence suggests that ultraviolet (UV) radiation signature mutations are present in at least some cases {27067779; 29269125}.

Pathogenesis

Porocarcinomas may arise de novo or as malignant transformation of pre-existing poromas, possibly related to tumour suppressor loss {29269125}. Activating mutations in HRAS have been described {27067779; 29269125}, as well as YAP1::MAML2, YAP1::NUTM1, and (more infrequently) WWTR1::NUTM1 fusions {31145701}. Oncogene mutations in EGFR, ERBB2, FGFR3, KRAS, NRAS, and PIK3CA, as well as mutations in the tumour suppressor genes TP53, RB1, and CDKN2A, have also been reported {21423156; 23056620; 27067779; 29269125; 29872726; 31038235; 34023104}. Recently, porocarcinomas with PAK1/PAK2/PAK3 fusions have been described {38785043}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Porocarcinoma in situ is confined to the epidermis and usually shows the pattern of classic poroma accompanied by cytological features of malignancy {730865; 1229958; 5120180; 7894103}. About 10% of all porocarcinomas are in situ lesions {11395548}. Invasive porocarcinoma additionally displays an intradermal proliferation with infiltrative borders and atypical cytological features that vary from moderate to frankly anaplastic. At least focal ductal differentiation is usually found. Perineural invasion, intravascular invasion, and necrosis en masse are variable features. Rare features include clear cell change, focal squamous cell differentiation, sarcomatoid transformation, intratumoural melanin deposits, melanocytic colonization, and focal sebaceous differentiation {17214856; 18212539; 17704263; 9335250}. There is no completely sensitive and specific immunohistochemical marker for porocarcinoma. Immunohistochemical expression of the fusion partner NUT has been reported, with a sensitivity of 50–58% {35158743; 34023104}. Ductal differentiation, including abortive attempts in the form of intracytoplasmic lumina, can be highlighted by staining for EMA or CEA {6092444}.

Differential diagnosis

Porocarcinomas with low-grade atypia can be very difficult to distinguish from poroma. Focal enlarged, atypical-appearing p53-positive cuticular cells can be observed in some poromas {8599450}. Porocarcinomas, as opposed to poromas, often show diffuse or focal loss of RB1 expression {31012122}. Poorly differentiated porocarcinoma may be difficult to classify as such, because the typical composition of poroid cells and cuticular cells is often no longer recognizable. Single-cell necrosis in other neoplasms may result in pseudoductal structures that can mimic porocarcinoma. In such cases, the distinction can be made by using CEA and EMA to highlight true ductal structures. Porocarcinoma can manifest variable histopathological features (squamous cell areas, clear cells, etc.) that raise a broad histopathological differential diagnosis. Identifying a parent lesion (i.e. a pre-existing poroma or porocarcinoma in situ) is the best way to avoid misinterpretation in such cases. NUT immunohistochemistry is highly specific for porocarcinoma as compared with other cutaneous malignancies, but it is not completely sensitive {33222286; 33739783; 34023104; 35158743}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although not clinically relevant, fusions (of YAP1, NUTM1, PAK2) have been described in poromas {37609771; 37199682}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an infiltrative neoplasm connected to the epidermis, composed of poroid cells and displaying at least focal ductal differentiation.

Staging

Staging follows the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

A meta-analysis of 105 published cases showed both local recurrence and regional metastatic rates to be approximately 20% and the distant metastatic rate to be 12% {1325487}. However, no distinction between in situ and malignant lesions was made {1325487}. In another study, of 54 invasive lesions, 9 (17%) recurred locally, 10 (19%) metastasized to regional lymph nodes, and 6 (11%) metastasized to distant sites and/or caused the death of the patient. In a meta-analysis of 453 cases, the metastatic rate was 31%; of the metastatic cases, 57% displayed regional lymph node metastasis {28721214}. A high mitotic index (> 8 mitoses/mm2) and lymphovascular invasion are strongly predictive of death {11395548}; a high mitotic index also correlates with lymph node involvement {11395548}. The gross size of the tumour has no significant relationship with prognosis, but a tumour depth of > 7 mm predicts death or lymph node involvement {11395548}. An infiltrative tumour margin has been found to be associated with increased local recurrence {11395548}.
