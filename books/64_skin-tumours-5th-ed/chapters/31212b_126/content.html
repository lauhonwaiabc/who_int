3.1.2.12B Squamoid eccrine ductal carcinoma

Definition

Squamoid eccrine ductal carcinoma (SEDC) is a biphasic malignant tumour showing squamous and ductal differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8560/3 Squamoid eccrine ductal carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C33 & XH95Y6 Adnexal carcinoma of skin & Squamoid eccrine ductal carcinoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: adenosquamous carcinoma of the skin.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The head and neck area is typically affected. The limbs and trunk are rarely involved {25873411; 19841403; 26796504; 9088963}.

Clinical features

The tumours present as large nodules and plaques (as large as a few centimetres) {25873411; 19841403; 26796504; 9088963}.

Epidemiology

With only about 60 cases reported, these tumours appear to be rare, but they are probably underrecognized {26796504}. They occur in sun-damaged skin of elderly (median age: 80 years) White individuals, with a male predominance {25873411; 19841403; 26796504; 9088963}.

Etiology

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation–induced damage and immunosuppression may play a role {25873411}.

Pathogenesis

Many cases of what is currently recognized as SEDC were formerly called adenosquamous carcinoma. Molecular studies have shown that SEDC has a global transcriptome profile intermediate between those of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and sweat gland tumours. UV radiation signature mutations were found in the majority of cases, including frequent TP53 and NOTCH mutations {39154783}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

SEDC is a poorly marginated and frequently ulcerated dermal-based neoplasm with diffusely infiltrative margins and frequent invasion of the subcutis. The median tumour thickness is 4.3 mm (range: 1.5–18 mm), and the tumour has a biphasic appearance. In the superficial aspects, the tumour is composed of lobules and nests with overt squamous differentiation and multifocal epidermal connection, closely resembling well-differentiated to moderately differentiated SCC. In the deeper areas, the tumour is characterized by a diffusely infiltrative growth pattern of irregular cords and strands of cuboidal cells with prominent cytological atypia, nuclear pleomorphism, and ductal differentiation in a desmoplastic stroma. Perineural infiltration is relatively common. Lymphovascular invasion and tumour necrosis may be additional findings. Immunohistochemically, the tumour cells express cytokeratins, and ductal differentiation can be confirmed by staining for EMA and CEA {19278431; 26796504}.

Differential diagnosis

On superficial biopsies, SEDC may be indistinguishable from invasive SCC if the deeper-seated ductal component is not represented in the biopsy. This may be one reason for the underrecognition of this entity. However, this distinction is of little clinical consequence, because the prognosis and treatment recommendations are similar. Similarly, porocarcinoma may show squamous differentiation. It lacks zonation, and the tumour cells show poroid features. The presence of a pre-existing poroma is helpful. The behaviour and treatment are similar to those of squamoid eccrine carcinoma. Microcystic adnexal carcinoma shows follicular and ductal differentiation, with zonation similar to that seen in squamoid eccrine carcinoma. It is cytologically bland, lacks prominent cytological atypia, and does not show areas resembling outright SCC.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a biphasic tumour with a desmoplastic stroma, a component of SCC usually in the superficial to mid dermis, and a ductal component in the mid to deep dermis.

Staging

Staging follows the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

Despite complete surgical excision, local recurrence rates are high (25%), and there is a 13% chance of regional lymph node metastasis. However, distant metastasis and disease-related mortality are rare, occurring in only 3% of cases {26796504}. Adverse prognostic factors have not been comprehensively studied but may include subcutaneous tissue invasion, perineural invasion, and lymphovascular invasion.
