2.3.0.3 Spitz melanoma

Definition

Spitz melanoma is a malignant melanocytic neoplasm harbouring the initiating genomic alteration characteristic of Spitz naevus.

Related terminology

Acceptable: malignant Spitz tumour.

Not recommended: mixed epithelioid and spindle cell melanoma.

Subtype(s)

The subtypes of Spitz melanoma are similar to the genomic subtypes of Spitz naevus. See #38596Table 3.08 in section Spitz naevus.

Localization

Spitz melanomas can occur at any anatomical site but are most common on the extremities and trunk {25308882}.

Clinical features

Patients typically present with a changing or enlarging amelanotic or pigmented papule, nodule, or plaque {25308882}, typically > 6 mm {25308882; 22257900}, which can be pigmented and have a smooth, verrucous, or ulcerated surface. Dermoscopy may reveal a starburst pattern with a peripheral rim of pseudopods or streaks, or there may be a homogeneous pink, blue, or black appearance with or without shiny white streaks.

Epidemiology

Spitz melanoma is rare {24445538; 31498175; 31152596}. It can occur at any age, although it is rare in prepubertal children and more common in individuals aged > 40 years (mean age: 55 years) {25308882}. It appears to be more common in men {25308882}.

Etiology

Spitz melanomas arise through genetic progression from a Spitz naevus or Spitz melanocytoma.

Pathogenesis

Spitz melanoma is initiated by the same spectrum of genetic alterations as Spitz naevus. Progression occurs through additional genetic aberrations in melanoma-associated genes such as CDKN2A, PTEN, and TERT. These additional mutations may be caused by ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure {24691053}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Tumours may have many of the characteristics of a Spitz naevus and melanocytoma, such as epithelioid and spindle cell cytology with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, but also show characteristics of a malignant neoplasm, including a higher degree of asymmetry, pleomorphism, poor circumscription, effacement or epidermal consumption, ulceration, larger aggregates with sheets, or areas of necrosis. Mitoses, including atypical forms, are usually present.

Immunohistochemistry

p16 expression is often absent, and the Ki-67 proliferation index is often > 20% {26503349}. Spitz melanoma may demonstrate deep rather than stratified HMB45 staining, and patchy rather than diffuse melan-A staining. Positive immunohistochemical staining for ALK, ROS1, and NTRK can suggest an underlying respective fusion but is not useful for distinction from Spitz melanocytoma.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes Spitz naevus, Spitz melanocytoma, and conventional melanoma (see the various immunomarkers and genomic findings described above and below). The term “spitzoid melanoma” is sometimes used for melanomas with morphological resemblance to a Spitz tumour but lacking a defining Spitz-related genetic alteration. Many such melanomas have characteristics of melanomas in skin with a low degree of cumulative sun damage (low-CSD melanomas) and have BRAF p.V600E mutations {28186096}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The identification of a Spitz-associated genomic fusion in the setting of a tumour with spitzoid morphology can confirm Spitz lineage and can help exclude spitzoid melanoma {32004563}. The presence of melanoma-associated chromosomal copy-number aberrations or the presence of a TERT promoter mutation supports a diagnosis of melanoma over Spitz melanocytoma {32004563; 26061100; 10594753}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant melanocytic proliferation with Spitz morphology; high-grade nuclear atypia; highly expansile or sheet-like growth patterns; high mitotic activity (often ≥ 6 mitoses/mm2), with atypical mitoses.

Desirable: tumour size > 5 mm; asymmetrical silhouette; necrosis; molecular anomalies confirming Spitz lineage and consistent with a malignant proliferation such as TERT promoter mutation or multiple chromosomal copy-number aberrations typical of melanoma; see also #35968Table 3.09 in section Spitz melanocytoma (atypical Spitz tumour).

Staging

Spitz melanoma is staged according to the current Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification for cutaneous melanoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Because of the rarity of bona fide Spitz melanoma, its prognosis is not fully established. In cohorts likely representing a mixture of Spitz melanocytomas and Spitz melanomas, 40–50% of cases showed involvement of sentinel lymph nodes with no further disease spread or fatalities {19123453}. Adverse prognosticators are older patient age, larger tumour size (> 10 mm), ulceration, higher mitotic count (> 6 mitoses/mm2), and the presence of multiple chromosomal copy-number aberrations including biallelic deletion of 9p21 and TERT promoter mutation.
