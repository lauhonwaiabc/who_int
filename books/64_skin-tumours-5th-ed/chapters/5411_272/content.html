5.4.1.1 Glomus tumour

Definition

Glomus tumours are mesenchymal neoplasms composed of cells resembling the modified smooth muscle cells of the normal glomus body.

ICD-O coding

8711/0 Glomus tumour

8713/0 Glomuvenous malformation (glomangiomyoma)

8711/1 Glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential

8711/3 Malignant glomus tumour

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Y & XH47J2 Other specified neoplastic haemangioma & Glomus tumour, NOS

2E81.0Y & XH21E6 Other specified neoplastic haemangioma & Glomus tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Not recommended: glomangioma; glomangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Glomuvenous malformation (glomangiomyoma); glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential; malignant glomus tumour

Localization

Glomus tumours are most frequently located in the distal extremities, particularly in the subungual regions of the hands and feet {11145243; 14080348; 20530151; 35137564}; rarely, other sites are involved {27065433; 27867327; 27015262; 34660105}. Glomuvenous malformation is more common on the trunk {30677819}. Rare malignant cutaneous glomus tumours have been reported in a variety of locations {18422692; 11145243; 33344314}.

Clinical features

Most cutaneous glomus tumours come to clinical attention as solitary, small (< 10 mm), painful nodules or plaques, with a pink to blue hue {28163461; 28435574; 28116265; 34953423; 34900456}. Glomuvenous malformations are often multiple or familial {30677819}.

Epidemiology

Most glomus tumours occur in the skin {21187877; 28203512}. Digital and subungual lesions account for 1–4% of benign hand tumours and have a slight female predominance {34609426}. Glomus tumours are most common in the fourth to sixth decades of life {25614464}; paediatric tumours are very rare {28294349}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Multiple familial cutaneous glomus tumours appear to have an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance {10727310; 8141540} and are caused by inactivating loss-of-function mutations in GLMN (1p22.1), which encodes the protein glomulin {11145243; 15689436}. Rare glomus tumours are associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 {19738042; 23849732; 25426397}. Sporadic glomus tumours often show rearrangements involving MIR143 and NOTCH genes {23999936; 32604167}. BRAF p.V600E mutations have been reported in small numbers of histologically malignant tumours {22317887; 28834810}.

Macroscopic appearance

Patients typically present with a firm nodule with a greyish-white, greyish-red, or brown cut surface.

Histopathology

Glomus cells are small, uniform, and round, with a central round nucleus, amphophilic to lightly eosinophilic cytoplasm, and a sharp cellular outline with peripheral basal lamina. Oncocytic, epithelioid, myxoid, and hyalinized patterns are recognized {7672784; 3038728}. Solid glomus tumours, which account for approximately 75% of all cases, are composed of nests of glomus cells surrounding capillary-sized vessels. Small cuffs of glomus cells are often seen around small vessels located outside the main mass. Glomuvenous malformations, which account for 20% of cases, feature dilated vascular channels with intervening small clusters of glomus cells. Glomus tumours may also contain spindled cells resembling smooth muscle (glomangiomyoma) or a branching, haemangiopericytoma-like vasculature (glomangiopericytoma) {9591720}. Glomangiomatosis is an extremely rare pattern of glomus tumour with an overall architectural resemblance to diffuse angiomatosis {11145243; 15372213; 19267531}, but containing nests of glomus cells within vessel walls. Symplastic tumours show striking nuclear atypia in the absence of other worrisome features {11145243; 19602065; 25051107}.

Histologically and/or clinically malignant glomus tumours are extremely rare {11145243; 22228777; 27772590}. The diagnosis of malignant glomus tumour should be reserved only for tumours showing marked nuclear atypia (with any level of mitotic activity) and/or atypical mitotic figures. A component of pre-existing benign-looking glomus tumour is often present. Malignant glomus tumours may retain an overall architectural resemblance to normal glomus tumour (but with malignant-looking round cells), or they may consist of a spindled component resembling leiomyosarcoma or fibrosarcoma. In the absence of a clear-cut benign precursor, immunohistochemical demonstration of SMA and pericellular collagen IV is required for diagnosis of round cell forms of malignant glomus tumour, in the absence of a clear-cut benign precursor.

Glomus tumours that show atypical features but lack severe nuclear atypia or atypical mitotic activity should instead be classified as glomus tumours of uncertain malignant potential.

Glomus tumours of all types typically express SMA, h-caldesmon, and pericellular collagen IV {11145243}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of MIR143::NOTCH1, MIR143::NOTCH2, or MIR143::NOTCH3 fusions may be helpful in selected cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: monomorphic round to epithelioid cells with centrally placed, round nuclei and well-defined cell borders, arranged in perivascular nests; malignant glomus tumours may usually be recognized by an adjacent benign component and show either a spindle or round cell sarcomatous phenotype; in malignant examples lacking a benign component, immunohistochemistry and/or molecular diagnosis is required for a definitive diagnosis.

Desirable: immunohistochemical expression of SMA, h-caldesmon, and collagen IV; demonstration of NOTCH gene rearrangements in malignant cases.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Typical glomus tumours, glomuvenous malformations, and symplastic glomus tumours are benign. Malignant glomus tumours are aggressive, with metastases and death from disease in as many as 40% of patients. Some large, visceral glomus tumours without other atypical features (uncertain malignant potential) have behaved aggressively. The natural history of histologically malignant glomus tumours in the skin may be favourable, although more data are needed {30314644}.
