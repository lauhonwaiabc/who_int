4.2.2.1 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Definition

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a clonal neoplasm of myeloid dendritic cells expressing a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a and CD207 expression) and lacking high-grade cytological features.

ICD-O coding

9751/1 Langerhans cell histiocytosis, NOS

9751/3 Langerhans cell histiocytosis, disseminated

ICD-11 coding

2B31.2 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: Langerhans cell granulomatosis; histiocytosis X; eosinophilic granuloma (solitary lesions); Hand–Schüller–Christian disease (multifocal); Abt–Letterer–Siwe disease (disseminated or visceral involvement); Hashimoto–Pritzker disease (congenital self-healing reticulohistiocytosis).

Subtype(s)

Langerhans cell histiocytosis, disseminated

Localization

Single-system LCH (SS-LCH) affects one organ or one system; it may be unifocal or multifocal. Cutaneous involvement of the face, scalp, or trunk is common; mucous membranes (including vulvar, perianal, and gingival) are less frequently involved.

Multisystem LCH (MS-LCH) affects two or more organ systems. It is commonly multifocal and affects the skin, bone, pituitary, liver, and/or spleen, with additional extraosseous involvement including lymph node involvement, visceral mass lesions, and cystic pulmonary nodules. Bone marrow, spleen, or liver involvement is considered high-risk MS-LCH {23589673}.

Clinical features

In SS-LCH, skin involvement is typically multifocal but may present as a solitary lesion. Skin rash ranges from erythematous, crusted, and vesiculopustular (in early infancy) to eczematous dermatitis resembling seborrhoeic dermatitis (cradle cap; up to late infancy) and/or or papular with reddish-brown nodules, some with central crusting (in neonates) {9561900; 17224372; 30252693}. Although the latter has been associated with the congenital self-healing form of SS-LCH, clinical features alone cannot predict which neonatal skin lesions may spontaneously involute; therefore, staging and follow-up are mandatory {9561900; 17224372; 17097374; 11434843; 11496348; 15770639; 16261594; 25441388; 34958507}. In infants, the differential diagnosis of ulcerative lesions may include candidiasis and other fungal infections. In the neonatal period, multifocal vesiculopustular and papular presentations include a differential diagnosis of congenital infections (TORCH infections: toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex, and others), congenital malignancy (i.e. leukaemia cutis and metastases, including neuroblastoma), and dermal erythropoiesis {34958507}.

In MS-LCH, skin rash may precede bone or organ involvement. Patients often have fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly with jaundice, increased direct hyperbilirubinaemia, and hypoalbuminaemia. Active sites of disease are often PET-avid. Endocrine dysfunction and diabetes insipidus may occur with CNS involvement {23109216; 30157397; 18571550; 30421536}.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence rate in the paediatric population is 5–9 new cases per 1 million population {18260117; 32106306}, and in adults it is 1–2 cases per 1 million population. Skin LCH is estimated to occur in 40–50% of paediatric LCH cases, but most are later associated with MS-LCH; SS-LCH of the skin is less frequent {34958507; 25441388; 11434843}. The so-called congenital “self-healing” SS-LCH occurs in patients aged < 1 year {17224372}. MS-LCH with skin involvement is typical in infants aged > 18 months. Adults presenting with cutaneous SS-LCH may go on to develop systemic LCH in 20% of cases {34119604}. There is a slight male predominance {18260117}. The disease is more common among individuals of European descent and Hispanics. LCH can be associated with Erdheim–Chester disease, either concomitantly or preceding it, with shared molecular alterations {26966089; 26454140}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

MAPK pathway gene mutations are present in a majority (> 85%), with near-universal phosphorylated ERK upregulation and expression. BRAF p.V600E mutation is the most prevalent; MAP2K1 mutation is the second most frequent {30157397}.

In MS-LCH, BRAF p.V600E mutation is associated with circulating mutant mononuclear cells in the blood, increased risk of relapse, and more aggressive behaviour {25646268; 27382093; 30157397}. In cutaneous LCH, the presence of BRAF p.V600E in the lesion does not predict behaviour {25441388; 30252693}, but the presence of BRAF p.V600E in circulating mononuclear cells is associated with MS-LCH {25441388}.

The cell of origin of LCH is now thought to be a bone marrow myeloid dendritic cell precursor and not an epidermal Langerhans cell. The MAPK pathway plays a central role in pathogenesis {20220088; 25646268; 28566492; 28512190; 32750121; 30157397; 31899802; 34620874; 33901419}. A “misguided” myeloid differentiation model has been proposed, in which activating MAPK mutations at a pluripotent haematopoietic, tissue-restricted, or local precursor level give rise to high-risk multisystem, low-risk multifocal, or unifocal LCH, respectively {25646268; 30157397}; however, an oligopotent progenitor is also possible {32750121}. These mutations, especially BRAF p.V006E, seem to confer an advantage for tissue site accumulation rather than proliferation {29263218; 33958797}. One theory (although still speculative) to explain self-resolving LCH is that mutated precursors are derived from fetal haematopoietic sources (i.e. yolk sac, fetal liver) rather than a long-lived bone marrow myeloid precursor {30157397}.

Rare cases of LCH occur in association with or following a preceding lymphoma or lymphoblastic leukaemia (of B or T lineage). Often there is shared clonality, considered as a transdifferentiation phenomenon of the original disease. Such cases may be associated with more aggressive behaviour {23759932; 15925822; 19642834; 20551822}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

LCH is characterized by the accumulation of large (10–25 µm) round to oval cells with a complex nuclear contour that often assumes a nuclear groove and moderately abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Nuclear atypia is minimal and mitotic activity is variable, but there are no unusual forms. The inflammatory milieu includes eosinophils, lymphocytes, multinucleated cells, and rare neutrophils and plasma cells. Necrosis is not prognostic. Cutaneous infiltrates expand the upper dermis, first filling the papillary dermis, with or without periadnexal involvement. Epidermotropism, scale crust, and ulceration are not infrequent. Rare neonatal cases with single indolent nodules demonstrate a peculiar deep infiltration extending into the subcutis with a range of phenotypes (i.e. Langerhans cell histiocytoma) {30252693}.

Electron microscopy

Historically, the ultrastructural hallmark is the Birbeck granule, which is a 200–400 nm long and 33 nm wide cytoplasmic structure with a tennis-racket shape and a zipper-like appearance.

Immunohistochemistry

LCH cells express CD1a (membranous), CD207 (langerin; granular cytoplasmic; the surrogate for Birbeck granules), and CD68 (Golgi dot-like staining) {15630529; 26461144}. S100 is nuclear and cytoplasmic, and CD45 expression is low membranous. The Ki-67 proliferation index is low (typically < 10% by double staining) {12121233; 29263218}. Downstream markers of MAPK activation include cyclin D1 and phosphorylated ERK positivity in LCH cells {28622183}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis is age- and site-dependent.

LCH should be distinguished from inflammatory dermal CD1a+ dendritic cell hyperplasia by the presence of branched morphology and low/negative CD207 (langerin) expression with a tight perivascular distribution {26461144; 25629953; 34958507}. Associated dermal macrophages and eosinophils also accompany these features, especially in postinflammatory hypersensitive reactions. The clinical differential includes scabies, viral infections (e.g. orf), prurigo / arthropod bite reactions, and spongiotic dermatoses.

Other histiocytosis, including juvenile xanthogranuloma, Erdheim–Chester disease, and ALK-positive histiocytosis, should be considered {26454140; 25629953}. In patients with a previous diagnosis of LCH, tissue sampling demonstrating only histiocytes with monocyte-macrophage immunophenotype and BRAF p.V600E can be demonstrated in the blood, bone marrow {28194436}, or CNS {29624648}; however, at cutaneous sites, other concomitant histiocytic lesions (i.e. juvenile xanthogranuloma and Erdheim–Chester disease) should be considered first. Moderate to severe atypia with nuclear enlargement, nucleoli, atypical mitoses, and CD1a / CD207 (langerin) expression should prompt consideration of a malignant histiocytic neoplasm, such as Langerhans cell sarcoma {34620874; 26461144} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Properly validated molecular-based immunohistochemistry (mutant-specific VE1 antibody clone with moderate to strong cytoplasmic staining) may be used in conjunction with sensitive nucleic-based assays in detecting BRAF p.V600E {30923995; 28219109}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large, round to oval epithelioid cells/histiocytes with grooved to convoluted nuclei and a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a+, CD207+) in the correct histological/anatomical context.

Desirable: mutation analysis of the MAPK pathway, with sensitive testing for low-frequency variant allelic detection.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Diagnostic and staging evaluation should document the extent of systemic disease involvement after tissue biopsy, even in congenital LCH. Radiographic skeletal survey, 99mTc bone scan, and MRI brain imaging with gadolinium are used in diagnostic staging, but FDG PET-CT–guided imaging is increasingly used for detection of active lesions, especially in follow-up {25827831; 33442960; 32106306}. SS-LCH and MS-LCH may be distinguished to assist choice of treatment {23109216; 34620874; 33901419}.

Prognosis is dependent on stage at presentation. In SS-LCH there is a 99% survival rate, although a subset of patients may subsequently develop permanent consequences or MS-LCH {11496348}. MS-LCH with risk organ involvement has a variable course, with only about 60% of cases achieving no evidence of disease after 1 year on standard LCH-III therapy. In MS-LCH without high-risk organ involvement, there is still about a 37% relapse/reactivation rate after 1 year of standard therapy with vinblastine and prednisone {23589673}.

Although there is no formal recommendation set forth for upfront molecular testing, evidence shows that BRAF p.V600E is highly associated with increased risk of relapse, MS-LCH {24638167; 27382093}, and worse clinical outcomes (including permanent consequences with diabetes insipidus and neurodegenerative complications), when associated with circulating mutant cells {27382093}. Therapy targeted at BRAF p.V600E has shown efficacy as a single agent {31513482; 30718231; 32092141; 32985015; 30157397}, and its role in therapy is being explored.
