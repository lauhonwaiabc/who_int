4.4.3.4 T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

Definition

T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma (T-ALL/LBL) arises from haematopoietic progenitors committed to T-cell lineage.

By convention, the term “leukaemia” (T-ALL) is used when peripheral blood and bone marrow are the primary site of involvement, and the term “lymphoma” (T-LBL) when the primary involvement is of the lymph nodes, thymus, or other extranodal sites. The distinction becomes arbitrary when both marrow and non-marrow sites are involved, although many treatment protocols use > 25% marrow blasts to define leukaemia.

ICD-O coding

9837/3 T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2A71 & XH50W7 Precursor T-lymphoblastic neoplasms & T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: precursor T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma; T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Cutaneous lesions are most commonly seen in the head and neck. Involvement of the chest, back, and extremities has been described {24314877; 1810981}. Presentation at extramedullary sites (such as skin, but also tonsils, liver, spleen, CNS, and testes) without nodal or mediastinal involvement is uncommon {24314877; 25966987}.

Clinical features

Cutaneous involvement usually occurs at the time of original diagnosis with bone marrow or nodal disease {24314877}. Solitary leukaemia cutis is exceedingly rare {12780731; 21895759}. Patients typically show one or multiple subcutaneous, erythematous to violaceous, firm, painless nodules {24314877}. Presentation as a maculopapular eruption is rare {10343465; 32732689}.

Epidemiology

T-ALL accounts for about 15% of child­hood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia cases; it is more common in adolescents than in younger children, and more common in males than in females. Cutaneous involvement occurs in < 5% of T-ALL/LBL cases {24314877}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown; however, one report of T-ALL cases in monozygotic twins suggested an in utero origin of the earliest genetic lesions {8978302}.

Pathogenesis

T-ALL/LBL develops through a multistep accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations in oncogenic and tumour suppressor pathways, ribosomal genes, and deregulated expression of oncogenic microRNAs or long non-coding RNAs leading to altered cell growth, survival, and differentiation {32185137}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

T-ALL/LBL most commonly presents as a prominent monotonous infiltrate of blasts involving the dermis and subcutis, and sparing the epidermis with a grenz zone. Adnexal structures are intact; however, focal infiltration of the adnexa can occur. An infiltrative single-file pattern of blasts dissecting collagen bundles can occur. T-ALL/LBL rarely shows subtle periadnexal and perivascular distribution {32732689}, or epidermotropism {29443697; 17667175}, which can present a diagnostic challenge. Blasts are small to medium-sized, with high N:C ratios, round to irregular nuclei, finely dispersed chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant cytoplasm. A starry-sky pattern has been described. Mitoses and apoptotic debris are associated with a high proliferation rate.

Immunophenotype

The immunophenotype has been extensively studied in bone marrow, peripheral blood, and lymph nodes. Blasts are positive for CD3, CD7, and CD5 in 84–97% of cases {22994934}. CD3 antigen defines T-cell lineage. The intensity of immunohistochemical CD3 expression varies and can be weaker than in mature T cells, especially in cases with only cytoplasmic CD3 expression. CD7 expression is often uniform. The expression of CD5 is variable and may be absent, particularly in more immature precortical stages including an early T-precursor stage. The expression of CD2 and CD10 is common, and CD4 and CD8 are coexpressed in approximately 50% of cases. TdT is present in the majority of cases. Additional markers of immaturity include CD1a, CD34, KIT (CD117), and CD99 {22994934; 9110287; 26276771}. TAL1 and LMO2 are frequent {7672800; 26796495}. Rarely, heterogeneous expression of CD79a, CD19, CD13, and CD33 has been reported.

Differential diagnosis

Because of similar cytomorphological features, the differential diagnosis includes other examples of leukaemia cutis, such as rare cases of B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma and acute myeloid leukaemia (specifically, acute myeloid leukaemia with minimal differentiation and without maturation).

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clonal TRB and/or TRG gene rearrangements are seen in > 90% of cases, and IGH gene rearrangements in approximately 20% of cases {10419594; 10361104}. The chromosomal abnormalities are seen in 50–70% of bone marrow samples {16826225; 17369128} but are rarely studied in cutaneous lesions. The most common genetic alterations are mutations in the NOTCH1 pathway, translocations of transcription factors to chromosomal regions of TR loci, and mutations in epigenetic regulators and chromatin modifiers. In addition, the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, and RAS/MAPK signalling pathways, as well as cell-cycle regulatory genes, are frequently involved {32185137; 24140475; 33467425; 29034206; 34661151}. Several genetic subgroups and molecular markers have been shown to correlate with prognosis {21112032; 18691165; 28671688; 33152759}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cutaneous involvement by a proliferation of immature T cells defined by the expression of CD3; an aberrant immunophenotype including markers of immaturity such as TdT.

Desirable: involvement of the mediastinum, nodal sites, or bone marrow.

Staging

None

Prognosis and prediction

T-ALL/LBL is an aggressive disease with favourable outcomes when treated with systemic chemotherapy. The long-term response rates approach 85% in children and adolescents, and 60% in adults {32813610; 26888258; 24708207; 33026184}. Outcomes of relapsed T-ALL/LBL are poor, because of limited therapeutic options {34010787}.
