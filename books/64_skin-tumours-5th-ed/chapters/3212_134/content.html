3.2.1.2 Pilomatricoma

Definition

Pilomatricoma is a benign cutaneous adnexal neoplasm that shows differentiation towards matrical cells and the hair cortex.

ICD-O coding

8110/0 Pilomatricoma

ICD-11 coding

2F22 & XH9E37 Benign neoplasms of epidermal appendages & Pilomatricoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: pilomatrixoma.

Not recommended: calcifying epithelioma of Malherbe.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lesions have a predilection for the head and neck (affected in 50% of cases), followed by the extremities (upper: 25% of cases, lower: 10% of cases) and the trunk (15% of cases) {20711110}.

Clinical features

Patients with pilomatricoma present with a solitary, mobile, often hard (due to calcification) papule or nodule, measuring 10–30 mm {24838222}. The overlying skin may look normal, show red or bluish discolouration, or be anetodermic or bullous/lymphangiectatic {22307232; 22937738}. Giant lesions {34291738} and perforating tumours {25516090; 22937738} due to the transepidermal elimination of neoplastic cells have been reported. Approximately 5% of patients have multiple pilomatricomas, usually in association with genetic syndromes (see below), although rare non-syndromic cases have been described {6504775; 9436837; 11254436}.

Epidemiology

Pilomatricoma is common, accounting for about 1% of all pathologically diagnosed benign skin lesions {20711110}. It is the most common non-melanocytic cutaneous tumour excised in children {21034707}. Pilomatricomas occur across a wide age range (3 months to 93 years) with two peaks of incidence, the first in children and adolescents (between the first and second decade of life) and the second in adults and elderly patients. Nearly half of all cases are diagnosed during the first two decades of life {20711110}. Most studies have noted a slight female predominance {23895971; 20594627; 27240514}.

Etiology

The majority of pilomatricomas are sporadic; however, pilomatricomas can also be familial {24838222} or multiple {23347315}, often in the setting of complex genetic syndromes such as myotonic dystrophy, Gardner syndrome, Turner syndrome, Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome, Goldenhar syndrome, Kabuki syndrome, Sotos syndrome, trisomy 9, gliomatosis cerebri, panhypopituitarism, MUTYH-associated polyposis, and DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome.

Pathogenesis

Most pilomatricomas harbour activating mutations in exon 3 of the gene encoding β-catenin (CTNNB1); the 92 kDa protein encoded by this gene is involved in cell–cell adhesion and in the WNT signalling pathway, which regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival {24878016}. Matrical cells often harbour trisomy of chromosome 18, which carries the antiapoptotic gene BCL2, suggesting that the BCL2 oncoprotein may play a role in the growth and differentiation of pilomatricoma {20495544}. Germline mutation of PLCD1 contributes to familial multiple pilomatricomas through the PKD / ERK1/2 cascade and TRPV6 {32795530}.

Macroscopic appearance

Pilomatricomas are nodulocystic and often hard due to calcification/ossification.

Histopathology

Pilomatricomas consist of one or multiple well-circumscribed dermal nodules, often involving the subcutis. Neoplastic cells have a variable appearance. Peripheral cells are monomorphic, round, small, and deeply basophilic, with indistinct borders, central vesicular nuclei, and prominent nucleoli, and they are mitotically active. These peripheral matrical cells keratinize towards shadow (or ghost or mummified) cells. In rare examples, acantholysis between matrical cells has been described {2073963}. Shadow cells show distinct borders, eosinophilic cytoplasm, and an empty space where the nucleus was once located. Aggregates of shadow cells show yellow-orange staining and are refractile under polarized light. The transition from matrical cells to shadow cells occurs via intermediate cells characterized by a small, pyknotic, basophilic nucleus and pale cytoplasm. The ratio of matrical cells to shadow cells decreases with increasing age of the lesion. Pilomatricomas are often cystic (most commonly those associated with Gardner syndrome). Calcification and ossification occur in longstanding lesions; in some of these, very few shadow cells remain. The surrounding dermis is fibrotic and often contains foreign body granulomas around shadow cells and calcium deposits. Rare cases are connected to the surface epidermis. Pathological patterns include pigmented {7309977} and proliferating pilomatricoma {9138114} (consisting predominantly of mitotically active matrical cells). In cases with bone metaplasia, extramedullary haematopoiesis may occur {8600776}. Basaloid matrical cells express nuclear β-catenin {21239898; 22307232}.

Cytology

FNAB produces smears with recognizable clusters of basaloid cells and foreign body–type multinucleated cells {29370510; 30498290}. Ghost cells can be difficult to find {29370510}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: neoplastic aggregates of matrical, intermediate, and shadow (ghost) cells in variable proportions, depending of the age of the lesion.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Pilomatricomas are benign tumours, and simple excision is curative. Rarely, tumours may persist, usually when incompletely excised {20711110; 23895971}.
