4.3.1.5 EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer

Definition

EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer (EBVMCU) is an immune deficiency/dysregulation–associated B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder with a polymorphous infiltrate that includes EBV-positive atypical large and Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg (HRS)-like cells and involves the mucosal surfaces and the skin.

Localization

EBVMCU involves the skin and mucosal sites including the oral/genital mucosa, tonsils, palate, and gastrointestinal tract {20154586; 19889052; 29314151; 30615240; 30417601}.

Clinical features

EBVMCU typically manifests as a well-circumscribed, often painful ulcer in mucosal or cutaneous sites, without systemic symptoms, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or bone marrow involvement {20154586; 22769419; 25007145; 30418184; 32561847; 27988064}. Multifocal lesions within one anatomical region may be seen {20154586; 30418184}.

Epidemiology

No significant data are currently available on the incidence of EBVMCU. Presentation is generally in elderly patients (> 70 years). EBVMCU occurs in a variety of inborn and acquired immune deficiency/dysregulation contexts, including autoimmunity, therapy-related immune dysregulation (including with methotrexate), HIV infection, post–organ transplantation, and immunosenescence {20154586; 34441310; 29314151; 22769419; 25007145; 32561847}.

Etiology

EBV is present in all cases.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is for the most part unknown. IG gene rearrangements are detected in 50% of cases and are most likely due to clonal expansions of EBV-infected B cells; rare clonal TR gene rearrangements can occur {20154586; 28395107; 32561847}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

A shallow mucosal or cutaneous ulcer is seen with a polymorphous infiltrate ranging from small to large immunoblasts and HRS-like cells. There are admixed plasma cells, histiocytes, and eosinophils. Angioinvasion, thrombosis, and necrosis are often prominent. Vague granulomatous changes can be present. The overlying epithelium may show pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia. The base of the lesion is sharply demarcated by a dense rim of small lymphocytes {33739792}. One study (of 26 cases) reported that a nearly constant finding is the presence of apoptotic cells with plasmacytoid features {20154586}.

At least four histological patterns have been reported: polymorphous; large cell–rich; classic Hodgkin lymphoma–like; and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma–like {32561847}.

Immunophenotype

The polymorphic infiltrate consists of a mixture of CD20-positive and CD3-positive lymphoid cells. CD8-positive T cells may predominate in some cases. The atypical large B cells and HRS-like cells are EBV-positive, express B-cell markers (PAX5 dim or strong, OCT2, BOB1), and are frequently positive for CD30 and IRF4 (MUM1), but they only rarely express CD15 {20154586; 25007145; 28395107; 30418184} or PDL1 (CD274) {30450620}. Small T cells typically form a boundary with the uninvolved adjacent tissue.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of EBVMCU includes polymorphic lymphoproliferative disorders (LPDs), EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and classic Hodgkin lymphoma, especially when there are increased large immunoblasts and HRS-like cells. The presence of necrosis and angiocentric infiltrates containing EBV in nasal or oropharyngeal locations may morphologically mimic extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma. The differential diagnoses are summarized in #36578Table 6.21 .

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Monoclonal BCR or TR gene rearrangements are found in more than 30–40% of cases and therefore do not separate EBVMCU from polymorphic LPDs and overt lymphomas {20154586; 32561847}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: setting confirmed or highly suspicious for immune deficiency/dysregulation or immunosenescence; careful clinicopathological correlation of a well-circumscribed shallow ulcer in a mucosal or cutaneous site in the absence of a tumour/mass associated with a polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate without systemic manifestations; atypical large cells positive for CD20 (variable), other B-cell markers, CD30 (variable), and PAX5; EBV positive in tissue (EBV viral load measurements in blood are not sufficient for diagnosis).

Desirable: band of small T cells at the periphery.

Staging

Formal staging procedures for EBVMCU are not indicated; however, when there is clinical suspicion for more extensive LPDs in a patient with known or suspected immune deficiency disease, including suspected inborn errors of immunity, staging workup should be performed to exclude additional and/or more aggressive LPDs at other sites. Imaging should also be undertaken to exclude mass lesions or deep-seated lesions.

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of EBVMCUs regress spontaneously or respond to withdrawal of immunosuppression where immune reconstitution is possible. Localized therapy may be needed in some cases {30417601}. Occasional cases may have a relapsing and remitting course without progression {20154586; 25007145; 28395107; 22769419; 19889052; 24456137}.
