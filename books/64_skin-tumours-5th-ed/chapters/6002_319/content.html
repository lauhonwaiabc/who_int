6.0.0.2 Familial melanoma

Definition

Familial melanoma is generally defined as melanoma occurring in two or more first-degree relatives. The term “familial melanoma” is generally considered to exclude conditions such as xeroderma pigmentosum and albinism associated with sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

See #36605Table 9.02 .

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Not recommended: familial dysplastic naevi/melanoma; familial atypical mole–malignant melanoma syndrome; hereditary melanoma.

Subtype(s)

Familial melanoma of the skin; familial uveal melanoma (BAP1 and MDB4 mutations)

Localization

Familial melanomas arise in the skin, in the uveal tract, or more rarely in other organs.

Clinical features

In past attempts to identify highly penetrant melanoma susceptibility genes, families were selected for inclusion on the basis of melanoma clustering alone (see #36605Table 9.02). The most common germline variants established as causal for familial clustering of melanoma disrupt CDKN2A. However, germline CDKN2A variants have also been associated with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer; this association is reported as particularly strong in families living in North America and mainland Europe, and weaker in the United Kingdom and Australia {16905682}. The apparent variation in susceptibility to pancreatic cancer may reflect genetic diversity (given that the melanoma-susceptible populations in the United Kingdom and Australia are genetically similar), but this has not yet been confirmed. More recently, smoking-related cancers have also been shown to be more common in families with CDKN2A founder variants (in the Kingdom of the Netherlands {18981015} and in Sweden {24935963}), suggesting that environmental factors other than UV radiation exposure may also explain some of the variation in susceptibility to cancers other than cutaneous melanoma. CDKN2A encodes p16 (p16INK4a), which binds to CDK4, and recurrent, activating germline CDK4 variants also lead to an increased melanoma risk. Families with CDKN2A variants are phenotypically very similar to those with CDK4 variants {23384855}; both groups tend to show a relatively early onset of melanoma (~10 years earlier than in the general population), multiple primaries, and a variable association with clinically atypical naevi.

The identification of genes that harbour germline risk variants (e.g. POT1) in families at risk of mixed cancer types has resulted in the recognition of different phenotypes. Some families with germline POT1 variants may have susceptibility to cutaneous melanoma {24686849; 24686846}, but other families studied for different cancers have shown different susceptibilities, such as to angiosarcoma {26403419} or glioma {25524796}. The true risk of various cancers in POT1 variant carriers remains to be determined. Families with inactivating germline variants in the BAP1 gene have a distinctive phenotype, described in a separate section (see section BAP1-related tumour predisposition syndrome).

In a study of sporadic melanomas and of familial melanomas from families with and without CDKN2A germline variants, the tumours in variant carriers tended to have histological features of superficial spreading melanoma, including higher levels of pigmentation and increased pagetoid scatter. Familial acral and mucosal melanoma has also not been reported. The presence of spindle cell morphology in the vertical growth phase was a strong negative predictor of CDKN2A carrier status; of the 15 cases with this phenotype, none harboured a pathogenic CDKN2A variant {25592620}. To date, no large series of melanomas in the setting of familial melanoma have been reported with histopathological and outcome data.

Epidemiology

In the time since familial melanoma was first described by Norton (in 1820) and later by Cawley et al. (in 1952) {14902112}, a family history of melanoma has been recognized as an important melanoma risk factor. A meta-analysis of 60 observational studies found the relative risk conferred by a family history of melanoma (defined as more than one first-degree relative with the disease) to be 1.74 (95% CI: 1.41–2.14) {16125929}. Generally, countries with higher levels of UV radiation exposure have a greater proportion of melanoma cases associated with a family history (Australia: 10.8% {27829101}, USA: 8.1% {15529312}, Denmark: 3% {3403065}), perhaps due to phenocopies within families from environmental risk. In a pooled dataset from 15 case–control studies, 6.3% of cases were associated with a family history of melanoma, with a very similar relative risk of melanoma of 1.74 (95% CI: 1.21–2.46) {25713022}. Many families with familial melanoma also have dysplastic (atypical) naevi, identifying individuals at an increased risk of developing melanoma. However, even in these families, melanoma may occur in individuals who lack overt atypical naevi. In about half of all families with familial melanoma, heritability is due to high-risk susceptibility genes (see Etiology, below). In a substantial proportion of the other half, heritability is probably due to intermediate/lower-penetrance susceptibility genes, most likely related to pigmentation and naevi {26337759}.

Etiology

Intermittent exposure to intense sunlight is an important environmental risk factor for melanoma {19359257}, but inherited genetic variation is key. Melanoma is largely a disease of light-skinned people, in whom the two well-established high-penetrance genes are CDKN2A and CDK4. Variants in these genes are identified in 30–40% of families with ≥ 3 cases of melanoma. Several other genes (BAP1, POT1 {24686849; 24686846}, ACD, TERF2IP {25505254}, TERT {26433962; 23348503}, ATM {3426215}, and EBF3 {29522175}) have recently been identified as very rare high-penetrance melanoma susceptibility genes (with variants of each currently found in < 1% of families with familial melanoma). Genetic susceptibility to melanoma can also be due to the combined effect of multiple inherited medium- to low-penetrance genes. To date, three medium-penetrance genes (MC1R, MITF, and SLC45A2) have been identified {26337759; 32341527}, all of which are involved in pigmentation. Further, genome-wide association studies have revealed 54 low/medium-risk loci, including MC1R and SLC45A2. The core 19 loci are shown in #36606Table 9.03 (adapted from {32341527}). These loci are associated with pigmentation or number of naevi; others are involved in telomere biology, DNA repair, or regulation of the cell cycle. For some of the loci, the biological role in melanoma susceptibility is still unknown. Gene–gene interactions may also contribute to the polygenic inheritance of melanoma {25892537}. Interactions between melanoma susceptibility genes and environmental factors (e.g. exposure to UV radiation and tobacco smoke) are also likely to exist; further study is needed.

For melanoma, the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA {26091043}) in the USA and the Australian Melanoma Genome Project {28467829} initiatives have provided the most comprehensive view of somatic changes in melanoma tumours. A common theme between the two efforts is the high percentage of activating mutations (> 90%) in the MAPK pathway, with about 50%, 30%, and 14% of the tumours exhibiting single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) or insertions/deletions (indels) in BRAF, NRAS, and NF1, respectively, indicating that constitutive MAPK stimulation is a core driver of melanoma growth. The RB1 and MDM2/p53 tumour-suppressor pathways are also abrogated in about 70% and 20% of tumours, respectively. Non-coding TERT promoter mutations are present in about 65% of specimens, and TERT itself is amplified in about 10% of cases. There are no known unique somatic signatures associated with any of the familial melanoma syndromes, but certain MC1R variants are associated with an increased somatic mutation burden; some (called R alleles {27403562}) show a strong association with red hair, and others (called r alleles {28024115}) show only a weak association.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis involves germline mutations in the same pathways described in sporadic melanoma (see individual sections on melanoma).

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance varies with the site of the tumour, with or without pigmentation.

Histopathology

See individual sections on melanoma.

Cytology

See individual sections on melanoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

See individual sections on melanoma. Germline sequencing is often required.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The essential criteria for a diagnosis of familial melanoma are based on family history of melanoma and other cancers, and they can vary geographically and also in terms of patient age. See #37896Box 9.01 .

Staging

Familial melanoma should be staged according to the site of the tumour using the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system.

Prognosis and prediction

The mutation penetrance of the most common high-penetrance susceptibility gene (CDKN2A) has been estimated in both the familial context {12072543} and in population-based sampling {16234564}. With familial cases, the overall penetrance was estimated to be 0.30 (95% CI: 0.12–0.62) by the age of 50 years and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.31–0.96) by the age of 80 years. There was a statistically significant effect of residing in a geographical area with a high population incidence rate of melanoma (P = 0.003). CDKN2A variant penetrance by the age of 50 years was found to be 0.13 in Europe, 0.50 in the USA, and 0.32 in Australia; by the age of 80 years, it was 0.58 in Europe, 0.76 in the USA, and 0.91 in Australia. In contrast, the lifetime risk of melanoma in CDKN2A variant carriers was approximately 0.14 (95% CI: 0.08–0.22) by the age of 50 years, 0.24 (95% CI: 0.15–0.34) at 70 years, and 0.28 (95% CI: 0.18–0.40) at 80 years using population-based estimates. The penetrance of rarer gene variants (e.g. POT1 and BAP1 mutations) in melanoma and other cancers has not yet been established. The penetrance of all genes may be modulated by other factors; this has been observed for atypical (dysplastic) naevi and MC1R variants, which increase melanoma risk in CDKN2A mutation carriers {20876876}. Participation in a programme providing skin cancer screening and education about skin self-examinations has been associated with thinner melanomas in members of melanoma-prone families {3381116}.

Questions to ask:

·    Is all the melanoma on the same side of the family?

·    Does there seem to be an excess of other cancers as well as melanoma, including but not limited to pancreatic cancer, uveal melanoma, sarcoma, early-onset breast cancer, brain tumours, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, mesothelioma, or renal cell carcinoma? If so, referral to clinical genetics will be appropriate.

·    How many cases of melanoma are there? The more cases there are, the more likely it is that there is a high-penetrance susceptibility gene in the family.

·    Have there been cases in young individuals (e.g. aged < 45 years)?

The families who require specialist counselling about risk include those with:

·    2 first-degree relatives with melanoma (in a low-risk region, e.g. southern Europe, this drops to 1 case; in a high-risk region, e.g. Australia or New Zealand, it increases to 3 cases)

·    2 cases (even including more-distant relatives) if ≥ 1 had multiple primaries or if the cases have atypical mole syndrome (dysplastic naevi), as self-examination is more difficult

·    ≥ 3 cases of melanoma first diagnosed before the age of 45 years
