4.2.3.2 Erdheim-Chester disease

Definition

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a systemic chronic histiocytic neoplasm characterized by infiltration of mature macrophages in different organs. The diagnosis of ECD requires the integration of clinical, histopathological, radiological, and molecular findings.

ICD-O coding

9749/3 Erdheim–Chester disease

ICD-11 coding

2B31.Y & XH1VJ3 Other specified histiocytic or dendritic cell neoplasms & Erdheim–Chester disease

Related terminology

Not recommended: lipogranulomatosis; lipoid granulomatosis; polyostotic sclerosing histiocytosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ECD typically manifests with bilateral symmetrical involvement of the metadiaphysis of long bones (> 80%), retroperitoneal fibrosis (60%), and/or periaortic infiltration (50%); it is often associated with CNS involvement (including pituitary involvement). Cutaneous lesions are present in 33% of patients {26785805}. Haematopoietic organs (lymph nodes, spleen, and liver) are generally spared {32187362; 29396850; 33901419}.

Clinical features

Patients display systemic symptoms (e.g. fever) and other symptoms related to local inflammation, fibrosis, and tissue infiltration by histiocytes (e.g. bone pain in nearly 50%). Diabetes insipidus and CNS involvement (e.g. ataxia) are also frequently observed (30–40%) {32187362; 26966089}. Hydroureteronephrosis (ureteric compression) is frequent, while cardiac tamponade is rarer {31423997; 34020934}. About 10% of cases are associated with myeloid neoplasms (chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia) {28679734}. The most frequent skin localization of ECD is xanthelasma (25%) {26785805}. Xanthelasmas are usually normolipaemic, are large and/or periocular, relapse after surgical ablation, and may reveal ECD. Cutaneous lesions include patches or papulonodules. Other skin lesions may reveal Langerhans cell histiocytosis, which occurs in about 10% of patients {24894769; 32969291}. The radiological hallmark of ECD is symmetrical metadiaphysial osteosclerosis of long bones, especially the distal femur and proximal tibia. Retroperitoneal/perinephric infiltration (“hairy kidneys”) and periaortic soft tissue involvement (“coated aorta”) are highly suggestive of ECD.

Epidemiology

ECD is a rare disorder of adulthood (median age at diagnosis: 40–60 years; M:F ratio: 3:1) {28214412}.

Etiology

The majority of patients with ECD have a somatic molecular alteration activating the MAPK cell signalling pathway. Clonal haematopoiesis has been described in 40% of patients {22879539; 26566875; 31768065; 33067622; 31221777}.

Pathogenesis

ECD often displays an oncogenic mutation in MAPK pathway genes, with BRAF p.V600E being observed in 50–60% of cases {22879539}. Other mutations are described in ARAF, NRAS, KRAS, PIK3CA, or MAP2K1 genes {25150293; 26566875}. MAPK pathway mutation can also be observed in myeloid progenitors in some patients with ECD {28566492; 28679734}. A model with sequential acquisition of driver mutations leading to the development of myeloid neoplasms and ECD from underlying clonal haematopoiesis has been proposed {28566492; 28679734; 31624111}. However, some ECD cases can occur in the absence of clonal haematopoiesis {33067622}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Histology alone is not diagnostic. Indeed, the histopathological findings of ECD are identical to those observed in the histiocytoses of the xanthogranuloma family. The most typical picture is an infiltrate of foamy macrophages, associated with Touton giant cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils {26966089; 29192649}. In xanthelasma, Touton cells are more abundant in ECD than in xanthelasmas with other etiologies {26785805}. Tissue fibrosis is frequently observed and may predominate, leading to a misdiagnosis of reactive fibroinflammatory process. Admixed Langerhans cell histiocytosis may be present in the same biopsy or other tissues, in cases of mixed histiocytosis {24894769; 32969291}. Occasionally, a morphological and immunophenotypic overlap with Rosai–Dorfman disease or with reticulohistiocytosis may be seen {31123032; 31955466}.

Immunohistochemistry

ECD histiocytes are positive for CD163, CD68 (KP1 and PGM1), CD14, and CD4, and at least partially positive for factor XIIIa, fascin, and S100; they are negative for CD1a and CD207 (langerin). Expression of phosphorylate ERK is frequent. Strong cytoplasmic positivity for VE1 may correlate to the presence of BRAF p.V600E.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis is broad and includes Rosai–Dorfman disease, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, adult-onset xanthogranuloma, juvenile xanthogranuloma, necrobiotic xanthogranuloma {16622091; 17135338; 19961259}, metastatic tumours {33782321}, and malignant histiocytosis. In the eyelid and orbit, it also includes adult-onset asthma and periocular xanthogranuloma {34637661} and xanthelasma. However, some clinical settings may help to exclude them easily.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

BRAF p.V600E or other MAPK pathway gene mutations are frequently observed {33901419}. The allele frequency of BRAF p.V600E mutations may be < 5% in a quarter of cases, thus not detectable by less sensitive molecular methods {30262559}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: collections of bland, foamy, CD163+ and/or CD68+ histiocytes in the appropriate clinical and radiological context.

Desirable: mutation analysis of the MAPK pathway genes.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

ECD is generally a chronic, progressive disorder. Prognosis depends on site and extent of the disease. Baseline assessment includes CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; FDG PET of the entire body; MRI of the brain with gadolinium; and cardiac MRI. CNS involvement is linked to a worse prognosis {29396850}. Targeted therapies (BRAF or MEK inhibitors) are very efficient in patients with severe forms {25422482; 30867592}. Approximately 10–15% of patients with ECD co-develop an additional haematological malignancy, negatively affecting their prognosis {32852896}.
