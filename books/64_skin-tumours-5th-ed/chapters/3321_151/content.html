3.3.2.1 Sebaceous carcinoma

Definition

Sebaceous carcinoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm demonstrating sebaceous differentiation. Depending on their anatomical location, sebaceous carcinomas are divided into extraocular and periocular (eyelid) sebaceous carcinomas.

ICD-O coding

8410/3 Sebaceous carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C33 & XH4VR2 Adnexal carcinoma of skin & Sebaceous carcinoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: Meibomian gland carcinoma (periocular).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Sebaceous carcinoma can occur anywhere but shows a predilection for the head and neck and occurs most commonly around the eyelids {7076199}: 75% of all cases arise in a periocular (eyelid) location {15749305;7076199; 11346408; 14663642;17728556; 24227981; 29696467; 32749765}. They may involve the conjunctival and/or skin surface.

Extraocular sebaceous carcinomas occur most commonly on the skin of the head and neck, followed by the trunk, genitals, and extremities. Rare cases occur in the oral cavity, salivary gland, lung, and breast {25748747; 21267703; 26247527}.

Clinical features

Patients with sebaceous carcinoma present with a tan-pink-yellow, firm, occasionally nodular lesion measuring 10–30 mm. Periocular sebaceous carcinoma may be mistaken clinically for benign inflammatory or neoplastic conditions, leading to a delay in diagnosis and to higher morbidity and mortality {8958530; 10218701; 6465419; 24227981; 32788569}. Pagetoid sebaceous carcinoma may resemble chronic blepharoconjunctivitis {24227981}.

Epidemiology

Sebaceous carcinoma develops in older adults, with no sex predilection. The median age at diagnosis is 73 years. The estimated incidence rate per 1 million person-years is approximately 2 cases in White populations, 1 among Asians and Pacific Islanders, and 0.5 in Black populations {18988294}. Asians and Pacific Islanders appear to be more predisposed to develop a periocular sebaceous carcinoma than other populations {18988294}.

Etiology

Risk factors include prior radiotherapy, sun exposure, viral infections (HIV and HPV), immunosuppression, and Muir–Torre syndrome {24227981; 9708943}, although periocular tumours have almost no association with Muir–Torre syndrome or mismatch repair defects {27287813; 31148199; 19515040; 24321472; 25427047; 28323777; 20055911}.

Pathogenesis

Extraocular sebaceous carcinomas can be grouped into three molecular-genetic classes based on whether they show an ultraviolet (UV) radiation signature, a paucimutational signature, or a microsatellite instability (MSI) signature {34023105; 29760388}. Sebaceous carcinomas with a UV radiation signature carry the highest frequency of mutations, including TP53, RREB1, and NOTCH family mutations. MSI-related sebaceous carcinomas more commonly carry HRAS and KRAS mutations together with TP53, RB1, RREB1, and NOTCH family member mutations. Although defective mismatch repair may be seen in some extraocular sebaceous carcinomas {27287813; 31148199}, most of these mutations are somatically acquired, rather than related to Muir–Torre syndrome {29760388; 27287813}. Finally, paucimutational sebaceous carcinomas show fewer mutations, but HRAS and NOTCH1 mutations are observed.

Periocular sebaceous carcinomas often harbour mutations in TP53, RB1, NOTCH family genes, and ZNF750, as well as gain of the MYC locus {27287813; 34445161}. A small subset of cases are related to high-risk HPV infection {30420449; 33745190; 29760388; 34023105}, and these occur in younger patients, with fewer recurrences.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Well-differentiated sebaceous carcinoma consists of lobules of polygonal tumour cells with atypical, oval vesicular nuclei and abundant intracytoplasmic vacuoles that impart a foamy appearance and impinge on the nucleus, creating scalloping. Central comedo-type necrosis may be seen. More poorly differentiated tumours show a higher N:C ratio, nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, basophilic cytoplasm with fewer intracellular vacuoles {28323778}, and brisk mitotic activity. Sebaceous carcinoma may show basaloid, squamoid (often with keratin pearl formation), spindle cell / sarcomatoid, or neuroendocrine (rippled or organoid growth) features {6465419; 15900121; 25521100}. Invasive tumours can adopt various architectural patterns, including well-circumscribed lobules with pushing borders and variable comedonecrosis, papillary architecture, and highly infiltrative cords; combinations of these patterns are often observed. Of note, whereas sebaceous carcinomas with a UV radiation signature tend to be more poorly differentiated, MSI-related tumours show more well-differentiated histopathological features {34023105; 29760388}. Approximately 60% of cases carry an intraepithelial component (pagetoid spread) with extension along and partial replacement of pre-existing pilosebaceous units in the skin, and pseudoglands of Henle in the conjunctiva. Notably, intraepithelial skip lesions may also occur {16008694}, and sebaceous carcinoma in situ (restricted to the epithelium) may be encountered {24666211; 20966738; 33201015}.

Immunohistochemistry

Sebaceous carcinoma shows immunoreactivity for pancytokeratin (and also CK7), adipophilin, EMA, BerEP4, AR, p40, and p63 {29087003; 28256768; 20118912}. EMA labelling may enhance the cytoplasmic vacuolization of the tumour cells, but it is often only positive in well-differentiated tumours and lacks specificity. Adipophilin is a more sensitive and specific marker of sebaceous differentiation {20118912; 29087003}. Positivity for CD10, DOG1, KIT (CD117), p16, p53, perforin, and factor XIIIa has also been reported in sebaceous carcinoma {25864700; 28394798; 26843360; 27171611}.

Differential diagnosis

Sebaceous carcinoma should be excluded when examining basaloid, squamoid, and clear cell epithelial tumours in periocular locations. The differential diagnosis includes sebaceoma, balloon cell melanoma, clear cell squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, and metastatic clear cell carcinomas from visceral organs.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not relevant for individual tumours. Germline sequencing may be indicated in some cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a firm, tan-pink-yellow, occasionally nodular lesion, sometimes resembling inflammation; an invasive or intraepithelial tumour showing sebaceous differentiation, manifesting as multivesicular cytoplasmic clearing with nuclear scalloping.

Desirable: immunohistochemical confirmation of sebaceous differentiation in cases lacking obvious histopathological evidence of sebaceous differentiation.

Staging

The eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system forms the basis for staging. The application of the eighth edition of the AJCC system was found to be useful in predicting nodal metastasis; however, local recurrence and distant metastasis were not significantly correlated to the T classification {32567368}.

Prognosis and prediction

Both periocular and extraocular sebaceous carcinomas have a 30–40% risk of local tumour recurrence, a 20–25% risk of distant metastasis, and a 10–30% risk of tumour-related mortality {18988294; 11801779}. Approximately 70% of all patients with sebaceous carcinoma survive for ≥ 5 years after diagnosis {18988294}.
