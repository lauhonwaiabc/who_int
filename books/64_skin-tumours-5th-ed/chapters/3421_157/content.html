3.4.2.1 Mammary Paget disease

Definition

Mammary Paget disease (MPD) is a neoplastic lesion composed of malignant epithelial cells within the epidermis of the nipple and areola. In > 95% of cases, there is an underlying breast carcinoma.

ICD-O coding

8540/3 Mammary Paget disease

ICD-11 coding

2E65.5 & XH3E21 Paget disease of nipple & Paget disease, mammary

2C6Y & XH3E21 Other specified malignant neoplasms of breast & Paget disease, mammary

Related terminology

Acceptable: Paget disease of breast; Paget disease of the nipple; breast carcinoma involving nipple skin.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

MPD involves the nipple and areola; in advanced cases, it may extend to the adjacent skin of the breast. One breast is typically involved, but there may be both synchronous and metachronous involvement {14102422}. A supernumerary nipple (or ectopic breast tissue adjacent to a supernumerary nipple) can also very rarely be involved {7962832} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/581.]].

Clinical features

There is usually an eczematous scaly or crusted lesion confined to the nipple or areola, sometimes associated with ulceration or inversion of the nipple and discharge. Rare cases are pigmented, resembling a melanocytic lesion.

Epidemiology

MPD accounts for 1–4% of all breast carcinomas. It almost exclusively affects women; < 1% of reported cases occurred in men {8955252}. The age range is 26–88 years. About 10–28% of cases are incidental findings in the nipple in mastectomy specimens, particularly in prophylactic mastectomies in patients with BRCA mutations {9704964}.

Etiology

Most cases are associated with an underlying mammary carcinoma.

Pathogenesis

More than 95% of cases are associated with an underlying mammary cancer, representing a retrograde extension of the breast carcinoma into the epidermis in a contiguous fashion through spread along the lactiferous ducts {35626023}. Cases without underlying carcinoma have been postulated to originate from Toker cells, a hypothesis supported by the observation of rare cases in which the Paget cells are genetically different from the neoplastic cells that form an underlying breast carcinoma {14691919}.

Macroscopic appearance

The nipple may appear normal or (more commonly) erythematous.

Histopathology

The neoplastic cells (Paget cells) have abundant weakly eosinophilic or pale-staining cytoplasm and large vesicular nuclei. They are scattered in the epidermis (pagetoid spread) in the form of solitary units, clusters, or (rarely) acinar/glandular structures. Skin adnexa can also be involved. Paget cells contain intracellular mucin in 40% of cases, and they may contain melanin due to phagocytosis (a possible diagnostic pitfall {19384061}). The epidermis is often hyperkeratotic and acanthotic, especially in longstanding lesions. An underlying carcinoma (most commonly a high-grade invasive carcinoma) may be present in the specimen. Paget cells usually have an immunoprofile identical to that of the underlying carcinoma. They express low-molecular-weight cytokeratins (with CAM5.2), CK7, human milk fat globule, and mammary-type apomucin EMA. As many as 90% of cases are positive for ERBB2 (HER2). Approximately half of the cases express GCDFP-15. There is variable positivity for polyclonal CEA. AR has been found in most cases; ER and/or PR are present in approximately 10–30% of cases {15920545}. Paget cells are negative for CK20. Rare CK7-negative cases retain expression of GATA3 {26944832}.

Differential diagnosis

Clinically, MPD must be distinguished from eczema and nipple adenoma. The histological differential diagnosis includes spongiotic dermatoses, candidiasis/dermatophytes, squamous cell carcinoma in situ (Bowen disease), and superficial spreading melanoma. Rare cases of clinically pigmented MPD contain melanin pigment, usually in isolated dendritic melanocytes, thus raising the possibility of melanoma. These isolated cells express melanocytic markers (melan-A [MART1], SOX10), while the surrounding Paget cells express CK7, EMA, and ERBB2 (HER2) {19384061}. In some cases, hyperplasia of Toker cells, a normal component of the nipple, may present a diagnostic dilemma {18614197; 19601945}.

Cytology

Some authors have suggested the use of imprint cytology diagnosis {35965905}, although this is not a widely used technique.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There is no known association with a specific gene mutation, although it has been suggested that the MSI1/mTOR pathway may drive the pathogenesis of mammary and extramammary Paget disease {32457396}. Genetically, Paget cells are similar to the underlying carcinoma cells in most cases {14691919}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large intraepidermal atypical cells with abundant pale cytoplasm and large nuclei with prominent nucleoli; single cells or small clusters typically leaving a single layer of basal keratinocytes; positivity for low-molecular-weight cytokeratins and ERBB2.

Staging

If no underlying carcinoma or ductal carcinoma in situ is present, MPD is staged as Tis according to the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification. If there is invasive carcinoma, it is staged according to the invasive component [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]].

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of MPD is determined by the features of the associated mammary carcinoma and the stage of the disease. The 5-year overall survival rates are 94–98% and 73–93% in the presence of ductal carcinoma in situ and underlying invasive carcinoma, respectively {35626023}. Detection of invasion from the epidermis does not seem to correlate with an impaired prognosis {23276177}.
