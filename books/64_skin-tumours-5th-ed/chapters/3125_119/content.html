3.1.2.5 Malignant mixed tumour

Definition

Cutaneous malignant mixed tumour is a very rare neoplasm resulting from malignant transformation of a pre-existing benign mixed tumour and showing microscopic heterogeneity.

ICD-O coding

8940/3 Malignant mixed tumour

ICD-11 coding

2C33 & XH0V86 Adnexal carcinoma of skin & Mixed tumour, malignant, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: malignant chondroid syringoma; malignant apocrine mixed tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The anatomical distribution is wide, with no specific site predilection.

Clinical features

The neoplasms occur as a solitary nodule or tumour and may reach a large size (as large as 100 mm). There is sometimes a history of sudden rapid growth. Multiple cutaneous lesions have been reported in rare patients who presented with metastatic disease {14501823}.

Epidemiology

This neoplasm occurs predominantly in the elderly, with no sex predilection.

Etiology

Malignant mixed tumours are thought to progress from a benign precursor. Although some cases show no evidence of a benign residuum, it is disputed whether these tumours can occur de novo [[Requena L, Hiromano K, Ackerman AB, et al., editors. Neoplasms with apocrine differentiation. Philadelphia (PA): Lippincott Williams & Wilkins; 1998. (Ackerman’s histologic diagnosis of neoplastic diseases series).]]. The tumours have been shown to be composed of epithelial cells and myoepithelial cells on electron microscopy {6194872; 6279274}.

Pathogenesis

By FISH, recurrent rearrangements of PLAG1 are detected in about 33% of benign (precursor) mixed tumours. Next-generation sequencing studies revealed NDRG1::PLAG1 and TRPS1::PLAG1 fusions {32345665}. Similar data for malignant mixed tumours are lacking. Array-based comparative genomic hybridization performed on samples from benign and malignant areas of 1 tumour demonstrated additional loss of 17p and 19p, gain of 19q, and loss of heterozygosity on 16q, supportive of malignant transformation {33470448}.

Macroscopic appearance

Patients present with variably sized, poorly demarcated nodules or tumours with an inconspicuous or focally necrotic cut surface.

Histopathology

A pre-existing benign mixed tumour (usually an apocrine mixed tumour) in association with a malignant component is a prerequisite for the diagnosis of malignant mixed tumour. The remnants of the benign neoplasm are either juxtaposed with the malignant tissue or blend gradually with it. The malignant component shows heterogeneity from case to case and can display the following patterns: adenocarcinoma (apocrine), myoepithelial carcinoma, carcinoma NOS, and sarcomatoid (metaplastic) carcinoma. Adenocarcinoma is composed of sheets of tumour cells arranged in nodules, with glands and papillary structures in which apocrine secretion can be identified. In the absence of convincing apocrine features, the malignant component may be referred to as a carcinoma NOS. Myoepithelial carcinoma is usually diagnosed in lesions in which the pre-existing apocrine mixed tumour displays prominent myoepithelial cell differentiation (i.e. hyaline cell–rich tumours). Like the benign residual tumour cells, the malignant myoepithelial cells have eccentric nuclei and abundant eosinophilic hyaline cytoplasm but display marked pleomorphism, atypical mitoses, and (sometimes) vascular involvement. Sarcomatoid (metaplastic) carcinoma is characterized by a gradual transition from a carcinomatous element to sarcomatoid tissue, which has the appearance of pleomorphic or spindle cell sarcoma NOS or of chondrosarcoma {14501823} [[Kazakov DV, Michal M, Kacerovska D, et al., editors. Cutaneous adnexal tumors. Philadelphia (PA): Lippincott Williams & Wilkins; 2012.]] {2458054}.

Differential diagnosis

As strictly defined, the diagnosis of cutaneous malignant mixed tumour should only be made when a neoplasm has a clearly identifiable pre-existing benign component. However, in most reports published to date, this diagnosis was instead based on the biphasic (so-called mixed) appearance of a neoplasm in the absence of a recognizable pre-existing benign mixed tumour. In some cases, the benign neoplasm may have been completely overgrown by the evolving malignant neoplasm, but it is likely that tumours of different lineages were also included in these reports.

Approximately 10% of cases of benign apocrine mixed tumour show focal atypical cytological features, including enlarged hyperchromatic cells and multinucleated bizarre cells, which are mainly seen in the areas with squamous cell metaplasia, among hyaline cells, or in foci of oxyphilic metaplasia {17707152; 17592277}. However, most of these lesions manifest benign architectural features and follow a benign course. An important diagnostic pitfall is the presence of intravascular tumour deposits in an otherwise benign apocrine mixed tumour, which occurs in 1–2% of the neoplasms {21785330}. An origin in another anatomical structure should be considered for tumours developing in skin overlying salivary glands, orbital tumours, and external auditory meatus tumours.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant as yet.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: adenocarcinoma, myoepithelial carcinoma, carcinoma NOS, and sarcomatoid (metaplastic) carcinoma associated with a pre-existing benign mixed tumour, the remnants of which either are juxtaposed with the malignant tissue or blend gradually with it.

Staging

Staging follows the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

There have been reports of recurrences and metastases to lymph nodes and distant sites in cases fulfilling the strict definition given above.
