4.2.3.1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Definition

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a clonal expansion of non–Langerhans cell histiocytes in the skin or mucosa that share a dermal macrophage phenotype.

ICD-O coding

9749/1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

ICD-11 coding

2B31.0 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Related terminology

Acceptable: disseminated juvenile xanthogranuloma.

Not recommended: naevoxanthoendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The head and neck, upper trunk, and proximal extremities are the most commonly affected cutaneous sites. The eye is the commonest extracutaneous site, followed by the lungs, brain, and spleen {8765620; 17520744}. Exceedingly rare systemic manifestations involving mucosa (mostly of the upper aerodigestive tract) have been described. The CNS, pituitary stalk, lung, liver, lymph nodes, and bone marrow may also be involved {8609257; 12717244; 15613853; 26189188; 29488239; 32468628; 31018833}.

Clinical features

Infants show one to several asymptomatic macules, papules, or nodules, developing in colour from pink to brown to yellowish, and frequently disappearing within months or years, leaving flat scars or anetoderma {7577591; 12717244; 15613853; 29488239; 32468628}. Dermoscopy shows a setting-sun appearance, with a yellow-orange background, surrounding erythema, and telangiectasia. About 5% of cases (mostly in paediatric patients) show multiple lesions {12717244}, either localized to the head and neck (benign cephalic histiocytosis) or more generalized (generalized eruptive histiocytomas) {8902096; 15613853; 23551579}. Adults show lesions that are usually a little larger and mostly solitary; extraordinarily rarely, lesions may be generalized and sometimes disfiguring (known as papular xanthomas, progressive nodular histiocytosis, or xanthoma disseminatum) {8902096}. Patients with such lesions may present with systemic symptoms, abnormal laboratory findings (blood count, liver enzymes, metabolites), or ocular involvement. Cytopenia is associated with bone marrow involvement; CNS lesions can cause seizures, hydrocephalus, or diabetes insipidus {8765620; 12717244; 15613853}.

Epidemiology

Most cases (70%) arise in the first year of life (20–35% are congenital), with a male predilection. Most deep, visceral, and disseminated forms occur before the age of 10 years, with onset during the first year of life in 50% {12717244; 34620874}. In the Kiel Pediatric Tumor Registry, JXG accounts for 0.5% of all paediatric lesions {15613853; 16416419; 32468628}. Solitary adult xanthogranulomas including vacuolated, spindle cell, and scalloped patterns are not uncommon and show no sex predilection; adult generalized or systemic patterns are extraordinarily rare {7577591; 8902096}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. JXG may occur in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 {15078345; 25516272} or Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome {23152840}. Whether there is a higher prevalence of juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia in patients with NF1 affected by JXG is debated {7632061; 28189268}; juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia has been reported in association with a preceding JXG sharing the same somatic PTPN11 mutation {32558336}. JXG in monozygotic twins may suggest genetic transmission {18789091; 21091658; 31768065}.

Pathogenesis

JXG is a proliferation of variably lipid-laden macrophages. Expression of fascin and factor XIIIa supports a histogenetic link with dermal macrophages {34620874}. MAPK/ERK pathway alterations with activating mutations of NRAS, KRAS, ARAF, MAP2K1, and CSF1R, as well as RNF11::BRAF fusion and NTRK1 fusions, have been reported {28752840; 31768065}. PIK3CD mutations, identified in a few cases, suggest a pathogenetic role of the PI3K pathway in some JXGs {25202140; 26637772}. BRAF p.V600E mutations have been identified in 2 cases associated with Langerhans cell histiocytosis, but not in pure JXG {22879539; 25202140; 26637772; 31685033}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

JXGs are unencapsulated, circumscribed lesions; the histological features vary according to site and temporal evolution. In recently formed lesions, histiocytes are intermixed with a variable number of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, neutrophils, and mast cells. JXGs are composed of macrophages {17786242; 34620874}, with a variable mixture of mononuclear vacuolated, xanthomatized, scalloped/dendritic to oncocytic, and spindle-shaped macrophages, as well as eye-catching Touton giant cells (that often develop in mature lesions). The Touton giant cells have a wreath of nuclei around a homogeneous, faintly eosinophilic core, and surrounding the wreath is a xanthomatized, poorly defined periphery. In the mononuclear macrophages, the nuclei are round/oval to spindly in vacuolated, xanthomatized, and spindle-shaped cells; and bland, ovoid, and indented in scalloped and oncocytic cells, occasionally similar to Langerhans cells. The cytoplasm is homogeneously eosinophilic to vacuolated, with prominent xanthomatization or a ground-glass appearance in oncocytic cells. Mitoses may be seen {12717244; 24582118}. In monomorphic JXG, one cell type predominates: vacuolated, spindle cell, scalloped, or oncocytic. Fibroblasts and fibrosis are dominant features in late-stage JXG lesions {9091465}.

Immunohistochemistry

Macrophages are positive for CD68, CD163, CD4, and CD14. In early lesions they are also positive for factor XIIIa, and in later and spindly lesions they express SMA and fascin {33901419; 34620874}. CD1a and CD207 (langerin) are negative except for a starry-sky background. A minority may display light nuclear and cytoplasmic staining for S100 {9550310; 29797336}. ALK is negative.

Differential diagnosis

Langerhans cell histiocytosis shows more kidney-bean to coffee-bean–shaped cells positive for CD1a and CD207 (langerin), accompanied by a more acute inflammatory process with a larger number of other inflammatory cells (predominantly eosinophils), oedematous stroma, and erythrocyte extravasates in the dermis, with spongiotic to psoriasiform epidermal changes. Ultrastructurally, Langerhans cells or Birbeck granules are characteristic. ALK-positive histiocytosis and epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma are ALK-positive by immunohistochemistry. Dermatofibroma / fibrous histiocytoma frequently shows epidermal, folliculosebaceous, and melanocytic induction; it usually shows peripheral entrapment of collagen and often shows haemosiderin deposits. Nevertheless, a precise delineation to dermatofibroma / fibrous histiocytoma may sometimes be difficult, and rare patterns of JXG/xanthosiderohistiocytosis reveal iron deposition on special stains {6491001; 32520307}. In adults, the greatest clinical and histological mimic of JXG, Erdheim–Chester disease, characteristically reveals metaphyseal long bone involvement, as well as CD163-positive macrophages that stain positive for mutant-specific BRAF VE1 {22879539; 26966089}; in children, Langerhans cell histiocytosis with BRAF p.V600E may indicate lineage switch related to primary disease {28194436; 29624648}.

Cytology

In extracutaneous lesions, cytology may help in the diagnosis of JXG; smears show mononuclear macrophages including Touton giant cells intermingled with lymphocytes, and eosinophils {21491610}. However, skin biopsies are more commonly used in cutaneous cases.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a cutaneous lesion with a variable mixture of vacuolated, xanthomatized, scalloped to oncocytic, spindle-shaped macrophages lacking significant nuclear pleomorphism, with an admixture of variable numbers of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils; positivity for CD68 and CD163; negativity for CD1a, CD207 (langerin), and ALK; for early lesions, positivity for factor XIIIa; for late lesions, positivity for SMA and fascin.

Desirable: presence of Touton giant cells; clinical exclusion of Erdheim–Chester disease.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Lesions in children usually show spontaneous involution within months to years. In adults, generalized and systemic lesions tend to persist or progress. Excision clears singular lesions, in particular those causing functional limitations {33901419}. Ocular JXG may be complicated by glaucoma, uveitis, and blindness. CNS and systemic JXG may behave aggressively {12717244; 26637774; 32383820}. Clofarabine, cladribine, and combined cytarabine with cladribine have been used in the treatment of systemic constellations, with variable outcomes {24106153; 34620874}. In therapeutic failures, targeted kinase inhibition is an alternative {32609843; 34620874; 31685033}. The mortality rate in systemic JXG is 5–10% {12717244; 26637774}.
