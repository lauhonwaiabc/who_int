4.0.0.1 Tumours of haematopoietic and lymphoid origin: Introduction

Histiocytic and dendritic cell disorders are presumed to originate from and/or show differentiation towards cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system, which includes monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The term “histiocyte” has been variably defined and used, equating with macrophages or also including dendritic cells. And the adjective “histiocytic” has been used for a broad spectrum of disorders that morphologically show large “epithelioid” cells with poorly defined, mostly moderately staining cytoplasm (e.g. histiocytic haemangioma, histiocytic breast carcinoma metastases).

In general, clinicopathological correlation is the gold standard of diagnosis for histiocytic and dendritic cell disorders. The current edition of the WHO classification of skin tumours describes four groups of histiocytic or dendritic cell neoplasms: plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasms, Langerhans cell neoplasms, histiocytic tumours, and other dendritic cell neoplasms (indeterminate dendritic cell tumour). It moderately differs from the current edition of the Haematolymphoid tumours volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] by leaving out interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma, while including xanthelasma and reticulohistiocytosis within the histiocytic group, which are not listed in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume. These differences and additional literature {8902096} indicate that the spectrum of so-called “histiocytic” neoplasms is broader than generally recognized, and the classification will need to be refined in the future as new information allows. In these entities, paediatric and adult presentations, as well as variable numbers of lesions (single or multiple), and presentations ranging from generalized to systemic are well established {8902096}.

This edition introduces ALK-positive histiocytosis and mature plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation associated with myeloid neoplasm for the first time. The term “indeterminate dendritic cell tumour” replaces the term “indeterminate dendritic cell histiocytosis/tumour” used in the previous edition of the WHO classification of skin tumours and refers to a clonal, generally skin-limited disorder presenting with single, multifocal, or generalized papules, nodules, and/or tumours.

Other amendments regard genetic alterations shown to be linked to biological behaviour, such as in Langerhans cell histiocytosis, in which BRAF p.V600E is highly associated with increased risk of relapse {27382093}. In those cases, BRAF p.V600E can serve as a therapeutic target {30157397}. Other genetic alterations have recently been identified in patients with histiocytosis, such as rearrangements in RET and ALK, which may be targeted by selective inhibition {31768065}. The clinical, pathological, and molecular findings of selected histiocytoses are summarized in #36566Table 6.01 .

Primary cutaneous lymphomas (CLs) are the second most common group of extranodal lymphomas {30635287}. Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas (CTCLs) represent the majority of CLs. They comprise a heterogeneous group of lymphoma entities with very diverse clinical features (papules, plaques, nodules, erythroderma), histological features (epidermotropic vs dermal and/or subcutaneous infiltrates of lymphocytes of variable size and atypia), phenotypic features (including cytotoxic and γ/δ-positive phenotypes), and genetic features {31352988}. Primary CLs present in the skin without extracutaneous disease at the time of diagnosis and often remain limited to the skin over long periods of disease evolution. The prognosis and treatment differ significantly among the entities within the CTCL group, as well as from those of systemic lymphomas with similar histological, phenotypic, and genetic features. Progression with extracutaneous spread usually occurs in advanced late stages.

Mycosis fungoides and cutaneous CD30-positive lymphoproliferative disorders account for 80% of all CTCLs (#36567Table 6.01). Although geographical differences in the prevalence of CTCLs and their subtypes exist, mycosis fungoides is the most common form of CTCL worldwide {29719015}. Primary cutaneous CD30-positive lymphoproliferative disorders, which are the second most common CTCL group, represent a disease spectrum including lymphomatoid papulosis and primary cutaneous anaplastic large lymphoma, as well as borderline cases that have overlapping clinical, histological, and phenotypic features {29719017}. The clinical presentation and course are crucial criteria for the definite diagnosis and choice of treatment. The other forms of CTCL are rare, with each entity accounting for < 1% of all CTCLs {30635287; 33940098}; this group encompasses subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma, extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous CD8-positive aggressive epidermotropic cytotoxic T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous gamma-delta T-cell lymphoma, primary cutaneous CD4-positive small or medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, and primary cutaneous acral CD8-positive T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder. This last entity was renamed from cutaneous CD8-positive acral T-cell lymphoma to cutaneous acral CD8-positive T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder based on its indolent behaviour and excellent prognosis {34988968}. In the previous, revised fourth edition of the WHO classification of tumours of haematopoietic and lymphoid tissues, these lymphomas were grouped together under the term “cutaneous peripheral T-cell lymphoma, rare subtypes”, but they are now listed as separate entities. The term “primary cutaneous peripheral T-cell lymphoma NOS” is used for the very rare cases that do not fit into any of the other known CTCL entities {30635287}. A new section on inborn error of immunity–associated T-cell lymphoproliferative disorders has been dedicated to cutaneous CD8-positive T-cell–rich (and histiocyte-rich) infiltrates that are associated with inborn immunodeficiencies but not with EBV infection. They manifest with generalized papulonodular lesions and show a chronic, protracted course, with slow and limited growth and variable prognosis {24813299; 30869812; 31953710; 34988968}.

CTCLs show diverse and complex genomic abnormalities, with genetic alterations in signalling pathways and epigenetic processes {27717961; 11929784; 30497668; 33162051}. The mechanisms that seem to be involved in regulating T-cell survival and proliferation include alteration of tumour suppressor pathways {26258847; 26192916; 26551667} and/or constitutive T-cell activation, as well as alterations of NF-κB signalling downstream to T-cell receptor {29981755; 28694326}, whereas alterations of the JAK-3/STAT3 and MAPK signal transduction pathways may contribute to the survival and proliferation of malignant T cells in CTCL {27540476; 20213502; 15141228; 31049933; 28182501; 21209378}.

Primary cutaneous B-cell lymphomas (CBCLs) account for approximately 25–30% of all primary CLs (#36568Table 6.03), with primary cutaneous follicle centre lymphoma (PCFCL) and primary cutaneous marginal zone lymphoma (PCMZL) representing the vast majority of CBCLs {30635287; 34958501}. These CBCLs show an indolent course, with an excellent prognosis despite a high rate of recurrence. In contrast, primary cutaneous diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type (DLBCL-LT) and other rare forms of large B-cell lymphomas, such as intravascular large B-cell lymphoma, have an unfavourable prognosis. Genetic analysis has demonstrated differences between PCFCL and DLBCL-LT such as MYD88 and CD79B mutations in the latter entity {28479318}, and it facilitates the identification of PCFCL cases with increased risk for systemic spread {33560380}. In PCMZL, three subtypes can be delineated {21107089}. The more common class-switched PCMZL represents a clonal chronic cutaneous lymphoproliferative disorder with similarities to a reactive B-cell–rich lymphoid proliferation rather than a true lymphoma {27986434}.

Reactive lymphocytic proliferations were discussed in the third edition (2005) under the term “cutaneous pseudolymphoma”, but they were not included in the fourth edition (2018). They are now included as cutaneous reactive T-cell–rich and B-cell–rich lymphoid proliferations. These benign processes can be induced by a wide variety of infectious and non-infectious agents {31237707; 35656820; 12271296; 34996655}.

Because many types of CTCL and CBCL show overlapping histological and phenotypic features, as do reactive T-cell–rich and B-cell–rich lymphoid proliferations, clinicopathological correlation is of the utmost importance to achieve final diagnosis {30635287; 34105822}.
