6.0.0.8 Brooke-Spiegler and related syndromes

Definition

Brooke–Spiegler syndrome (BSS) is an inherited autosomal dominant disease characterized by multiple cutaneous spiradenomas, cylindromas, spiradenocylindromas, and trichoepitheliomas (cribriform trichoblastomas), with rare salivary gland involvement.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

2F22 Benign neoplasms of epidermal appendages

Related terminology

Acceptable: CYLD cutaneous syndrome; familial cylindromatosis; multiple familial trichoepithelioma.

Not recommended: turban tumours; skin–salivary gland tumour diathesis.

Subtype(s)

Multiple familial trichoepithelioma

Localization

Spiradenoma, cylindroma, and spiradenocylindroma mainly involve the skin of the head and neck. Trichoepitheliomas predominantly affect the nasolabial folds but can affect the entire face. A phenotypic subtype of BSS is multiple familial trichoepithelioma (MFT), characterized by the development of trichoepitheliomas without other tumours {19730223; 23249834}. Salivary gland tumours mostly occur in the parotid gland {19455704}. Extremely rare occurrence of mammary cylindroma has also been reported {15252315}.

Clinical features

Spiradenomas, cylindromas, and spiradenocylindromas occur as multiple (dozens to hundreds), often coalescing, variably sized (mostly 5–30 mm) nodules that are skin-coloured, reddish, or blue. Trichoepitheliomas are bilateral, small (2–10 mm), often confluent, skin-coloured papules. Cutaneous tumours demonstrate growth throughout life, and their number increases with age. Unilateral presentations of tumours suggest genetic mosaicism. Rapid growth, ulceration, and bleeding (especially in older patients) usually indicate malignant transformation, especially for cylindroma, spiradenoma, and spiradenocylindroma. Salivary gland neoplasms (1–2% of cases) present as a freely movable mass usually measuring 5–50 mm in diameter, typically benign membranous basal cell adenoma. Multifocal unilateral involvement of the parotid gland, as well as bilateral metachronous and synchronous tumours, can be seen. Pulmonary metastasis from the skin is a rare feature {19194280; 19730223; 23249834; 29569226; 31085270}.

Epidemiology

BSS and MFT are rare, but the exact incidence is not known. About 1–2% of patients with BSS develop salivary gland tumours, whereas patients with MFT appear to have skin lesions only. Cutaneous tumours usually present from puberty, whereas salivary gland involvement is usually seen after the age of 40 years {23249834}.

Etiology

Germline heterozygous pathogenic sequence variants in CYLD are recognized to cause BSS {10835629; 31093340; 32298062}.

Pathogenesis

Most patients (80–85% with the classic BSS phenotype and 40–50% with the MFT phenotype) harbour germline sequence variants in CYLD, a tumour suppressor gene located on chromosome 16q12-q13 {10835629; 26329847; 16922728}. More than 100 private CYLD sequence variants have been reported {34744449}. Most are frameshift or nonsense and are predicted to result in truncated proteins. The remainder are missense and splice-site sequence variants. Most involve exons 9–20, which encode the catalytic domain of the ubiquitin hydrolase enzyme, whereas exons 4–8 are spared {20132422; 20502185}. Other rare mechanisms include large deletions in CYLD and sequence variants in intronic sequences leading to intronic exonization {19668078; 25347032}. Cutaneous tumours frequently show loss of heterozygosity or a second inactivating mutation of CYLD on the remaining normal allele {26861065}. Whole-genome sequencing of BSS skin tumours reveals recurrent sequence variants in DNMT3A and BCOR in addition to CYLD in as many as a third of neoplasms {31624251}.

Macroscopic appearance

Benign cutaneous tumours are usually circumscribed, unencapsulated, ovoid to round nodules with a smooth surface. In larger lesions (especially spiradenomas), the cut surface may show foci of haemorrhage and cystic change. Malignant tumours are poorly circumscribed and show a non-homogeneous cut surface {22978388; 25141971}.

Histopathology

Spiradenoma, cylindroma, and spiradenocylindroma are related tumours, usually well demarcated, multinodular or uninodular, with regular or somewhat pushing borders {21598248}. Spiradenoma is composed of small basaloid cells arranged in a trabecular, reticular, or solid fashion intermixed with paler cells and lymphocytes, an integral component of the tumour. A few tubular structures are often present. Droplets of PAS-positive hyaline basal membrane material can be seen. Cylindroma displays a jigsaw-puzzle (mosaic-like) arrangement of islands of basaloid cells surrounded by variably thickened eosinophilic basement membrane material. The peripheral cells often show palisading and are usually darker than centrally located cells. Intratumoural lymphocytes are absent or very few. Spiradenocylindroma combines areas of cylindroma and spiradenoma that are either closely intermingled or sharply segregated.

Malignant neoplasms evolving from cutaneous tumours include low-grade and high-grade lesions with a basaloid morphology, resembling salivary gland basal cell adenocarcinomas. Less common are adenocarcinoma NOS and sarcomatoid (metaplastic) carcinoma {19633533; 22588548}.

Salivary gland involvement in this syndrome is characterized by the presence of multicentric basal cell adenoma, which appears identical to cutaneous cylindroma. These tumours are usually bilateral/multicentric and well circumscribed, and they characteristically have a membranous growth pattern. The tumour nests show the characteristic peripheral palisading of dark cells, with paler cells located more centrally within these nests. The cells have a vesicular nucleus. The cells nests are separated by dense hyaline stroma {19455704; 28060371}. By immunohistochemistry, both epithelial and myoepithelial markers are present {23209336; 24206768}.

Membranous basal cell adenomas are distinguished from cellular pleomorphic adenomas by their consistent basaloid appearance, peripheral palisading, and the presence of thick hyaline basement membranes. Basal cell adenomas are distinguished from basal cell adenocarcinoma by the absence of invasion {25141971}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of biallelic pathogenic variants in CYLD will help in the diagnosis. ALPK1 hotspot mutations are a feature of sporadic spiradenomas and the malignant tumours that evolve from them; they are not seen in BSS skin tumours {31101826}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

The diagnosis of BSS is established in a proband with multiple skin tumours (multiple inherited and histologically confirmed cutaneous cylindromas, spiradenomas, spiradenocylindromas, and/or trichoepitheliomas).

Desirable:

Identification of a germline heterozygous pathogenic variant in CYLD by molecular genetic testing in a patient with multiple BSS skin tumours Cases with a cluster of a few lesions without a family history may be mosaic and not associated with a germline CYLD mutation; genetic testing of skin tumours may be necessary. BSS should also be suspected with a salivary gland multicentric basal cell adenoma, membranous-type, in a patient with single cylindroma, trichoepithelioma, or spiradenoma.

Staging

Not clinically relevant unless there are pulmonary signs or symptoms

Prognosis and prediction

The offspring of an individual with BSS have a 50% chance of inheriting the variant {32298062}. No genotype–phenotype correlations have been found with respect to the severity of the disease, the possibility of malignant transformation, or the development of extracutaneous lesions {15854031}.

Spiradenoma, cylindroma, and spiradenocylindroma are benign but progressively enlarge. Malignant tumours developing from these neoplasms may show local aggressive growth or distant metastases. Because of their rarity and microscopic heterogeneity, the exact prognosis is unknown.
