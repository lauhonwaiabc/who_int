2.8.0.6 Diffuse meningeal melanocytic neoplasms: Melanocytosis and melanomatosis

Definition

Meningeal melanocytosis is a diffuse or multifocal meningeal proliferation of cytologically bland melanocytes. Meningeal melanomatosis is a diffuse or multifocal meningeal proliferation of melanoma cells arising from leptomeningeal melanocytes and often shows CNS invasion.

ICD-O coding

8728/0 Meningeal melanocytosis

8728/3 Meningeal melanomatosis

ICD-11 coding

2A01.0Y & XH1BP7 Other specified meningeal tumours & Meningeal melanomatosis

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Meningeal melanocytosis; meningeal melanomatosis

Localization

Meningeal melanocytosis and melanomatosis involve the leptomeninges, often extending into Virchow–Robin spaces. Meningeal melanomatosis frequently invades the CNS parenchyma. The lesions generally involve large expanses of the subarachnoid space, with focal or multifocal nodularity occasionally present. Common sites are the temporal lobes, cerebellum, pons, medulla, and spinal cord {1869648}.

Clinical features

Neurological symptoms arise secondarily to either hydrocephalus or local effects on the CNS parenchyma. Once malignant transformation occurs, symptoms progress rapidly. Diffuse meningeal melanocytic tumours frequently occur in the setting of neurocutaneous melanosis. About 10–15% of patients with large congenital melanocytic naevi of the skin develop clinical symptoms related to meningeal melanocytosis {8859278}, and radiological evidence of CNS involvement has been reported in as many as 23% of asymptomatic children with giant congenital naevi {11252085}. The incidence of neurological involvement, melanoma, and death is significantly associated with the projected adult size of the largest congenital melanocytic naevus and presence of satellite lesions {23392294}.

Imaging

CT and MRI of meningeal melanocytosis and melanomatosis typically show diffuse thickening and enhancement of the leptomeninges, often with focal or multifocal nodularity {12533338}. Depending on melanin content, they may have an isodense or hyperintense signal on T1-weighted images and a hypointense signal on T2-weighted images {19755608}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of neurocutaneous melanosis is reported as 0.5–2 cases per 100 000 person-years. Melanocytosis mainly affects children, mostly in the context of neurocutaneous melanosis. Melanomatosis has a bimodal age distribution and may manifest in children with or without neurocutaneous melanosis, as well as in adults (mostly in the fourth decade of life) {25534128}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Neurocutaneous melanosis derives from melanocyte precursor cells that reach the CNS after acquiring postzygotic somatic mutations, mostly activating mutations of NRAS (chromosome 1p13) as a first hit in a multistep process {23392294; 23303902; 25534128}. Amplification of mutated NRAS has also been described in an aggressive form of neurocutaneous melanosis {26266759}. BRAF mutations occur in a minority of cases {25490715}. Copy-number variations overlap with those in cutaneous melanoma, even in the absence of malignant features at the histopathological level {27933403; 27988846}.

Macroscopic appearance

Dense black or dusky clouding of the subarachnoid space of the meninges is observed.

Histopathology

The tumour cells may assume a variety of shapes, including spindled, round, oval, and cuboidal. In meningeal melanocytosis, individual cells are cytologically bland and accumulate within the subarachnoid and Virchow–Robin spaces. Lesions that show unequivocal invasion of the CNS parenchyma {19755608} or that have cytological atypia, mitotic activity, or necrosis warrant a diagnosis of meningeal melanomatosis.

Immunohistochemistry

The tumour cells typically express S100, HMB45, melan-A, and SOX10 {25371847; 35060532; 34463389}.

Differential diagnosis

Distinction from metastasis of cutaneous melanoma may be impossible using microscopy alone without clinical correlation and additional molecular testing {24274719; 25534128; 25399693}.

Cytology

Cytology may reveal atypical, often pigmented cells that often have epithelioid features but immunocytochemically express melanocytic markers {27696542}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

See the Diagnostic molecular pathology subsection in section Circumscribed meningeal melanocytic neoplasms: Melanocytoma and melanoma . In children, meningeal melanocytosis and meningeal melanomatosis are often associated with somatic mutations in NRAS.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

See #37959Box 3.04 .

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Melanocytosis may remain asymptomatic for a variable period of time, but once symptoms develop the prognosis is usually poor {25534128}. Melanomatosis is usually a very aggressive disease with a dismal prognosis {28253523}. Distinction of a primary tumour from a metastasis of an extradural (usually cutaneous) melanoma is critical for prognostication and therapy.

 

Essential:

Diffuse or multifocal primary meningeal melanocytic neoplasm

AND

 

 

For meningeal melanocytosis: No CNS parenchyma invasion, no marked cytological atypia, no mitotic activity, and no necrosis

 

For meningeal melanomatosis: Invasion of the CNS parenchyma, marked cytological atypia, mitotic activity, or necrosis

Desirable:

In children, meningeal melanocytosis/melanomatosis is often NRAS-mutant and rarely BRAF-mutant
