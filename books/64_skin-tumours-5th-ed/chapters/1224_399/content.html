1.2.2.4 Spindle cell squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Spindle cell squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a rare, poorly differentiated type of SCC; it is composed predominantly of spindled tumour cells with partial or complete loss of morphological squamous differentiation.

ICD-O coding

8074/3 Spindle cell squamous cell carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C31.Z & XH6D80 Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma & Squamous cell carcinoma, spindle cell

Related terminology

Not recommended: pseudosarcomatous squamous cell carcinoma; sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Spindle cell SCC typically arises in the sun-exposed skin of the face and head, neck, chest, and upper extremities. Similar tumours are found in mucocutaneous regions of the head and neck (e.g. the larynx, hypopharynx, oropharynx, and nasal cavity) {26203085}, as well as within the urogenital tract, and are well described on the distal penis (e.g. the glans / prepuce and foreskin).

Clinical features

Spindle cell SCC is typically a raised or exophytic plaque, nodule, or tumour, and it is indistinguishable from classic SCC. It is often about 20 mm in largest dimension and can reach up to 100 mm. Patients often present with bleeding or ulceration, and they may report a history of rapid tumour growth.

Epidemiology

These skin tumours arise most commonly in elderly White men and rarely in Asian populations. The incidence is highest among individuals aged > 70 years. The reported age range for penile tumours is broad (28–81 years).

Etiology

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure, iatrogenic ionizing irradiation, scarring burns, solid-organ transplantation, and immunosuppression predispose individuals to this type of SCC. Penile tumours are frequently associated with high- or low-grade squamous intraepithelial dysplasia or lichen sclerosus.

Pathogenesis

A monoclonal origin for these tumours is the currently accepted theory {12960805}. The morphological changes have been postulated to be caused by epithelial–mesenchymal transition, in which the precursor squamous cells lose polarity and cohesiveness and transform into spindle-shaped cells {26203085}, acquiring increased motility and invasiveness {23261444}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Spindle cell SCC typically occurs in a background of severe solar elastosis and is composed of closely packed fascicles of pleomorphic spindle cells, with frequent mitotic activity. There may be a background of actinic keratosis or SCC in situ. Keratinization is usually absent. The tumour involves the dermis and may extend into the subcutis and along interlobular fat septa.

Differential diagnosis

The most common differential diagnoses are atypical fibroxanthoma, superficial undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, desmoplastic and spindle cell melanomas, leiomyosarcoma, and less common tumours in the differential diagnosis include spindle cell and sarcomatoid lymphomas. The finding of an overlying in situ or intermingled keratinizing component can be helpful, but immunohistochemistry is required to establish the diagnosis in most cases. Specifically, positive staining for p63, p40, 34βE12, CK5/6, and/or MNF116 is usually required for the diagnosis, particularly if no epithelioid squamous component is present {26203085}. EMA, AE1/AE3, and CAM5.2 are less sensitive. S100-positive intratumoural dendritic cells must not be confused with the cells of spindle cell melanoma; other melanoma-specific stains can facilitate this distinction.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a raised or exophytic plaque, nodule, or tumour, indistinguishable from conventional SCC; closely packed fascicles of pleomorphic spindle cells, with frequent mitotic activity.

Desirable: surrounding severe solar elastosis; keratinization is usually absent.

Staging

Tumours should be staged according to the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system.

Prognosis and prediction

Spindle cell morphology itself may be a predictor of poor prognosis. Depth of invasion (particularly invasion into bone or muscle), as well as origination at the site of a burn or radiation damage (including sun damage), may be associated with worse outcome or death. Positive margins are associated with significantly higher rates of recurrence and metastasis, and with significantly poorer survival. Penile spindle cell SCCs have a high rate of metastasis and are associated with a high rate of death due to metastatic disease {25677263}.
