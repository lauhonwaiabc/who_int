1.1.2.1 Verruca vulgaris

Definition

Verruca vulgaris is a common HPV-induced benign cutaneous squamoproliferative lesion.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

1E80 Common warts

Related terminology

Acceptable: common viral wart.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The hands and fingers are common sites of occurrence {22131115}. The elbows, knees, flexures, face, and genitalia are among other reported locations {22131115; 9666831}.

Clinical features

Lesions present as single or multiple verrucous, keratotic papules or nodules, 1–20 mm in diameter {22131115; 9666831}. Facial lesions may appear filiform {1647902}. Occasional verrucae present as a cutaneous horn.

Epidemiology

The reported prevalence rates are as high as 16% in children and 3.5% in adults {1647901; 9666831}. Immunosuppression is a risk factor {22131115}. Verrucae have been reported to account for 10% of skin lesions in renal transplant recipients {16182734}. Lesions may occur on tattoos, and on the scalp in a possible association with barber clippers {34646630; 32315659}.

Etiology

Verruca vulgaris is caused by various subtypes of HPV. The main HPV types implicated are HPV1, HPV2, and HPV4 {22131115}. Others include HPV7, and HPV26–29 {2156916}. HPV75–77 have been detected in immunosuppressed patients {9146528}. The genital HPV types HPV6 and HPV11 have rarely been detected in common warts among children {9185912}. HPV16 (a genital type with oncogenic potential) was detected in 6.6% of non-genital warts in 45 immunocompetent patients {17083881}. Verrucae sometimes occur on the vulva in children and adults {17414099}.

Pathogenesis

HPV infects the suprabasal keratinocytes, the interappendageal epidermis, and the stem cells of the follicular bulge {26193301}. HPV DNA synthesis takes place in the superficial prickle cell layer. Full virus assembly with capsid production occurs within the accentuated granular cell layer. Viral particles are released via desquamated cornified cells {2156916}.

Macroscopic appearance

Excised lesions mirror the clinical appearances.

Histopathology

The lesions feature hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis, with prominent, inwardly proliferating rete ridges. Columns of parakeratosis overlie foci of papillomatosis. There is hypergranulosis, with coarse keratohyalin granules. Koilocytes are often present in the superficial epidermis {22131115; 23932731}. Dilated, tortuous capillaries occupy the papillary dermis. A lichenoid inflammatory infiltrate is sometimes present and may correlate with regression {7430479}.

Differential diagnosis

Verrucae should be distinguished from seborrhoeic keratoses, verrucous psoriasis, and epidermolytic acanthomas {19942951; 15900122; 33911795}; this is particularly challenging when koilocytes are absent, which is more common in older warts. Occasionally, distinction from an inflamed seborrhoeic keratosis is not possible, leading to the use of terms such as “verruciform keratosis” or “benign squamous papilloma”. Keratoacanthoma shares architectural features with crateriform verruca {26970425}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Routine HPV subtyping is not indicated.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: single or multiple verrucous, keratotic papules or nodules; papillomatosis; parakeratotic columns; hypergranulosis.

Desirable: koilocytosis.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Spontaneous involution may occur {9666831}, although it is less frequent in immunocompromised patients {22131115}. In patients infected with HIV, antiretroviral therapy often expedites regression {11903251}. Squamous dysplasia occasionally supervenes {2540085; 12142612}. Rare verrucae may progress to squamous cell carcinoma, especially in the setting of immunosuppression {7949492}.
