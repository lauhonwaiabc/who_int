1.1.3.3 Lichen planus-like keratosis

Definition

Lichen planus–like keratosis is a benign intraepidermal keratinocytic neoplasm with features reminiscent of seborrhoeic keratosis and an additional superficial dermal chronic inflammatory infiltrate with interface change, probably reflecting regression.

ICD-O coding

8052/0 Lichen planus–like keratosis

ICD-11 coding

2F21.Y & XH63L8 Other specified benign keratinocytic acanthomas & Lichen planus–like keratosis

Related terminology

Acceptable: lichenoid keratosis; lichenoid benign keratosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The trunk is most commonly affected, followed by the limbs. Presentation on the head and neck is rare {30565224}.

Clinical features

Lichen planus–like keratoses are solitary erythematous, violaceous, or brown macules and plaques with variable scale, measuring 5–20 mm {16148406}. They may be mistaken for basal cell carcinoma.

Epidemiology

Lichen planus–like keratoses are common and affect middle-aged to elderly adults, with a female predilection {16148406}.

Etiology

The etiology is similar to that of seborrhoeic keratosis, from which a large proportion of these lesions arise; there is an age relationship, but the cause is otherwise unknown.

Pathogenesis

Like in seborrhoeic keratoses, mutations in FGFR3, PIK3CA, HRAS, and KRAS have been identified in a subset of lichen planus–like keratoses {22188534}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

Lichen planus–like keratoses are well-circumscribed intraepidermal keratinocytic proliferations with variable epidermal hyperplasia and overlying hyperkeratosis or parakeratosis. There is an associated superficial dermal band-like inflammatory infiltrate composed mainly of lymphocytes and histiocytes. There is interface change with vacuolar degeneration of basal cell layer keratinocytes. Apoptotic keratinocytes are present, and pigment incontinence may be seen in the superficial dermis. In severe examples there may be subepidermal clefting and bulla formation. Late-stage examples may show epidermal atrophy and superficial dermal fibrosis {16148406}. A background of a solar lentigo or a seborrhoeic keratosis is sometimes recognizable.

Differential diagnosis

The most important differential diagnosis is with a regressed melanocytic lesion, especially regressed melanoma. Deeper levels and immunohistochemistry for SOX10 may be useful in the identification of a residual melanoma in situ component. Immunohistochemistry with melan-A or SOX10 may highlight clusters of small, cytologically benign melanocytes in lichen planus–like keratosis, often admixed with degenerated keratinocytes and macrophages. Benign cytology, a lack of pagetoid growth, and the presence of apoptotic keratinocytes can exclude melanoma in situ {33350494}. The presence of tumoural melanosis is a further indication of a regressed melanoma. Lichenoid actinic keratosis is separated by the presence of squamous dysplasia. Lichenoid drug eruptions and lichen planus occur in a different clinical setting.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a well-circumscribed intraepidermal keratinocyte proliferation with seborrhoeic keratosis–like features and a superficial dermal chronic inflammatory infiltrate with interface change.

Prognosis and prediction

Lichen planus–like keratoses are benign.
