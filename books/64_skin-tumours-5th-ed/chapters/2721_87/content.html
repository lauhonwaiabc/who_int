2.7.2.1 Melanoma arising in giant congenital naevus

Definition

Melanoma arising in giant congenital naevus is a melanoma that occurs in a pre-existing giant congenital naevus.

ICD-O coding

8761/3 Melanoma arising in giant congenital naevus

ICD-11 coding

2C30.Y & XH5L25 Other specified melanoma of skin & Malignant melanoma arising in giant congenital naevus

Related terminology

Acceptable: melanoma in large, bathing-trunk, or garment-like congenital naevus.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The scalp and back are commonly involved sites {16792745; 28078671}.

Clinical features

Melanomas arising in this setting are rapidly growing, often ulcerated nodules or plaques that differ in colour and texture from the congenital naevus in the background. Synchronous lymph node metastases are common.

Epidemiology

The lifetime risk for melanoma arising in a congenital melanocytic naevus (CMN) of any size is approximately 1–2%, and up to 10–15% for large CMNs with satellite naevi or CNS involvement {29999207; 28078671}. The risk of melanoma may be overstated in the literature because of the misclassification of proliferating nodules as melanoma in the past.

Etiology

The etiology is related to clonal evolution from the precursor congenital naevus.

Pathogenesis

See the sections on section congenital naevi for the spectrum of initiating mutations in giant congenital naevi. The genetic alterations that demarcate malignant transformation remain poorly understood. Like in common naevi, ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure probably plays a role in the generation of secondary mutations.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features, above.

Histopathology

In prepubescent children, the principal site of melanoma development is the dermis or subcutaneous fat. In contrast, in adults, most melanomas arise from the dermal–epidermal junction. Obvious malignant features are often present and include large expansile nodules with sharp circumscription, necrosis, and mitoses. Cytomorphological features may include epithelioid cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli, small cells resembling Ewing sarcoma cells, or spindle cells, and may be strikingly poorly differentiated or dedifferentiated. Focal neural or blue naevus–like, smooth or striated muscle, or adipocyte differentiation may be present {6261595}.

Immunohistochemistry

PRAME positivity, loss of p16 and H3 p.K27me3 immunoreactivity, and increased Ki-67 labelling favour melanoma {30045064; 34164835; 27849631; 27456754}.

Differential diagnosis

The most important differential diagnosis is proliferative nodules in CMN (see section Proliferative nodules in congenital melanocytic naevus). Features indicating melanoma are sharp circumscription with an expansive pattern, necrosis, ulceration, severe cytological atypia, poorly differentiated tumour cells, and a high mitotic count with atypical forms. In very young children, the distinction can be difficult {27849631; 27456754}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Copy-number analysis typically shows gains and losses of arms or segments of chromosomes in melanoma, whereas proliferative nodules typically harbour copy-number changes involving entire chromosomes {12368190; 28078671}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a dermal-based, frequently ulcerated malignant neoplasm, comprising expansile, sharply demarcated tumour aggregates; adjacent congenital naevus.

Desirable: high mitotic count; necrosis.

Staging

Melanoma arising in giant CMN is staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM criteria, like other cutaneous melanomas {29028110}.

Prognosis and prediction

Melanomas arising in giant CMNs have a poor prognosis, which worsens with increasing tumour thickness {25594732}.
