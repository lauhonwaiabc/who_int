5.6.1.5 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a benign neuroectodermal tumour derived from Schwann cells and composed of epithelioid cells with abundant lysosome-rich granular eosinophilic cytoplasm.

ICD-O coding

9580/0 Granular cell tumour

9580/3 Granular cell tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2E89.1 & XH09A9 Benign tumours of uncertain differentiation, soft tissue & Granular cell tumour, NOS

2C3Y & XH90D3 Other specified malignant neoplasms of skin & Granular cell tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Not recommended: Abrikossoff tumour; granular cell myoblastoma; granular cell schwannoma; granular cell nerve sheath tumour.

Subtype(s)

Granular cell tumour, malignant

Localization

Granular cell tumours preferentially affect the deep dermis and subcutaneous and mucosal tissues, with a predilection for the head and neck, trunk, and proximal extremities {26637199; 6246310}. The most frequently affected anatomical sites include the tongue, oesophagus, and breast. Granular cell tumours are most frequently solitary; however, 7–29% of patients may display multiple lesions, of which as many as 30% affect the skin and subcutaneous tissues {24082205}.

Clinical features

Granular cell tumours typically manifest as solitary, firm, skin-coloured to reddish-brown painless nodules that are not always well defined {26637199}. At times, however, these lesions can be pruritic and/or painful. The lesions vary in size, but they are slow-growing and commonly 5–30 mm in diameter.

Epidemiology

Granular cell tumours can be diagnosed in all age groups; however, they predominantly occur in the fourth to sixth decades of life {26637199}. Although they have been reported to occur more frequently in women than in men (M:F ratio ranging from 1:1.8 to 1:2.4) {11314193}, large retrospective studies have suggested a higher frequency in men {10089680; 6246310}. These tumours are most prevalent in African Americans {6246310}. The vast majority of granular cell tumours are benign. The malignant form is vanishingly rare (1–2%); this subtype has an age distribution of 3–87 years (median: 53 years) and is reported to be more prevalent in women (65% of cases) and in White populations (70%), affecting the trunk in as many as one third of the cases {28989104}.

Etiology

These tumours have been described in association with several syndromes, including Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome {12833416}, neurofibromatosis type 1 {26637199}, Watson syndrome, LEOPARD syndrome (multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, retardation of growth, and sensorineural deafness) {19054014}, and Noonan syndrome {22915371; 20537083}. Most cases, however, are sporadic in nature.

Pathogenesis

In 72% of cases, whole-exome and targeted sequencing analyses of these cancers have revealed mutually exclusive loss-of-function mutations affecting ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, which encode accessory components of the vacuolar ATPase and play pivotal roles in the regulation of endosomal pH and vesicle trafficking {30166553}. In a subset of granular cell tumours with wildtype ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2, loss-of-function mutations affecting other genes encoding components of the vacuolar H+-ATPase have been detected {30597645; 33289181}. These mutations are not only oncogenic but also shown to result in impaired vesicular acidification and altered distribution of endosomes – and consequently, in a massive intracytoplasmic accumulation of early and recycling endosomes {30166553}. The mechanistic basis for the oncogenic properties of these loss-of-function mutations remains to be fully characterized. These tumours have a low mutation burden and appear not to harbour recurrent oncogenic fusion genes {30166553}.

Macroscopic appearance

Granular cell tumours often form uninodular, firm, homogeneous masses that are white or tan in colour, involve the subcutis, and commonly display overlying epithelial hyperplasia {26637199}. The malignant form of these tumours usually ranges in size from 50 to > 180 mm; on sectioning, malignant granular cell tumours are firmer and deeper than their benign counterparts, and they may display a variegated texture and infiltrating borders {28989104; 9669341}.

Histopathology

Granular cell tumours have an infiltrating growth pattern with poorly defined borders and are composed of sheets, nests, and trabeculae of large epithelioid to polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic and distinctly granular cytoplasm. Cell borders are sometimes not distinctive, resulting in a syncytial growth pattern. Nuclei are usually centrally located, ranging from small, uniform, and mildly hyperchromatic to larger and vesicular with prominent nucleoli {10410172; 26637199; 9669341}. The finely granular cytoplasm is composed of granules of varying sizes and morphology, which are the result of a massive accumulation of lysosomes; larger intracytoplasmic granules surrounded by clear haloes (pustulo-ovoid bodies of Milian), which can be PAS-positive/diastase-resistant, are commonly observed {17448196; 26637199}. Perineural and perivascular involvement is frequent {26637199}. Mitotic figures are generally scant. When affecting the skin, granular cell tumours often elicit exuberant pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, which may result in the consideration of a diagnosis of squamous cell carcinoma {6246310; 26637199}.

The vanishingly rare malignant form of granular cell tumour is recognized by overt features of malignancy, including increased cellularity, prominent spindling, high N:C ratio, marked pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity (including aberrant mitotic figures), and necrosis {9669341; 21282016; 26637199}. It should be noted, however, that malignant granular cell tumours may display bland histological features, and neither mitotic figures nor necrosis alone can identify all tumours with metastatic potential {11584326; 19734486; 21745132; 24617529}.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunohistochemical profile of granular cell tumours includes strong and diffuse positivity for S100, SOX10, inhibin, and calretinin {22495377; 22804786; 26637199}. CD68 and CD63 (NKI/C3) are also common expressed, and NSE can also be positive {26637199}. Nuclear expression of TFE3 and MITF, in the absence of rearrangements of these genes, is common {26009539; 31788112; 17284958}, whereas melan-A, GFAP, keratins, and NFP are largely negative. HMB45, however, has been reported to be uniformly negative in these tumours {17284958; 26637199}. The Ki-67 proliferation index is typically low (< 2%) {26637199; 15214825}, but it is often higher in atypical and malignant forms {17227124}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes non-neural granular cell tumours, which can be distinguished from granular cell tumours by the lack of expression of S100 and SOX10, as well as by the absence of ATP6AP1 or ATP6AP2 mutations and the presence of DCTN1::ALK and SQSTM1::ALK fusion genes {34958499}. In addition, other diverse tumours with granular cell change, including apocrine neoplasms, naevi, melanomas, fibrous histiocytomas, carcinomas, and alveolar soft part sarcoma, should be considered.

Cytology

In FNAC specimens, discohesive cells, abundant granular cytoplasm, a low N:C ratio, and bland nuclei are useful cytological features {33493367; 30589232; 25379050}; however, carcinomas (in particular apocrine carcinomas) and histiocytic processes must be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Given that loss-of-function mutations affecting ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2 are probably pathognomonic of granular cell tumours, being detected in < 0.3% of other tumour types analysed to date {30166553}, sequencing analyses of these genes may provide additional confirmatory evidence for a diagnosis of granular cell tumours.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: nests, sheets and trabeculae of large, epithelioid to polygonal cells with abundant cytoplasm containing copious amounts of intracytoplasmic eosinophilic granules of varying sizes; S100 and SOX10 strongly and diffusely positive; must exclude other, more aggressive, neoplasms showing granular cell changes (e.g. melanoma, breast carcinoma).

Staging

Staging is not applicable for granular cell tumours; there is currently no evidence of the prognostic value of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system for patients with malignant granular cell tumour.

Prognosis and prediction

Given the benign nature of the vast majority of granular cell tumours, treatment is local excision alone; however, local recurrences may be observed in incompletely excised tumours. The rare malignant form can metastasize in as many as 50% of cases, including distant dissemination {26637199}. Features suggestive of malignancy include large tumour size (> 50 mm), older age at diagnosis, increased cellularity, nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, increased mitotic activity, necrosis, and local recurrence {26637199; 28989104}. In addition, there evidence to suggest that the SEER Program staging groups may be associated with the outcome of patients with malignant granular cell tumours {28989104}.
