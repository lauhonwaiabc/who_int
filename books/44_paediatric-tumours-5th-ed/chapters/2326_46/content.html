2.3.2.6 ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma (ALK+ LBCL) is an aggressive B-cell lymphoma with a plasmablastic/immunoblastic morphology and expression of ALK fusion proteins.

ICD-O coding

9737/3 ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2A81.8 ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: ALK-positive plasmablastic B-cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The tumour typically involves the lymph nodes, with mediastinal or extranodal involvement reported in approximately 20% of cases {28665943}. Bone marrow, gastrointestinal tract, liver, skin, and bone are relatively frequently involved {15852431; 26781614; 12750159; 16107887; 12816858; 19636007}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with localized or generalized lymphadenopathy, accompanied by B (bone marrow) symptoms in more than half of the patients {19636007; 22394191; 26781614; 28665943}. The clear male predominance in adult patients {19636007} has not been reported in children. The average age at presentation is 12–13 years.

Epidemiology

ALK+ LBCL is very rare in children, with < 20 cases reported in the literature {9057627; 15852431; 28665943; 19636398; 16107887; 12750159; 19250532; 12816858; 19636007; 16086416}. There is no association with immunosuppression.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The activation of STAT3 signalling downstream of the ALK fusion protein is probably required for oncogenesis, because phosphorylated STAT3 is highly expressed {18755494; 23599149}. In addition, the PI3K/AKT, PLCG2, and ERK pathways are important in the physiopathology of ALK+ LBCL {34151703}.

CLTC is the most frequently reported fusion partner for ALK in paediatric ALK+ LBCL {15852431; 16107887; 12750159; 16086416; 19250532; 9057627; 19636007}. Two cases associated with NPM1::ALK fusion have also been reported in children {12816858}. The other ALK fusion partner genes described in adults, such as SQSTM1 {21134980; 23588372}, SEC31A {20207848; 21109691; 24965113}, RANBP2 {24470379}, and EML4 {26781614}, have not yet been observed in children.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The tumours are composed of monomorphic large plasmablastic or immunoblastic cells with a round pale nucleus, a large central nucleolus, and abundant basophilic cytoplasm. A sinusoidal growth pattern is common in lymph nodes {9057627; 12763927}.

Immunophenotype

Most cases show restricted granular cytoplasmic ALK staining, characteristic of the CLTC::ALK fusion {9057627; 12763927; 26781614}. Rare cases with an NPM1::ALK fusion show nuclear and cytoplasmic staining {12816858}. Tumour cells are positive for EMA and plasma cell markers including intracytoplasmic immunoglobulin (IgA, more rarely IgG) with light chain restriction, CD138, VS38, Blimp1 (PRDM1), XBP1, and IRF4 (MUM1) {9057627; 12763927; 23599149; 19636007}. However, they are mostly negative or only weakly positive for pan–B-cell antigens (CD19, CD20, CD79, and PAX5) but can express B-cell transcriptional factors BOB1 and OCT2 {27740969}. They are negative for CD30 and for most T cell–specific markers, but they can be positive for CD4, CD57, CD43, and perforin {9057627; 12763927}. They weakly express or are negative for CD45 {9057627; 12763927; 19636007}. All cases are negative for EBV and KSHV/HHV8 {19636007; 23599149}.

Differential diagnosis

Plasmablastic lymphoma cells are typically EMA-positive and often CD79a-positive. An absence of ALK expression and a context of immunodeficiency are helpful in establishing the diagnosis of plasmablastic lymphoma. ALK+ LBCL and anaplastic large cell lymphoma share EMA positivity, but their distinct morphology, the strong expression of CD30 in anaplastic large cell lymphoma, and the positive plasma cell markers in ALK+ LBCL help in discriminating these two entities. An immunohistochemical panel consisting only of CD3, CD20, CD45, and anti-EMA can lead to a pathological misdiagnosis of poorly differentiated carcinoma. The dot-like paranuclear cytokeratin staining reported in some patients may render this distinction even more difficult {17277765; 19636007}.

Cytology

Cytology specimens show large cells with prominent nucleoli and abundant amphophilic cytoplasm {22351377; 24228132}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular detection of ALK translocation is usually not required for diagnosis, because the immunohistochemical positivity is sufficient. It can be useful in selected cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large monomorphic plasmablastic/immunoblastic lymphoid cells expressing ALK and plasma cell markers; CD30 negativity.

Desirable: EMA expression; immunoglobulin light chain restriction, IG gene rearrangement; ALK rearrangement in selected EBV-negative cases.

Staging

The majority of patients present with advanced-stage disease (stage III or IV according to the International Pediatric Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma Staging System [IPNHLSS]). Bone marrow infiltration is observed in 15–25% of cases {28665943; 19636007}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is poor, with a 5-year overall survival of 25% {19636007}. Limited disease (stage I–II) {19636007} and granular cytoplasmic ALK staining {26781614} are associated with better overall survival in adults. No definite prognostic factors for ALK+ LBCL have been established in children because of its rarity in this group.
