5.4.3.2C Non-ossifying fibroma

Definition

Non-ossifying fibroma (NOF) is a benign and generally self-limiting spindle cell tumour of bone, containing osteoclast-like giant cells.

ICD-O coding

8830/0 Non-ossifying fibroma

ICD-11 coding

2E85.Y Benign fibrohistiocytic tumour of other specified sites

2E85.Y & XA5GG8 Benign fibrohistiocytic tumour of other specified sites & Bones

Related terminology

Not recommended: fibrous cortical defect (confined to the cortex); metaphyseal fibrous defect; benign fibrous histiocytoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

NOF arises in the metaphysis of long bones of the lower extremities, especially around the knee and in the distal tibia. Multifocal lesions may occur.

Clinical features

NOF arises in skeletally immature individuals, with a peak incidence in the second decade of life. Although usually asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, larger tumours can be painful (probably related to microfractures) and/or cause pathological fracture {7240338; 18591758}.

Imaging

Radiographs show a well-defined, lobulated lesion centred in the cortical bone with sclerotic and scalloped borders. The tumours are aligned to the long axis of the bone; they are often eccentric and appear radiolucent. CT shows a well-demarcated and purely lytic appearance; MRI shows low T1-weighted and heterogeneous T2-weighted signal intensity. Multiple NOFs can develop sporadically but they also develop in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1, Jaffe–Campanacci syndrome, and the rare oculoectodermal syndrome {6643569; 7240338; 25808193; 30549028}.

Epidemiology

Although the true incidence of NOF is unknown, it is estimated to occur in 30–40% of children, rendering it the most common tumour of bone {7408072}. Radiographic studies have shown that 54% of boys and 22% of girls have NOF at the age of 4 years {19970624}.

Etiology

The cause of sporadic NOFs is not known. However, NOFs (single or multiple) may also occur on a background of germline or postzygotic mosaic mutations in NF1, causing neurofibromatosis type 1 and Jaffe–Campanacci syndrome, and in KRAS (oculoectodermal syndrome). Notably, in Jaffe–Campanacci syndrome and oculoectodermal syndrome, patients may develop multifocal NOFs and giant cell lesions of the jaws.

Pathogenesis

In sporadic NOFs, mutually exclusive hotspot mutations in KRAS and FGFR1 have been identified in > 80% of cases, indicating that NOFs represent true neoplasms driven by activated MAPK signalling {30549028; 30809793}.

Macroscopic appearance

NOFs are well circumscribed and appear reddish brown with areas of yellow discolouration, with sclerotic borders. Cystic changes may be present. Haemorrhage and necrosis can be seen in tumours with pathological fracture.

Histopathology

Tumours consist of bland, spindle-shaped cells that are arranged in a storiform growth pattern. Mitotic activity is generally low. Osteoclast-type giant cells are scattered throughout the lesion. Reactive features include haemosiderin deposition and collections of foamy macrophages. Reactive woven bone formation and cystic change may occur focally.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

KRAS and FGFR1 mutation testing can be considered in ambiguous cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a bone tumour with compatible imaging findings.

Desirable: storiform proliferation of plump spindle cells; metaphyseal location in long bones in skeletally immature patients; intermingled giant cells, haemosiderophages, and clusters of foamy histiocytes.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of NOF is excellent. Asymptomatic lesions detected incidentally do not require biopsy confirmation if imaging findings are typical. Treatment is not necessary as long as there is biomechanical stability of the involved bone. When ≥ 50% of the bone width is replaced or there is a pathological fracture, the lesion can be curetted. Local recurrences are rare; malignant transformation has not been reported.
