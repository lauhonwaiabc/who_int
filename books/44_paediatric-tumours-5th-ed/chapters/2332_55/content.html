2.3.3.2 Mycosis fungoides

Definition

Mycosis fungoides is a primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma with characteristic clinical and histopathological features. It is composed of clonal infiltrates of small to medium-sized mature T cells, which are tropic (at least initially) for the epidermis and skin adnexal epithelium.

2B01 Mycosis fungoides

Related terminology

Not recommended: Alibert–Bazin type of mycosis fungoides; cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

Hypopigmented mycosis fungoides; adnexotropic (folliculotropic and/or syringotropic) mycosis fungoides; pagetoid reticulosis (Woringer–Kolopp type); granulomatous slack skin disease

Localization

Mycosis fungoides is a lymphoma occurring in skin, mainly at the trunk and extremities. Extracutaneous dissemination may occur at advanced stages. Granulomatous slack skin disease affects the axillae and groins. Pagetoid reticulosis usually affects the extremities (hands and feet). Folliculotropic (follicular) mycosis fungoides often manifests with hypopigmented follicle-based patches associated with alopecia {29719015}.

Clinical features

Mycosis fungoides has an indolent clinical course. Hypopigmented mycosis fungoides is the most common type in children, usually manifesting as patches and plaques {29719015}.

Folliculotropic mycosis fungoides manifests as hypopigmented follicle-based patches and alopecia.

Granulomatous slack skin disease starts with patches and plaques and evolves to lesions simulating cutis laxa in the axillae and groins.

Pagetoid reticulosis usually manifests as solitary hyperkeratotic plaques at the extremities (hands or feet).

Epidemiology

Mycosis fungoides in children is rare. There are no exact data in the literature. Its reported prevalence varies from 5.4% to 18% of all mycosis fungoides cases {31277835; 9092741}. The subtypes of mycosis fungoides mentioned above are extremely rare.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Array comparative genomic hybridization studies, whole-exome sequencing studies, and genomic CpG methylation analysis demonstrated high chromosomal instability combined with a limited number of recurrent gene copy-number alterations and mutations leading to increased NF-κB and STAT3 signalling activation with resistance to apoptosis and deregulation of multiple levels of epigenetic control {29719024; 26551667; 26258847; 26082451; 26192916}. However, these observations have not been translated into clinical practice as potential biomarkers.

Macroscopic appearance

Children with mycosis fungoides most commonly present with hypopigmented macules, which may evolve in patches. Additionally, alopecia areata may be present in folliculotropic mycosis fungoides.

Pagetoid reticulosis usually comes to clinical attention with a single hypertrophic plaque.

In its early stage, granulomatous slack skin disease is indistinguishable from classic mycosis fungoides. The diagnosis can be made when pendulous skin folds in the axillae or groins develop similarly to cutis laxa.

Histopathology

The histopathology of mycosis fungoides varies with the stage of the disease.

Usually, there is a predominantly dermal, band-like, perivascular and periadnexal infiltrate of small lymphocytes with variable nuclear atypia. Nuclei may be cerebriform and hyperchromatic. Intraepidermal clusters of lymphocytes (Pautrier microabscesses) may be present. Microabscesses are absent in cases with previous local corticosteroid treatment. Also characteristic are lymphocytes infiltrating the basal layer of the epidermis. Skin changes include epidermal hyperplasia with psoriasiform changes, and dermal fibrosis with melanophages.

Neoplastic lymphocytes in mycosis fungoides and its subtypes (with the exception of pagetoid reticulosis) show an αβ memory T-helper immunophenotype. The lymphocytes are positive for CD2, CD3, CD4, and CD5, and they are negative for CD8. A CD4−/CD8+ phenotype may be more common in children than in adults {11914622}. Characteristic is an absence of CD7 staining.

There may be a progression to plaques and (later on) to tumours. The neoplastic lymphocytes progress to medium-sized and large cerebriform cells.

In folliculotropic (follicular) mycosis fungoides, neoplastic lymphocytes infiltrate the follicular epithelium. The lymphocytes have small nuclei often showing only slight atypia. Additionally, there is mucinous degeneration of the hair follicles. Later there is complete destruction of the hair follicles. Eosinophils are present in most cases. Reactive germinal centres can be present. Because the lymphocytes show only slight nuclear atypia, these lesions may be misdiagnosed as pseudolymphomas {11737522}.

In pagetoid reticulosis there is a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate of small lymphocytes located at the dermal–epidermal junction. The lymphocytes lack cytological atypia. The characteristic histological feature for the recognition of this subtype is lymphocytes infiltrating the epidermis in a pagetoid pattern. However, these lymphocytes display medium-sized or large cerebriform nuclei. The lymphocytes show a CD4−/CD8+ immunophenotype or (less commonly) a CD4+/CD8− or CD4−/CD8− phenotype {11914622}. CD30 expression is common.

Granulomatous slack skin disease is characterized by a dermal and subcutaneous infiltrate of lymphocytes, macrophages, and multinucleated giant cells, along with elastophagocytosis. Elastic stains highlight a characteristic loss of the elastic fibres.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

TR genes are clonally rearranged in the majority of cases. However, in early lesions the molecular analysis may show a polyclonal infiltrate, so molecular analysis has limited utility in the diagnostic workup.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraepithelial T lymphocytes with hyperchromatic, hyperconvoluted (cerebriform) nuclei; absence of spongiosis; dermal T-lymphocytic infiltrate; relevant clinical features.

Desirable: loss of pan–T-cell antigens.

Staging

See #36338Table 2.10 .

Prognosis and prediction

Generally, the prognosis of mycosis fungoides in children is good. Progression to an advanced stage is very rare {24890628; 31277835; 22176010; 9092741}.

Notes:

• For skin, “patch” means a skin lesion of any size with no significant elevation or induration; the presence or absence of hypopigmentation, hyperpigmentation, scale, crusting, and/or poikiloderma should be noted.

• For skin, “plaque” means a skin lesion of any size with elevation or induration; the presence or absence of ulceration, scale, crusting, and/or poikiloderma should be noted; histological features such as folliculotropism, large-cell transformation, and CD30 expression should be noted.

• For skin, “tumour” means a solid or nodular lesion ≥ 10 mm in diameter with evidence of depth and/or vertical growth; the total number and volume of lesions, largest lesion size, and body region involved should be noted; also note whether there is histological evidence of large-cell transformation; phenotyping for CD30 is encouraged.

• For node, “abnormal peripheral lymph node” means a palpable lymph node that is firm, irregular, clustered, fixed, and/or ≥ 15 mm in diameter.

• Node groups examined: cervical, supraclavicular, epitrochlear, axillary, and inguinal; central nodes are not assessed unless used to establish N3 histopathologically.

• Visceral involvement of spleen and liver can be assessed by radiological imaging.

• For blood, Sézary cells are defined as lymphocytes with hyperconvoluted/cerebriform nuclei; if Sézary cells cannot be used to determine tumour burden for B2, then a positive monoclonal rearrangement of the TR gene can be used instead: (1) expanded CD4+ or CD3+ cells with a CD4:CD8 ratio of ≥ 10, or (2) expanded CD4+ cells with abnormal immunophenotype including loss of CD7 or CD26.

• T-cell clones are identified by PCR or Southern blot analysis of the TR gene.
