5.2.5.1 EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour

Definition

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours (EBV-SMTs) are spindle cell neoplasms that show smooth muscle differentiation and are associated with EBV infection, usually in the setting of immunosuppression.

ICD-O coding

8897/1 EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour

ICD-11 coding

2F9C & XH00B4 Neoplasms of unknown behaviour of connective or other soft tissue & Smooth muscle tumour, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: smooth muscle tumour in an immunocompromised patient.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours can be seen at any site in the body. The main sites are the liver, lung, gastrointestinal tract, and the CNS, but the spleen, adrenal gland, thyroid, or any other site can be also affected. Cutaneous involvement may be frequent in HIV-related smooth muscle tumours {31700854}. Most patients with EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours in the setting of primary immunodeficiency are children, and the tumours tend to be multicentric.

Clinical features

The symptoms are usually related to the site of involvement by the tumour: neurological symptoms in cases of intra-axial or extra-axial CNS tumours; abdominal pain caused by bleeding or obstruction in intestinal cases; and cyanosis or infection caused by obstruction in endobronchial cases.

Epidemiology

The age range is wide (1–66 years). There is a slight female predominance. Three groups of EBV-SMT are recognized: HIV-associated, posttransplant (solid organs or haematopoietic stem cells), and congenital immunodeficiency–associated. Two thirds of patients in the first two groups are adults, although EBV-SMTs are the second most frequent tumour in children affected by AIDS. EBV-SMTs associated with primary congenital immunodeficiency are very rare; the few cases reported have mostly been in children affected by severe combined immunodeficiency and common variable immunodeficiency. Recent cases associated with GATA2 mutation or CARMIL2 deficiency have been reported {25027306; 29535735; 30298678; 29905497; 32625199}.

Etiology

The etiology involves EBV infection in the setting of T-lymphocyte immunosuppression.

Pathogenesis

The mechanisms of EBV entry into the putative cell of origin are still not elucidated. The latency pattern is probably characteristic of the tumour being positive for EBNA2 and/or EBNA3 expression indicative of type III latency, but LMP1 is not detected. MYC overexpression and AKT/mTOR signalling are thought to represent a major pathogenetic pathway in this tumour {19706821; 22420456; 23682851}. Viral episome analysis reveals that multicentricity is caused by viral multiple infections rather than the dissemination or metastasis of a single clone {16330945}.

Macroscopic appearance

The mass is solid and firm, with a grey to white cut surface. The tumour diameter varies from a few millimetres to > 200 mm {16330945; 22427204; 25027306}.

Histopathology

The tumours show short interlacing fascicles of spindle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and blunt-ended nuclei. Areas containing more primitive round cells are frequent {16330945}. Cellular atypia or necrotic foci (i.e. sarcoma-like features) may be seen, especially in HIV-related smooth muscle tumours {25027306}. Intratumoural T lymphocytes are present. Immunohistochemically, the spindle cells are diffusely positive for SMA and h-caldesmon. Desmin may be focally expressed. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization is a powerful tool for detecting EBV in tumour cells and is essential for the diagnosis.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnosis is confirmed by EBER in situ hybridization or any other molecular analysis revealing EBV infection.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a tumour with smooth muscle differentiation; a clinical history of immunosuppression; positivity of EBER in situ hybridization or equivalent.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is mainly dependent on the immune condition of the patient, but even in cases of multicentric tumours, the mortality rate is very low and mostly related to underlying conditions.
