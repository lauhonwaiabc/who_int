2.2.5.2 Acute myeloid leukaemias (AMLs) defined by differentiation

Definition

Acute myeloid leukaemias (AMLs) defined by differentiation are acute leukaemias with evidence of myeloid differentiation, with or without evidence of specific maturation, that lack defining genetic abnormalities.

Subtype(s)

AML with minimal differentiation; AML without maturation; AML with maturation; acute myelomonocytic leukaemia; acute monoblastic and monocytic leukaemia; acute erythroid leukaemia; acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia

Localization

The bone marrow and blood are the primary sites of involvement. Extramedullary myeloid tumours, such as those observed in the skin, CNS, lymph nodes, and gastrointestinal tract, have also been reported at diagnosis and relapse {29338495}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms of AML include fever, bone pain, fatigue, petechial rash, bruising, and bleeding {20113895; 29338495}. Presentation with hyperleukocytosis may occur and is associated with the risk of stroke or pulmonary haemorrhage.

Epidemiology

Globally there are about 17 000 new cases of paediatric AML annually, with a slightly higher incidence in south-eastern Asia, South America, and Central America {30824204}. In Europe and North America the incidence of AML in patients aged < 20 years is 9 cases per 1 million person-years. The incidence is highest in infants (16 cases per 1 million person-years) and lowest in children aged 5–9 years (4 cases per 1 million person-years), then it rises again through adolescence and the teenage years {30824204}. AML accounts for nearly 45% of cases of paediatric AML using previous WHO criteria, with the most common types being AML without maturation, AML with maturation, acute myelomonocytic leukaemia, and acute monoblastic and monocytic leukaemia {25819835}. The reported frequency of paediatric acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia (AMKL) varies between studies, approaching 15% of AML cases in one study {11389009}.

Etiology

AML is defined by a wide variety of somatic structural variants (resulting most notably in fusion oncoproteins) and mutations in the RAS/MAPK pathway, FLT3, and WT1 {23634996; 30926971}. In addition to the fusions described within the category of AML with defining genetic abnormalities, other rearrangements include PICALM::MLLT10, fusions involving ETS family members, and alterations that lead to aberrant expression of MECOM {31648321; 29227476}. Unlike in adults, in children there is a relative scarcity of mutations in DNMT3A, splicing factors, and TET2 {23634996}. In addition to the genetic factors associated with an increased risk of paediatric myeloid disorders described elsewhere, other rare predispositions include genes implicated in Fanconi anaemia {25455269} and telomere biology {31470354}.

Pathogenesis

Paediatric AML results from a wide variety of genetic alterations that block myeloid differentiation and/or induce cell proliferation. Many of these mutations alter epigenetic states and are not specific to morphological subtype.

Histopathology

By definition, blasts or blast equivalents account for ≥ 20% of the nucleated cells in the bone marrow and/or peripheral blood (see #17906Table 2.03). There is wide variation in the morphological features due to the diversity of morphological subtypes in AML. Invariably the bone marrow is hypercellular with abundant blasts. Dysplasia can be present. The other major differential diagnoses include other acute leukaemias or AML subtypes with recurrent genetic alterations. Leukaemias developing in patients with previous exposure to radiation therapy or cytotoxic chemotherapy should be classified as therapy-related myeloid neoplasms. Patterns of protein expression, as measured by immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry, can be used to establish the diagnosis of AML and to subclassify it into different morphological subtypes. Importantly, the megakaryocytic markers CD41, CD42b, and CD61 are also expressed by platelets; nonspecific staining during flow cytometric evaluation is a notable consideration {1349244} and intracellular staining may improve the detection of these markers {10550559}. Considering the fibrosis that occurs in patients with AMKL, a bone marrow core biopsy with immunohistochemistry is often more representative than an aspirate smear. Immunohistochemistry for CD42b has been shown to have high sensitivity for AMKL {30052718}. In addition, tumour masses of myeloid blasts that efface the normal tissue architecture can also occur in extramedullary sites (e.g. skin, testes, lymph nodes, and gastrointestinal tract). These myeloid sarcomas require extensive workup by immunohistochemistry to distinguish them from soft tissue sarcomas, other acute leukaemia subtypes, and mature B-cell lymphomas {21883967; 31682773}.

Cytology

Blasts have variable chromatin patterns, nucleoli, and cytoplasm qualities, depending on the morphological subtype. Auer rods may or may not be present.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Morphological assessment of bone marrow aspirate and biopsy provides the definitive diagnosis and is complemented by genetic testing and protein expression, ideally by flow cytometry. When available, peripheral blood immunophenotyping can reliably detect leukaemic cells {28333411}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: identification of ≥ 20% blasts (or blast equivalents) in the bone marrow or blood, or the presence of a myeloid sarcoma; evidence of myeloid derivation and an absence of definitive T-cell differentiation (cytoplasmic CD3) or B-cell differentiation (CD19, bright) by immunophenotyping, immunohistochemistry, and/or enzyme cytochemistry; absence of features that allow for classification into other categories, namely AML with defining genetic abnormalities, acute leukaemia of ambiguous lineage, myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition, or therapy-related myeloid neoplasms.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Current approaches to therapy include intensive multiagent chemotherapy given over four to five courses {29338495; 22566607}. The determination of relapse risk is based on prognostic genetic mutations and response to the first two courses of multiagent chemotherapy; this is commonly assessed using minimal residual disease testing, most often via multidimensional flow cytometry for a leukaemia-associated immunophenotype {20451454; 21149663}. Genomic approaches to detect residual somatic alterations have been shown to have clinical benefit in adults with AML and are under investigation in paediatric patients {26305651}. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation remains a part of therapy for patients at high risk of relapse as determined by genetic features, or with poorly responsive leukaemia as measured by minimal residual disease {12139721; 12058234}. Overall survival is 60–70% with current risk-stratified therapy (across cooperative groups internationally), but event-free survival is 55–60% because relapse remains a significant clinical challenge {20113895; 26304895; 31246522; 29550834; 23673857}. Paediatric AMKLs without CBFA2T3::GLIS2, KMT2A, or NUP98::KDM5A fusions have a low rate of relapse and a 5-year overall survival rate of about 80% {28112737}. Evaluation of the role of epigenetic therapy and immunotherapy is ongoing.

Immunophenotype

AML with minimal differentiation

Blasts with agranular cytoplasm with variable basophilia; no Auer rods; round to indented nucleus with dispersed chromatin and one or more nucleoli; granulocytes are < 10% of nucleated marrow cells

< 3% of blasts positive for myeloperoxidase or Sudan Black B

Positive for early stem cell markers (CD34, HLA-DR, CD38); negative for mature myeloid and monocytic markers (CD11b, CD64, CD36, CD15, CD14, CD65); expression of ≥ 2 myeloid markers (CD13, CD33, KIT [CD117])

AML without maturation

Blasts may have cytoplasmic granules, Auer rods, and finely dispersed chromatin with nucleoli; granulocytes are < 10% of nucleated marrow cells

Myeloperoxidase and Sudan Black B positive in ≥ 3% of blasts

Myeloid markers expressed (CD13, CD33, KIT [CD117]); CD34 and HLA-DR expressed in 70%; negative for granulocytic (CD15, CD65) and monocytic (CD14, CD64, CD36) markers; variable expression of CD7, CD2, CD4, CD19 (dim), CD56

AML with maturation

Blasts have prominent nucleoli; Auer rods and azurophilic granules can be present; granulocytes are ≥ 10% of nucleated marrow cells

Myeloperoxidase and Sudan Black B positive in ≥ 3% of blasts

Myeloid and granulocytic markers expressed (CD13, CD33, CD65, CD11b, CD15); variable expression of CD34, KIT (CD117), HLA-DR, CD7; monocytic markers usually absent (CD14, CD64, CD36)

Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia

Both neutrophilic and monocytic precursors are > 20% of cells; monoblasts have abundant basophilic cytoplasm +/− fine azurophilic granules and vacuoles, fine lacy chromatin, and large prominent nucleoli; promonocytes have more convoluted, folded reniform nuclei with fine, delicate chromatin without visible nucleoli

Myeloperoxidase and Sudan Black B positive in ≥ 3% of blasts; nonspecific esterase positive in monocytic component

Different blast populations (myeloid/granulocytic and monocytic) may be identified; variable expression of myeloid antigens (CD13, CD33, CD65, CD15); variable expression of monocytic markers (CD14, CD64, CD36, CD11b, CD11c, CD4, CD68 [PGM1], CD163, lysozyme); subpopulation of blasts with CD34 and/or KIT (CD117) may be seen

Acute monocytic leukaemia

> 80% of blasts are of monocytic lineage; in acute monoblastic leukaemia ≥ 80% are monoblasts; in acute monocytic leukaemia the majority of cells are promonocytes and monocytes

Nonspecific esterase usually positive; myeloperoxidase usually negative in monoblasts but may be positive in promonocytes

Variable expression of myeloid antigens (CD13, CD33, CD15, CD65); ≥ 2 monocytic markers (CD14, CD64, CD36, CD4, CD11b, CD11c, CD68 [PGM1], CD163, lysozyme); positive for HLA-DR; variable expression of CD34, KIT (CD117), CD7, and CD56

Acute erythroid leukaemia

> 80% of bone marrow cells are erythroid, with > 30% proerythroblasts; no significant myeloblast component; erythroblasts are medium to large with round nuclei, fine chromatin, and ≥ 1 nucleoli; deeply basophilic agranular cytoplasm with frequent vacuoles

Negative for myeloperoxidase and Sudan Black B; positive for PAS (block-like pattern)

Expression of erythroid markers (glycophorin A, haemoglobin A, CD71); E-cadherin more sensitive and positive in majority of cases; positive for KIT (CD117); positive for CD36; negative for CD34, HLA-DR; usually negative for CD41 and CD61

Acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia

> 50% of the blasts have features of megakaryocyte lineage; classic megakaryoblasts with basophilic cytoplasm and cytoplasmic blebs can be seen but are not universal; occasionally blasts can form clusters that can mimic non-haematopoietic tumours; fibrosis is common

Negative for myeloperoxidase and Sudan Black B; variably positive for PAS with a weak granular pattern

Expression of myeloid-associated antigens (CD13, CD33) but not myeloperoxidase; CD34 can be negative and expression of CD45 is dim to negative; variable expression of CD2, CD7, CD56, and KIT (CD117); expression of megakaryocyte lineage markers (CD41, CD42b, and/or CD61) by immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry is necessary
