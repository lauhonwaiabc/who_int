2.3.3.6 Systemic EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood

Definition

Systemic EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood is a life-threatening illness of immunocompetent children and young adults, characterized by a clonal proliferation of EBV-infected T cells with an activated cytotoxic phenotype and usually associated with a haemophagocytic syndrome.

ICD-O coding

9724/3 Systemic EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood

ICD-11 coding

2A90.4 Systemic Epstein–Barr virus–positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood

Related terminology

Not recommended: severe chronic active EBV infection; severe chronic active EBV infection with monoclonal EBV-positive T-cell proliferation; fulminant EBV-positive T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder of childhood; sporadic fatal infectious mononucleosis; fatal EBV-associated haemophagocytic syndrome; fulminant haemophagocytic syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

This is a systemic disease. The most commonly involved sites are the spleen and liver, followed by bone marrow, lymph nodes, skin, and lungs. {8203462; 8241498; 10887104; 11435294; 15067338}.

Clinical features

Previously healthy patients develop symptoms of infectious mononucleosis with fever, general malaise, and/or upper respiratory illness after primary EBV infection. Within a period of weeks, patients develop hepatosplenomegaly and liver failure, sometimes accompanied by lymphadenopathy and a rash. Laboratory tests show pancytopenia, abnormal liver function tests, and often an abnormal EBV serology with low or absent anti-VCA IgM antibodies (despite the acute EBV infection), which usually delays the diagnosis. The disease is usually complicated by haemophagocytic syndrome, coagulopathy, multiorgan failure, and sepsis {10887104; 31099136}. Some cases occur in patients with a well-documented history of chronic active EBV infection {10887104; 2831453; 9490694; 31099136}. A disorder that is probably related but that manifests mainly with lymphadenopathy and high LDH levels has been reported in children from Peru {22175898}.

Epidemiology

Systemic EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma of childhood is most prevalent in Asia – primarily in Japan; in Taiwan, China; and in southwestern China {8241498; 11435294; 8203462; 15067338; 32317607}. It has been reported in Mexico and (rarely) other countries in the Western Hemisphere {10887104; 31833428; 31099136}. It occurs most often in children and young adults. There is no sex predilection.

Etiology

Although the etiology is unknown, its geographical predilection and its association with primary EBV infection strongly suggest a genetic defect in the host immune response to EBV {19515747; 11435294; 10887104; 15067338; 31099136}.

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The infiltrating T cells are usually small and lack significant cytological atypia. However, cases with pleomorphic medium-sized to large lymphoid cells, irregular nuclei, and frequent mitoses have been described {10887104; 32317607; 29518976}. The liver and spleen show mild to marked sinusoidal infiltration, with striking haemophagocytosis. The splenic white pulp is depleted. The liver has prominent portal and sinusoidal infiltration, cholestasis, steatosis, and necrosis. The lymph nodes usually show preserved architecture with open sinuses. The B-cell areas are depleted, whereas the paracortical areas might be expanded and show a subtle to dense infiltration and a broad cytological spectrum, ranging from small to medium-sized lymphocytes to large atypical lymphocytes with hyperchromatic and irregular nuclei. The more advanced the disease is, the more depleted the lymph nodes look. A variable degree of sinus histiocytosis with erythrophagocytosis is present. Bone marrow biopsies show histiocytic hyperplasia with prominent erythrophagocytosis.

Immunophenotype

The most typical immunophenotype of the neoplastic cells is CD2+, CD3+, CD56−, and TIA1+. Most cases secondary to acute primary EBV infection are CD8+ {10887104; 29518976; 31099136; 32317607; 31833428}, whereas cases in the setting of severe chronic active EBV infection are CD4+ {2831453; 9490694; 10887104; 31099136}. Rare cases show both CD4+ and CD8+ EBV-infected T cells {31099136; 10887104}. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is positive.

Differential diagnosis

This includes other EBV-positive T- and NK-cell lymphoproliferative disorders, such as systemic chronic active EBV infection {18324913; 32598475}, aggressive NK-cell leukaemia, and EBV-associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) {31099136; 25337215}. Because HLH is often associated with systemic EBV-positive T-cell lymphoma, the distinction between these two disorders is often difficult.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The cells have monoclonally rearranged TR genes. All cases harbour EBV in a clonal episomal form {2831453; 8241498; 11435294; 15067338}. All cases analysed carry type A EBV, either with the wildtype or the 30 bp–deleted product of the LMP-1 gene {15067338; 11535525}. In situ hybridization for EBER shows that the majority of the infiltrating lymphoid cells are positive. No consistent chromosomal aberrations have been identified {31099136; 9407723; 11435294; 25337215}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of a monoclonal EBV+ T-cell proliferation often complicated by haemophagocytic syndrome and without known immunodeficiency.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Most cases have a fulminant clinical course resulting in death, usually within days to weeks of diagnosis. The disease is usually complicated by HLH. Few cases have been reported to respond to etoposide- and dexamethasone-based regimens followed for allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HLH-2004 protocol) {16937360; 21828139; 25337215; 32598475}. The rapidly progressive clinical course is similar to that of aggressive NK-cell leukaemia.
