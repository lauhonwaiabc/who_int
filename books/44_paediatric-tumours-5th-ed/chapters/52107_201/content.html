5.2.10.7 Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma

Definition

Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma (AFH) is a rare mesenchymal tumour of uncertain lineage and with low to intermediate malignant potential. It is typically seen in children and young adults, most commonly arising in the subcutis. It is histologically characterized by nodules of ovoid, epithelioid, or spindle cells with haemorrhagic pseudoangiomatoid spaces and a surrounding dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate.

ICD-O coding

8836/1 Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma

ICD-11 coding

2F7C & XH9362 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of connective or other soft tissue & Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: angiomatoid malignant fibrous histiocytoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

AFH most commonly arises in the subcutis or deep dermis of the extremities, followed by the trunk and the head and neck. Approximately two thirds arise in areas of normal lymph node distribution, such as the antecubital and popliteal fossae or the inguinal, axillary, and supraclavicular areas {10571514}. Rare cases occur in the female genital tract, bone, brain, retroperitoneum, and mediastinum {21822206; 20090505; 18300800}.

Clinical features

Typically, patients present with a superficial, painless, slow-growing, and variably fluctuant soft tissue mass. Occasional patients have an associated paraneoplastic syndrome of anaemia, fever, and weight loss, related to tumour cytokine production {7813103}.

Imaging

Ultrasound and MRI may show a nonspecific predominantly solid mass. Demonstration of intralesional blood-filled cystic spaces with fluid-fluid levels and an enhancing fibrous pseudocapsule with haemosiderin deposition may suggest the diagnosis {26162466}.

Epidemiology

AFH can occur in adults, but it is most common in the first two decades of life and may occur congenitally {2174650; 2845834}. There is no known sex predilection.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

More than 90% of AFHs harbour a t(2;22)(q33;q12) translocation resulting in an EWSR1::CREB1 fusion gene {17724745; 27166030; 18094413}. Rare cases have an alternative t(12;22)(q13;q12) translocation creating an EWSR1::ATF1 fusion {17188428}. Even less commonly seen is a t(12;16)(q13;p11) translocation resulting in FUS::ATF1 {12218455} or EWSR1::CREM {31305268}. The first two fusion genes are also seen in clear cell sarcoma of soft tissue, but unlike in clear cell sarcoma of soft tissue there is no downstream activation of the microphthalmia transcription pathway in AFH {17188428}. EWSR1::CREB-family fusions have also been seen in some intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumours {28281318; 28009602} and in malignant epithelioid neoplasms with a predilection for the peritoneal cavity {32770123}.

Macroscopic appearance

These tumours appear grossly as small, multinodular, firm to cystic masses, with a wide size range (7–120 mm, median: 20 mm) {228836; 2174650}. The cut surface of the lesion commonly shows multilocular blood-filled spaces, simulating a haematoma or intranodal haemorrhage, with solid areas that are greyish-yellow to white {228836; 2174650; 25927151}.

Histopathology

These tumours are usually well circumscribed and lobulated, with several morphological components. These include (1) sheets or fascicles of spindled or histiocytic-like cells arranged in nodules, containing a moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm and bland vesicular nuclei; (2) non-endothelial lined and blood-filled pseudoangiomatous spaces surrounded by tumour cells; (3) a thick, fibrous pseudocapsule with haemosiderin deposition; and (4) a pericapsular lymphoplasmacytic cell cuff with germinal centres. Nearly one third of tumours lack pseudoangiomatous spaces and are solid, simulating a lymph node. Mitotic figures are usually few, although atypical mitotic figures may be present. Pleomorphic cells, including multinucleated giant cells may be observed. The most common morphological pattern is the spindle cell type, followed by the pleomorphic cell type {25927151; 27166030; 24043718}. Cellular atypia and an increased number of mitotic figures are not associated with aggressive behaviour {2174650}. Some tumours contain myxoid stroma {24503754; 24043718; 27166030}. A rare subtype of AFH is characterized by CD99-positive small blue round cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant eosinophilic cytoplasm, mimicking an undifferentiated round cell sarcoma {10571514; 11012987}.

Immunohistochemistry

Most are positive for SOX9 and are often focally or diffusely immunoreactive for desmin. Perimembranous CD99 staining is common. Tumour cells are consistently negative for MYOD1, myogenin, cytokeratins, S100, and CD34. EMA, ALK, and CD68 are variably immunopositive {10571514; 11012987; 31567275; 29877921}.

Cytology

The cytological features are nonspecific and include moderate to marked cellularity, ovoid to spindled histiocytoid cells (some of which are atypical, arranged individually and in loose clusters in a bloody background), and a variable number of inflammatory cells {22045622}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of EWSR1::CREB1, EWSR1::ATF1, or FUS::ATF1 fusion is supportive of the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a small, painless subcutaneous nodule; nodules of epithelioid to ovoid cells arranged in syncytial-like sheets; pseudoangiomatoid spaces; peripheral rims of lymphoplasmacytic cells, including germinal centres; variable desmin, CD99, and EMA immunoreactivity.

Desirable (in selected cases): molecular studies confirming EWSR1 gene rearrangement.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The local recurrence rate is 10–15% {2174650; 10571514}. Metastases, mainly to locoregional lymph nodes, have been documented in 1–5% of patients. Distant metastases to the liver, lungs, and brain may occur but are rare, and disease-related death is exceptional {28114920}. Histological and molecular features have not been correlated with prognosis, although local recurrence is associated with incomplete surgical excision {2174650; 21822206}.
