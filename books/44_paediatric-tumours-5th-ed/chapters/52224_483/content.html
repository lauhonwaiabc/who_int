5.2.2.2.4 Fibromatosis colli

Definition

Fibromatosis colli is a benign, self-limiting fibrous proliferation in the sternocleidomastoid muscle of infants. Some cases progress to muscle shortening, resulting in torticollis.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

FB51.Y Other specified fibroblastic disorders

Related terminology

Not recommended: sternocleidomastoid tumour of infancy; pseudotumour of infancy; congenital muscular torticollis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Fibromatosis colli affects the sternocleidomastoid muscle (the right more often than the left). Most commonly, the middle or distal third of the muscle is affected; about 15% of cases involve the entire muscle {7814599; 10356139}.

Clinical features

Fibromatosis colli manifests as a mass in the sternocleidomastoid muscle, most often congenitally or within 2–4 weeks of birth. The mass may continue to grow over several weeks, then stabilize and spontaneously resolve in most cases {7814599; 10356139}. Approximately 10–20% of patients develop torticollis; such patients show head tilt towards the involved sternocleidomastoid muscle (chin directed towards the contralateral shoulder) {7814599}. Plagiocephaly may also occur. Fibromatosis colli may be associated with hip dysplasia, metatarsus adductus, talipes equinovarus, and prominent ears {7814599; 10356139}. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging method, demonstrating an isoechoic to hypoechoic, solid, well-defined ovoid mass in the muscle {9545475; 9719983}.

Epidemiology

Although uncommon, fibromatosis colli is the most common cause of a neck mass in the perinatal period. The reported incidence is 0.3–2.0% of live births {11379737}, with a male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.5:1) {7814599}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

A scar-like reactive process due to sternocleidomastoid muscle injury acquired in utero or during delivery is most likely {24205484; 8459000; 20091898}. Possible mechanisms include fetal malposition/crowding, birth trauma, vascular compromise, and infection {8459000}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumour is a firm to hard tan-white mass with associated skeletal muscle.

Histopathology

Surgical specimens are uncommon; only a minority of patients require tenotomy for persistent torticollis. Histology demonstrates a paucicellular, scar-like, fibroblastic proliferation entrapping skeletal muscle {20091898}.

Cytology

FNAB may be used for diagnosis. Smears are hypocellular, composed of spindled fibroblasts associated with collagen fibres and multinucleated atrophic myocytes {2762289; 20091898; 11074630; 25584233}. The fibroblastic cells are singly dispersed or loosely cohesive, with bland oval to elongated nuclei and unipolar or bipolar cytoplasmic tails; bare nuclei may also be present {2762289; 12789914}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a fusiform mass associated with the sternocleidomastoid muscle of an infant; biopsy or FNAB (if performed) shows a bland fibroblastic proliferation with associated atrophic skeletal muscle.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of fibromatosis colli lesions are self-limiting, resolving within 6 months. A subset of cases progress with tumour growth and/or the development of torticollis, which will worsen with patient growth if untreated {11379737}. Physiotherapy is effective in as many as 95% of cases. In the remaining cases, surgery may be required {10356139; 11379737; 20859860; 7814599}.
