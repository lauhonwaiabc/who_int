5.2.3.7 Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma

Definition

Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma is a rarely metastasizing endothelial neoplasm occurring in adolescents and young adults, with a propensity for occurring as multiple discontiguous nodules in different tissue planes, mimicking myoid neoplasms and epithelioid sarcoma.

ICD-O coding

9138/1 Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma

ICD-11 coding

2E81.0Z & XH26F6 Neoplastic haemangioma, unspecified & Pseudomyogenic (epithelioid sarcoma–like) haemangioendothelioma

Related terminology

Not recommended: epithelioid sarcoma–like haemangioendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma usually arises in the lower extremities (55% of cases); the upper extremities (20%) and trunk (20%) are less commonly affected; rarely, it involves the head and neck region (5%) {12502927; 21263239}. Primary bone involvement alone also occurs rarely {26872012; 32271190}.

Clinical features

The typical presentation is painful or painless cutaneous and subcutaneous nodules that may be multifocal in approximately 60% of patients. Multiple tissue planes are usually affected, with 50% of patients having intramuscular lesions and about 20% having lytic bony lesions {21263239; 32271190; 23381465}. The tumours are highly avid on FDG-PET, a technique used to identify multifocal lesions.

Epidemiology

Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma typically affects young adults (median age: 28 years, range: 10–64 years), and as many as 20% of cases arise in adolescents. The M:F ratio is 3:1 {32227592}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma is associated with a SERPINE1::FOSB gene fusion arising from a t(7;19)(q22;q13) balanced translocation, as well as an ACTB::FOSB rearrangement in an equal number of cases {24374978; 30256258; 30459475}. More recently, FOSB fusions with WWTR1 or CLTC have been described, the latter in a bone lesion in an adolescent {31243110; 32749039}. The fusions result in the upregulation of FOSB, a member of the FOS family of transcription factors of leucine zipper proteins that interact with JUN-family proteins to regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, angiogenesis, and survival. The type of fusion does not affect the clinicopathological features, although tumours with the ACTB variant usually manifest as solitary lesions {30256258}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, the cut surface is firm and grey or white with poorly defined margins. The nodules vary in size from 10 to 25 mm, with about 10% of tumours measuring > 30 mm.

Histopathology

The tumour is composed of sheets and fascicles of plump spindle cells with abundant bright eosinophilic cytoplasm resembling rhabdomyoblasts (hence the term “pseudomyogenic”) {21263239; 29104110}. A minor component of epithelioid cells is present and rarely predominates {12502927}. Intraosseous tumours are often dominated by epithelioid cells with cytoplasmic eosinophilia, associated with reactive woven bone, haemorrhage, and osteoclast giant cell reaction {32271190}. The tumour cells contain vesicular nuclei with small nucleoli. The nuclear atypia is generally mild, and mitoses are scarce. About 10% of tumours show pleomorphism and large nucleoli {21263239}. Focal myxoid stroma may be seen. Neutrophils may be seen in the background in half of the cases. The tumours show infiltrative margins.

Immunohistochemistry

The tumour cells show diffuse expression of keratins (AE1/AE3) and endothelial markers (FLI1 and ERG) {21263239; 12502927; 31980959}. CD31 is positive, while CD34 is negative. SMARCB1 (INI1) is retained. FOSB immunohistochemistry shows diffuse nuclear staining {28009608}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of FOSB rearrangements may be helpful in the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: plump spindle or epithelioid cells with vesicular nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm; fascicular or sheet-like growth; expression of ERG, FOSB, and keratins (AE1/AE3).

Desirable: demonstration of FOSB fusion.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

About 60% of tumours recur locally (often in multiple sites), or additional nodules may appear {21263239; 31980959; 32227592}. The interval between primary tumour resection and recurrence is 1–2 years. Regional lymph node and distant metastases are rare and may include lungs, bones, and soft tissues. Adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy are of limited use.
