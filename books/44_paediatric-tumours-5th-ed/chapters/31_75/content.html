3.1 CNS tumours: Introduction

CNS tumours account for about 25% of all tumours arising in children aged < 18 years and have become the most common cause of tumour-related death in this age group. Particular challenges in further improving survival rates in children with these tumours include the unique vulnerability of the CNS, substantial intertumoural heterogeneity meaning that most entities are very rare, intratumoural heterogeneity, resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic regimens, and lack of availability of brain-penetrant treatments.

With novel approaches for molecular tumour classification and characterization to complement traditional histopathology, over the past few years the extraordinary intertumoural heterogeneity of paediatric CNS tumours has started to fully come to light. For example, more than 150 distinct molecular CNS tumour entities can now be recognized on the basis of methylome profiling analysis. Additionally, it has become evident that the spectrum and prevalence of childhood CNS tumours are fundamentally different from those of CNS tumours in adults, which include some entities that used to carry the same name (e.g. glioblastoma) but are in fact vastly different diseases in children and adults. Also, the number of molecularly distinct groups within a larger group of tumours (e.g. high-grade glioma) tends to be much higher in children than in adults. Meanwhile, there is currently no standard-of-care treatment available for many such emerging, molecularly defined entities.

Obviously, the advances in molecular diagnostics in combination with the availability of a much wider variety of potential treatment options impact the scope of and clinical demands for tumour classification. Traditionally, a major focus of tumour classification has been assessment of prognosis, previously according to the natural disease course. However, for many of the molecularly defined malignant CNS tumours, information about the natural disease course will never become available, because essentially all patients receive intensive treatment. The focus must therefore shift towards assessment of prognosis in relation to state-of-the-art treatment. Additionally, tumour classification will increasingly contribute to mechanism-of-action–based treatment decisions, for example for single-pathway diseases such as pilocytic astrocytoma (MAPK-driven) or subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (mTOR-driven). For this reason, it is of utmost importance to systematically assess specific treatment options for these tumours (as well as potential primary resistance mechanisms) as part of the diagnostic process. Such assessment of predictive biomarkers should, however, be seen as complementing current tumour classification rather than constituting an alternative approach. Finally, in at least 10% of paediatric CNS tumour patients, next-generation molecular diagnostic methods reveal suspected familial cancer predisposition. This relatively high frequency, along with the fact that cancer predisposition is currently the only known etiological factor for the development of childhood CNS tumours (other than treatment-related secondary malignancies), requires a much closer alignment between tumour and germline analysis, neuropathology, and human genetics.

This new understanding of paediatric CNS tumour biology, and thus clinical management, has fundamental practical implications for the way that CNS tumours in childhood are classified and defined, and it therefore strongly influenced the fifth-edition WHO classification of CNS tumours. That classification serves as the basis for this chapter on CNS tumours in the WHO classification of paediatric tumours. Within this chapter, tumour entities are discussed in detail either when they are commonly observed in children or when they are more common in adults but have a fundamentally different biology in children. Entities that are rare in children are only summarized in #22678Box 3.01  and are covered in more detail in the fifth-edition WHO classification of CNS tumours volume. However, the backbone of the classification in the CNS and paediatric volumes is exactly the same and also includes a few provisional entities that are equally relevant in the context of paediatric neuro-oncology (diffuse glioneuronal tumour with oligodendroglioma-like features and nuclear clusters, cribriform neuroepithelial tumour).

In terms of methodology, it has become evident (through the advances described above) that whenever economically feasible, the previous armamentarium (immunohistochemistry, FISH, cytogenetics) of the neuropathologist must be complemented with new methods, including next-generation sequencing and DNA methylation analysis, to meet the clinical needs described above.

Overall, the fifth edition of the WHO classification of CNS tumours involves a major leap forward in diagnosing CNS tumours. And this is even more evident in the context of paediatric CNS tumours, where intertumoural heterogeneity is even greater and many molecularly very sharply delineated entities are exceedingly rare yet must still be reliably identified for optimal treatment decisions today and for the development of better treatment options for future patients.

Gliomas, glioneuronal tumours, and neuronal tumours

 

Adult-type diffuse gliomas

 

Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, is a diffusely infiltrating IDH1- or IDH2-mutant glioma with frequent ATRX and/or TP53 mutation and absence of 1p/19q codeletion (CNS WHO grade 2, 3, or 4).

 

Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, is a diffusely infiltrating glioma with IDH1 or IDH2 mutation and codeletion of chromosome arms 1p and 19q (CNS WHO grade 2 or 3).

 

Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, is a diffuse, astrocytic glioma that is IDH-wildtype and H3-wildtype and has one or more of the following histological or genetic features: microvascular proliferation, necrosis, TERT promoter mutation, EGFR gene amplification, +7/−10 chromosome copy-number changes (CNS WHO grade 4).

 

Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas

 

Chordoid glioma is a well-circumscribed glial neoplasm that arises in the anterior third ventricle, is histologically characterized by clusters and cords of GFAP-expressing epithelioid cells, and exhibits a recurrent p.D463H missense mutation in the PRKCA gene (CNS WHO grade 2).

 

Glioneuronal and neuronal tumours

 

Papillary glioneuronal tumour is a glioneuronal tumour exhibiting a biphasic pattern with variable representation of pseudopapillary glial structures and interpapillary neuronal components, and with PRKCA gene fusion (mainly SLC44A1::PRKCA fusion) (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Rosette-forming glioneuronal tumour is a glioneuronal tumour composed of two distinct histological components: one containing uniform neurocytes forming rosettes and/or perivascular pseudorosettes, and the other being glial in nature, with piloid and oligodendroglia-like cells, resembling pilocytic astrocytoma. These tumours are characterized by FGFR1 mutation with frequent co-occurrence of a PIK3CA and/or NF1 mutation (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Myxoid glioneuronal tumour is a low-grade glioneuronal tumour typically arising in the septal nuclei, septum pellucidum, corpus callosum, or periventricular white matter. The tumour is characterized by a proliferation of oligodendrocyte-like tumour cells embedded in a prominent myxoid stroma, often including admixed floating neurons, neurocytic rosettes, and/or perivascular neuropil. There is a recurrent dinucleotide mutation at codon p.K385 in the PDGFRA gene (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Gangliocytoma is a neuroepithelial neoplasm composed of irregular clusters of mostly mature neoplastic ganglion cells, often with dysplastic features (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma (Lhermitte–Duclos disease) is a cerebellar mass composed of dysplastic ganglion cells that conform to the existing cortical architecture and thicken the cerebellar folia (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Central neurocytoma is an intraventricular neuroepithelial tumour composed of uniform round cells with a neuronal immunophenotype and low proliferation index (CNS WHO grade 2).

 

Extraventricular neurocytoma is a usually well-circumscribed neuronal neoplasm that arises throughout the CNS outside the ventricular system, with histopathological characteristics resembling those of central neurocytoma but demonstrating a much wider morphological spectrum, and with frequent FGFR1::TACC1 fusions (CNS WHO grade 2).

 

Cerebellar liponeurocytoma is a cerebellar neoplasm with advanced neuronal or neurocytic differentiation, variable glial differentiation, and focal lipoma-like changes (CNS WHO grade 2).

 

Ependymal tumoursb

 

Subependymoma is a glioma characterized by the clustering of uniform to mildly pleomorphic tumour cell nuclei in an abundant fibrillary matrix prone to microcystic change (CNS WHO grade 1).

Pineal tumoursb

 

Pineocytoma is a well-differentiated pineal parenchymal neoplasm composed of (1) uniform cells forming large pineocytomatous rosettes and/or (2) pleomorphic cells showing gangliocytic differentiation (CNS WHO grade 1).

 

Pineal parenchymal tumour of intermediate differentiation is a tumour of the pineal parenchyma that is intermediate in malignancy between pineocytoma and pineoblastoma, composed of diffuse sheets or large lobules of monomorphic round cells that appear more differentiated than those observed in pineoblastoma (CNS WHO grade 2 or 3).

 

Papillary tumour of the pineal region is a neuroepithelial tumour characterized by a combination of papillary and solid areas, with epithelial-like cells and immunoreactivity for cytokeratins (CNS WHO grade 2 or 3).

 

Desmoplastic myxoid tumour of the pineal region, SMARCB1-mutant, is a tumour showing desmoplasia and myxoid changes but lacking histopathological signs of malignancy, with alterations of the SMARCB1 region on chromosome 22q11.

Meningioma

 

Meningiomas comprise a family of neoplasms that are most likely derived from the meningothelial cells of the arachnoid mater (CNS WHO grades 1, 2, or 3).

Melanocytic tumoursb

 

Circumscribed meningeal melanocytic neoplasms (melanocytoma and melanoma) are tumours that arise from leptomeningeal melanocytes and range histologically from well-differentiated tumours (meningeal melanocytoma) to frankly malignant neoplasms with aggressive growth properties (meningeal melanoma). Tumours with a bland histological appearance but increased mitotic activity or invasion of the CNS parenchyma have been defined as melanocytoma of intermediate grade.

Tumours of the sellar regionb

 

Papillary craniopharyngioma is a solid or partially cystic, non-keratinizing squamous epithelial tumour that develops in the infundibulotuberal region of the third ventricle floor, most often in adults, and is characterized by BRAF p.V600E mutations.

 

Pituicytoma, granular cell tumour of the sellar region, and spindle cell oncocytoma constitute a distinct family of low-grade neoplasms that arise from pituicytes of the posterior pituitary or infundibulum, most likely representing a spectrum of a single nosological entity, all showing expression of thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1).

Metastases to the CNS

 

Metastases to the brain and spinal cord parenchyma are tumours originating outside the CNS and spreading into the brain and spinal cord parenchyma via a haematogenous route or (less frequently) directly from adjacent anatomical structures.

 

Metastases to the meninges are tumours originating outside the CNS with diffuse and/or multifocal spread within the leptomeninges and the subarachnoid space.
