4.1 Eye tumours: Introduction

This short chapter describes the major ophthalmic tumours seen in children. Choristomas of the conjunctiva are not neoplastic, but they form tumours that can cause considerable concern to parents and doctors unfamiliar with them. Of equal concern are conjunctival melanocytic naevi, which mimic the architecture of naevi in the skin and can be junctional, subepithelial (stromal), or compound. They show maturation with age and can be severely inflamed. The juvenile inflammatory melanocytic naevus may be confused with melanoma and is covered separately, although it may represent a subtype of compound naevus of the conjunctiva.

Retinoblastoma is the most important childhood tumour of the eye and may arise as a sporadic or germline tumour. Germline tumours are usually multiple and bilateral, whereas sporadic tumours are single and uniocular. The relationship between retinoblastoma and the much rarer retinocytoma remains uncertain. There are several staging systems in use for retinoblastoma, and the harmonization of these is awaited. The prognosis of untreated disease depends largely on the degree of extraocular extension, but with early diagnosis, retinoblastoma has an excellent prognosis.

The examination of ocular specimens is challenging, and involving an ophthalmic pathologist is recommended. Cutting fixed eyes and eyelids and performing exenteration requires specialist knowledge. The guide given for retinoblastoma is for fixed specimens: cutting these specimens without fixation is not recommended, although it is possible to use a needle aspirate under transillumination of the eye after removal and before fixation to obtain fresh material for sequencing, should that be required. FNAB is generally contraindicated clinically (see section Retinoblastoma).

Retinocytoma represents a differentiated and benign retinal tumour that is rare in comparison with retinoblastoma. Similar appearances are seen in treated retinoblastomas. Medulloepithelioma is another tumour of early childhood, although considerably less common than retinoblastoma. It may be associated with DICER1 syndrome, although most cases are sporadic. Other retinal tumours (e.g. hamartomas) are rare and not covered here; for details on these, please refer to the current volume on eye tumours.

Optic nerve gliomas are rare, but they do occur in children and are usually pilocytic astrocytomas. These are covered in more detail in the CNS tumours volume.
