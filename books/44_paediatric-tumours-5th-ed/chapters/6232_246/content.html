6.2.3.2 Yolk sac tumour

Definition

Yolk sac tumour (YST) is a malignant neoplasm resembling extraembryonic structures, including the yolk sac, allantois, and extraembryonic mesenchyme.

ICD-O coding

9071/3 Yolk sac tumour

9071/3 Prepubertal (type I) yolk sac tumour

9071/3 Postpubertal (type II) yolk sac tumour

ICD-11 coding

2C73.Y & XH09W7 Other specified malignant neoplasms of the ovary & Yolk sac tumour

2C80.Y & XH09W7 Other specified malignant neoplasms of testis & Yolk sac tumour

2A00.1Y & XH09W7 Other specified embryonal tumours of brain & Yolk sac tumour

Related terminology

Not recommended: endodermal sinus tumour; yolk sac carcinoma; orchioblastoma; infantile embryonal carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Prepubertal (type I) yolk sac tumour; postpubertal (type II) yolk sac tumour (germ cell neoplasia in situ–related)

Localization

Prepubertal YST arises in the gonads, mediastinum, and many other sites. Prepubertal and postpubertal YSTs occur within the CNS, most frequently in the pineal and suprasellar compartment, followed by the basal ganglia / thalamus, spinal cord, and other locations {9046301; 20728859; 9840366; 19940403}. During the first 2 years of life the most common germ cell tumour in the vagina is a YST.

Clinical features

Prepubertal YST develops at a median age of 16–20 months (range: 3 months to 8 years); it rarely arises beyond 6 years of age {25828390; 15247758; 12352332; 22320869}. Postpubertal YST is rarely pure (only in 0.6% of tumours containing YST). Although AFP is positive in most cases (95%) {15247758}, neonates have high AFP normally (≥ 100 ng/mL at ≤ 6 months {9592840}), limiting its significance. About 80% of paediatric cases are clinical stage I {15247758; 12352332}, and only 6% recur during surveillance (retroperitoneal and pulmonary {15247758}). In contrast, 33% of postpubertal YSTs show occult retroperitoneal metastases {11061887}. The risk for haematogenous spread is higher in prepubertal YST than in postpubertal YST. CNS YST occurs at 2.6–14.5 years of age (peak incidence: 11–15 years {31420671}; mean age: ~13 years {9046301; 16220552}). Although systemic dissemination is rare {2991485}, intra-abdominal metastasis via ventriculoperitoneal shunt can occur {9035204}. Clinical symptoms and signs correlate with tumour location and associated hydrocephalus.

Epidemiology

For postpubertal YST, see section Intratubular germ cell neoplasia (male gonadal) .

Prepubertal YST has a stable incidence in the first 6 years of life of (2–3 cases per 1 million person-years) {22320869}, with no racial or geographical predilection {17208136}. Testicular prepubertal YSTs have no association with cryptorchidism or germ cell neoplasia in situ {6119099; 9104938; 7674089; 2837162}, are pure in most cases, and represent the most common neoplasm (accounting for 48–62% of all neoplasms) at this age {15247758; 12352332}. There are no established epidemiological associations. Within the CNS, YSTs constitute 2–7.2% of intracranial germ cell tumours and 3.3–19.1% of the non-germinomas {9046301; 16220552; 2991485}. There is an equal sex distribution {16220522}.

Etiology

For postpubertal YST see section Intratubular germ cell neoplasia (male gonadal) ; for CNS YST see section Embryonal carcinoma .

Pathogenesis

See section Intratubular germ cell neoplasia (male gonadal) . Clinical and epigenetic studies of germ cell tumours arising after infancy / early childhood {26956871; 27391150; 28078450} suggest a primordial germ cell origin, although alternative origins have also been suggested {17705807; 22926997}. Prepubertal-type YST is a type I germ cell tumour {15738984}, which probably progressed from a pure (type I) teratoma, showing recurrent gains of 1q, 12p13, 20q, and 22, and losses of 1p, 4, and 6q {10908150; 11381369; 11585765; 12752256}. Association with a SNP variant of BAK1 has been reported {22072546}, suggesting that primitive germ cell apoptosis plays a pathogenetic role.

Macroscopic appearance

YSTs are predominantly solid, relatively homogeneous nodular masses, yellow to tan in colour and possibly friable or gelatinous (owing to myxoid changes). Focal cysts may be present. Necrosis and haemorrhage are uncommon.

Histopathology

Similar morphological patterns (possibly in combination) and immunophenotypes are seen in prepubertal and postpubertal YST. Both show intracytoplasmic and extracellular hyaline globules with a refractile eosinophilic quality and irregular, often band-like intercellular deposits of basement membrane, called parietal differentiation. Schiller–Duval bodies are commonly present. In testicular postpubertal-type YST, germ cell neoplasia in situ and regressive changes in the surrounding parenchyma are absent. The prepubertal type may be mixed with teratoma, whereas the other elements can also be found in the postpubertal type (all associated with 12p gain). The prepubertal type rarely arises after puberty {25427839}. Immunoreactivity for AFP (which is potentially also found in the glandular components of teratomas) characterizes YST {4038412; 1325276; 25400782}, in addition to cytokeratins, SALL4 {19390421}, glypican-3 (GPC3) {16896894; 17122513}, LIN28A {21057460}, possibly KIT (CD117) {22261704}, and PLAP {19820689}.

Cytology

Smear and squash preparations show cohesive clusters of epithelioid cells with distinct nucleoli and feature spindle mesenchymal elements and myxoid material.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: epithelioid cells in various patterns, with or without mesenchymal constituents.

Desirable: AFP expression by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

See section Intratubular germ cell neoplasia (male gonadal) . For CNS pure YST with leptomeningeal metastasis, treatment follows the regimens for poor prognostic groups with metastasis {11767296}. Staging of these tumours follows the Delphi consensus {26370356}.

Prognosis and prediction

Limited prognostic information is available for YST, owing to its rarity. Patients aged > 2 years may be more likely to have advanced-stage disease at diagnosis {3832112}, but effective chemotherapy results in survival rates approaching 100% {15247758; 15337806; 12218592}. Specific microRNA elevation after orchiectomy may predict metastasis {24012110; 22549310; 32157202}. Surgical excision of residual masses after chemotherapy may be performed but is usually not necessary. Postpubertal patients with stage III germ cell tumours containing YST elements have a poorer outcome than patients with tumours lacking such elements, probably indicating relative chemoresistance. This also correlates with an increased prevalence of YST in late recurrences {10680894} and in autopsy assessments of patients who died of progressive germ cell tumours in the current treatment era {2982043}. Therefore, it may be that the histological patterns of YST disproportionately found in chemoresistant cases and late recurrences (glandular predominant, hepatoid, parietal, and sarcomatoid) are adverse findings in the primary tumour, but this remains to be proved. Within the CNS, combined irradiation and chemotherapy resulted in 5- and 10-year survival rates of 75–80% {29048505; 31420671}, in which logarithmic decreases of AFP and hCG predict favourable outcomes {21359564}. AFP levels of > 1000 ng/mL and residual disease after treatment have negative implications {29048505}. Long-term survivors are at risk of premature death due to subsequent treatment-associated malignant neoplasms {25422317}.
