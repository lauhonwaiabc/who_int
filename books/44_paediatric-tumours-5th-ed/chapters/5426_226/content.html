5.4.2.6 Chondrosarcoma

Definition

Chondrosarcoma of bone is a malignant intramedullary (central) or surface (peripheral) cartilage-producing neoplasm. It may arise as a primary condition (conventional or classic chondrosarcoma) or be secondary to a precursor lesion (e.g. enchondroma or osteochondroma). Chondrosarcomas are graded according to a three-tier system, based predominantly on cellularity (which is inversely related to the quantity of matrix) and, to a lesser extent, on nuclear features. Given the fact that grade 1 lesions (especially when arising outside of the pelvis) only very rarely metastasize, the term “atypical cartilaginous tumour” is used for tumours involving the appendicular skeleton.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In children, conventional central chondrosarcomasmost commonly arise within the diaphysis (and less frequently in the metaphysis) of the long bones. They are less likely to arise from the pelvis or spinal column, where they are often secondary forms. The long bones, especially the humerus and femur, appear to represent the most common locations {2208016; 26408960}.

Children develop head and neck chondrosarcoma rarely (less frequently than adults). When primary chondrosarcomas arise in the head and neck of paediatric patients, the majority involve the maxillary sinus and mandibular condyle {10813732}.

Clinical features

Chondrosarcoma is the third most common primary malignancy of bone, usually arising in the fifth to seventh decades of life, with < 5% occurring in patients aged < 18 years. There appears to be a slight male predominance. Patients with chondrosarcoma usually experience localized pain in the affected region, and some report soft tissue swelling. The duration of clinical symptoms is variable and may be quite long {2208016}. Periosteal chondrosarcomas occur in the second decade of life in approximately 20% of cases, most commonly involving the proximal and distal femur and the proximal humerus. Pain, soft tissue swelling, and limitation of movement are the most common presenting symptoms {4051099}. When head and neck localization occurs, the symptoms vary depending upon the specific anatomical site but may include a mass effect (most common), nasal obstruction, headaches, epistaxis, and proptosis. Involved anatomical sites in the head and neck include (in decreasing order of frequency) the maxillary sinus, mandible, nasal cavity, and neck {10813732}.

Radiologically, regardless of anatomical site, most childhood chondrosarcomas appear lytic and poorly marginated. Within the long bones, chondrosarcomas arise within the metaphysis or, most commonly, the diaphysis (70% of cases), frequently showing areas of bone expansion, cortical thickening, and/or cortical endosteal erosion/destruction. Nevertheless, the most reliable radiological features of malignancy are cortical erosion and poor margination. Varying amounts of punctate and ring-like calcified densities are present in the majority of tumours. Periosteal reaction is observed in about one third of paediatric patients; however, soft tissue extension is present in as many as 50% of cases {2208016}. The radiographic features of periosteal chondrosarcoma typically demonstrate a relatively large mass, usually > 50 mm, with irregular margins, matrix calcifications, and predominantly bone surface involvement with periosteal buttresses {4051099}.

Epidemiology

Chondrosarcoma is a very rare neoplasm in patients aged < 18 years. Most cartilage-producing neoplasms in children are chondroblastic osteosarcomas. In the literature, childhood examples of chondrosarcoma represent < 5% of all conventional chondrosarcoma cases, more commonly arising in the second decade of life; they are very rare in the first decade of life {32175095; 2208016}. In this age group, a higher proportion (25–35%) of chondrosarcomas are secondary, most often arising from osteochondromas and less often in the setting of enchondromatosis {2208016; 26408960}. Central chondrosarcomas are three times as common as peripheral chondrosarcomas.

Etiology

The majority of paediatric chondrosarcomas appear to be sporadic, and the etiology is unknown. Among paediatric patients, it appears that most secondary chondrosarcomas are the result of chondrosarcomatous transformation of an osteochondroma, which may be solitary or multiple, and that they occur less commonly in the setting of enchondromatosis. In a series of 47 cases from the Mayo Clinic, 12 (26%) were secondary (8 were solitary osteochondromas; 3, multiple osteochondromatosis; 1, enchondromatosis) {2208016}. The Rizzoli Institute documented 5 peripheral chondrosarcomas, all within the setting of multiple osteochondromatosis, and 1 chondrosarcoma in a patient with Maffucci syndrome, among a total of 17 paediatric chondrosarcoma cases {26408960}.

Pathogenesis

Patients with osteochondromas, whether solitary or multiple, are at increased risk of developing chondrosarcoma (estimated to be ~1% and ~5%, respectively). Chromosomal aberrations involve either EXT1 (8q24.11) or EXT2 (11p11.2) {21703028}. IDH1 and IDH2 mutations are not seen in osteochondromas.

Patients with enchondromatosis (both those with Ollier syndrome and those with Maffucci syndrome), have somatic mutations involving IDH1 or IDH2. They are at increased risk of developing chondrosarcoma. The actual likelihood of malignant transformation is largely dependent upon the anatomical localization of the lesions, with the greatest risk seen in cases involving both small and long/flat bones (46%) and a lower risk seen in cases involving only the small bones of the hands and feet (15%) {22147000}.

Macroscopic appearance

Regardless of location – central or peripheral – the neoplasm is often lobular, with a bluish-grey to white glistening cut surface. More myxoid areas, as well as cystic change, may be present. Yellow-white chalky areas indicate mineralization (calcifications). When arising in intramedullary locations, cortical erosion and destruction (sometimes with soft tissue extension) is frequent. Occasionally, cortical thickening may be prominent. Fleshy brown areas may indicate dedifferentiation.

Peripheral chondrosarcomas, arising from an osteochondroma, initially manifest as an obviously thick (> 20 mm) and often irregular cartilage cap. Myxoid and cystic changes may occur. More advanced and/or higher-grade examples may obscure the stalk.

Periosteal chondrosarcomas display a lobulated, bluish-grey glistening mass, usually > 50 mm, localized to the bone surface (juxtacortical) but extending into adjacent soft tissues. Although the cortex is generally at least eroded, medullary involvement is not typically seen.

Histopathology

Conventional chondrosarcoma is a hyaline cartilage neoplasm that may be significantly myxoid but is not associated with osteoid production. The morphological features depend in part on the histological grade, according to a three-tiered system based predominantly on cellularity, which is inversely related to the quantity and quality of matrix, and (to a lesser extent) on nuclear features. Mitotic figures are generally only seen in the higher-grade forms. Björnsson and colleagues reported that among 338 patients of all ages with chondrosarcoma of the pelvic girdles and long bones of the extremities, most examples (61%) were grade 1 lesions, approximately 36% were grade 2, and only 3% were grade 3 {9827715}. In a series of 47 patients aged < 17 years, Young et al. reported an even greater percentage of lower-grade paediatric tumours, with 39 (83%) being grade 1, 7 (15%) grade 2; and 1 (2%) grade 3.{2208016} Among 10 central paediatric chondrosarcomas, Gambarotti et al. documented 5 (50%) as grade 1, 4 (40%) as grade 2, and 1 (10%) as grade 3, and among 5 peripheral chondrosarcomas, 3 (60%) were grade 1, 1 (20%) was grade 2, and 1 (20%) was grade 3 {26408960}. Gambarotti et al. also documented two grade 2 periosteal chondrosarcomas. However, there is interobserver variability in regard to histological grading of cartilaginous tumours {18852676}.

Grade 1 chondrosarcomas represent one of the most challenging diagnoses in bone pathology. The tumours are generally composed of confluent sheets of well-developed hyaline cartilage with mild to moderate cellularity, overlapping the degrees of cellularity observed in enchondroma. Individual chondrocytes may be seen solitarily and in small clusters but generally show only minimal atypia with slightly enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei. Binucleated chondrocytes may be observed, but these cells may also be seen in enchondroma. The most helpful diagnostic feature for chondrosarcoma is the presence of permeation of cortical and/or medullary bone. However, this may be difficult to establish in curettage specimens, although it is easily found in resections. Permeation is defined as the presence of lobules of hyaline cartilage that infiltrate and replace portions of medullary and/or cortical bone, entrapping completely residual lamellar bone within the cartilaginous matrix. The presence of myxoid change tends to be more prominent in chondrosarcoma than in enchondroma. Tumour necrosis, high cellularity, and mitotic activity are features usually seen in higher-grade lesions and are generally not helpful in the differential diagnosis of grade 1 chondrosarcoma and enchondroma. Extension of tumour into adjacent soft tissues is generally required to establish a diagnosis of grade 1 chondrosarcoma of the digits, because permeation of pre-existing bone is less commonly observed. It is essential that clinical and radiographic correlation be undertaken.

Within the long bones and limb girdles, grade 2 chondrosarcomas are easily recognized as malignant. The tumours are moderately cellular and tend to show greater nuclear atypia and pleomorphism than grade 1 tumours. Nevertheless, the hyaline cartilage matrix remains well-differentiated and easily recognizable, despite an increase in myxoid stromal changes. Mitoses are usually present but may not be obvious.

Rarest of all are the grade 3 chondrosarcomas. Such tumours are highly cellular, with at least a focal tendency towards spindling of the tumour cells. The hyaline cartilage differentiation tends to be less well developed than in the lower-grade forms, and myxoid changes may dominate. Mitotic figures are easily detected.

Accurate grading of chondrosarcomas requires meticulous sampling of the resection specimens, because there may be variations in the grade of the tumour among different areas. However, the presence of osteoid production and/or more solid areas lacking entirely hyaline cartilage differentiation should prompt the consideration of other diagnoses, primarily chondroblastic osteosarcoma and (less likely) dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. The degree of cellularity in chondroblastic osteosarcomas tends to be more variable from one microscopic field to the next, and the cytological atypia is more severe. An abrupt transition from a conventional low-grade chondrosarcoma to a higher-grade non-chondrosarcomatous sarcoma is typical of dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. The dedifferentiated component may be heterogeneous, ranging from undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma to osteosarcoma, or even showing rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation {3719521}. For all practical purposes, dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma is virtually never observed in children.

Periosteal chondrosarcoma is distinguished from periosteal chondroma by its large size (≥ 50 mm), absence of a limiting periosteum and of buttressing at the margins, and presence of soft tissue invasion {26408960; 4051099}. Not surprisingly, periosteal chondrosarcoma shows an even higher degree of cellularity and chondrocyte atypia than periosteal chondroma. Soft tissue invasion is frequently observed, but medullary involvement is only rarely seen. The clinical and radiological features may be more reliable than the histological findings for establishing the diagnosis.

Cytology

FNAB may be an accurate tool for diagnosing paediatric bone and soft tissue tumours; however, it is generally only possible when sufficient cortical thinning or soft tissue extension is present, allowing the fine needle access to the tumour {9796716}. For this reason, the diagnosis of enchondroma is generally not possible; therefore, the differential diagnosis is mainly between chondrosarcoma and chondroblastic osteosarcoma. Cytological smears of low-grade chondrosarcoma typically exhibit abundant amounts of mostly hypocellular matrix material, appearing metachromatic to magenta (best observed in Romanowsky-type stains), accompanied by scattered, mostly uniform, chondrocytes, sometimes within lacunae. The tumour cells are uninucleated and larger than those cells typically observed in enchondroma. Binucleated forms are rarely observed. Myxoid change/degeneration is a helpful feature and appears as a metachromatic granular background film in addition to the hyaline cartilage fragments. Higher-grade forms tend to be associated with greater cellularity, nuclear atypia, and less obvious background matrix material. Nevertheless, the degree of cytological atypia is usually greater in chondroblastic osteosarcomas; cell block material may be helpful in establishing the presence of osteoid production by the neoplastic cells {11486348}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis is not helpful for the distinction of benign cartilaginous tumours (enchondroma) from chondrosarcoma, because both frequently harbour mutations in the genes encoding the metabolic enzymes IDH1 and (less commonly) IDH2 {23598960}. However, on rare occasions, the presence of these mutations may help distinguish chondrosarcoma from chondroblastic osteosarcoma, which lacks IDH1 and IDH2 mutations.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intramedullary localization with cortical erosion or thickening (for central long bone forms); soft tissue extension; mature hyaline cartilage tumour with variable amounts of myxoid matrix and cellularity dependent upon histological grade; presence of mitotic figures among higher-grade tumours; permeation of bone with entrapment of bone trabeculae.

Staging

Tumour staging is per bone sarcoma protocols.

Prognosis and prediction

The preferred treatment depends upon histological grade, stage, and anatomical location. Grade 1 chondrosarcomas of the appendicular skeleton and long bones may be treated with curettage and adjuvants, whereas axial and pelvic lesions require en bloc resections for local control. However, local recurrence may occur in as many as 63% of patients treated with intralesional curettage alone {2208016}. Grade 2 and 3 chondrosarcomas should be completely excised via en bloc resection or (if necessary) amputation, to obtain negative margins. Distant metastasis is typically observed in high-grade chondrosarcomas, but it may be rarely seen with grade 1 tumours, including those of the appendicular skeleton {9210708}.

Earlier studies suggested that the prognosis of childhood chondrosarcoma was poorer than that of its adult counterpart, but this was presumably due to inclusion of mesenchymal chondrosarcomas and what most would consider today as chondroblastic osteosarcomas among the data for chondrosarcoma {27148438}. More recent literature, evaluating only pathologically confirmed conventional chondrosarcomas in the paediatric age group, has shown no significant differences in prognosis or survival between children and adults {2208016; 26408960}. For children, like for adults, the prognosis correlates with histological grade and anatomical location. However, most paediatric conventional chondrosarcoma cases, regardless of anatomical origin, are low-grade and tend to have an excellent prognosis {10813732; 2208016}. Of the 27 paediatric chondrosarcomas with clinical follow-up in the Mayo Clinic series, none developed metastases {2208016}. At the Rizzoli Institute, follow-up data were available for 15 patients; 1 patient with Maffucci syndrome reportedly developed two grade 2 chondrosarcomas, involving the third metacarpal bone and a rib, resulting in lung metastases and death 2 years after the initial surgery {26408960}. Dedifferentiation, even among secondary chondrosarcomas, is extraordinarily rare in paediatric patients, documented only as isolated case reports {8577508}.
