5.4.1.2 Osteoid osteoma

Definition

Osteoid osteoma is a benign bone-forming tumour characterized by its small size (< 20 mm), limited growth potential, and disproportionate pain that is usually responsive to NSAIDs.

ICD-O coding

9191/0 Osteoid osteoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E83.Z & XH61J9 Benign osteogenic tumours, unspecified & Osteoid osteoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Long bones, particularly the femur and tibia, are affected in 50% of cases. Other typical locations include the small bones of the hands and feet and the posterior elements of the spine {22052644; 8272884}. Uncommon locations include flat bones and craniofacial bones. Osteoid osteoma preferentially involves the cortex (in 75% of cases); less frequently, the medulla is involved (in 25%). Subperiosteal/surface lesions are rare {1546333; 22528893}.

Clinical features

The usual presenting symptom is pain. At first intermittent and mild with nocturnal exacerbation, the pain eventually becomes relentless to the point of interfering with sleep. In about 80% of patients, NSAIDs, even in small doses, completely (albeit temporarily) relieve the pain {3956019}. On physical examination, there is often an area of localized tenderness, redness, and swelling. Less common manifestations are site-dependent. For example, osteoid osteomas at the ends of long bones may occur with swelling and effusion of the nearest joint, whereas intra-articular osteoid osteomas can result in secondary osteoarthritis and ectopic ossification {27325304; 3941866}. With cases in the spine, patients may present with painful scoliosis due to spasm of the spinal muscles {6693472}. In the fingers, the persistent soft tissue swelling and periosteal reactions may result in functional loss that leads to numerous surgeries and large en bloc excisions {9457591}.

Imaging

On plain radiographs, the cortical lesions show eccentric cortical thickening, within which may be seen a faint focus of lucency (the nidus, often occult on plain films). CT, the best imaging method by which to demonstrate the nidus, often shows a small ovoid lucency containing variable central mineralization, surrounded by thickened cortex and frequently endosteal new bone formation. Lucent channels representing vessels may be seen in the thickened cortex {21178063; 8134575}. MRI shows predominantly reactive changes, including hyperintensity, suggesting oedema in bone marrow and soft tissue. 99mTc scintigraphy shows intense uptake at the site of the nidus and less marked activity in the surrounding reactive bone. Subperiosteal lesions show little reactive sclerosis but cause erosion of the underlying cortex. Oedema-like hyperintensity should be seen on MRI in the vicinity of active lesions. Intra-articular osteoid osteomas show imaging features of a monoarthropathy, with altered joint space, effusion/synovitis, marrow oedema, and poor visualization of the nidus unless CT is performed; diagnosis is often delayed owing to the nonspecific presentation {11120413}.

Epidemiology

Osteoid osteomas represent 10–12% of primary bone tumours. They usually affect children and adolescents, although they are occasionally seen in older individuals. There is a male predominance (M:F ratio: 2:1).

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Rearrangement of FOS has been identified in both osteoblastomas and osteoid osteomas, further strengthening the link between these two lesions {29858576}. The rearrangement is present in 89% of cases and may be detected at the protein level using FOS N-terminus immunohistochemistry, which results in nuclear immunoreactivity in the osteoblastic cells.

Only 3 cases of osteoid osteoma have shown clonal chromosomal aberrations. Structural alterations involving 22q13.1 were found in 2 of these cases {11172903; 9703280}. Prostaglandins (especially PGE2 and PGI2) and COX-2 have been implicated in the characteristic pain syndrome of osteoid osteoma and may explain the exquisite sensitivity to NSAIDs {11853083; 8344659}.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteoid osteoma is a small, round, red, gritty or granular, often cortically based lesion surrounded by (and sharply circumscribed from) ivory-white sclerotic bone. The lesion seldom exceeds 10 mm in greatest dimension.

Histopathology

The central portion (nidus) of the lesion contains differentiated plump osteoblasts present as a single layer around trabeculae of woven osteoid or mineralized bone {13120827}. Vascularized connective tissue, within which are fibroblast-like stromal cells and cells differentiating into osteoblasts, separates the trabeculae. The osteoid matrix may be microscopically disposed in a sheet-like configuration, but very often it is organized into microtrabecular arrays. Osteoclasts may also be seen engaging in remodelling. Significant nuclear pleomorphism and cartilage are absent. Hypervascular sclerotic bone with enlarged Haversian canals surrounds the nidus and tends to be more pronounced as lesions approach the bone surface. The interface between the nidus and the surrounding reactive bone is very abrupt and circumscribed, a finding that supports the indolent local behaviour of osteoid osteoma. The central area of the nidus may display intense mineralization. The nidus can be histologically indistinguishable from osteoblastomas, and both lesions are conventionally separated by size: osteoid osteomas are smaller lesions (< 20 mm). The pathological evaluation of osteoid osteoma has undergone changes because of the increasing use of minimally invasive techniques to ablate these tumours. If the diagnosis can be made clinically and radiographically, the osteoid osteoma is sometimes ablated without prior biopsy, although a core biopsy may be obtained immediately before ablation. In other cases, fragments obtained from a drill procedure are sent for histological opinion. The yield of scant diagnostic tissue in these cases is not only lower than with reselection specimens, but the histopathology is often obscured by heat or crush artefacts {20204353}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FOS gene rearrangement may be found in osteoid osteomas, but it is also found in osteoblastomas and epithelioid haemangiomas {29858576}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: radiographic demonstration of a small (< 20 mm) central nidus, often surrounded by reactive sclerosis; histological evidence of a bone-forming tumour composed of microtrabeculae of woven bone rimmed by plump osteoblasts in a vascularized stroma.

Desirable: evident abrupt transition between a central nidus and peripheral sclerotic bone; typical nocturnal pain relieved by NSAIDs.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of osteoid osteoma is excellent, and recurrences are uncommon. Some lesions have been reported to disappear without surgical therapy {21675379}. Although traditional surgical excision is curative, it is sometimes challenging to resect small lesions, particularly when there is prominent reactive sclerosis. Less invasive techniques including CT-guided core drill excision, image-guided percutaneous radiofrequency ablation (RFA), cryoablation, high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU), and laser photocoagulation have largely replaced the traditional approach {14663625; 21675379; 26612546; 20066410}. Arthroscopic excision is used for intra-articular tumours.
