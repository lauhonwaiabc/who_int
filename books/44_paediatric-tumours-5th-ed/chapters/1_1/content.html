1 Chapter 1: Introduction to paediatric tumours

“If you do not know the names of things, the knowledge of them is lost too.”

—Carl Linnaeus, Philosophia Botanica, 1751 [[Beil KM. What Linnaeus saw: a scientist’s quest to name every living thing. New York (NY): W. W. Norton & Company; 2019.]]

Ever since the mid-1700s, when Carl Linnaeus developed a system to consistently classify living species [[Blunt W. The compleat naturalist: a life of Linnaeus. London (UK): Frances Lincoln; 2001.]], scientists in the biological disciplines have continued to classify phenomena using a reproducible taxonomical approach in an effort to improve our conceptualization and understanding of the items under study. This approach offers flexibility in two dimensions: it allows for horizontal growth with the addition of new classes, and it permits vertical expansion with the recognition of new subtypes within existing classes. Over the past two and a half centuries, this system has proved its usefulness.

Since 1956, WHO has been responsible for the classification of tumours in all organs and systems. However, despite this publication history spanning more than six decades, this is the first volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series (the WHO Blue Books) dedicated to paediatric tumours. This is explained, in part, by the fact that the subspecialty dedicated to the formal study of paediatric pathology is also relatively new. But there are other reasons too. For example, cancer is much less frequent in children than in adults, affecting only about 1 in 400 children by their fifteenth birthday, and only 1 in 320 people by their nineteenth birthday {28410997}. This makes paediatric cancer a relatively rare disease, with only 1–5% of all cancers occurring before the age of 20 years {32433739} [[Cancer Research UK [Internet]. Oxford (UK): Cancer Research UK; 2020. Cancer incidence by age: All cancers combined incidence by age; last reviewed 2020 Mar 10. Available from: https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/health-professional/cancer-statistics/incidence/age.]]. In global statistics, cancer does not appear among the primary causes of death in children aged < 5 years [[Global Health Observatory [Internet]. Geneva (Switzerland): World Health Organization; 2021. Distribution of causes of death among children aged < 5 years (%). Available from: https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/distribution-of-causes-of-death-among-children-aged-5-years-(-).]]; it is relegated to sixteenth position by other causes such as infections, prematurity complications, malformations, malnutrition, and accidents [[Roser M, Ritchie H, Dadonaite B. Our World In Data [Internet]. 2013. Child and infant mortality; updated 2019 Nov. Available from: https://ourworldindata.org/child-mortality.]]. These causes are often related to the prevailing socioeconomic factors in low- and middle-income countries, where the majority of the world’s population of children live. However, it should be stressed that cancer is a leading cause of death from disease beyond infancy in high-income countries, and this is likely to become true also in low- and middle-income countries as poverty-related causes of death are gradually reduced.

The ability to diagnose cancer in children increases in parallel with the level of socioeconomic development. This is why modelling studies suggest that as many as 50% of cases may remain undiagnosed in some regions of the world {30824204}. Given the suboptimal diagnostic and treatment facilities in low- and middle-income countries, along with financial hardship and a lack of awareness at the individual and societal levels, the survival of children with cancer is much lower than in the most privileged settings {29395269}, mostly owing to late diagnosis and lack of effective treatment [[Cancer Atlas [Internet]. Atlanta (GA): American Cancer Society; 2019. Cancer in children. Available from: https://canceratlas.cancer.org/the-burden/cancer-in-children/.]]. This emphasizes the importance of addressing paediatric cancer in all income settings {32240612}, and this WHO Blue Book responds thoroughly to this need by formalizing the classification standards.

The paediatric pathology subspecialty is not defined on the basis of the anatomical location or common physiology of the conditions studied, but rather by the age and developmental stage of the patients. The consequences of this are two-fold: (1) this subspecialty is very comprehensive, because it studies all organs and systems; and (2) the quantity of information that must be taken into account is extremely high, because the epidemiology patterns in this age group change rapidly as the morphological features of a given tissue evolve throughout the stages of childhood development. This makes the study of paediatric pathology demanding, if only for the simple reason that multiple organs and systems are studied while still in the process of development. The critical importance of developmental stage in this subspecialty is evidenced by the first question a paediatric pathologist always asks when confronted with a specimen: “How old is the patient?” Furthermore, the spectrum of paediatric cancers is completely different from that of adult cases, with very few epithelial tumours occurring in children and the majority of paediatric entities almost never occurring in adults.

Many other volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series have dealt with paediatric tumours within the context of their various anatomical or physiological categories. However, this is the first volume in which a concerted effort has been made, by an international group of many specialists, to establish a consensus classification of paediatric tumours that also incorporates, wherever possible and appropriate, their developmental context. This is of utmost importance, because in paediatric pathology development is the key.

In 1878, the distinguished German pathologist Julius Cohnheim proposed that the proliferation of embryonal tissues was the cause of cancer – a theory he debated with his mentor, Rudolf Virchow {31308849}. This concept was revisited and supported in the mid-20th century by Rupert Allan Willis in his seminal lecture “The Borderland of Embryology and Pathology” {15426876}.

Cancer in children, given its biological basis, may be simultaneously considered a paradox and a predictable event: a paradox because cancer is generally considered a disease associated with a more mature phase of life, resulting from the accumulation of damage over the course of a lifetime (either through natural cell ageing or due to carcinogenic external factors), but a predictable event because cancer is also a product of incorrect development, which explains why children can also be affected. In fact, many paediatric cancers are considered to be the result of a blockage in maturation or in a developmental process.

Cancer chemotherapy was first introduced by Sidney Farber, a founder of paediatric pathology, with his original 1948 description of the temporary remission of acute leukaemia in children treated with folic acid antagonists {16803563}. Nowadays, modern molecular analyses are necessary – not only to improve diagnostic precision, but also to identify molecular pathways responsible for different chemotherapeutic responses, or potentially targetable genetic changes, leading to the development of risk stratification models and better treatments. Therefore, the current WHO classification of paediatric tumours incorporates the molecular data required for an integrated pathology report whenever possible. These efforts require collaboration among pathologists, oncologists, radiologists, surgeons, and informed patients/families in order to appropriately obtain, triage, and process diagnostic tissue, facilitating our ability to provide the best possible medical treatment for children with tumours. Incorporating molecular data into the classification will also be important for cancer registries, which will be able to provide detailed information on the distribution of various entities, often very rare, on a global scale. This also helps to reduce interobserver variability even in regions of the world where expertise in paediatric pathology is scarce.

Because paediatric pathology is defined by the criterion of patient age rather than organ or topography, this paediatric tumours volume has been developed through a combined effort of experts in the pathology of the various organs and systems affected by paediatric tumours and of experts in the field of paediatric pathology, who are continually exposed to these relatively uncommon tumours. This is an important collaboration, because many children, especially older ones, can have adult-type tumours (and in rare cases adults can have paediatric tumours), and tumours/cancers in children commonly occur in the setting of a genetic tumour syndrome – which has important implications for these patients and their families and often necessitates lifelong medical follow-up. We have striven to produce a well-balanced presentation of the state of the art in the pathology of paediatric tumours. A correct understanding of neoplasms in children allows us to classify them appropriately in light of current protocols used by the paediatric oncology community, to apply the accepted nomenclature, and to provide the best treatment approaches. More and more, diagnostic pathology will also (we hope) play an increasingly important role by providing information about predictive biomarkers, independent of whether such markers are also important diagnostically.

Long awaited, this publication is timely for supporting the WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer, which aims to save lives and reduce suffering for children with cancer [[St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital [Internet]. Memphis (TN): St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital; 2021. WHO Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer. Available from: https://www.stjude.org/global/collaborating-to-cure/global-initiative.html.]]. By giving (the correct) names to childhood cancers, we equip ourselves for the mission to defeat them.
