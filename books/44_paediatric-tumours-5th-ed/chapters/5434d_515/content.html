5.4.3.4D Fibrous dysplasia

Definition

Fibrous dysplasia is a benign intramedullary fibro-osseous neoplasm that can be multifocal and is characterized by distorted, poorly organized, and inadequately mineralized woven bone and intervening fibrous tissue.

ICD-O coding

8818/0 Fibrous dysplasia

ICD-11 coding

FB80.0 Fibrous dysplasia of bone

Related terminology

Not recommended: liposclerosing myxofibrous tumour; fibrocartilaginous dysplasia; ossifying fibroma of long bones; Kempson–Campanacci lesion.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Fibrous dysplasia can affect any bone, most commonly those of the skull, femur, and rib, in both monostotic and polyostotic forms {12703030}.

Clinical features

Osseous deformity with associated pain and pathological fractures can occur in fibrous dysplasia; rare cases produce excess FGF23, causing paraneoplastic osteomalacia {12952917}. In McCune–Albright syndrome, fibrous dysplasia is associated with endocrinopathies and abnormalities of cutaneous pigmentation; in Mazabraud syndrome it is associated with intramuscular myxomas {14691924}. Fibrous dysplasia appears in standard radiographs as a well-defined, non-aggressive geographical lesion with a ground-glass matrix; in the proximal femur, shepherd’s crook deformity is characteristic.

Epidemiology

Fibrous dysplasia occurs both in children and in adults, with no sex predilection.

Etiology

Fibrous dysplasia is caused by postzygotic activating missense mutations in GNAS (20q13.32), encoding Gsα {22245114}.

Pathogenesis

Fibrous dysplasia is characterized by GNAS activating mutations in 50–70% of cases; p.R201H and p.R201C are the most common {22245114}.

Macroscopic appearance

Fibrous dysplasia is a tan-grey lesion that expands the host bone. Cystic degeneration is common; grossly identifiable chondroid nodules are exceptional.

Histopathology

Fibrous dysplasia is composed of a variably cellular proliferation of bland fibroblastic cells with irregular curvilinear trabeculae of woven or (rarely) lamellar bone without conspicuous osteoblastic rimming. Rounded psammomatous calcifications resembling cementum are much more often seen in craniofacial lesions than in appendicular lesions. Nodules of benign hyaline cartilage (also with endochondral ossification), degenerative aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes, and/or fibrohistiocytic replacement with numerous foam cells (mimicking fibroxanthoma) can be seen.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The detection of GNAS mutations can be useful to confirm a diagnosis of fibrous dysplasia.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a fibro-osseous lesion consisting of variable amounts of irregular curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone without osteoblastic rimming embedded within a fibrous component composed of bland fibroblastic cells and abundant collagenous stroma.

Desirable: activating missense mutations in GNAS (p.R201H, p.R201C).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Monostotic fibrous dysplasia is a benign tumour that may result in skeletal deformity, whereas polyostotic fibrous dysplasia may be crippling. Exceptional cases of malignant transformation have been described {10525796}.
