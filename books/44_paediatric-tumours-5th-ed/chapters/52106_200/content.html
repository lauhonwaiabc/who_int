5.2.10.6 Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour

Definition

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumours (PMTs) are neoplasms with a distinctive morphology that usually secrete FGF23, inducing phosphaturic oncogenic osteomalacia in most patients.

ICD-O coding

8990/0 Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, NOS

8990/3 Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2F7C & XH9T96 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of connective or other soft tissue & Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, benign

2F7C & XH3B27 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of connective or other soft tissue & Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, malignant

Related terminology

Acceptable: phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, mixed connective tissue type.

Subtype(s)

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, malignant

Localization

PMTs may arise in any soft tissue or bone location {1755097; 3545439; 14707860; 19609206; 27443518; 28614212}. Retroperitoneal, mediastinal {10846577}, visceral {19125258}, and intracranial {26722864} locations are exceptional.

Clinical features

Longstanding osteomalacia is the most frequent manifestation. Laboratory studies show severe hypophosphataemia and increased levels of FGF23, which can also be demonstrated by RT-PCR in tissue sections of PMTs, even before the development of tumour-induced osteomalacia {19609206; 14707860}. PMTs are notoriously difficult to identify because of their tendency to occur as small and inconspicuous lesions in unsuspected places, requiring a meticulous clinical and imaging exploration {10594133; 12170016; 12170004}. 68Ga-DOTATATE PET imaging is particularly useful for revealing PMTs in patients with renal phosphate wasting {23295468; 27533306}.

Epidemiology

PMTs usually occur in middle-aged adults and show no sex predilection. They are rare and account for < 0.01% of all bone and soft tissue tumours {19609206; 14707860; 3545439; 27443518; 28614212}. They are extremely rare in children, but well-documented cases have been reported in relatively hidden locations in the first decade of life {10594133} and even in young infants {10594133}.

Etiology

A substantial proportion of cases carry FN1::FGFR1 gene fusions {27443518}, suggesting that FGFR1 pathways are implicated in the pathogenesis of PMTs. In a case affecting a young infant, a translocation causing increased levels of α-Klotho resulted in hypophosphataemic rickets and hyperparathyroidism {18308935}. Furthermore, fusion-negative PMTs frequently overexpress α-Klotho (or less frequently, β-Klotho). α-Klotho is an obligatory co-receptor for FGF23–FGFR1 binding, and its aberrant expression in osteoid-producing cells may result in the overexpression of FGF23 and in tumorigenesis {31792355}.

Pathogenesis

Most PMTs express FGFR1, independent of their fusion status {26464698; 25319834}, suggesting that activated receptor tyrosine kinase FGFR1 signalling pathways could drive PMT tumorigenesis and the secretion of FGF23, leading to the paraneoplastic phosphaturic effect through the inhibition of phosphate reabsorption by the renal proximal tubule {12711747}. This results in tumour-induced osteomalacia in most patients {11344269; 14733920; 11409890; 12187320}. Some PMTs are not associated with osteomalacia, probably because they have been identified and resected before the paraneoplastic syndrome develops, and this has also been described in children {26722864}. Low levels of FGF23 have been identified in non-PMTs, including examples of fibrous dysplasia {12952917} aneurysmal bone cyst, and chondromyxoid fibroma of bone {22933546}.

Macroscopic appearance

PMTs have a nonspecific macroscopic appearance, forming masses in the soft tissues or bone. Adipose tissue and extensive calcification are frequently present.

Histopathology

Most PMTs feature fibroblast-like spindle mesenchymal cells embedded in a hyalinized matrix, which may show coarse calcification. Poorly differentiated chondroid and areas of osseous metaplasia are frequent. Rich vascularization, with capillaries and larger vessels arranged in a pericytomatous or cavernous haemangioma pattern, is characteristic. An array of cells (including osteoclasts, fibrohistiocytic elements, and adipose tissue), frequently surrounded by woven bone, is frequently present {10594133; 19609206; 14707860; 3545439; 16447065}. PMTs may show odontogenic elements if they arise in the jaws {30206408}.

Recurring PMTs may herald malignancy and show atypia, pleomorphism, and necrosis, as well as increased cellularity and mitotic count, with a pleomorphic sarcomatous or fibrosarcomatous appearance {11310207; 18329207}.

The immunophenotype of most PMTs includes the expression of CD56, ERG, FGFR1, SATB2, and/or SSTR2A {27443518; 28614212; 28858396; 26464698}. FGF23 may be detected, but insufficient antibody specificity and a scarcity of commercially available reagents remain unresolved challenges {14707860; 19609206}.

Cytology

Diagnosis by FNAB has been reported. FNAB shows spindle cells with bland cytological features, scant cytoplasm, scattered osteoclasts, and the characteristic stromal matrix {18181193; 22927293}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FN1::FGFR1 or FN1::FGF1 fusions are confirmatory ancillary diagnostic techniques.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bland spindle cells embedded in a hyalinized matrix with coarse calcification; rich vascularity; phosphaturic wasting and/or osteomalacia, which are cured by tumour removal.

Desirable: elevated FGF23 expression in serum or in tumour tissue; demonstration of FN1::FGFR1 or FN1::FGF1 fusion.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The vast majority of PMTs are benign and cured by tumour removal, with quick resolution of phosphate depletion and osteomalacia {12711747; 14707860; 19841566; 12054166}. Malignant tumours produce metastasis and can be lethal.
