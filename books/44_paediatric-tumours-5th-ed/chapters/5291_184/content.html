5.2.9.1 Schwannoma

Definition

Schwannoma is a benign nerve sheath tumour composed entirely or nearly entirely of differentiated neoplastic Schwann cells.

ICD-O coding

9560/0 Schwannoma

9560/0 Cellular schwannoma

ICD-11 coding

2F38 & XH98Z3 Benign neoplasm of other or unspecified sites & Schwannoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: neurilemoma.

Subtype(s)

Ancient schwannoma; cellular schwannoma; plexiform schwannoma; epithelioid schwannoma; microcystic/reticular schwannoma

Localization

Common sites of origin are peripheral nerves in the skin and subcutaneous tissues of the head and neck or along the flexor surfaces of the extremities. Spinal intradural extramedullary examples are also common and form dumbbell tumours when growing through neural foramina. Multiple paraspinal schwannomas are common in neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). Another frequent location is the vestibular division of the eighth cranial nerve, and bilateral involvement is a definitional criterion for NF2 {19545378}. Spinal intramedullary and CNS sites are rare {8331403}, as are those involving viscera (such as the gastrointestinal tract) and bone {22137423}.

Clinical features

Schwannomas are slow-growing tumours, often manifesting as asymptomatic masses or incidental findings on imaging studies. Spinal schwannomas may elicit sensory or, less frequently, motor symptoms. Patients with vestibular schwannomas often present with hearing loss and vertigo. Painful schwannomas may be associated with schwannomatosis.

Epidemiology

More than 90% of schwannomas are solitary and sporadic. Patients of all ages are affected, but the peak incidence is in the fourth to sixth decades of life. There is no known sex or race predisposition. Schwannomas represent 7% of peripheral neurogenic tumours in the paediatric age group {22420725}.

Etiology

The etiology of most sporadic schwannomas is unknown, although there is an established increased incidence associated with prior irradiation {3173432; 12381708}. There is an association with NF2 or schwannomatosis in some cases (see section Neurofibromatosis type 1, and section Neurofibromatosis type 2).

Pathogenesis

A causal relationship exists between schwannoma tumorigenesis and loss of expression of merlin (NF2, also known as schwannomin), the growth inhibitory protein product of the NF2 tumour suppressor gene located at 22q12.2 {9403715}. NF2-inactivating mutations have been detected in approximately 50–75% of sporadic cases {8889506; 23921927; 26360373; 28409725}. Underlying genetic events are predominantly frameshift and nonsense mutations, with loss of the remaining wildtype allele on chromosome 22. Other common mutations involve LATS1, LATS2, ARID1A, ARID1B, and DDR1, whereas a recurrent in-frame SH3PXD2A::HTRA1 fusion is found in roughly 10% of cases {26360373; 27723760}.

Multiple schwannomas are a feature of NF2 and schwannomatosis, both of which can also occur in mosaic or segmental forms {26851632; 27921248}.

Patients with NF2-associated schwannomas commonly present before the age of 30 years, whereas tumours usually manifest later in schwannomatosis. Bilateral vestibular schwannoma is a hallmark of NF2, often showing multifocal nerve involvement and a distinct nodular growth pattern. Patients may also develop multiple meningiomas (which are associated with increased morbidity and mortality) and spinal ependymomas {19545378}. NF2 is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, with 50% of cases representing new or sporadic mutations.

Schwannomatosis is characterized by the presence of multiple schwannomas, mostly (but not invariably) in the absence of vestibular nerve involvement. Cranial and cutaneous nerves are infrequently affected. Tumours are often associated with pain. Germline mutations of either SMARCB1 or LZTR1 (tumour suppressor genes) are found in 69–86% of patients with familial schwannomatosis {24362817; 25480913; 25335493} and in 40% of patients with sporadic schwannomatosis {27921248}. The tumorigenesis appears to be complex given that these tumours arise from a four-hit mechanism that involves two genes: a germline SMARCB1 or LZTR1 mutation is followed by a somatic NF2 mutation on the same chromosome 22 and a deletion of the entire other chromosome 22, leading to biallelic inactivation of both tumour suppressor genes simultaneously. Not surprisingly, therefore, the somatic NF2 mutation often differs among the schwannomas of any one patient, as well as between the schwannomas of different family members.

Macroscopic appearance

Schwannomas are mainly solitary and globoid, have a smooth surface, and measure < 100 mm in greatest dimension, except for giant schwannomas, which are mostly encountered in the lumbosacral region. Fewer than half have an evident attached nerve, which is most often small and draped over the tumour capsule. Except for those arising in CNS parenchyma, skin, viscera, and bone, the tumours are usually encapsulated. Sectioned tumours reveal firm, light-tan, glistening tissue, interrupted by white/yellow areas and/or patches of haemorrhage.

Histopathology

Conventional schwannoma is usually an encapsulated spindle cell tumour that is composed nearly entirely of well-differentiated Schwann cells. Schwannomas have a broad morphological range. The large majority are biphasic tumours with compact areas (Antoni A tissue) showing occasional nuclear palisading (Verocay bodies), alternating with loosely arranged foci (Antoni B tissue). Antoni A areas are the predominant component in the majority of paediatric schwannomas {28420320; 22420725}. Cells possess modest eosinophilic cytoplasm; no discernible cell borders; and normochromatic, elongated, tapered nuclei. Cytoplasmic nuclear inclusions, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic figures may be seen. Palisading (Verocay bodies) takes the form of parallel rows of Schwann cell nuclei separated by their aligned cell processes. Antoni B tissue commonly contains a cobweb-like network of tumour processes with collections of lipid-laden histiocytes and thick-walled, hyalinized blood vessels. Lymphoid aggregates under the capsule, or at the periphery in unencapsulated tumours, are less frequent in paediatric schwannomas than in adult cases {28420320; 22420725}. Tumours of the eighth cranial nerve show predominantly Antoni A tissue, and intestinal schwannomas typically lack Antoni B tissue. The most extreme deviation is seen in the morphological subtypes (see below).

Diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining for S100, which is more prominent in Antoni A areas than in Antoni B areas, is found in all tumours and subtypes. Similarly, SOX10 immunoreactivity is usually extensive {18636017; 22495377}. Expression of GFAP is less frequent and more variable. Retroperitoneal and mediastinal lesions are commonly positive for pankeratin AE1/AE3, owing to cross-reactivity with GFAP {16357842}. CD34 is commonly positive only in subcapsular areas, in contrast to the lattice-like staining pattern in neurofibromas, although a small subset of cases show more extensive positivity. Staining for NFP is helpful in identifying entrapped intratumoural axons, found in many sporadic schwannomas, albeit most often at their periphery {17721192}. EMA highlights perineurial cells in the capsule, if present.

Cellular schwannoma: This is the most frequent subtype in children {28420320; 31379028}. The tumours are composed exclusively or predominantly of Antoni A tissue, without Verocay bodies. In addition to the cells being closely packed, they are often hyperchromatic and mitotically active. Small areas of necrosis may be seen. These features may raise concern for malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour; however, the presence of conventional features of schwannoma aid in this distinction (encapsulation, subcapsular lymphocytes, hyalinized blood vessels, and Schwannian whorls). Cellular schwannoma shows hotspots of Ki-67 labelling (rather than diffuse increases), often with an index of < 20% (but an index of > 20% does not exclude the diagnosis) and p16 and H3 p.K27me3 positivity are retained {25189642}.

Plexiform schwannoma: These conventional or cellular subtypes often arise in skin or subcutaneous tissue, growing as thinly encapsulated plexiform or multinodular tumours {18439936; 3096870}. Less frequently these tumours can occur in the deep soft tissues {16006798}. The tumours come to clinical attention earlier in life, often in childhood and even at birth {14508393}, with some predilection for the trunk and the head and neck region. Most are sporadic, but some occur in patients with NF2 or schwannomatosis. Biphasic plexiform schwannomas are more readily identified pathologically than plexiform cellular examples. The tumours generally differ from the conventional schwannoma in their absence of a well-formed capsule and thick-walled vessels.

Other patterns: Some rare subtypes, such as ancient, epithelioid, and microcystic schwannoma, are described in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.fr/588.]].

Although most syndrome-associated schwannomas are not histologically distinguishable from their sporadic counterparts, several clinicopathological clues may indicate a setting of NF2 or schwannomatosis: young patient age, multiple tumours, extensive longitudinal involvement of a nerve, discontinuous or multinodular growth pattern, and a mosaic SMARCB1 immunostaining pattern (an admixture of positive and negative nuclei) {18422762; 28365909}. Some schwannomas feature predominantly small blue round cells (with or without structures resembling Homer Wright rosettes or giant rosettes) surrounding collagen fibres resembling those of low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma; these cases are often referred to as neuroblastoma-like, although they lack increased proliferative activity and show a typical schwannoma immunoprofile {30582192}. Another rare pitfall is a schwannoma with neuromelanin-like pigment accumulation that is positive on Fontana–Masson staining. Nevertheless, the histology is otherwise typical of schwannoma and the tumour cells are negative for more specific melanocytic markers such as HMB45 {10792787}; therefore, these should not be equated with the more aggressive Carney complex / PRKAR1A–associated malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumour (previously termed “melanotic schwannoma”).

Cytology

Aspirate smears of schwannoma typically yield cohesive syncytial fragments of spindle cells {25641870}. Within the fragments, variably wavy and bent tumour cell nuclei with tapered edges are seen, as well as fibrillary cytoplasm. Nuclear pleomorphism or degenerative atypia and intranuclear inclusions may be seen. Schwannomas may be difficult to distinguish from other spindle cell neoplasms on cytological preparations alone, and their diagnosis requires correlation with core biopsy and/or immunohistochemical staining {30457183}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Loss of chromosome 22q and/or mutation of NF2 are frequent but nonspecific molecular alterations. Schwannomas exhibit a distinct DNA methylation pattern {29539639; 26857854; 25399693; 27723760}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: classic schwannoma histopathology (e.g. Antoni A or Antoni B areas); extensive S100 or SOX10 expression.

Desirable: Verocay bodies; hyalinized blood vessels; for epithelioid schwannoma: absence of lattice-like CD34 staining; loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression; for syndrome-associated schwannoma: a mosaic pattern of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Schwannomas are benign and do not usually recur if treated by gross total resection. Cellular and plexiform examples are least amenable to total removal and sometimes can only be debulked. Malignant transformation of conventional schwannoma is extremely rare. In the small number of cases reported to date, it has most often taken the form of epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour {8067509; 11145248; 22082606}. Less common examples feature foci of conventional malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour, primitive neuroectodermal cells, rhabdomyosarcoma, and/or angiosarcoma {8067509; 8898847; 11145248; 12766593; 22082606; 27512546}.
