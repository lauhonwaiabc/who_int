5.2.6.2 Rhabdomyosarcomas

Definition

Rhabdomyosarcomas are a family of sarcomas with morphological and/or immunophenotypic evidence of skeletal muscle differentiation, comprising embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS), alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS), spindle cell / sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma (SCSRMS), and pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma.

Related terminology

Not recommended: myosarcoma; sarcoma botryoides.

Subtype(s)

Anaplastic embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma

Localization

ERMS arises most frequently in the head and neck region (including the orbit) and the genitourinary system, and less often in the biliary tract, retroperitoneum, or abdomen {25267746; 30091945}. ARMS arises in the extremities, with the head and neck, paraspinal, or perineal regions involved less frequently {30091945; 7882714; 6620082; 11835233}. SCSRMS most often involves the head and neck (parameningeal), axial/paraspinal region, paratestis, and extremities {8434703; 1599014; 30181563; 26501226}.

Clinical features

Patients with ERMS often present with site-specific symptoms (e.g. orbital tumours result in proptosis; genitourinary tumours result in bladder obstruction). ARMS is highly aggressive, typically manifesting as a rapidly growing soft tissue mass with distant or locoregional metastases present at diagnosis in 25–30% of patients {18467730; 22378628}. Patients with ARMS may also present primarily with bone marrow involvement mimicking leukaemia. SCSRMS manifests as a painless soft tissue mass, and although locoregional metastases may be present, distant metastases are rare at initial diagnosis {26823695; 31339226}.

Epidemiology

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft tissue sarcoma in children. ERMS accounts for 70–75% of all rhabdomyosarcoma cases in children, affecting younger children (median age: 6.5 years). ARMS accounts for 20–25% of all rhabdomyosarcoma cases and affects older children (median age: 15 years). SCSRMS accounts for 3–10% of all rhabdomyosarcoma cases and may arise at any age {30617281; 19536876; 22378628}.

Etiology

ERMS is associated with several syndromes involving RAS pathway alterations, including Costello syndrome (HRAS mutations), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1 mutations), and Noonan syndrome (mutations in several genes). Cases have also been described in Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (dysregulation of imprinting of the 11p15.5 region) and Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53 mutations). Uterine ERMS occurs in DICER1 syndrome. ARMS and SCSRMS are not associated with any syndromes {30617281}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic ERMS has a complex genome relative to other paediatric tumours. ERMS is characterized by whole-chromosome alterations including gains of chromosomes 2, 8, 11, 12, 13, and/or 20, or losses including monosomies 10 and 15. Loss of one of the two alleles at 11p15.5, containing multiple imprinted genes encoding growth factors (e.g. IGF2) or growth suppressors (e.g. H19 and CDKN1C), occurs as a result of whole-chromosome loss, deletion, or uniparental disomy {26349418}. Somatic driver mutations involving the RAS pathway (NRAS, KRAS, HRAS, NF1, FGFR4) have been identified in about 50% of ERMS cases, with RAS isoform mutations occurring in one third. Mutations in TP53 occur in 10% of ERMS cases and may be associated with histological anaplasia {24382691; 34166060}.

ARMS is characterized by a t(2;13)(q36;q14) translocation in 70–80% of cases, or a t(1;13)(p36;q14) translocation in 20–25% of cases {8098985; 8187070; 8275086; 22447499; 28521080}. These translocations juxtapose PAX3 (2q36.1) or PAX7 (1p36.13) with FOXO1 (13q14.11). PAX7::FOXO1 fusions are associated with amplification of the PAX7 locus. Variant gene rearrangements are seen in < 5% of cases of ARMS; they result from PAX3 fusing with FOXO4, NCOA1, NCOA2, or INO80D, or less commonly FOXO1 may pair with FGFR1 {12183429; 19953635; 21666686; 24436047}.

In infants (aged < 2 years) SCSRMS is associated with fusions involving VGLL2 or NCOA2 with partners including SRF, TEAD1, and CITED2 {23463663; 26501226; 32187044}. In older children, SCSRMS may have a MYOD1 p.L122R point mutation at the DNA-binding domain, which blocks MYOD1 function and imparts a MYC-binding capability {30181563; 24272621; 24824843; 24793135}. SCSRMS without recurrent identifiable genetic alterations may represent a spindle cell pattern of ERMS {30181563}. Recently described rhabdomyosarcomas with epithelioid and/or spindle cell morphology, mostly intraosseous, have been associated with either an EWSR1::TFCP2 or FUS::TFCP2 gene fusion and ALK overexpression or a MEIS1::NCOA2 gene fusion {30720533; 29758589; 31383960}.

Macroscopic appearance

Rhabdomyosarcomas are poorly circumscribed and infiltrative. ERMSs associated with a mucosal surface may have a characteristic polypoid gross appearance, although most are simply fleshy, pale-tan masses. ARMSs are frequently necrotic or haemorrhagic, whereas SCSRMS may be more whorled, in accordance with the increased collagenous matrix.

Histopathology

ERMS recapitulates embryonic skeletal muscle development with primitive mesenchymal cells in various stages of myogenesis. ERMS is variably cellular, ranging from stellate cells in an abundant myxoid stroma to sheets of poorly differentiated, densely packed cells with round to slightly angulated nuclei {23765537}. ERMS may have a prominent spindle cell morphology, although foci of typical ERMS are usually present {8434703}. Heterologous cartilage may be seen, particularly in tumours associated with DICER1 syndrome. Typical ERMS shows alternating loose and dense cellularity, often with scattered differentiated rhabdomyoblasts. Differentiation often becomes more evident after therapy {17006261}. Botryoid ERMS tightly abuts an epithelial surface and characteristically shows hypercellularity just under the epithelium (cambium layer).

Anaplastic ERMS is defined by the presence of markedly enlarged, atypical cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and/or large atypical mitotic figures {18985676}. Anaplastic features may be focal or diffuse; in children, tumours with extensive pleomorphism are considered to be anaplastic ERMS rather than pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma.

Classic ARMS shows delicate fibrovascular septa to which malignant cells cling and surround discohesive islands of tumour cells. The solid pattern of ARMS lacks these fibrovascular septa. In both patterns, tumour nuclei are round and more relatively uniform than in ERMS, with variable amounts of cytoplasm {28521080}. Multinucleated tumour cells are common, but true differentiated rhabdomyoblasts are rare. Occasional tumours show mixed embryonal and alveolar patterns; these tumours may harbour PAX7::FOXO1 gene fusions but are usually FOXO1 fusion–negative {23765537; 26756883}.

SCSRMS may be purely spindled with long, intersecting fascicles {26501226; 1599014; 8434703}. The sclerosing pattern is characterized by round cells, which may cling to collagen bands resulting in a microalveolar or pseudovascular pattern. Sclerosing areas may mimic osteosarcoma or (mesenchymal) chondrosarcoma {30181563}. Infantile SCSRMS harbouring VGLL2::NCOA2 fusions demonstrates a pure spindled morphology, sometimes with admixed maturing rhabdomyoblasts. MYOD1-mutated SCSRMS may be spindled or sclerosing.

For all rhabdomyosarcomas, skeletal muscle differentiation is confirmed by immunohistochemical expression of desmin, myogenin (MYF4), and/or MYOD1. ARMS with FOXO1 rearrangements shows strong diffuse nuclear staining for myogenin, whereas ERMS shows variable myogenin expression (10–80% of tumour nuclei) {23765537}. SCSRMS may have little to absent desmin or myogenin expression but show strong and diffuse nuclear staining for MYOD1 {26823695}. HMGA2 and EGFR (overexpressed in ERMS) and AP-2β and P-cadherin (overexpressed in ARMS) may help in assigning subtype {19469909; 24618610}.

Cytology

Cytological features vary depending on subtype, mirroring the cytomorphology described above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

PAX3::FOXO1 or PAX7::FOXO1 translocations are present in approximately 85% of morphological ARMS. SCSRMS may harbour MYOD1 p.L122R mutations or VGLL2::NCOA2 rearrangements {30617281}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for ERMS: primitive spindle to round cell morphology with differentiating rhabdomyoblasts; heterogeneous nuclear staining for myogenin; for ARMS: monomorphic round cells; homogeneous strong nuclear staining for myogenin; presence of PAX3, PAX7, or FOXO1 rearrangement; for SCSRMS: purely spindled morphology with intersecting fascicles or admixed sclerosing patterns with round cells and abundant collagenous/hyalinized matrix.

Desirable (in selected cases): MYOD1 mutations or VGLL2::NCOA2 gene rearrangements in SCSRMS; diffuse nuclear staining for MYOD1 in SCSRMS.

Staging

Modified TNM staging (the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group [IRSG] grouping system) is used in paediatric rhabdomyosarcoma. This system relies on site of origin, with risk stratification based on favourable site (e.g. bile ducts, orbit, head and neck, or genitourinary [excluding bladder, prostate, or parameningeal sites]) or unfavourable (other) site. The IRSG grouping system is used for surgical-pathological evaluation, including assessment of margins and residual disease {22378628; 33306276}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis depends upon histological and molecular features. ERMS generally has a favourable prognosis (although patients aged 1–9 years have a better outcome than infants or adolescents), whereas ARMS is more aggressive. Patients with FOXO1 fusion–positive rhabdomyosarcoma have a worse prognosis than those with FOXO1 fusion–negative rhabdomyosarcoma, and those with a PAX3::FOXO1 fusion seem to have an inferior outcome to those with a PAX7::FOXO1 fusion {30617281}. SCSRMS with MYOD1 mutations has a poor prognosis, whereas infants with VGLL2::NCOA2-positive SCSRMS have an excellent outcome {30181563; 26501226}.
