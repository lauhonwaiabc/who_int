2.3.2.10 Burkitt lymphoma

Definition

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a highly proliferative mature B-cell lymphoma composed of monomorphic, medium-sized cells with multiple small nucleoli and basophilic cytoplasm, which frequently arises at extranodal sites or as acute leukaemia and includes endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated subtypes. The lymphoma is characterized by a germinal-centre phenotype and a MYC gene translocation to an IG gene.

Related terminology

Not recommended: Burkitt cell leukaemia; atypical Burkitt leukaemia; Burkitt-like lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

The traditional subtypes of BL are endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated BL; however, recent molecular data suggest that the presence or absence of EBV might better correlate with distinct biological subtypes of BL.

Localization

Disease localization is strongly related to epidemiological subtype. In endemic BL the jaws and other parts of the facial bone are the most frequent sites of presentation {13628987}. The most common sites of sporadic BL are the abdomen and the head and neck region. Bone marrow involvement occurs in 20–30% of cases {16173961; 21030984} but is rare in immunodeficiency-associated BL {1868025}.

Immunodeficiency-associated BL may more commonly have nodal involvement, although extranodal involvement is also frequent {32735838}. Patients with all BL subtypes are at high risk of CNS involvement {29769263; 17761975}, with a frequency of 10–30% reported in clinical studies from Malawi {26914979} and the USA {32453640}.

Clinical features

BL has the shortest cellular doubling time of all human tumours; frequently, patients present with rapidly growing masses of recent onset and a high tumour burden. Symptoms are mass-related. Lymphadenopathy mainly occurs in immunodeficiency-associated BL. Burkitt leukaemia is diagnosed in cases with ≥ 25% neoplastic cells in bone marrow smears and is usually associated with tumour formation {31207026; 31961927}. It occurs in as many as 20% of cases, especially in immunodeficiency-associated BL {31955214}.

Epidemiology

Historically, three manifestations of BL were recognized: endemic, non-endemic (sporadic), and immunodeficiency-associated. These terms have been used to describe the epidemiology of BL {10577857}.

  • Endemic BL affects mainly young children in equatorial Africa. It is strongly related to malaria and EBV infection.
  • Sporadic BL usually affects children and young adults worldwide. It accounts for 30–40% of paediatric non-Hodgkin lymphomas but only 1–2% of adult lymphomas.
  • Immunodeficiency-associated BL is seen frequently in patients with HIV infection, but it also occurs in congenital immunodeficiencies and in posttransplant cases.

However, the designations of the endemic and sporadic subtypes are primarily based on incidence, whereas the subtypes may well differ in cellular biology and pathogenetic mechanisms {4306129; 10215347; 10025990} and there is no molecular-pathological definition of each historical subtype {10215347}. Immunodeficiency-associated BL may arbitrarily be defined as a lymphoma occurring in the context of immunodeficiency {10215347}. Current research suggests that EBV-positive BL and EBV-negative BL form discrete groups with similar molecular features regardless of epidemiological context and geographical region {15840698; 26468873; 28465297; 30617194; 34617271; 34725813}. The M:F ratio is highest before puberty and decreases with age {19810101}; it appears to be higher (as high as 10:1) for tumours involving the face or head structures {19434731}. In the USA, 50% of all BL cases occur in adults (aged 20–59 years), 28% occur in the elderly (> 60 years), and 22% occur in the paediatric age group (0–19 years). Age-specific incidence of BL shows heterogeneous age-specific peaks (at ~10 years and ~60 years) {18767045; 19810101; 22488262}, suggesting that there might be biological differences in BL diagnosed at different ages {34617271}.

Etiology

Gene rearrangements of MYC are a hallmark of BL; they have been shown to be related to activation-induced cytidine deaminase activity and are present in all epidemiological subtypes, although breakpoints differ between endemic and sporadic/immunodeficiency-associated BL {30617194}. It is assumed that antigenic stimulation that leads to B-cell proliferation in the germinal centre increases the chance of translocation of MYC. One such condition is Plasmodium falciparum infection. Recent studies demonstrate that children exposed to multiclonal P. falciparum infection have a higher risk of developing BL, which explains the association of malaria with endemic BL only in specific geographical areas {32718346; 26276629}. In sporadic BL and immunodeficiency-associated BL, etiological factors are not well defined. The role of EBV is diverse because the virus is present in most patients with endemic or immunosuppression-associated BL. The prevalence of EBV in sporadic BL varies according to geographical distribution, and early EBV exposure may increase the risk of sporadic BL {30977593}. Genetic factors appear to be important. This is supported by a consistent finding of a high M:F ratio in children, particularly in prepubescent children {26304908}. Other clues about genetic contribution include reports of familial BL clusters, including two recent cases in whom germline variants in TCF4 and CHD8 were reported {33051549}. Reports of high BL risk in rare inherited disorders, including inborn disorders of immune regulation (e.g. X-linked lymphoproliferative syndrome, also known as Purtilo syndrome or Duncan disease, MIM number: 308240; X-linked immunodeficiency with magnesium defect, EBV infection, and neoplasia [XMEN], MIM number: 300853) or DNA repair (e.g. ataxia telangiectasia, MIM number: 615919), or developmental disorders like Williams–Beuren syndrome (MIM number: 194050), also support a genetic predisposition for BL.

Pathogenesis

The chromosomal translocation deregulating the expression of MYC is probably the primary event in the pathogenesis of BL, but it is not sufficient to initiate and maintain neoplastic proliferation. Additional molecular alterations, and particularly recurrent mutations of ID3, TCF3 (activating the TCF3 pathway), and CCND3 play a central role in the pathogenesis of BL {23143595; 23143597; 22885699}. Some differences in the mutation profile are detected between the different epidemiological subtypes {31558468}. Mutational activation of B-cell receptor signalling seems to be a common mechanism in addition to MYC overexpression {22897848; 28562582}. In patients with endemic BL, and in variable numbers of patients with immunodeficiency-associated or sporadic BL, EBV seems to participate in lymphomagenesis and the presence of the virus in the neoplastic cells is associated with a distinct mutation signature {30617194}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The typical morphological appearance of BL is a diffuse monomorphic population of medium-sized cells with many mitotic figures and a starry-sky pattern due to the presence of tingible-body macrophages, and with very few other intermingled reactive cells. Cytologically, the tumour cells have basophilic cytoplasm, squared-off borders (sometimes), and round nuclei with multiple paracentrically located medium-sized nucleoli (resembling small centroblasts). Smears or imprints stained with MGG show a deeply basophilic cytoplasm, typically with lipid vacuoles that are not seen well on histological sections.

In a subset of cases, the distinction of BL from diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) can be difficult because BLs have a broader morphological spectrum than previously anticipated {16760442; 22885699} and may resemble DLBCL with the presence of more irregular nuclei and larger nucleoli. A subgroup of BL (mostly the immunodeficiency-associated subtype) shows plasmacytoid differentiation {7741100}.

Areas of extensive necrosis or a florid granulomatous reaction may be detected {15104301}. However, despite the high cell proliferation, necrosis is not a dominant feature of BL. The starry-sky pattern is helpful, particularly if tissue is not well preserved.

Immunophenotype

The immunophenotype of BL is intratumourally and intertumourally homogeneous and resembles mature B-cell lymphoma (TdT−, CD19+, CD20+, CD22+, CD79a+, PAX5+) with germinal-centre markers (CD10+, BCL6+, HGAL+). LMO2 is negative {31484540}. Of note, CD10 is virtually never negative in BL. BCL2 is negative or weakly expressed {26218299}. A very important diagnostic feature is the proliferation index determined by Ki-67, which is usually close to 100%. There is strong expression of MYC protein in most cells; rare cases of BL, more frequently endemic BL, can be completely negative for MYC immunohistochemistry despite a documented MYC rearrangement {31748534}. BLs are invariably strongly positive for CD38 and negative for CD44 {18162778}, and they may express SOX11 {19880779; 27869516}. Aberrant expression of CD43, LEF1, and TCL1 is seen {15258997; 10191768; 21718280; 14607063; 18162778; 25203428}.

Flow cytometry

Characteristically, BL tumour cells are CD45low. They express CD19, CD20, CD10, CD38, CD43, CD81, FMC7, and monotypic surface kappa or lambda light chain; CD38 and CD81 expression is particularly bright. The tumour cells do not express BCL2, CD44, or TdT {28509416; 32497402; 24397618}.

Differential diagnosis

Given the high incidence of BL in young patients, it is advisable to classify mature B-cell lymphoma with IG::MYC translocation as BL in young children, even when minor variability of the morphology and immunophenotype are detected, but not in cases with unambiguous features of DLBCL or high-grade B-cell lymphoma {26980727}. Leukaemias with IG::MYC rearrangement and a B-cell precursor immunophenotype (TdT+) biologically represent B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma and not BL {30567752; 31611629}.

Despite morphological, immunophenotypic, and clinical similarities, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration (formerly called Burkitt-like lymphoma with 11q aberrations) is considered a disease entity molecularly distinct from BL (see below).

Cytology

Prominent vacuolization (L3 morphology) is a characteristic but not specific feature. Classification of lymphoma by FNAB alone is not recommended. However, a combination of cytology with immunophenotyping (e.g. by flow cytometry) and detection of a MYC translocation is commonly used to establish the diagnosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The primary molecular event in BL is the rearrangement of MYC, either to the IGH locus (t(8;14)(q24;q32); 80% of cases), or less commonly to the IGL or IGK locus (t(8;22)(q24;q11) and t(2;8)(p12;q24), respectively). The MYC breakpoint is near or within MYC in most cases of sporadic and immunodeficiency-associated BL, but it maps hundreds of kilobases upstream from the MYC basal promoter in endemic BL {3458257; 22711821}. MYC translocations with non-IGH genes are uncommon in BL and are associated with high-grade B-cell lymphomas and DLBCL {16760442}. However, both entities are rare in the childhood, adolescent, and young adult (CAYA) age group {28643426}.

Rarely, BLs lack identifiable MYC rearrangements, either because current genetic techniques fail to detect them {20823134} or because alternative mechanisms modulate MYC expression {18802929; 30617194; 31073073}. Any case of lymphoma with histological features compatible with BL but no MYC rearrangement should be checked for aberrations of 11q, because a reasonable number of lymphomas with these pathological features in CAYA patients belong to the entity of high-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration (see below) {32239695}.

A molecular signature for BL has been identified by gene expression profiling studies {16760442}. Slightly different signatures occur in endemic and sporadic BL {21245480}, as well as in adult sporadic BL {28801451}, but gene expression profiling is not established for diagnostic use.

Next-generation sequencing has documented a high frequency of mutations in TCF3 and in its negative regulator ID3 (activating PI3K pathways, promoting cell survival), and in cell-cycle related genes such as CCND3 (activating cell proliferation) {22885699; 23143597; 23143595; 26468873}. The mutation spectrum may guide differential diagnosis in challenging cases {18509088; 28801451}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphology compatible with BL; mature B-cell phenotype (TdT-negative) with expression of CD10, low or absent BCL2 expression, and a Ki-67 index close to 100%; high expression of MYC (> 80%) and/or demonstration of IG::MYC translocation.

Desirable: given the rarity of double-hit lymphomas in CAYA patients, exclusion of BCL2 and BCL6 translocations can be restricted to cases with non-typical clinical or pathological features.

Staging

Staging is performed according to the revised International Pediatric Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma Staging System (IPNHLSS) {25940716}.

Prognosis and prediction

Adolescent age, advanced stage, blasts in the cerebrospinal fluid, Burkitt leukaemia with CNS involvement, and poor response to prephase therapy are parameters associated with inferior outcome {17761975; 21030984; 22215753}.
