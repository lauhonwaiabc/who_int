2.3.4.2 Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma

Definition

Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL) is a germinal-centre–derived B-cell neoplasm composed of scattered large neoplastic B cells with multilobed nuclei (known as lymphocyte-predominant [LP] cells) within nodules dominated by mantle zone B cells and follicular dendritic cells. Other histological growth patterns also occur, in which small B cells are few and/or nodules are infrequent.

ICD-O coding

9659/3 Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2B30.0 Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: Hodgkin paragranuloma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

NLPHL involves mainly the cervical, supraclavicular, and submandibular lymph nodes; less frequently it involves the axillary and inguinal lymph nodes or other sites; and rarely it involves the spleen {27185849; 12885814; 28802087}.

Clinical features

Most patients present with limited disease (stage I or II) and an indolent course {12885814; 18091054; 26115355; 27185849}. B symptoms, bulky disease, or a mediastinal mass are unusual and should incite exclusion of classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL), B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or composite lymphoma.

Epidemiology

NLPHL accounts for 5–10% of Hodgkin lymphomas in children and adolescents. In paediatric NLPHL, the male predominance (> 80%) is even higher than in adult patients {18091054; 26115355; 27185849; 12885814; 28802087}. There is a wide age range (1–90 years) {26732883}. In adults, the incidence of NLPHL is higher in Black Americans than in White Americans, but no data are available for children {26149294}. Several reports of families as well as a Finnish population-based study show a familial component, with a 19-fold increased risk for NLPHL in first-degree relatives, and a lower risk for CHL and B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Only a minority of familial NLPHL cases occur before the age of 20 years {21517803; 23284040}. NLPHL also occurs in children with primary immunodeficiency, including autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome, Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome, and a STAT1 gain-of-function mutation {11418480; 24302998; 31114772}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

NLPHL is a clonal proliferation of germinal-centre–derived B cells that still reside in a follicular environment. They show signs of ongoing aberrant somatic hypermutation, resulting in several co-occurring mutations, often at hotspot sites of genes such as SOCS1, JUNB, DUSP2, SGK1, CREBBP, PIM1, PAX5, RHOH, and MYC, and rearrangements of BCL6 in a high number of cases {16614247; 17652621; 26658840; 12393409}. Adult NLPHL with transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) shows frequent mutations affecting the PI3K pathway (e.g. SGK1, ZFP36L1), the NF-κB pathway (e.g. CARD11, TNFAIP3, NFKBIA), and linker histones {26658840; 32054999}. Additional frequent events in transformation are loss of the tumour suppressor gene CDKN2A and gains of oncogenes (e.g. REL, BCL11A, BCL6, CARD11, JAK2) or ATM and of the CD274 (PDL1) and PDCD1L2 (PDL2) region {32054999}. All these genetic changes are not unique to NLPHL but show overlap with both DLBCL and CHL. EBV does not play a significant role, because only a few EBV+ NLPHL cases have been reported {24525501}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lymph nodes are enlarged and encapsulated and can show a vaguely nodular appearance.

Histopathology

The lymph node architecture in the typical NLPHL morphology is partly or totally replaced by large, vague nodules that lack germinal centres. These nodules consist of an expanded CD21-positive follicular dendritic meshwork with a varying admixture of small reactive mantle lymphocytes, small T lymphocytes, epithelioid histiocytes, and solitary large blastic tumour cells that also spread outside the follicles. The tumour cells (also called popcorn cells or LP cells) are aberrant centroblasts with a highly variable morphology, resembling large centroblasts, multilobed blasts, or even HRS cells. They strongly express the B-cell markers CD20, CD79a, and PAX5. They also express BCL6, OCT2, BOB1, IRF4 (MUM1), and often EMA, and they lack CD10. CD15 is usually negative, although a few cases with CD15 expression have been reported {19465900; 21457163}. IgD expression of the tumour cells in about one third of cases identifies a disease subset characterized by a striking male predominance, frequent involvement of cervical lymph nodes, and a predominantly extrafollicular distribution of the neoplastic cells {16699312; 28802087; 26115355}. Positivity for CD30, usually weak and heterogeneous, is reported in as many as 28% of NLPHLs, and more frequently in the presence of EBV, which is rare {24525501; 28802087; 12653955}.

The background is composed of a variable number of CD20+ reactive B cells and CD3+ T lymphocytes; the T-cell component is typically rich in CD4+, CD57+, and PD1+ T follicular helper (TFH) cells forming rosettes around the neoplastic cells {18594468}, and a double-positive CD4+/CD8+ T-cell population is frequently identified by flow cytometry {17050078}. Occasionally NLPHL contains a background of T cells with cytological atypia and an elevated proliferation index, which usually does not reflect (concurrent) peripheral T-cell lymphoma {21997687}.

Most cases of NLPHL are characterized by scattered neoplastic cells ringed by T lymphocytes within a nodular reactive background dominated by small non-neoplastic B cells. This histology is called pattern A (B cell–rich nodular) {14508396}. Other immunoarchitectural patterns have been described: pattern B (serpiginous/interconnected nodular), pattern C (nodular with prominent extranodular LP cells), pattern D (T cell–rich nodular), pattern E (diffuse with a T cell–rich background, T cell–rich B-cell lymphoma–like), and pattern F (diffuse, moth-eaten, B cell–rich) {14508396}.

Other patterns, especially those rich in T lymphocytes, have a higher stage at diagnosis and a poorer treatment response {26115355; 28802087}, and they can be particularly difficult to differentiate from T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma. In NLPHL, however, at least a partial nodular pattern may be identified morphologically or with the help of markers of follicular dendritic cells if a sample of sufficient size is investigated. In addition, there are small B cells, usually a large number of PD1+/CD57+ T cells, and lower numbers of CD8+ and TIA1+ T cells present in NLPHL {31785822}.

NLPHL pattern A can be difficult to differentiate from progressive transformation of germinal centres if few neoplastic cells are present. These progressively transformed germinal centres are large lymphoid follicles that are expanded by an accumulation of mantle zone cells, and their occurrence before, at, or after the initial diagnosis of NLPHL is well known {26410017}. In these cases there are no complete PD1+ T-cell rosettes and IgG4 immunostaining can sometimes be a helpful diagnostic tool, because a high number of IgG4+ plasma cells are detected in about 40% of progressively transformed germinal centres but not in NLPHL {30259184}.

Morphology is key to the diagnosis of NLPHL. The wide variation in patterns can make the diagnosis challenging, but the immunophenotype of the tumour cells and the composition of the background can be helpful to discriminate NLPHL from reactive lesions and CHL, especially in small biopsies. T cell–rich B-cell lymphoma can be impossible to discriminate in small biopsies and a lymph node excision is preferred.

Cytology

Admixtures of small lymphocytes and epithelioid histiocytes make up the aspirate, with only a few very large atypical tumour cells. Granulocytes are not common. Histological confirmation and exclusion of differential diagnoses is required.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Because of the paucity of tumour cells, clonal rearrangements can be difficult to detect, especially in cases with residual reactive follicular B cells in the background. There are no NLPHL-specific gene mutations, and BCL6 rearrangements are also seen in follicular lymphoma and DLBCL.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: partial or total replacement of lymph node architecture by large, vague nodules that lack germinal centres; presence of popcorn cells (LP cells) with a highly variable morphology, resembling large centroblasts, multilobated blasts, or even HRS cells; LP cell immunophenotype: uniform positivity for several B-cell antigens (usually CD20+); immune microenvironment: many small lymphocytes, histiocytes, and follicular dendritic cells, with no eosinophils.

Desirable: lymph node excision is preferred, because architectural changes are difficult to assess in small biopsies; expression of the B-cell markers CD20, CD79a, and PAX5, usually without expression of CD15; characteristic immunophenotype of LP cells (CD20, OCT2) and background TFH-cell rosetting (PD1).

Staging

NLPHL is staged according to the Ann Arbor system. Early-stage disease (IA and IIA) makes up 90% of cases {26115355; 27185849}. A bone marrow biopsy is only recommended in patients with advanced-stage NLPHL, unless PET-CT shows a normal bone marrow appearance {26538004}.

Prognosis and prediction

Disease-related long-term survival is excellent (> 95%) {18091054} and treatment is therefore focused on limiting late toxicity. Treatment options depend on the stage of the disease and include active surveillance, excision, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, rituximab (monotherapy or combination therapy), and combined-modality therapy {26115355; 27185849; 26538004}.

The morphological patterns of NLPHL, especially the T cell–rich B-cell lymphoma–like pattern (with related IgD expression), tend to have a higher stage and worse treatment response, with more relapses, than typical NLPHL, but nevertheless show the same survival rates {28802087; 26115355}. NLPHL can recur after as long as 15 years and then manifest predominantly as localized disease, often at the same site or region, with a good response to salvage chemotherapy. Only sporadic transformation to DLBCL has been reported in children {26996288; 27185849; 12885814; 18091054}.
