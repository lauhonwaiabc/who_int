2.3.3.8 Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma

Definition

Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma (SPTCL) is a lymphoma derived from cytotoxic αβ T cells, involving subcutaneous tissue and typically mimicking panniculitis. Other cytotoxic lymphomas frequently involving the subcutaneous tissue, like γδ T-cell lymphomas and NK/T-cell lymphomas, are excluded.

ICD-O coding

9708/3 Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2B00 Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Patients typically present with multiple subcutaneous nodules or deep plaques, most frequently on the extremities or trunk. The mesentery or bone marrow may rarely be involved {24201122; 24128129; 25596253; 32924664}, but other sites are generally spared.

Clinical features

Symptoms are primarily related to the subcutaneous lesions, which range in diameter from 5 mm to several centimetres. Larger nodules may become necrotic, but ulceration is rare. Lesions may resolve, leaving an area of lipoatrophy. Systemic symptoms are seen in approximately 60% of patients. Cytopenias and abnormal liver function test results are common. Lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly may be present but are generally not due to lymphomatous involvement. Haemophagocytic syndrome is seen in 15–35% of cases {1985499; 17934071; 21909203; 23382035; 25456748; 25727090; 27722764; 30126736; 30350476; 32005988}.

Epidemiology

SPTCL is rare at all ages. Patients span a broad age range (median: ~35 years) but approximately 20% are aged < 20 years {17934071; 27722764}, with numerous cases reported in children (including infants) {23382035; 25456748; 25727090; 31808238}. Overall, SPTCL accounts for approximately 0.1–0.25% of non-Hodgkin lymphomas in children {27270301}. There is a slight female predominance.

Etiology

As many as 20–40% of patients with SPTCL (more frequently adults than children) have a prior history of autoimmune disease, most commonly systemic lupus erythematosus, and a large number have autoantibodies including antinuclear antibodies {17934071; 23382035; 27722764; 30126736; 32005988}. In some patients, an inflammatory panniculitis (particularly lupus erythematosus panniculitis [LEP]) may precede SPTCL, and some SPTCLs have morphological features overlapping with those of LEP, but the relationship between these lesions remains to be clarified {19590424; 25118815; 27722764; 30126736; 32005988}. Individuals harbouring biallelic germline loss-of-function HAVCR2 (TIM3) variants are highly susceptible to SPTCL, often with associated haemophagocytic syndrome. This association may be more prevalent in patients of Asian heritage {30374066; 30792187; 32005988; 33003142}.

Pathogenesis

SPTCL is derived from mature cytotoxic αβ T cells. Recurrent somatic variants in several genes associated with epigenetic regulation and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and JAK/STAT signal transduction pathways have been reported {28294301; 30792187}. EBV is absent.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features (above).

Histopathology

SPTCL shows a dense panniculitis-like infiltrate involving the subcutaneous fat lobules, usually sparing the septa. Minimal periadnexal extension into the reticular dermis is occasionally present, but superficial dermal and epidermal involvement is absent. The lymphoma cells are usually small or medium-sized with irregular and hyperchromatic nuclei and often with a rim of pale-staining cytoplasm. They infiltrate between adipocytes diffusely and in small aggregates, with a characteristic rimming of individual adipocytes, and they are often admixed with numerous histiocytes. Other inflammatory cells, including plasma cells, are generally scarce, although plasma cells are more prominent in some cases. Vascular invasion is occasionally seen. Necrosis and karyorrhexis are common. Erythrophagocytic histiocytes may be seen in cases with an associated haemophagocytic syndrome {9563791; 9669350; 17934071; 23382035; 25456748; 27722764}.

Immunophenotype

The lymphoma cells have a mature αβ T-cell phenotype (CD3+ and usually CD8+), with expression of cytotoxic molecules including granzyme B, perforin, and TIA1. The cells express TCRβ (if negative for TCRβ, they should also be negative for TCRγ and/or TCRδ) and are negative for CD56 and CD30. Variable loss of pan–T-cell antigens, particularly CD5, may be seen. EBV is absent {9563791; 9669350; 17934071; 23382035; 25456748; 27722764}.

Differential diagnosis

SPTCL and LEP demonstrate clinical and histopathological overlap. The presence of atypical CD8+ T cells with frequent Ki-67 positivity in clusters and/or rimming individual adipocytes is highly specific for the diagnosis of SPTCL. By contrast, the presence of a predominance of CD4+ T cells, B-cell follicles, clusters of CD123+ dendritic cells, numerous plasma cells, and hyaline fat necrosis favours LEP {15953372; 26796503; 29742552; 30597607}. Primary cutaneous γδ T-cell lymphomas may show panniculitis-like features. In these cases, dermal and epidermal involvement, ulceration, expression of TCRγδ, and positivity for CD56 exclude the diagnosis of SPTCL [[Swerdlow SH, Campo E, Harris NL, et al., editors. Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The neoplastic cells show clonal rearrangement of TR genes.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: lobular subcutaneous infiltrate composed of CD8+ αβ T lymphocytes with atypia; elevated Ki-67 cell proliferation index and cytotoxic protein expression; EBV negativity.

Desirable: lymphocytes rimming individual adipocytes; CD56 negativity (almost always); demonstration of clonal TR gene rearrangement.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year overall survival rate across all patients with SPTCL, including children, is approximately 85% {17934071; 21909203; 25456748; 27722764; 30126736}. However, the presence of haemophagocytic syndrome portends an adverse prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 45% in one European series {17934071}. HAVCR2 mutations have been associated with severe haemophagocytic syndrome {32005988}. In general, initial therapy with corticosteroids and/or other immunomodulatory agents is currently recommended for patients without haemophagocytic syndrome. Some patients with haemophagocytic syndrome may also benefit from immunosuppressive therapy, but those with more severe disease or an inadequate response to immunosuppression require multiagent chemotherapy {29878045; 27722764}. Both immunosuppression and chemotherapy are therapeutic options in children, and occasional cases in children have shown durable spontaneous remissions {24684413; 26146844; 32452165}.
