5.2.2.1 Fasciitis/myositis

Definition

Nodular fasciitis is a self-limiting myofibroblastic neoplasm typically occurring in subcutaneous tissue. Proliferative myositis is an intramuscular proliferation of plump myofibroblasts/fibroblasts and large ganglion-like cells. Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous tumour of digits are self-limiting neoplasms composed of fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and osteoblasts, related to soft tissue aneurysmal bone cyst.

Related terminology

Not recommended: for nodular fasciitis: pseudosarcomatous fasciitis; for myositis ossificans: myositis ossificans circumscripta; for fibro-osseous tumour of digits: florid reactive periostitis.

Subtype(s)

Nodular fasciitis

Cranial fasciitis; intravascular fasciitis

Localization

Nodular fasciitis has a wide anatomical distribution; common sites include the upper extremities and the head and neck. Most cases involve subcutaneous tissue, whereas a smaller subset are intramuscular. Cranial fasciitis typically involves the skull outer table and contiguous soft tissues {7351023}.

Proliferative myositis in children has a predilection for muscles of the shoulder and the upper arm, and (less often) the thigh and the head and neck {6073898}.

Myositis ossificans can develop throughout the body; the most common sites are the elbow, back, and gluteal region {26320160; 28275867}. Commonly involving skeletal muscle, it may also arise in the subcutis and tendons.

Fibro-osseous tumour of digits occurs most commonly in the subcutis of proximal fingers, followed by the toes {18164411; 19678826}.

Clinical features

Both nodular fasciitis and proliferative myositis may simulate sarcoma owing to their rapid growth. Soreness may be present. Nodular fasciitis usually measures ≤ 20 mm and is always < 50 mm in maximum dimension, whereas cranial fasciitis may be larger. Myositis ossificans manifests as a rapidly growing, painful mass in its early stage, with a plateau in growth after 2 weeks, when flocculent mineralization appears on imaging. In classic myositis ossificans, radiodensity is greatest peripherally, and in fibro-osseous tumour of digits it is located randomly {1566115; 18164411; 19678826; 26320160; 28275867}.

Epidemiology

Nodular fasciitis is relatively common. Approximately one quarter of cases occur in the paediatric age, mostly during the second decade of life, although infants and young children may be affected. Cranial fasciitis is rare and develops predominantly in infants aged < 2 years. Nodular fasciitis has no sex predilection, but cranial fasciitis is more common in boys {6279273; 6462780; 7351023}. Proliferative myositis is very rare in children and infants {6073898; 21912882}. Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous tumour of digits occur in all age groups.

Etiology

The etiology of nodular fasciitis and myositis ossificans is unknown. A history of trauma is sometimes reported for patients with proliferative myositis {6073898; 14408614}.

Pathogenesis

Recurrent USP6 gene rearrangements have been identified in both nodular fasciitis (in 85–90% of cases) and cranial fasciitis. Multiple partners have been described, most frequently MYH9 (22q12.3). USP6 rearrangements are also found in aneurysmal bone cyst, myositis ossificans, cellular fibroma of tendon sheath, and fibro-osseous tumour of digits, which share some histological features with nodular fasciitis, suggesting a biological relationship among these tumour types {21826056; 28752842; 27113271; 31827231; 30758758; 23769422; 15026324; 29787930; 18265974; 27125357}. PPP6R3::USP6 fusion has been reported in 2 cases of nodular fasciitis with malignant features {27113271}.

The pathogenesis of proliferative myositis is unknown.

Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous tumour of digits are associated with a COL1A1::USP6 gene fusion {30946936}.

Macroscopic appearance

Nodular fasciitis is a well-circumscribed or infiltrative mass with a myxoid to fibrous cut surface. Proliferative myositis usually appears as a poorly marginated, pale-grey to white intramuscular mass {6073898}. Myositis ossificans is a firm, circumscribed, tan-grey, gritty, haemorrhagic mass.

Histopathology

Nodular fasciitis is composed of loose intersecting fascicles of plump, bland myofibroblasts with fine chromatin and small nucleoli. Lesions may be highly cellular with a high mitotic count. Abundant myxoid stroma, a feathery tissue culture–like appearance, microcysts, osteoclast-like giant cells, and extravasated erythrocytes are common features. Later lesions contain more collagen and may show keloidal hyalinization. Cranial fasciitis has similar histological features. By immunohistochemistry, nodular fasciitis and cranial fasciitis are usually positive for SMA and MSA; desmin positivity is occasionally found {1928550}.

The differential diagnosis for nodular fasciitis includes low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma.

Proliferative myositis grows between individual muscle fibres, creating the characteristic checkerboard pattern. It is composed of plump myofibroblastic/fibroblastic spindle cells with large ganglion-like cells with rounded nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and abundant amphophilic to basophilic cytoplasm. Mitotic figures are present but never atypical. The stroma varies from myxoid to collagenous {6073898}. Myofibroblasts are positive for SMA and MSA, and negative for desmin. The ganglion-like cells are often negative for actins {31933833}. Cytological atypia may be more prominent in children than in adults.

Myositis ossificans and fibro-osseous tumour of digits are moderately cellular and composed of fascicles of spindle cells with elongate nuclei, tapered ends, fine chromatin, and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Mitoses are present but not abnormal, and the stroma is variably myxocollagenous {1566115; 18164411; 19678826; 26320160; 28275867}. Myositis ossificans has a zonal architecture with central immature woven bone that is peripherally better formed and rimmed by osteoblasts. Fibro-osseous tumour of digits does not have a zonal architecture. The differential diagnosis of myositis ossificans includes osteosarcoma of soft tissue, which is extremely rare in children, lacks zonation, and generally shows atypical mitoses.

Cytology

Nodular fasciitis is characterized by bland spindle cells with unipolar curved to bipolar processes and round to oval elongated nuclei; occasionally, it contains tissue culture–like myxoid stroma {30311731}. Proliferative myositis typically features a mixture of spindle cells with long cytoplasmic processes and scattered large ganglion-like cells. Admixed skeletal muscle can be appreciated {19926576}. Spindle cells with fine chromatin and polyhedral cells with eccentric nuclei and prominent nucleoli enmeshed in myxomatous stroma characterize myositis ossificans {20890998}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Recurrent USP6 gene rearrangements have been identified in nodular fasciitis, cranial fasciitis, and myositis ossificans.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for nodular fasciitis: a bland myofibroblastic proliferation with a tissue culture–like growth pattern, extravasated red cells, and myxoid stroma; for proliferative myositis: a myofibroblastic/fibroblastic proliferation with ganglion-like cells, variable collagenous/myxoid stroma, and an intramuscular checkerboard growth pattern; for myositis ossificans: fascicles of benign-appearing fibroblasts and myofibroblasts in myxocollagenous stroma, with woven bone rimmed by osteoblasts in a zonal pattern.

Desirable: for nodular fasciitis and myositis ossificans: demonstration of a USP6 gene rearrangement.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

All these lesions are benign; they rarely recur after excision in childhood, and they never metastasize.
