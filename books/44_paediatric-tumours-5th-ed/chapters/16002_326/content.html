16.0.0.2 Neuroblastoma

Definition

Neuroblastoma is a peripheral neuroblastic tumour (pNT) of neural crest origin containing neoplastic cells of various grades of neuroblastic differentiation proliferating in tumour tissue with < 50% Schwannian stromal content.

ICD-O coding

9500/3 Neuroblastoma, NOS

9500/3 Undifferentiated neuroblastoma

9500/3 Poorly differentiated neuroblastoma

9500/3 Differentiating neuroblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2D11.2 Neuroblastoma of adrenal gland

2A02.11 Paraspinal neuroblastoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Undifferentiated neuroblastoma; poorly differentiated neuroblastoma; differentiating neuroblastoma

Localization

The primary sites follow the migration of neural crest cells during their development and include the adrenal glands (40%) and the abdominal (25%), thoracic (15%), pelvic (5%), and cervical (~3–5%) sympathetic ganglia. Rare tumours are found in the paratesticular region, and 1% of patients are recorded to have no detectable primary tumour {666647; 8591082; 15235064; 15278374; 19415016; 25154816}.

Clinical features

Clinical symptoms are closely associated with the genomic/molecular properties of individual tumours. The primary tumour may cause abdominal distension and/or constipation due to an abdominal mass, and respiratory distress due to a mediastinal mass {27823645}. Neurological symptoms are seen with a paraspinal tumour extending through the spinal canal into the extradural space. Metastases can cause lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, bone pain, and so-called raccoon eyes (exophthalmia associated with bruising and swelling around the eyes). Metastatic dissemination to the lung and brain is rare. Neuroblastomas can show spontaneous regression, tumour maturation, or aggressive progression refractory to therapy.

Several paraneoplastic syndromes are reported, including (1) WDHA syndrome (watery diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, and achlorhydria, also called Verner–Morrison syndrome), with intractable diarrhoea caused by tumour secretion of VIP {15051093}; (2) Horner syndrome (unilateral ptosis, enophthalmos, miosis, and anhidrosis associated with a thoracic or cervical tumour) {17011859}; (3) opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome (dancing eyes and acute cerebellar ataxia, possibly through an autoimmune mechanism) {19430769}; and (4) hypertension (due to catecholamine secretion or renal artery compression by an adrenal tumour) {15960647; 14694383}. Rare neuroblastoma cases with constitutional mutations of the PHOX2B gene have also been documented, and these patients present with congenital central hypoventilation and Hirschsprung disease {26375764}.

Epidemiology

pNTs are the third most common childhood neoplasms, after leukaemias and brain tumours. They are the most common neoplasms during the first year of life, and the most common extracranial solid tumours in the first 2 years of life. In the USA, the incidence rate of pNTs is 14 cases per 100 000 live births. The disease is slightly more common in boys than girls (M:F ratio: 1.2:1) [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2020. SEER Cancer Statistics Review (CSR) 1975-2017. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/archive/csr/1975_2017/.]]. Similar incidence rates and M:F ratios have been observed in Europe {27823645}, China {32020595; 23927803}, and Japan {29378002; 29178401}.

Familial neuroblastoma occurs as an autosomal dominant disorder and seems to constitute approximately 1–2% of all pNTs, a substantial fraction of which are caused by germline ALK mutations {28458126; 18724359}. The penetrance of the disease is incomplete and has been estimated at about 50% {28674118}. In addition, about 6–10% of familial neuroblastoma cases have a mutation in PHOX2B {15338462; 17637745}.

Etiology

Neuroblastoma is caused by the transformation of neural crest cells due to genetic or epigenetic events, but the etiology remains poorly understood. The early age of onset of the disease, as well as the concept of in situ neuroblastoma {16010489}, suggests that the tumour develops during fetal life. To date, however, there are no definite preconceptional or gestational factors accepted as consistently increasing the incidence of neuroblastoma. Molecular/genetic events leading to MYC-family oncogene overexpression, ALK gene overexpression, and abnormal telomere maintenance/elongation are considered important drivers of neuroblastoma tumorigenesis.

Pathogenesis

MYC-family oncogene (MYCN and MYC) overexpression

The MYCN oncogene is located on chromosome 2p24.3 and is considered a major oncogenic driver of neuroblastoma. MYCN amplification is seen in approximately 20–25% of neuroblastoma cases. About 90% of MYCN-amplified tumours overexpress MYCN protein {29464082}. There are also neuroblastomas overexpressing MYC (c-myc). Unlike in MYCN-overexpressing neuroblastoma, it is extremely rare to see gene amplification in MYC-overexpressing neuroblastoma {29426276}. In this regard, other molecular mechanisms for high MYC protein expression independent of genomic amplification, such as oncogene activation through enhancer hijacking by translocations, focal enhancer amplification near to the MYC (MYCC)-coding region (8q24), or MK2-mediated OCT4 transcriptional activation of the MYC gene, have been reported {29284669; 32409685}. Tumours overexpressing a MYC-family protein (MYCN or MYC) are collectively named MYC-driven neuroblastomas {26035700}.

Abnormal maintenance/elongation of telomeres

Two mechanisms associated with telomere abnormalities providing the tumour cells with infinite proliferation capability have been reported in neuroblastoma: TERT overexpression and the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres (ALT) phenotype. TERT is the protein component of telomerase, and higher levels of expression are observed in both MYC-driven and non–MYC-driven neuroblastomas. In the former, TERT is the direct transcriptional target of MYC-family proteins {25893605}. In the latter, it is probably a result of long-range genomic rearrangements of TERT. TERT promoter hypermethylation may also be involved in its activating mechanism {26171145; 24337442; 28284778; 26523776}, whereas most of the tumours with ATRX loss due to ATRX gene mutation show the ALT phenotype {27760112}. The ALT phenotype has also been reported in rare neuroblastomas with loss of DAXX {25487495}.

ALK gene overexpression

Constitutive activating germline mutations in the ALK oncogene, located on chromosome 2p23, seem to account for most cases of hereditary neuroblastoma {18724359; 29374774}. ALK abnormalities are also found in approximately 10% of sporadic neuroblastomas: genomic rearrangements or amplification of ALK are observed in sporadic neuroblastoma only and have not been found in familial neuroblastoma thus far. ALK is a receptor tyrosine kinase whose expression is physiologically restricted mainly to the developing central and peripheral nervous system {29374774}. ALK mutations at hotspots such as p.R1275, p.F1174, and p.F1245 lead to the activation of multiple signalling pathways, including the RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways {29374774}. ALK mutations are single-nucleotide variants in the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD)-encoding region of the gene {28674118}. The ALK variant p.R1275Q occurs most frequently in familial neuroblastoma and is also one of the most prevalent mutations in sporadic neuroblastic tumours {28674118; 25517749}. By contrast, ALK substitutions at p.F1174 and p.F1245, which are frequent mutations in sporadic neuroblastoma, have rarely been detected in familial cases and only in the context of severe neurocognitive defects and brain stem abnormalities {21972109}. ALK protein overexpression as a result of ALK mutation/rearrangement/amplification seems to be responsible for its oncogenic activity {26503946}. However, in mouse and zebrafish models, activated ALK has been shown to efficiently induce neuroblastoma development in combination with MYCN overexpression {22764207; 22789543; 22439933; 24811913}. By contrast, neuroblastoma rarely or never developed in these models when activated ALK was expressed in the absence of a MYCN transgene. It is noted that the development of ALK-driven malignancies other than neuroblastoma has not been reported in children with germline ALK mutations.

Macroscopic appearance

Neuroblastoma forms a soft grey-tan mass, 10 to > 100 mm in diameter, and is often haemorrhagic, with or without foci of necrosis and calcification. Tumours can be encapsulated or show invasive growth into the surrounding tissue.

Histopathology

Among all the pNTs in the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) Neuroblastoma Pathology Reference Laboratory, about 80% are in the neuroblastoma category and the rest are intermixed ganglioneuroblastoma (9%), nodular ganglioneuroblastoma (8%), and ganglioneuroma (3%). Histologically, neuroblastomas are composed of neuroblastic cells forming groups or nests demarcated by thin fibrovascular septa, with no or limited Schwannian proliferation {10421272}. Three subtypes are recognized, based on the grade of neuroblastic differentiation: undifferentiated, poorly differentiated, and differentiating.

The undifferentiated subtype (~3% of neuroblastomas) is rare and requires supplementary studies, such as immunohistochemistry and/or molecular tests, to establish the diagnosis, because the proliferating cells have a uniformly primitive small blue round cell appearance without clearly recognizable neurite formation.

The poorly differentiated subtype (~89% of neuroblastomas) is defined as a tumour with a background of readily detectable neurites with or without Homer Wright rosette formation. Fewer than 5% of neuroblasts have cytomorphological features of differentiation towards mature neurons. This is the most common histological subtype. The conventional nuclear morphology of the neuroblasts in this subtype is often described as a salt-and-pepper appearance. Infrequently, undifferentiated or poorly differentiated tumours show unusual histological features (large, pleomorphic, fusiform, spindle-shaped, and pseudorhabdoid cells) focally or diffusely.

The differentiating subtype (~8% of neuroblastomas) is a tumour with (usually) abundant neurites and in which > 5% of the tumour cells have an appearance of differentiating neuroblasts, characterized by synchronous differentiation of the nucleus (enlarged and eccentrically located, with a vesicular chromatin pattern and usually a single prominent nucleolus) and of the cytoplasm (eosinophilic/amphophilic with a diameter at least twice that of the nucleus).

MYCN-amplified neuroblastomas show the characteristic histology of the undifferentiated or poorly differentiated subtype with a high mitosis-karyorrhexis index, and they are classified into the unfavourable histology group according to the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) (see below) {7674334}. MYC-driven neuroblastomas overexpressing a MYC-family protein (either MYCN or MYC) often demonstrate prominent nucleolar formation (with nucleolar hypertrophy) that is considered to be a putative site of MYCN/MYC RNA synthesis and accumulation {29464082; 22744966; 23901000; 26035700; 15549714}. Because of the presence of prominent nucleoli, MYC-driven neuroblastomas are morphologically distinguishable from conventional neuroblastomas with salt-and-pepper nuclei.

The histologically defined, rare, and very aggressive large cell neuroblastoma (LCN) is also included in this MYC-driven neuroblastoma group {14716776}. The tumour cells of LCN are characterized by enlarged and uniquely open nuclei containing one or a few very prominent nucleoli, consistent with higher levels of either MYCN or MYC protein expression. Their euchromatin-rich open nuclei suggest stemness {23479628}. Although the nuclear morphology of LCN cells is often similar to that of differentiating neuroblasts, these two cell types should be strictly distinguished. The tumour cells in LCN have larger nuclei than conventional neuroblasts and their cytoplasm is similarly scant. In contrast, differentiating neuroblasts have abundant cytoplasm with a diameter at least twice that of the nucleus {18158560}.

Immunohistochemistry

An immunohistochemistry panel including neuronal markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin, PGP9.5, CD56, and neurofilament), NB84 {9500774}, and neural crest markers (tyrosine hydroxylase and PHOX2B) {22790854; 25822764} is usually advised. Among these markers, PHOX2B is the most reliable for supporting a diagnosis of neuroblastoma (even the undifferentiated subtype) {28986989}. Generally, a combination of positive staining for these markers along with negative staining for markers of other small round cell tumours is suggested. Staining for S100 can reveal some Schwannian stromal cells and putative Schwann cells (Schwannian blasts) in the fibrovascular septal tissue, especially in biologically favourable tumours in the neuroblastoma category {3886523}.

International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification

The INPC was established in 1999, based on the original Shimada classification with minor modifications {10421273}, and it was revised in 2003 {14601099}. The system classifies tumours in the neuroblastoma category into favourable or unfavourable histology groups based on the age-dependent evaluation of grade of neuroblastic differentiation (i.e. undifferentiated, poorly differentiated, or differentiating) and the mitosis-karyorrhexis index, which is defined as the number of cells undergoing mitosis or karyorrhexis per 5000 cells: low (< 100), intermediate (100–200), or high (> 200) (see #32721Table 4.01). Tumours in the favourable histology group are within a framework of age-appropriate maturation sequence and age-appropriate mitosis-karyorrhexis index class, with two age cut-off points: 18 months and 60 months. For both primary and metastatic tumours, prognostic classification with this system should be performed only before treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy).

Cytology

Because of the heterogeneity of neuroblastic tumours, FNAB or other cytological evaluation is not recommended.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The genomic and molecular characteristics significantly associated with the biological and clinical behaviour of neuroblastomas include tumour DNA content and segmental chromosomal aberrations, MYCN oncogene status and MYC-family protein overexpression, telomere abnormalities, and ALK mutations/amplification (see Prognosis and prediction, below).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a primitive tumour that has a prominent neuroblastic component with little or no Schwannian stroma; assessment of the degree of neuroblastic differentiation and mitosis-karyorrhexis index (for subtype); assessment of nuclear morphology – conventional salt-and-pepper appearance versus prominent nucleolar formation (for prognostication and/or correlation with molecular testing).

Desirable (as available): molecular/genomic testing for MYCN oncogene status, ALK oncogene status, telomere maintenance gene (TERT and ATRX) status, DNA index, and segmental chromosomal aberrations (for prognostication).

Staging

There are two widely used clinical staging systems: the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) {8336186} and the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) Staging System (INRGSS) {19047290}. The INSS is based on the postsurgical evaluation of disease status, whereas the INRGSS depends on the presurgical determination of the presence or absence of image-defined risk factors.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognostic genomic/molecular properties

Besides clinical stage, age at diagnosis, and histopathology (INPC), certain genomic/molecular properties of individual tumours also have significant prognostic implications for patients with neuroblastomas. They are summarized below.

Tumour DNA content and segmental chromosomal aberrations

Flow cytometric analysis for DNA index determination has been used for measuring total cell DNA in neuroblastomas. Tumours with numeric whole-chromosome copy-number gains without structural abnormalities (hyperdiploid; DNA index > 1) are associated with an excellent prognosis. In contrast, diploid tumours (DNA index = 1) are associated with a poor clinical outcome {6738617; 16116152}. The prognostic effect of ploidy seems to be lost in children aged > 2 years, possibly because tumours with DNA index > 1 in older children typically have a number of structural abnormalities.

Various segmental chromosomal aberrations are reported to be indicators of poor clinical outcome in neuroblastoma {8608986; 16306521; 20145112; 10379019}. The presence of chromosomal alterations (1p deletion, 11q deletion, or 17q gain) was reported to add prognostic information to patient age and tumour stage for neuroblastoma without MYCN amplification {22976801}. Loss of 6q was also reported in a subgroup of extremely aggressive neuroblastomas {29514301}.

MYCN oncogene status and MYC-family protein overexpression

MYCN-amplified neuroblastomas are associated with advanced stage and aggressive clinical behaviour {6719137; 4047115}. By FISH, MYCN status is determined as amplified (MYCN signal increased more than 4-fold compared to reference signals of chromosome 2), gain (MYCN signal increased up to 4-fold), or non-amplified {19401703}. In the past few years, heterogeneous MYCN intratumoural amplification has been appreciated in association with a diverse aggressive phenotype {26119945}.

Recent studies support the notion that MYCN protein overexpression rather than gene amplification is critical for determining aggressive tumour behaviour {22744966}. Although the vast majority of MYCN-amplified tumours overexpress MYCN protein, there are also neuroblastomas overexpressing MYC (c-myc) protein. Those MYC-driven neuroblastomas defined by augmented expression of either MYC protein (detected in ~10% of neuroblastomas of the undifferentiated or poorly differentiated subtypes) or MYCN protein (detected in ~20% of neuroblastomas of the same subtypes) are highly aggressive {23901000}.

Telomere abnormalities

Both TERT overexpression and the ALT phenotype are reported to be predictive of reduced patient survival. TERT overexpression is found in patients in any age group and with or without MYC-family protein overexpression. Neuroblastoma with the ALT phenotype due to ATRX mutation is encountered in children aged > 5 years {22416102}. ATRX mutation is rarely observed in MYC-driven neuroblastoma, and it is not associated with TERT overexpression.

ALK mutation/rearrangement/amplification

ALK gene abnormalities causing overexpression of the protein are reported to confer a poor prognosis in neuroblastoma {22585002; 26503946}. However, the prognostic significance of ALK overexpression has been controversial. For example, clinical outcomes of patients within the same family bearing a germline ALK mutation are different and not always poor {28674118}. In addition, a recent report indicated that activating ALK mutations, detected by ultra-deep sequencing analysis, did not influence the survival of patients with neuroblastoma, regardless of MYCN amplification status {30778092}. It is conceivable that, in addition to ALK mutations, further genomic alterations (e.g. TERT rearrangement, ATRX mutation) might be required for oncogenic development and fully malignant transformation of neuroblastoma cells in both familial and sporadic tumours. In this regard, Regairaz et al. reported that expression of ALK and its active form (phosphorylated ALK) was immunohistochemically detected in many neuroblastomas independent of ALK mutation/amplification {26687816}.

The therapeutic effect of ALK inhibitors in ALK-mutated neuroblastoma is currently under investigation in clinical trials {18724359; 24166094}. Notably, autophagy has been observed in neuroblastoma treated with ALK inhibitors, opening a new avenue for neuroblastoma therapy {26735175; 27486382}. However, the fact that ALK-activating mutations have little effect on the survival of patients with neuroblastoma {30778092} casts doubt on the therapeutic efficacy of ALK inhibitors in these patients.

Risk-grouping systems

Because pNTs are biologically and clinically heterogeneous, patients are divided into different risk groups. There are two major risk-grouping systems: the COG risk classification system (see #22104Table 4.02) and the INRG classification system (see #22105Table 4.03). Both systems use a combination of various prognostic factors, such as clinical stage, age at diagnosis, histopathology, and molecular/genomic properties. Historically, the COG risk classification system (which categorizes risk as low, intermediate, or high) has contributed to actual patient stratification and protocol assignment {23255319; 30652588}. In contrast, the primary purpose of the INRG system (which categorizes risk as very low, low, intermediate, or high) has been to facilitate comparison of risk-based clinical trials conducted in different parts of the world {19047291}.

Subgrouping of unfavourable-histology neuroblastomas

Recent advances in neuroblastoma research demonstrate that unfavourable-histology neuroblastomas are heterogeneous. Four subgroups have been proposed: the MYC subgroup (overexpressing MYCN or MYC protein), the TERT subgroup (TERT overexpression due to genomic abnormalities), the ALT subgroup (ALT phenotype due to ATRX loss), and the null subgroup. These subgroups are immunohistochemically identified by their protein markers, which could be potential targets for future precision therapy {31840131}.
