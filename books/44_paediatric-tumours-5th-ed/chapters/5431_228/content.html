5.4.3.1 Vascular tumours of bone

Definition

Haemangioma is a benign endothelial tumour of bone composed of vascular channels of varying calibre.

Epithelioid haemangioma (EH) is a locally aggressive vascular tumour of bone composed of endothelial cells with epithelioid morphology.

Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma (EHE) is a malignant vascular tumour of bone composed of epithelioid endothelial cells that elaborate and grow within a myxohyaline stroma.

Related terminology

Acceptable: for haemangioma: Gorham–Stout syndrome (vanishing bone disease); massive osteolysis; cystic angiomatosis.

Subtype(s)

Haemangioma

Cavernous haemangioma; capillary haemangioma; angiomatosis

Localization

Haemangiomas most frequently occur in vertebral bodies, followed by craniofacial bones and long bones {10741493}. Multifocality is frequent.

EH most frequently occurs in long tubular bones; epiphyseal involvement is the most common localization in paediatric patients {28721475}. Other sites include (in descending frequency) short tubular bones of the distal lower extremities, flat bones, vertebrae, and small bones of the hands {18852673}. Multicentric disease is usually limited to an anatomical region and is not generalized {18852673; 21948309}.

The majority of EHEs arise in long tubular bones; less frequently, they arise in the pelvis, ribs, and spine {8898834}. The skeleton can be the only organ involved or a component of multiorgan disease {8898834; 21546438}.

Clinical features

Haemangiomas in children are usually symptomatic and manifest with pain and pathological fracture; tumours in the axial skeleton may cause neurological symptoms. Gorham–Stout syndrome is a rare, non-hereditary disorder, in which lesions are often composed of lymphatic-like vessels, that tends to involve one bone but readily crosses joints to involve adjacent bones, resulting in progressive bony destruction over the course of months. It may be associated with lesions in the soft tissues and thoracic cavity and as a component of a generalized lymphatic anomaly.

EH and EHE are usually symptomatic and manifest with localized pain.

Haemangiomas of the skull and long bones show a classic starburst pattern on conventional radiographs, whereas vertebral haemangiomas show a corduroy appearance as the normal trabecular pattern is replaced by coarsened vertical trabecula with relative demineralization of the vertebra. On CT, they manifest as well-defined lytic lesions with a polka-dot pattern of high-density round foci corresponding to thickened vertical trabeculae mixed with low-attenuation fat. On MRI, they exhibit high signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted images due to the presence of fat within the tumour {10741493}.

EH and EHE typically manifest as well-defined lytic, septated masses that may erode the cortex and extend into the soft tissue. Signal intensities are not specific on MRI {10741493}.

Epidemiology

The peak incidences of haemangioma, EH, and EHE are in the fifth, third, and second to third decades of life, respectively, although they may occur at any age {10741493; 18852673; 21948309; 8898834}.

Etiology

The etiology of haemangioma is unknown. Both developmental and neoplastic origins have been postulated.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of haemangioma is unknown.

In 59–71% of cases, EH of the bone is characterized by recurrent fusions involving FOS and various fusion partners (MBNL1, VIM, lincRNA, and LMNA) {26173738; 26135557}. Alternatively, FOSB is fused to ZFP36 or (rarely) to WWTR1 {25043949}.

In 90% of cases, EHE harbours the recurrent t(1;3)(p36;q25) translocation, resulting in a WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion {21885404; 21584898; 31537895}, whereas < 5% have a t(X;11)(p11;q22) translocation, resulting in a YAP1::TFE3 fusion {23737213; 31537895}.

Macroscopic appearance

Haemangiomas are soft, well-defined, dark-red masses.

EHs are relatively well-defined solid and haemorrhagic masses that range in size from < 10 to 70 mm.

EHEs are well-defined solid and tan-white masses that lack the haemorrhagic appearance of vascular tumours and range in size from < 10 to 100 mm.

Histopathology

Haemangiomas have a lobular architecture and are composed of thin-walled, blood-filled, capillary-like vessels lined by a single layer of flat, uniform, cytologically bland endothelial cells. Some tumours are cellular, and the endothelial cells are plump and obscure the lumina.

EH is a lobulated lesion composed of epithelioid cells that line well-formed vascular spaces and grow in cords, aggregates, and sheets. The tumour cells are large and polyhedral, with oval or kidney bean–shaped nuclei that may be hyperlobated, cleaved, and grooved. The cytoplasm is abundant, deeply eosinophilic, and often contains vacuoles that may harbour intact or fragments of red blood cells. The tumour cells can protrude into the lumina in a hobnail fashion. Intratumoural haemorrhage associated with a spindle cell component may be present. The stroma may contain a mixed inflammatory infiltrate including eosinophils, osteoclast-type giant cells, and reactive woven bone. Scattered mitoses of normal configuration and small foci of necrosis may be present {25043949}.

EHE is characterized by large epithelioid cells, often with intracytoplasmic lumina that may contain red blood cells (blister cells), arranged in cords and single cells in a myxohyaline or sclerotic stroma. A small subset of EHEs have atypical features, including nuclear pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity, solid sheet-like growth, and necrosis {31537895}. TFE3-rearranged EHEs show well-formed vascular channels lined by large epithelioid endothelial cells with moderate nuclear atypia and abundant pale, glassy cytoplasm {23737213}. Myxohyaline stroma is absent or inconspicuous.

Endothelial markers (CD31, CD34, ERG, FLI1) are expressed. Keratin and EMA are often positive in EH and EHE. A subset of EHs express FOSB {28009608}. In 86–88% of EHEs there is nuclear positivity for CAMTA1, whereas TFE3 positivity identifies EHE with YAP1::TFE3 fusion {23737213; 25879300}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FOS or FOSB rearrangements can be detected in the majority of EHs of bone.

WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion can be detected in 89–100% of EHEs with classic histological features, whereas YAP1::TFE3 fusions are found in < 5% of cases with distinct histology.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for haemangioma: compatible imaging; thin-walled blood vessels lined by a single layer of bland endothelial cells; for EH: large epithelioid endothelial cells with densely eosinophilic cytoplasm and evidence of vasoformation with tombstone pattern; for EHE: cords and nests of epithelioid cells within a myxohyaline stroma lacking vasoformation, with an endothelial immunophenotype; well-formed vascular channels lined by epithelioid endothelial cells and nests of cells with moderate nuclear atypia and abundant pale cytoplasm (in EHE with TFE3 fusion).

Desirable: for EHE: CAMTA1 expression by immunohistochemistry and/or WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion; TFE3 overexpression by immunohistochemistry and/or TFE3 gene rearrangement.

Staging

For haemangioma and EH, staging is not relevant. For EHE, staging follows bone sarcoma protocols.

Prognosis and prediction

Haemangiomas have an excellent prognosis. Patients with Gorham–Stout syndrome can have a guarded outcome when the skull base and spine are involved.

The local recurrence rate of EH in paediatric cases is 43% (vs 10% in adults) {18852673; 30207085}. The treatment usually consists of curettage; less frequently, marginal en bloc excision is performed. Radiation has been used for inaccessible sites.

The mainstay of treatment for EHE is wide resection. Patients with conventional EHE with WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion had a less favourable outcome than those with YAP1::TFE3 EHE (5-year overall survival rates: 59% and 86%, respectively) {31537895}. Cases with multifocality, pleural involvement, and lymph node or distant metastases had a significantly worse outcome {31537895}.
