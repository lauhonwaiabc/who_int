5.4.1.1 Osteoblastoma

Definition

Osteoblastoma is a locally aggressive bone-forming tumour that is generally > 20 mm in diameter and composed of trabeculae of woven bone rimmed by plump osteoblasts in a vascularized stroma.

ICD-O coding

9200/1 Osteoblastoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E83.Z & XH4316 Benign osteogenic tumours, unspecified & Osteoblastoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: epithelioid osteoblastoma.

Not recommended: pseudomalignant osteoblastoma; aggressive osteoblastoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Osteoblastomas show a predilection for the axial skeleton, with approximately 30% of cases occurring in the posterior elements of the spine. Secondary involvement of vertebral bodies may be seen. Other sites of involvement include long bones (particularly the femur and tibia), foot and ankle bones, craniofacial bones (especially the jaw), and the pelvis {17052641; 8119712}. However, any bone may be involved {8119712}.

Clinical features

The most common presenting symptom is pain and swelling. NSAIDs do not typically provide pain relief from osteoblastomas like they do from osteoid osteomas {8119712}. Vertebral osteoblastomas may result in cord compression and produce neurological symptoms ranging from tingling and numbness to paraparesis or paraplegia. Infrequently, patients with osteoblastomas may present with systemic symptoms including fever, weight loss, and diffuse periostitis {11479753}. Extremely rare cases have been associated with oncogenic osteomalacia {12971642}.

Imaging

Osteoblastomas often manifest as round to oval, expansile, well-demarcated, mixed lytic and blastic lesions with a zone of reactive sclerosis. Osteoblastomas of the spine are often located posteriorly within the dorsal elements of the neural arch and may show aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes. On MRI, they manifest as well-delineated masses with low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and intermediate to high signal intensity on T2-weighted images. Bone scintigraphy shows nonspecific increased radiotracer uptake {8119712; 27476846}. Fluid-fluid levels can be seen in larger osteoblastomas with aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes.

Epidemiology

Osteoblastomas are uncommon, accounting for only 1% of primary bone tumours {18561158; 8119712}. They commonly affect adolescents and young adults and show a male predilection (M:F ratio: 2:1).

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Recurrent rearrangements of FOS (89%) and its analogue FOSB (3%) have been detected in osteoblastoma and osteoid osteoma, providing further evidence that these tumours are related {29858576}.

Both FOS and FOSB are part of the FOS family of transcription factors that play a role in a diverse array of biological processes, including osteoblast maturation and proliferation {16313345}.

FOS rearrangements may be detected at the protein level by immunohistochemistry using an antibody targeted against the FOS N-terminus, resulting in nuclear immunoreactivity in the osteoblastic cells {31490237; 30582658; 31768625}.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteoblastomas are fairly well delineated masses that are often reddish brown (owing to their rich vascularity) and gritty (owing to mineralization) {8119712}. Aneurysmal bone cyst–like changes may be present.

Histopathology

Osteoblastomas and osteoid osteomas are histologically indistinguishable and are characterized by a delicate network of haphazardly arranged trabeculae of woven bone interspersed in vascular connective tissue. The trabeculae, lined by a single layer of plump osteoblasts, show different levels of mineralization, ranging from osteoid to densely mineralized woven bone with multiple cement lines (pagetoid appearance) {8119712}. Lace-like osteoid and cartilaginous differentiation are infrequent. Osteoclast-like giant cells are often scattered throughout the tumour. The host–tumour interface is sharp, often with the tumour showing peripheral bony maturation. Infiltration of host bone and a lack of maturation towards the periphery are worrisome features and should raise the consideration of osteoblastoma-like osteosarcoma {30152881; 8780939; 6600112}. Epithelioid osteoblastomas are characterized by the presence of large epithelioid osteoblasts that are frequently mitotically active {6600112; 8119712}. The presence of epithelioid osteoblasts does not seem to predict an aggressive clinical course in osteoblastoma, so the term “aggressive osteoblastoma” is not recommended {8119712; 8669532}. Osteoblastomas may infrequently show bizarre degenerated cells that should not be mistaken for malignancy; however, atypical mitotic figures should be absent {8119712}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

FOS (and rarely FOSB) rearrangement can be detected in the majority of osteoblastomas and can be useful to confirm the diagnosis {29858576}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: imaging consistent with osteoblastoma (a tumour > 20 mm in size with well-defined borders); a bone-forming tumour composed of irregular trabeculae of bone lined by plump osteoblasts in a vascularized stroma.

Desirable: demonstration of FOS overexpression by immunohistochemistry, or of FOS or (rarely) FOSB rearrangement by FISH (in selected cases).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Osteoblastomas have a good prognosis, but local recurrences occur in 23% of cases {18561158}. Treatment is usually curettage and less frequently en bloc resection. Malignant transformation in osteoblastoma is extremely rare {4764948; 8780939}.
