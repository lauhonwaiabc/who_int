5.2.3.8 Kaposi sarcoma

Definition

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is an aggressive angiogenic tumour associated with the herpesvirus KSHV/HHV8.

ICD-O coding

9140/3 Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Endemic Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Epidemic Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Iatrogenic/transplantation-related Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Sporadic/immunodeficiency-related Kaposi sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B57.Z & XH36A5 Kaposi sarcoma of unspecified primary site & Kaposi sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: idiopathic multiple pigmented sarcoma of the skin; angiosarcoma multiplex; Kaposi disease; granuloma multiplex haemorrhagicum.

Subtype(s)

Endemic Kaposi sarcoma; epidemic Kaposi sarcoma; iatrogenic/transplantation-related Kaposi sarcoma; sporadic/immunodeficiency-related Kaposi sarcoma

Localization

Paediatric KS primarily affects the lymph nodes, skin, intraoral mucosa, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory system, but any organ can be involved.

Clinical features

In children, KS may be disseminated and rapidly progressive. Clinical manifestations in children differ from those seen in adults {24567845}. Iatrogenic/transplantation-related KS is variable, with lymph node, visceral, and mucocutaneous involvement, as well as cytopenia and fast progression. An absence of cutaneous lesions in paediatric KS may represent a diagnostic challenge {30362299}.

Endemic KS affects younger children (mean age: 6.6 years). It is characterized by a rapidly progressing lymphadenopathy with variable skin or oral mucosa manifestations: patches, gingival hypertrophy, and palate and salivary gland nodules {30455728; 29722363}.

Epidemic KS occurs at a mean age of 8.8 years. Oral manifestations are more frequent, accompanied by visceral involvement of mostly gastrointestinal and respiratory organs (with pleural effusion); severe cytopenia may be an early manifestation {24567845; 24396347}. Twenty per cent of children treated with highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) may develop a KS immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) {23487320}.

Epidemiology

Endemic KS is most prevalent in equatorial Africa (known as the KS belt), where the seroprevalence of KSHV/HHV8 is high and increases with age {24567845}. Patients are HIV-negative.

Epidemic KS involves HIV-positive patients, mostly in endemic regions. The HIV pandemic led to a worldwide diffusion of KS in children, making this form the most frequent KS in the paediatric age group {29722363; 23255282}.

Iatrogenic/transplantation-related KS is extremely rare and occurs in the setting of solid-organ transplantation.

Only rare cases of sporadic/immunodeficiency-related KS have been reported. Some patients have confirmed single-gene inborn errors of immunity (WAS, IFNGR1, STIM1, and TNFRSF4) {20156905}. Specific inborn errors of immunity may underlie the pathogenesis of the sporadic form, and perhaps even the endemic form, of the disease {26469702}.

Etiology

KSHV/HHV8, the etiological agent responsible for KS, is a DNA virus encoding a latent nuclear antigen derived from the viral gene ORF73 {7997879; 7700310}. KSHV/HHV8 is transmitted in saliva, by blood transfusion or solid-organ transplantation, or vertically through vaginal and cervical secretions {30362299; 29722363; 24567845}. Major risks for developing KS include an inefficient immune T-cell response to the virus, HIV infection, immunosuppression after transplantation, and congenital immunodeficiencies such as those caused by mutations in IFNGR1, STAT4, MAGT1, and WAS (WASP), as well as common variable immunodeficiency {30649299}. Good syndrome (thymoma plus acquired immunodeficiency) confers a risk, although patients with this syndrome are generally adults {30649299}.

Pathogenesis

KSHV/HHV8 is a necessary but insufficient cause of malignancy. Seropositivity alone does not predict progression to KS or other diseases. The virus is capable of infecting several cell types, including endothelial cells. Like with other herpesviruses, infection with KSHV/HHV8 is lifelong and the virus exists in both lytic and latent forms {30705286}. The viral pathogenesis of KS depends upon regulation between these two phases, although triggers for this phase transition have not been fully elucidated.

Macroscopic appearance

In disseminated disease, chains of lymph nodes are often seen, measuring 3–50 mm. Bright-red or dark nodules of different sizes can be seen at the surface or deep inside almost any organ.

Histopathology

KS lesions start with a proliferation of spindle cells with no or mild atypia, where lumen formation is not quite evident; then slit-like spaces become more obvious, with intraluminal erythrocytes, sparse lymphocytes, plasma cells, and haemosiderin. Spindle cells form short fascicles in between vessels. Mitoses are easily detected. Central areas with necrosis are rich in tiny vessels, erythrocytes, hyaline globules, and eosinophilic debris. Vascular visceral dissemination reaches different organs, forming multiple enlarging unencapsulated nodules. Immunohistochemically, endothelial and spindle cells express CD31, CD34, ERG, and lymphatic markers (podoplanin, LYVE1). Nuclear staining for KSHV/HHV8 latency-associated nuclear antigen (LANA) confirms the diagnosis (positive cells: 10–90%) {30705286}.

Differential diagnosis

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours occur in the context of organ transplantation or immunodeficiency. They can develop in any organ, at single or multiple sites, and are EBV-positive by in situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) {29535735}.

Bacillary angiomatosis often occurs in HIV-positive patients. Skin manifestations overlap with KS, so a biopsy is essential to establish the correct diagnosis {25076357}. Bacillary angiomatosis is a vascular proliferation with round lumina and prominent neutrophils. Warthin–Starry staining highlights the organisms (Bartonella bacteria) within clumps of eosinophilic debris.

Kaposiform haemangioendothelioma has some histological similarities to KS (proliferating spindle cells, slit-like lumina, and positive staining for lymphatic endothelial markers), but the inflammatory infiltrate of KS is absent and the clinical setting is different.

Cytology

FNAB can be helpful, particularly in low-income countries. The diagnosis is based on the detection of bland spindle cells with radial arrangements, occasional eosinophilic globules, extravasated erythrocytes, and a tendency to streak during smearing {11074626}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

KSHV/HHV8 detection by PCR is a well-established approach for the confirmation of viral infection {22963082; 28904666}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: appropriate clinical context; bland spindle cell proliferation containing small slit-like vessels lined by mildly atypical cells; extravasated erythrocytes; detection of nuclear KSHV/HHV8 expression by immunohistochemistry or PCR.

Prognosis and prediction

Lymphadenopathic KS is associated with better long-term complete remission than disease with visceral dissemination and skin/oral lesions, which has a poor response to chemotherapy in HIV-infected patients {27082863}. In high-resource settings, PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin and paclitaxel are highly effective in inducing the regression of advanced AIDS-associated KS {32145827}. Limited cases of iatrogenic/transplantation-related paediatric KS have shown a response to the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus and to paclitaxel chemotherapy {24092038; 30843320}.

Stage 1 – Mild and moderate cutaneous/oral Kaposi sarcoma

 

1A – Mild: disease limited to hyperpigmented skin lesion, flat oral mucosal lesions, and/or flesh-coloured subcutaneous nodules; total < 10 lesions
 

1B – Moderate: any of the following features, alone or in combination: 10–19 hyperpigmented skin/oral lesions; nodular oral involvement; conjunctival eye involvement; exophytic mass; or any patient not meeting criteria for stages 1A, 2, 3, or 4

Stage 2 – Lymphadenopathic Kaposi sarcoma: having lymph node involvement, with or without any of the criteria for stage 1

Stage 3 – Woody oedema: woody oedema with or without any of the criteria for stages 1 and 2

 

3A: oedema involving < 10% of BSA
 

3B: oedema involving > 10% of BSA

Stage 4 – Visceral and/or disseminated cutaneous/oral Kaposi sarcoma: pulmonary or abdominal visceral involvement and/or having ≥ 20 hyperpigmented skin/oral lesions in a widespread distribution (excluding flesh-coloured subcutaneous nodules), with or without criteria for stages 1, 2, and 3; coalescing or confluent hyperpigmented skin lesions localized to an anatomical region count as 1 lesion per cluster

BSA, body surface area (estimated using the Wallace rule of nines, as used for burn patients).
