2.3.3.7 Hydroa vacciniforme lymphoproliferative disorder

Definition

Hydroa vacciniforme lymphoproliferative disorder (HV-LPD) is a chronic EBV-positive skin disease associated with an increased risk of developing a systemic lymphoma. Monoclonal TR gene rearrangement is often present, but it does not correlate with the severity of the disease.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Classic hydroa vacciniforme lymphoproliferative disorder; systemic hydroa vacciniforme lymphoproliferative disorder

Localization

In the classic form the skin lesions involve the sun-exposed areas, whereas in the systemic form the skin lesions extend to the non-exposed skin areas.

Clinical features

Classic HV-LPD is characterized by recurrent papulovesicular eruption on sun-exposed areas that usually evolve to blisters and ulcers, leaving varioliform atrophic scars. Skin lesions show seasonal variation, with recurrences in spring and summer. Some cases resolve in adolescence. A few cases have a more protracted course. In the systemic form of the disease, unlike in the classic form, lesions are not clearly induced or exacerbated by sun exposure or exposure to ultraviolet (UV) A or B light {26368647}. As the disease progresses, the skin lesions become severe and extensive, and they are associated with fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy. Clinical progression is heralded by a lack of improvement with photoprotection, severe facial and lip swelling, systemic complications, and an increase in the number of EBV-positive cells. Episodes of hypersensitivity to mosquito bites and abnormal antibody titres to EBV may occur {22297643}. Systemic HV-LPD is an aggressive disease with a high mortality rate and an increased risk of developing a systemic lymphoma {23982171; 25234411}. There are cases with a peculiar clinical presentation, including prominent lip or periorbital oedema associated with systemic symptoms {26368647; 25162933}.

Epidemiology

HV-LPD is seen mainly in children and adolescents from Asia and from Indigenous populations of South America and Central America (including Mexico). It is rare in White people. The mean age of patients is 10–15 years (range: 4–27 years). There is a male predilection (M:F ratio: 2:1). This disease is rare in adults {30654082; 23982171; 23541598; 11893040; 17976208}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Genetic predisposition might play a major role {31064750}.

Pathogenesis

HV-LPD is a chronic EBV-positive skin disease {31248873; 31064750} that mainly affects children and young adults. It is associated with an increased risk of developing a systemic lymphoma {30654082; 23982171; 23541598}. The clinical presentation ranges from an indolent, self-limiting photodermatosis (classic HV-LPD) to a more aggressive systemic disorder with lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly (systemic HV-LPD) {31248873}. Skin manifestations include oedema, blisters, ulcers, and scars, which occur on the face, trunk, and extremities. In severe cases, the skin lesions are large and extend into the subcutaneous tissue, leaving disfiguring scars and areas of necrosis {23541598; 7822515; 3342168; 10642674}. Monoclonal TR gene rearrangement is often present, but it does not correlate with the severity of the disease.

Histopathology

There is epidermal reticular degeneration leading to intraepidermal spongiotic vesiculation. In severe cases, the epidermis is ulcerated. There is also a perivascular and periadnexal lymphocytic infiltrate of atypical small to medium cells in the papillary and reticular dermis, occasionally extending into the subcutis. Vasculitis, angiodestruction, and necrosis may also be present {30654082; 23982171; 23541598; 9580256; 27988064; 31889602}.

Many cases are associated with CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, expressing TIA1, granzyme B, or perforin. Patients from China have a higher prevalence of CD4+ cytotoxic cells or double CD4+/CD8+ cells {30654082; 31315684}. Less commonly, the lymphoid cells have an NK-cell phenotype (CD56-positive, CD5-negative, surface T-cell receptor–negative); such cases often show prominent subcutis involvement, morphologically mimicking subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma {30654082; 23982171; 23541598; 30998212}. Surface T-cell receptor expression is variable. Cases with an NK-cell phenotype lack T-cell receptor expression. Variable numbers of lymphoid cells show positive staining on in situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER).

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Most cases have clonal rearrangements of the TR genes. In situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is positive, with variable numbers of positive cells. EBV is monoclonal by terminal repeat analysis. Although LMP1 is often negative by immunohistochemistry, it can be detected in most cases by PCR in peripheral blood, indicating type II EBV latency {31064750; 30654082; 23982171; 23541598; 11893040; 27988064; 30542432; 20497358}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: papulovesicular skin eruption with or without photoexacerbation that heals with varioliform scarring; perivascular and periadnexal atypical lymphoid infiltrate of cytotoxic T or NK cells; EBER positivity; for classic HV-LPD: no persistent systemic symptoms, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatitis, haemophagocytic syndrome, or NK-cell lymphocytosis; for systemic HV-LPD: one or more persistent symptoms or signs of extracutaneous disease.

Prognosis and prediction

The clinical course is variable, and patients may have recurrent skin lesions for as long as 10–15 years before developing systemic involvement {25234411}. With systemic spread, the clinical course is much more aggressive {31064750; 23541598}. T-cell clonality, the number of EBV-positive cells, and/or the density of the infiltrate does not correlate with the clinical course {23982171; 25234411}. The disease is resistant to conventional chemotherapy, and treated patients often die of infectious complications {23541598; 11893040}. In indolent cases, a conservative approach is recommended {31064750}. Recent clinical studies suggest that systemic HV-LPD can be successfully treated with immunomodulators (thalidomide, antivirals, interferon) {25196949; 19688153; 19354331} and (in severe cases) with haematopoietic stem cell transplantation {32516422}.
