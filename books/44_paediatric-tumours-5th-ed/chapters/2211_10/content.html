2.2.1.1 Myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome

Definition

Myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome encompass two clonally related leukaemic conditions, transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM) and myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome (ML-DS), which uniquely affect children with constitutional trisomy 21 who acquire mutations in the GATA1 gene prenatally.

ICD-O coding

9898/1 Transient abnormal myelopoiesis

9898/3 Myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome

ICD-11 coding

2A60.4Y Other specified myeloid proliferation associated with Down syndrome

2A60.4Z Myeloid proliferation associated with Down syndrome, unspecified

Related terminology

Acceptable: for TAM: transient myeloproliferative disorder; transient leukaemia of Down syndrome; for ML-DS: acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) in Down syndrome; myelodysplastic syndrome in young children with Down syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Transient abnormal myelopoiesis; myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome

Localization

TAM: This manifests in peripheral blood. Severe cases affect the liver, spleen, pleura, and pericardium {18182574; 21849481}. Bone marrow involvement is usually mild.

ML-DS: The main site is the haematopoietic system (bone marrow, liver, and spleen). CNS involvement is rare (0 of 374 cases {28400376; 28389462}).

Clinical features

TAM: This varies from a life-threatening disease to an asymptomatic incidental finding. Most cases come to clinical attention within 7 days of birth. All symptomatic cases have increased blast cells in peripheral blood {24021668}. Severe disease manifests with hepatomegaly (40% of cases), splenomegaly (30%), vesiculopapular skin rash (11%), and/or pleural/pericardial effusion (9%) {29916557}. Cholestatic jaundice is common (70%) but not specific to TAM. Leukocytosis is common and may be extreme (> 100 000 × 109/L); basophilia and/or eosinophilia may be prominent; anaemia is uncommon; platelet counts may be normal, reduced, or increased {18182574; 21849481; 24021668}. Mortality in severe cases approaches 20% and is usually from progressive liver failure and coagulopathy. Asymptomatic disease may be clinically silent (increased blasts only) or clinically and haematologically silent (blasts not increased but GATA1 mutation present) {24021668}.

ML-DS: The median age of onset of ML-DS (1.6 years {28389462}) is lower than that of AML in the overall paediatric population (9.7 years {25092781}). Preceding cytopenias for weeks or months have been reported in 30–47% of patients {28400376; 28389462; 22392565}. At least one third of patients have a history of antecedent TAM {28400376; 28389462}.

Epidemiology

TAM: Of all newborns with Down syndrome, 25–30% have clinical or silent TAM (GATA1 mutation present) {24021668}. Clinical TAM occurs in 10% of newborns with Down syndrome {12648061}; a further 15% have silent TAM (no clinical or haematological features) {24021668}. Because the estimated incidence of Down syndrome worldwide is 91–200 cases per 100 000 live births, TAM is the commonest preleukaemic disorder in babies.

ML-DS: The incidence of AML is about 150 times as high in young children with Down syndrome as in the overall paediatric population {10675114}. The vast majority of patients present within the first 4 years of life {28400376; 28389462; 10675114}.

Etiology

TAM: In virtually all cases where a sensitive test is used, neonatal blood cells harbour hemizygous, prenatally acquired, somatic mutations in exon 2 or 3 of the GATA1 gene {24021668; 12586620}.

ML-DS: The cause of the increased frequency of leukaemia (and decreased incidence of most solid tumours) in individuals with trisomy 21 remains to be determined.

Pathogenesis

TAM: Somatic mutations in exon 2 or 3 of GATA1 cause the production of the short isoform of the GATA1 protein (GATA1s), which is leukaemogenic only in fetal blood cells that harbour trisomy 21 and only causes TAM in newborns with Down syndrome, including mosaic Down syndrome {18182574; 12172547}. GATA1s disrupts fetal blood cell development, causing blast cell accumulation in the blood, liver, and other tissues, as well as associated fibrosis in severe cases.

ML-DS: Somatic GATA1 mutations in the blasts of TAM and ML-DS are concordant within the same individual, consistent with a model in which ML-DS evolves from a subclone of TAM after the acquisition of additional mutations. The spectrum of ML-DS mutations is distinct from that of paediatric and adult AML and targets genes encoding cohesin components, signal transducers, and epigenetic regulators {34244384; 31303423; 24056718}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

TAM: Histopathology is rarely used. Liver biopsy may be useful in severe cases with prominent hepatopathy and typically shows diffuse blast cell infiltration and fibrosis {1387814}.

ML-DS: Bone marrow biopsy shows infiltration with blasts, which often (39–97% of cases) express megakaryocytic markers {28400376; 28389462; 22392565}. Reticulin fibrosis and clonal cytogenetic changes may be present.

Cytology

TAM: Increased blasts are seen in the peripheral blood of neonates with Down syndrome in all symptomatic cases. Blast morphology varies from undifferentiated myeloblasts to typical small megakaryoblasts with cytoplasmic blebbing or large, partially differentiated megakaryoblasts. TAM blasts usually express KIT (CD117) and variable proportions of CD34, CD7, CD36, CD41, CD42b, and/or CD235a {15858703}.

ML-DS: A specific bone marrow blast percentage is not essential for diagnosis. Fibrosis may result in non-diagnostic aspirate samples and the need for bone marrow biopsy. Non-leukaemic myeloid progenitors coexpressing CD56 and CD34 or CD33 physiologically occur in the bone marrow of children with Down syndrome {17043020; 33890142} and should not be mistaken for residual ML-DS.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

TAM: The current standard assay for a specific diagnosis of TAM requires detection of exon 2 or 3 GATA1 mutations by DNA sequencing of peripheral blood blasts {29916557}. In TAM, > 95% of mutations are in exon 2 {21715302}. Because small mutant GATA1 clones may transform to ML-DS, sensitive next-generation sequencing is necessary to avoid false negative results. GATA1 mutation analysis should be performed in the first week after birth while there are peripheral blood blasts on the blood smear to avoid false negatives {29916557}. Even using next-generation sequencing, large or complex deletions in exon 2 or 3 of the GATA1 gene may be missed or difficult to assess. Therefore, GATA1 analysis should always be performed in conjunction with a complete blood count, morphological review of a blood smear, and blast cell immunophenotyping of a peripheral blood sample {24021668; 29916557}. Where GATA1 analysis is unavailable, a presumptive diagnosis may be made based on characteristic features and age (< 3 months).

ML-DS: Exon 2 or 3 GATA1 mutations identical to those in TAM are found in 85% of cases {28400376}. False negatives may reflect low assay sensitivity, low blast percentage, or atypical deletions/mutations {21715302}. Because ML-DS is rare in children aged > 4 years {28400376; 28389462; 22392565} and may represent non–Down syndrome AML {18059480}, GATA1 mutation analysis is recommended to identify ML-DS and ensure correct treatment.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM)

Essential: complete blood count; peripheral blood smear; confirmation of trisomy 21 (FISH/karyotyping); flow cytometry of blasts in the peripheral blood; sequencing of GATA1 exon 2 in all cases with blasts > 10%; clinical assessment for life-threatening features; liver function tests and coagulation screen in severe cases {29916557}.

Desirable: next-generation sequencing of GATA1 exon 2 or 3 where direct sequencing does not detect GATA1 mutations in exon 2 and blasts are > 10%.

Myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome (ML-DS)

Essential: bone aspirate and biopsy to determine blast morphology, immunophenotype, and cytogenetics; cerebrospinal fluid cell count and cytospin.

Desirable: direct sequencing of GATA1 exon 2 or 3; targeted sequencing using an AML gene panel that includes the genes mutated in ML-DS (see Pathogenesis, above).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

TAM: More than 90% of cases resolve spontaneously in 2–3 months, without treatment; 20% of patients with clinical TAM later develop ML-DS {18182574; 21849481}. Persistence of blasts in the blood 3 months after diagnosis of TAM (as detected by flow cytometry or sequencing of GATA1 mutations) is associated with a 3-fold higher risk for developing ML-DS {18182574; 21849481; 24021668; 29916557; 29959152; 33654203}. Risk factors for early death in severe TAM include preterm delivery, ascites, leukocyte count > 100 × 109/L, hepatomegaly, and coagulopathy {18182574; 21849481; 29916557}.

ML-DS: The prognosis (event-free survival: 87–89%; overall survival: 89–93% {28400376; 28389462}) is markedly better than that for AML in children without Down syndrome (event-free survival: 47–53%; overall survival: 63–69% {25092781; 21902686}). Increased sensitivity of ML-DS blasts to chemotherapeutic agents, such as cytarabine {15390307; 11756178}, may contribute to the excellent outcomes of ML-DS. Therefore, chemotherapy protocols of lower intensity are used for ML-DS and stem cell transplantation is not required.
