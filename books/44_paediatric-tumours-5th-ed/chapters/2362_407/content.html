2.3.6.2 Posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorders

Definition

Posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLDs) are a spectrum of conditions related to a dysregulation of immune control with lymphocyte and/or plasma cell proliferation, associated in the majority of cases with EBV infection, in bone marrow or organ transplant recipients.

ICD-O coding

Related terminology

Not recommended: early posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder.

Subtype(s)

Non-destructive hyperplastic PTLD; polymorphic PTLDs; monomorphic PTLDs

Localization

The sites of involvement in childhood PTLDs mirror those in adult PTLDs: lymph nodes, gastrointestinal tract, liver, and CNS {11422823; 12482154}. PTLD can occur singly or as part of a multisystemic disease (see #19500Table 2.15). Allograft involvement in children is not uncommon and is enhanced in the settings of heart, lung, and liver transplants {17683183; 16427492; 12698085; 11472603}. Like in adults, allograft PTLD in children tends to occur close to the time of transplant {15877793; 15665775; 14974943; 23730927; 12698085}.

Clinical features

PTLDs in children are characterized by specific symptoms related to the organ involved and the age of the patient at the time of transplant {12185479; 12390426}. The majority of children show rising serum levels of EBV by quantitative PCR before overt manifestations of organ dysfunction or frank lesions are evident {9884252; 9163497; 20102529; 8043879; 11493441; 8167350}. Symptoms of PTLD in allograft recipients may mimic rejection or graft-versus-host disease in the organ {31230381; 16427492}. Specific systemic symptoms may be also present.

Non-destructive PTLDs (ND-PTLDs) and polymorphic PTLDs (P-PTLDs) tend to occur at a younger age than monomorphic PTLDs (M-PTLDs). ND-PTLDs and P-PTLDs are often seen in children without a prior EBV infection and after posttransplant primary EBV infection, respectively {16427492; 9587133; 7750936; 17134734}. These PTLDs, especially ND-PTLDs, involve lymph nodes or tonsils and adenoids more often than extranodal sites {10716151; 7750936}. Both may regress spontaneously with a reduction in immunosuppression or may be successfully treated by surgical excision. Infectious mononucleosis–like lesions can be fatal. ND-PTLDs may evolve into P-PTLDs, and both may evolve into M-PTLDs {2845789; 8617457; 7812011; 23010712; 17134734}.

M-PTLDs have clinical presentations similar to lymphomas or the plasma cell neoplasms that they resemble. Most T/NK-cell neoplasms occur at extranodal sites, sometimes with associated lymphadenopathy. The more common sites of involvement include the peripheral blood or bone marrow, spleen, skin, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and lung.

Epidemiology

The occurrence of PTLDs in adults and children is strongly associated with several factors, including the type of transplant, the immunosuppressive agents used, and recipient EBV serological status {31230381; 27365460; 10498590; 11785847; 22226225}.

The risk of developing a PTLD is greater in children than in adults receiving the same type of organ {10498590; 12698085; 17683183}. The difference in risk profile between children and adults has largely been attributed to the increased likelihood of paediatric recipients of transplanted organs being EBV-seronegative at the time of their transplant {11422823; 22273720; 22226225}. Approximately 50% of children aged ≤ 10 years are EBV-seronegative {19855238}. Additionally, studies have shown an appreciably higher risk of PTLDs in paediatric-age EBV-seropositive transplant recipients than in their organ-matched adult counterparts {11422823; 22226225}.

Etiology

The role of EBV infection as a major driver of PTLDs is well established. EBV can be detected using in situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER). About 80% of PTLD cases, including rare T-cell PTLDs, are EBV-positive. EBV negativity in PTLD is reported with increasing frequency in paediatric cohorts, mostly in cases of M-PTLD and T-cell PTLD. This may reflect sequelae in longer-term survivors of organ transplants, or improvements in the early detection and treatment of PTLDs associated with EBV reactivation using quantitative PCR surveillance of serum EBV {17683183; 12099380; 17122518; 17971586; 23635036}.

Pathogenesis

EBV infects B lymphocytes. In immunocompetent individuals, after the primary infection leading to infectious mononucleosis, the virus circularizes its genome, establishing chronic latency. Different latent EBV antigens expressed during B-cell development drive naïve B cells to develop into post–germinal-centre memory B cells and allow EBV to persist quiescently in peripheral blood and in the reticuloendothelial system. In normal conditions, these EBV-infected B cells are destroyed by EBV-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes. The reduction of T-cell immune surveillance caused by immunosuppression promotes EBV viraemia with an increased risk of mutations in EBV-infected cells (alterations of BCL6, MYC, NFKB genes, PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway genes, BCL2, and genes involved in immunoglobulin switching). These alterations promote the progression from P-PTLD to M-PTLD {31089285; 28419429; 32457824}.

Late-onset PTLDs are frequently EBV-negative. Different mechanisms have been proposed: hit-and-run EBV infection, other viral infections, persistent antigen stimulation by the graft, and long-term immunosuppression. The genomic features of EBV-negative PTLD are the same as those of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas in immunocompetent individuals, and their complex copy-number aberrations suggest the accumulation of genomic alterations. By contrast, EBV-positive PTLD exhibits different genomic features and minor molecular/genetic changes {26780579; 29414277; 28419429}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lymph nodes or tonsils are enlarged.

Histopathology

The classification of PTLDs in children is identical to the system used in adults (see #19374Table 2.16). In brief, the classification of PTLD patterns is broadly based on the presence or absence of destruction of the organ involved, the monoclonality of the proliferation in question, and the cellular lineage of the abnormal cellular component. The presence of EBV in tissues, often best seen using in situ hybridization, should be assessed in all cases.

Hyperplastic ND-PTLD

Hyperplastic ND-PTLD lesions (formerly known as early lesions) are mass-forming enlargements of lymphoid organs with retention of the underlying immunoarchitecture and significant EBV positivity. They show considerable overlap with patterns of reactive hyperplasia, and strict criteria separating the two are not well established {28395107}. Increased EBV positivity is key to establishing a diagnosis of the ND-PTLD pattern in children {23010712}.

Florid follicular hyperplasia: This pattern is characterized by the marked expansion of secondary follicles without excess paracortical immunoblasts or plasma cells, often involving tonsils and lymph nodes, with prominent EBV detection {28395107}. Special note should be made of the degree of EBV positivity: limited EBV may be observed in lymphoid organs (especially tonsils) removed in transplant patients for other reasons not necessarily related to serum EBV levels or predictive of evolution to more aggressive forms of PTLD {14963764; 11477143; 11278019}.

Plasmacytic hyperplasia: This pattern is characterized by the expansion of the paracortex by polytypic plasma cells present in large aggregates or sheets. Reactive follicles are present but not a prominent feature {28395107; 23010712}.

Infectious mononucleosis–like hyperplasia: This pattern is characterized by the expansion of the paracortical zone by a polymorph population of small and medium lymphocytes, immunoblasts, and (fewer) plasma cells. Reactive follicles are present but not a prominent feature. Rare HRS-like cells may be present {28395107}.

Immunohistochemistry shows polytypic B cells, plasma cells, and T cells. EBV is demonstrated by EBER in plasmacytic hyperplasia and florid follicular hyperplasia {7812011; 23010712; 17134734}, and its absence should be evaluated with caution, because histological/immunophenotypic findings are nonspecific. Infectious mononucleosis–like PTLD is always EBV-positive and shows immunohistochemical expression of LMP1 {7750936}.

P-PTLD

P-PTLDs are characterized by an effacement of the underlying lymphoid organ architecture by a population of small and medium-sized lymphocytes, immunoblasts, and plasma cells that represent the full spectrum of B-cell maturation {16427492; 16048596}. These lesions do not fulfil established criteria for lymphoma {28395107}. There can be considerable overlap between the changes of P-PTLD and the polymorphic patterns of M-PTLD such as EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma or T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma.

Immunostains demonstrate B cells and a frequently prominent T-cell component {11789726; 2845789}. Large B-cell immunoblasts may express CD30 {9104951; 25248878}. In most cases EBER is positive in numerous cells. Focal or multifocal light chain restriction, with different clonal populations, may be present {2845789}. However, in cases where there is clear-cut light chain restriction, it should be reported, because it could represent monomorphic diffuse large B-cell lymphoma PTLD with plasmacytic differentiation.

M-PTLD

These are lesions fulfilling the criteria for B-cell or T/NK-cell neoplasms that are recognized in immunocompetent hosts. M-PTLDs are characterized by the effacement of the underlying lymphoid organ architecture by a lymphoproliferative process that would otherwise meet criteria for a large B-cell, T-cell, plasmacytic cell, or Hodgkin lymphoma in a non-transplanted individual {28395107}. Classification of the various lymphoproliferative disorders is beyond the scope of this volume and can be reviewed elsewhere [[Swerdlow SH, Campo E, Harris NL, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of haematopoietic and lymphoid tissues. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th rev. ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.fr/556.]]. Small B-cell lymphoid neoplasms are not designated as PTLDs, except for EBV-positive lymphoplasmacytic proliferations that meet the criteria for MALT lymphoma {28395107}. Similarly, M-PTLDs lesions of T/NK-cell lineage or of the classic Hodgkin lymphoma type should morphologically and immunophenotypically resemble the lesions of their respective classifications in non-transplant patients. In one large population-based study, plasma cell neoplasms were EBV-positive less often than other neoplasms, although the frequency of EBV in paediatric plasma cell neoplasms is less well defined than in adults {17971586; 23635036; 16086426}.

Cytology

FNAB specimens in PTLDs vary from polymorphic smears (in ND-PTLD or P-PTLD) exhibiting the maturation spectrum of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and histiocytes to monomorphic smears (in M-PTLD) characterized by a monotonous population of large lymphoid cells {9143839}. However, intralesional heterogeneity and the need for architectural features for categorization are important limitations to the use of FNAB in PTLD.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clonality analysis for B cells is of limited utility: ND-PTLD lesions are often polyclonal or occasionally oligoclonal, and M-PTLD is invariably monoclonal {18457340; 17134734}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

ND-PTLD

Essential: history of transplant; preserved lymphoid tissue architecture with mass-like changes related to follicular hyperplasia or plasmacytic hyperplasia, or paracortical expansion with mixed mononucleosis-like infiltrate; demonstration of EBV infection by EBER.

P-PTLD

Essential: history of transplant; destructive pattern or effacement of underlying lymphoid organ architecture with B cells (whole range of maturation) and a variable number of T cells.

Desirable: demonstration of EBV infection by EBER.

M-PTLD

Essential: morphology and immunophenotype of otherwise well-defined B-cell, T/NK-cell, or plasmacytic neoplasm.

Desirable: demonstration of EBV infection by EBER.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The single most important predictor of PTLD development across all ages is EBV seronegativity of the recipient, with the highest risk associated with kidney and heart transplants {12234267; 15777111; 22273720; 19855238; 22226225}. The overall prognosis in children with a PTLD roughly correlates with the histological type and clinical stage at the time of diagnosis {17683183; 16427492; 20304682; 12698085}. Data reported from the modern era of PTLD management with quantitative monitoring of serum EBV and the use of anti-CD20 therapy show similar mortality rates for P-PTLDs and M-PTLDs {16048596; 16427492; 12099380}. Incidence rates of EBV-related PTLDs may be declining, as compared with rates reported in previous transplantation eras {23696264; 23072522}. Several factors, in particular bone marrow and CNS involvement, correlate with more aggressive behaviour {17971586; 16712612}.
