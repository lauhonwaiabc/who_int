2.3.6.3 HIV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders

Definition

HIV-associated lymphoproliferative disorders arise in the setting of immunodeficiency and dysregulation caused by HIV infection and include the spectrum of lymphoid hyperplasias, polymorphic lymphoproliferative disorders of varied malignant potential, and overt malignant lymphomas (predominantly of B-cell type). The majority of cases are associated with EBV infection.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Lymphoma types associated with HIV in the paediatric age group are summarized in #20038Table 2.17 .

Localization

Localization varies depending on the lymphoma type. Non-Hodgkin lymphomas in children involve any anatomical site or more than one site (see #20038Table 2.17). Classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL) may not follow the usual pattern of contiguous spread seen in the immunocompetent population, and may arise at extranodal sites such as the bone marrow or liver.

Clinical features

Clinical features are variable, depending on the type of lymphoma and the site of involvement. Symptoms of CNS lymphoma include headache and seizures, whereas chest lymphomas are associated with shortness of breath, cough, and recurrent pneumonia {7784591}. Systemic B symptoms, including fever, are common.

Epidemiology

The estimated prevalence of HIV-associated cancers in infected children is about 2.5% {22935659; 23588335}, and children with AIDS have a 650-fold increased risk for non-Hodgkin lymphoma {10889594}. The average age at diagnosis is 3–3.5 years. The median time at which non-Hodgkin lymphoma develops in children with AIDS is reported to be 14 months after AIDS onset, although cases of primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) occur as long as 98 months after AIDS onset {10889594}. The incidence increases with age, and boys are more often affected than girls. Most children who develop lymphoma have moderate to severe immunosuppression and a high viral load at diagnosis {22935659}. Since the introduction of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), which improves CD4+ cell counts, the incidence of CNS lymphoma has decreased. Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is the most frequent HIV-associated lymphoma in children. Compared to DLBCL, BL is associated with more severe immunosuppression and a greater incidence of opportunistic infections and other clinical manifestations of HIV infection.

Etiology

The lymphomas arise due to HIV infection and are often associated with EBV.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis relates to persistent antigenic stimulation, genetic abnormalities, cytokine dysregulation, and an impaired immune response. Critical microenvironmental factors include HIV products that are secreted and accumulate within germinal centres {26773045}. The HIV protein p17 can activate PI3K/AKT signalling, leading to B-cell growth and malignant transformation {21423810}. HIV-related lymphomas are frequently associated with EBV {12092768}, and the lymphoproliferative action of EBV contributes to oncogenesis by causing expansion of B-lymphoid cells and an increased probability of genetic alterations. EBV latent membrane protein (LMP) is expressed in some HIV-related lymphomas including Reed–Sternberg cells in CHL, and it has oncogenic potential.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, HIV-associated lymphoma resembles lymphoma in the immunocompetent population.

Histopathology

Burkitt lymphoma

BL shows a diffuse cohesive proliferation of intermediate-sized cells with round or slightly irregular nuclei, distinct nucleoli, and basophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm that may contain small vacuoles. There are few infiltrating small lymphocytes. Mitoses are frequent, as are phagocytic macrophages, imparting a starry-sky pattern in most cases. In the setting of HIV, the histology of BL includes cases with more nuclear pleomorphism than in the immunocompetent population, and many cases have a distinctive plasmacytoid appearance with eccentric nuclei and more abundant amphophilic cytoplasm. The germinal-centre markers CD20, CD10, and BCL6 are positive. BCL2 expression is characteristically absent in BL, but in HIV-related cases there may be weak or focal expression. The Ki-67 proliferation index approaches 100%. MYC immunohistochemistry is usually positive, associated with a MYC translocation. IRF4 (MUM1) expression may also be seen in HIV-related BL, and it may be associated with an adverse prognosis {28079574}. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is positive in 40–50% of cases, but EBV LMP is absent. Burkitt-like lymphoma with 11q aberration and without MYC translocations may rarely be seen in this setting, but it might not be directly related to HIV infection and is not considered an AIDS-defining lymphoma.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

DLBCLs consist of sheets of large lymphoid cells with round or oval nuclei and prominent nucleoli. Mitoses are frequent, but numerous phagocytic histiocytes and a starry-sky pattern are usually absent. In many cases, there is immunoblastic differentiation, characterized by cells with deeply basophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm and a large round nucleus with a central nucleolus. In the brain, tumours may be deep-seated or multifocal. Lymphoid cells characteristically form cuffs that follow vascular channels and surround blood vessels. Meningeal involvement also occurs.

Immunohistochemically, DLBCLs express the B-cell markers CD19 and CD20 and commonly show a non–germinal-centre B-cell (non-GCB) phenotype in the paediatric population. Non-GCB DLBCLs appear to be more aggressive than GCB-type DLBCLs, and both can be EBV-positive {16444194}. Aberrant expression of T-cell markers, including CD2 and CD4, may occur.

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma

HIV patients with CHL have a less impaired immune system than HIV patients with DLBCL, and cART might not protect against the development of CHL {25252101}. CHLs are of the nodular sclerosis or mixed-cellularity type. There is no known association between nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma and HIV infection. Reed–Sternberg cells express EBV in almost all cases {8386441}, with type II EBV latency and expression of EBNA1, LMP1, and LMP2. These proteins have oncogenic potential and can cause activation of the NF-kB pathway. The phenotype of Reed–Sternberg cells resembles that seen in the immunocompetent population, including expression of CD15 and CD30, but there may be aberrant expression of B- or T-cell markers {32134194}. Pulmonary nodular lymphoid hyperplasia has been described in patients treated for CHL, and it may be related to the immune reconstitution process {32131663}.

Polymorphic lymphoid proliferation

This is a polymorphic population of cells including small lymphocytes, immunoblasts, and plasma cells. EBER is highly expressed in most cases {10328088; 9923843}. Despite the polymorphic nature of the proliferations, they are always clonal, exhibiting rearrangements of genes encoding immunoglobulin heavy or light chain. The background population may be oligoclonal, suggesting clonal cells within a mixed background. Abnormalities in MYC, TP53, BCL6, and BCL2 are usually absent {12604885}.

MALT lymphoma

MALT lymphomas may arise in the background of pulmonary lymphocytic hyperplasia / lymphocytic interstitial pneumonitis and can be EBV-positive {9321782; 9128273}.

Rare lymphomas

Mature T- and NK-cell lymphomas, including subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma {23092204}, cutaneous CD30-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma {9856678; 12439041}, plasmablastic lymphoma {24771257}, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements, high-grade B-cell lymphoma NOS {25729731}, and KSHV/HHV8-related lymphomas, are exceedingly rare in paediatric HIV-positive patients. For their histopathology, please see the respective sections.

Cytology

BL is characterized by intermediate-sized cells with round, oval, or slightly irregular nuclei. The cytoplasm frequently has small cytoplasmic vacuoles, and there may be an amphophilic or plasmacytic quality to the cytoplasm, which is particular to HIV-associated BL.

DLBCL in cytological preparations is characterized by large cells with one or more prominent nucleoli frequently aligned at the cytoplasmic membrane. Cases with a plasmablastic appearance often have a single large nucleolus and amphophilic eccentric cytoplasm.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Non-Hodgkin lymphomas are monoclonal and have recurrent genetic abnormalities, including rearrangements of MYC, BCL6, and tumour suppressor genes. BCL2 rearrangements are less frequent than in the immunocompetent adult population, and double-hit/triple-hit high-grade B-cell lymphomas (with MYC and BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements) are uncommon. EBV infection identified by EBER in situ hybridization occurs in about 20% of DLBCLs, 40–50% of BLs, and in some MALT and NK/T-cell lymphomas.

Characteristic molecular findings relate to the lymphoma type. BL has translocations involving the MYC gene on chromosome 8, and the translocation partner gene is most often IGH on chromosome 14, IGK on chromosome 2, or IGL on chromosome 22. In most laboratories break-apart probes are used to identify the translocations, but they will not identify all cases, and MYC translocation is not required for diagnosis. The most common molecular abnormalities seen in DLBCL include the translocation t(14;18) involving IGH on chromosome 14 and BCL2 on chromosome 18. MYC rearrangements may be less common than in the immunocompetent population and are usually associated with the GCB phenotype.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a clinical history of HIV infection and

  • for BL: sheets of intermediate-sized cells with small nucleoli; frequent mitoses; phenotype: CD20+, CD10+, MYC+, BCL6+, BCL2−, high Ki-67 index (almost 100%);
  • for DLBCL: sheets of large lymphoid cells with round or oval nuclei and prominent nucleoli; frequent mitoses;
  • for CHL: classic Reed–Sternberg cells (CD30+, CD45−) within a mixed inflammatory infiltrate (and sclerotic bands); type II EBV latency (expression of EBNA1, LMP1, and LMP2);
  • for polymorphic lymphoid proliferation: a polymorphic population of small lymphocytes, immunoblasts, and plasma cells; EBER expression;
  • for other lymphomas: see specific sections.

Desirable:

  • for BL: translocation involving MYC;
  • for polymorphic lymphoid proliferation: clonal IG gene rearrangements;
  • for other lymphomas: see specific sections.

Staging

The St. Jude staging system is used for non-Hodgkin lymphomas and the Lugano classification is used for paediatric Hodgkin lymphoma associated with HIV [[Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]].

Prognosis and prediction

With cART there is a substantially decreased risk of opportunistic infections, and full-dose conventional chemotherapy has become the standard of care, leading to results comparable with those in HIV-negative patients {16685736}. Studies indicating adverse prognostic factors such as BCL2 and/or MYC expression and EBV infection {28610450} may not be relevant in the era of cART combined with effective combination chemotherapy.
