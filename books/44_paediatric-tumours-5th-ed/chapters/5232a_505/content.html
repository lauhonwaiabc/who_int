5.2.3.2A Infantile haemangioma

Definition

Infantile haemangioma (IH) is the most common vascular tumour of infancy and is characterized by a rapid proliferative phase during infancy followed by gradual involution.

ICD-O coding

9131/0 Infantile haemangioma

9131/0 Reticular infantile haemangioma

ICD-11 coding

2E81.2Y & XH3U29 Other specified benign vascular lesion of infancy and childhood & Capillary haemangioma

Related terminology

Not recommended: capillary haemangioma, juvenile haemangioma, strawberry naevus, capillary-cavernous haemangioma, infantile haemangioendothelioma.

Subtype(s)

Reticular infantile haemangioma (also commonly referred to as telangiectatic, abortive, and minimal- or arrested-growth infantile haemangioma)

Localization

Skin and subcutaneous tissue, particularly of the head and neck region, is the most common location. Viscera, primarily the liver or bowel with or without mesenteric extension, are sometimes involved. Contemporaneous hepatic involvement may be present with multiple cutaneous lesions.

Clinical features

IH affects approximately 5% of infants, usually manifests in the newborn period, reaches its apogee at about 10 months of age, and gradually involutes over several years. IH may be solitary, segmental, indeterminate, or multifocal. The most common appearance is a solitary bright-red raised tumour; less commonly, it is a patch-like lesion. IH restricted to the hypodermis may manifest only as a dome-shaped mass with a bluish cutaneous blush {32384034}. Extensive IH in the head and neck and in the lumbosacral and perineal regions may be associated with congenital abnormalities and hamartomas in regional structures and organs, engendering PHACE syndrome (posterior fossa brain abnormalities, haemangioma, arterial lesions, cardiac abnormalities, and eye abnormalities) and LUMBAR syndrome (lower body haemangioma, urogenital abnormalities/ulceration, myelopathy, bony deformities, anorectal malformations / arterial anomalies, and renal anomalies). The term “LUMBAR syndrome” incorporates and replaces two previously described syndromes: SACRAL syndrome (spinal dysraphism, anogenital anomalies, cutaneous anomalies, and renal and urological anomalies, associated with an angioma of the lumbosacral region), and PELVIS syndrome (perineal haemangioma, external genital malformations, lipomyelomeningocoele, vesicorenal abnormalities, imperforate anus, and skin tag) {31631401; 32692048}.

Patients with reticular IH present with a reticulated erythematous patch with fine telangiectasias, sometimes including bright red pebbling. It arises more commonly in the lower half of the body and can be remarkably extensive {20855695; 28681397}.

Epidemiology

IH is more common in girls, twins, White infants, premature or low-birth-weight infants, and infants with a family history of IH {32384034}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is unknown, although uterine, placental, or local fetal hypoxia have been postulated to play a role, supported by the demonstration of GLUT1, IGF2, VEGF-A, and HIF1α in endothelial cells {26940670; 12032304; 21079070}. Germline genetic variants and familial clustering have been reported as risk factors {27940781}.

Macroscopic appearance

The usual sectioned appearance is of a homogeneous red or maroon mass in the proliferative phase, which becomes variegated red, white, and yellow in the involutional phase.

Histopathology

IH in the proliferative phase is a lobular capillary lesion involving the dermis, hypodermis, or both. The closely approximated capillaries have neatly configured round and (at times) inconspicuous lumina, and the endothelium and pericytes are uniform, plump without atypia, and mitotically active. Extension into the perineurium and nerves themselves is sometimes seen {7737662}. Involution brings about larger lumina, a flattened endothelium, a thickened basement membrane, karyorrhectic forms, and intervascular fibrous stroma. In the involuted phase, only a few clusters of capillaries with hyalinized walls or obliterated lumina, remodelling arteries and veins, and a fibrofatty stroma remain. Stromal mast cells are common in all phases. Lesional endothelial cells display intense and diffuse cytoplasmic immunopositivity for GLUT1, with some waning in the involutional and involuted phases {10665907}. Immunopositivity occupies the entire cytoplasmic extent as opposed to artefactual adluminal membranous staining resulting from adherent haemolysed erythrocytic elements.

In reticular IH, dispersed capillary vessels infiltrate the dermis and hypodermis, sometimes with lobular aggregation, and vasculature may be oriented predominantly perpendicularly to the epidermis.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: lobular capillary growth with endothelial cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for GLUT1.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Involution begins at about 10 months of age.
