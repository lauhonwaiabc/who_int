2.3.1.2 T- and NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

Definition

T- and NK-lymphoblastic leukaemias/lymphomas are neoplasms of haematopoietic progenitors committed to T- and NK-lineage differentiation, respectively. Currently, robust criteria for the diagnosis of NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma are not well established.

ICD-O coding

9837/3 T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

9837/3 Early T-precursor lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

9835/3 NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2A71 Precursor T-lymphoblastic neoplasms

2A7Z Precursor lymphoid neoplasms, unspecified

Related terminology

Acceptable: T-cell lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma.

Not recommended: precursor T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma, T-cell acute lymphoid leukaemia, T-lymphoblastic lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma; early T-precursor lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma; NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma

Localization

T-lymphoblastic leukaemia (T-ALL) manifests in the bone marrow and peripheral blood or as an extramedullary mass with bone marrow involvement. By definition, T-lymphoblastic lymphoma (T-LBL) involves extramedullary sites, most commonly the anterior mediastinum (but other sites can be involved), with little to no bone marrow involvement.

Clinical features

Patients with T-ALL can present with cytopenias, bone pain, and/or extramedullary masses, typically in the mediastinum. T-LBL most commonly manifests as an anterior mediastinal mass, sometimes with pleural effusions and respiratory distress. The combination of rapid growth in a restricted compartment requires urgent diagnosis.

Epidemiology

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is the most common paediatric malignancy, with an incidence in children (age: < 20 years) of approximately 3.5 new cases per 100 000 person-years [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program [Internet]. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2020. SEER Explorer. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/explorer/.]]. T-ALL accounts for 15% of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia cases, and it is more common in boys than in girls {23523389}. T-LBL accounts for the majority of lymphoblastic leukaemia cases, and it usually manifests as an anterior mediastinal mass in adolescent boys. NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia is rare, and only single cases or small case series have been reported {20146030; 21271777}.

Etiology

Nearly all cases of T-ALL have no identifiable cause. In contrast to the many genetic risk factors identified for B-lymphoblastic leukaemia, only one genetic risk factor, USP7, has been identified for T-ALL {30938820}.

Pathogenesis

The development of T-ALL, like other cancers, occurs secondary to cumulative disruptions of key cell regulatory pathways {21376230}. Certain regulatory pathways appear to play a greater role than others in T-ALL, because most dysregulations involve genes for self-renewal, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and/or differentiation, or the signalling pathways for those processes {32185137}.

T-ALL/LBL shows clonal rearrangements of the TR genes in most cases (except in the early T-precursor [ETP] subset, which are typically non-rearranged {23975177}). Around 50% of patients show translocations involving the TR gene loci at 14q11 (TRA and TRD) or 7q34 (TRB), as well as loci of transcription factor genes: TAL1, TAL2, LYL1, OLIG2, LMO1, LMO2, TLX1, TLX3, NKX2-1, NKX2-2, NKX2-5, HOXA genes, MYC, or MYB {28297628}. Gene expression profiling studies identified four subtypes of T-ALL with different patterns of rearrangements/mutations: immature ETP with MEF2C-activated rearrangements of HOXA gene activation fusions; TLX with TLX3 or HOXA activating gene fusions; proliferative with NKX2-1, NKX2-2, or TLX1 rearrangements; and TALLMO with LMO1, LMO2, or LMO3 rearrangements {12086890; 32722786}.

NOTCH1 activation is common, occurring either by activating mutations (in 50–60% of cases) or by the inactivation of FBXW7 (in 10–20% of cases) that promotes the degradation of NOTCH1 {28297628; 28115373}. Deletions of chromosome 9p21 including the CDKN2A or CDKN2B locus are common (~60% of cases) except in the ETP subset of cases {28115373; 22237106}. Mutations that activate the IL-7 signalling pathway are present in 50% of paediatric T-ALL patients and include mutations in IL7R as well as in JAK/STAT, RAS/MAPK, and PI3K/AKT pathway genes {27174491; 28115373}.

Mutations in the ETP subtype of T-ALL are similar to those seen in myeloid leukaemic stem cells, consisting of activating mutations in RAS pathway signalling genes (NRAS, KRAS, FLT3, IL7R, JAK3, JAK1, SH2B3, and BRAF), inactivating mutations in genes involved in haematopoietic development (GATA3, ETV6, RUNX1, IKZF1, and EP300), and histone-modifying genes (EZH2, SETD2, and SUZ12) {22237106; 22162831}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

With extensive involvement, the bone marrow and mass biopsies show a dense infiltrate of small to medium-sized mononuclear cells consistent with blasts. The infiltrate can show increased mitoses and tingible-body macrophages, imparting a starry-sky appearance. T-LBL with a variable number of eosinophils and myeloid proliferation in the marrow or leukocytosis raises the possible diagnosis of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and gene rearrangement {1599015; 26276769}.

Bone marrow

Leukaemia is defined as ≥ 25% bone marrow T-cell lymphoblasts (normal bone marrow does not have T-cell lymphoblasts). The term “lymphoma” is used when the tumour occurs as a mass with minimal (< 25%) blasts in the peripheral blood and bone marrow. Without a mass, there is no specified required blast percentage for leukaemia, but ≥ 20% is often used.

Immunophenotype

CD3 (cytoplasmic or surface) detected by flow cytometry is the gold standard for definitive T-cell lineage differentiation. CD3 by immunohistochemistry is less specific because it can be positive in activated and neoplastic NK cells. Expression of other T-cell antigens (CD7, CD2, CD4, CD5, or CD8) is not as specific because they can be expressed in non–T-cell lineages. The presence of T-cell lymphoblasts is established by the expression of immature markers such as TdT, CD34, increased CD99, or CD1a. TdT expression is detected in most cases (90%) of T-ALL and in some cases (30%) of T-LBL. However, TdT and CD34 double-negative T-cell lymphoblasts can be seen in as many as 10% of cases {10191766; 23702731}, raising the differential diagnosis with mature T-cell lymphoma. In these cases, the diagnosis of T-ALL/LBL is supported by the clinical presentation and cytological findings. Most cases are CD4/CD8 double-positive, but some can be double-negative, and others can be single-positive for CD4 or CD8. CD10, CD79a, CD13, CD33, and/or CD36 can be seen in some T-ALL cases {10874883; 12115880; 10191766}. CD56 expression is rarely present in T-ALL, raising the differential diagnosis with NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia.

The ETP subtype is defined immunophenotypically by (1) negative expression of CD1a, CD8, and myeloperoxidase; (2) expression of one or more of the myeloid markers CD34, KIT (CD117), HLA-DR, CD13, CD33, CD11b, and CD65; and (3) negative or dim CD5 expression, defined as expression in < 75% of the blasts and 1 log lower fluorescence intensity than mature T cells {19147408}. The term “near-ETP” is used for cases with the ETP immunophenotype but showing brighter or uniform CD5 expression. The expression of myeloid markers in ETP cases raises the differential diagnosis of T/myeloid mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia, which can be excluded based on the absence of myeloperoxidase expression, best evaluated by cytochemical studies.

NK-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma is rare, and it is difficult to diagnose because of its considerable immunophenotypic overlap with T-ALL/LBL, acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), and blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm. The diagnosis can be considered in a case that expresses CD56, CD2, and CD7, with or without cCD3, and without B-cell or myeloid markers. TR and IG genes should be wildtype, and blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm must be excluded {17916099}. Markers of NK-cell lineage are being identified {31477722; 16046525} but their applicability in neoplastic tumours remains unknown.

Cytology

Wright-stained peripheral blood smear, bone marrow smear, or body fluid cytospin shows lymphoblasts that are morphologically similar to B-cell lymphoblasts. In some cases, the T-cell lymphoblasts can show prominent nuclear irregularities and convolutions, or they can resemble mature lymphocytes.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

T-ALL/LBL shows monoclonal rearrangements of the TR genes in most cases, except in the ETP subset, in which the TR genes are typically not rearranged {23975177}. See Pathogenesis (above) for further details.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • Cytoplasmic or surface expression of CD3 indicating T-cell lineage differentiation and an aberrant immunophenotype (preferably detected by flow cytometry or by immunohistochemistry if not available)
  • The criteria for the diagnosis of early T-precursor acute lymphoblastic leukaemia are not met

Desirable:

  • Monoclonal rearrangement of TR genes

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Minimal residual disease during treatment is a strong negative prognostic factor in T-ALL {21719599; 31360884}. NOTCH1 activating mutations have been associated with an improved response to therapy and better outcomes in T-ALL {20861920; 16614245; 20944675}. Recent strategies combining genetic factors and minimal residual disease can further refine outcome predictions {29051182}. The ETP subtype and an absence of biallelic deletions of TRG were initially associated with worse outcomes {19147408; 2064408}, but outcomes are now similar with current risk-adapted therapy {24708207; 29519867}.

Outcomes in T-LBL have been associated with disease stage, although more recent data show similarly good outcomes in high- and low-risk patients, with no relation to disease stage or bone marrow minimal disseminated disease {32552472}. In T-LBL, cases with NOTCH1 activating mutations have a better prognosis, whereas those with loss of heterozygosity on chromosome 6q have a poor prognosis {23396305}.
