5.2.1.2 Lipoblastoma/lipoblastomatosis

Definition

Lipoblastoma is a benign neoplasm of embryonal white fat, which may be a localized or diffuse tumour with a tendency for local recurrence if incompletely excised.

ICD-O coding

8881/0 Lipoblastoma (localized)

8881/0 Lipoblastomatosis (diffuse)

ICD-11 coding

2E80.1 & XH8L55 Lipoblastoma & Lipoblastomatosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: fetal lipoma; fetal fat tumour; fetocellular lipoma; embryonal lipoma; congenital lipomatoid tumour; lipoblastic tumour of childhood.

Subtype(s)

Lipoblastoma (localized); lipoblastomatosis (diffuse)

Localization

The trunk and extremities are the most common sites {19738456; 28083971; 29035641}. Lipoblastoma may arise in the abdomen, mesentery, retroperitoneum, pelvis, inguinoscrotal or labial region, perineum, mediastinum, and head/neck (including the retropharynx and oral submucosa) {23664430; 29035641}. Lung, liver, heart, colon, and parotid gland lipoblastomas have also been described {11370265; 30955788; 16180007; 12112785; 11666056; 26819289; 29035641}.

Clinical features

Superficial, circumscribed lipoblastoma clinically simulates lipoma or a vascular malformation, whereas lipoblastomatosis originates in deep soft tissue and is more infiltrative. Truncal tumours may infiltrate into the thoracic cavity from extrathoracic locations, or into the spine, with reports of neuroforaminal or intraspinal invasion {24040598; 30723667; 27444294; 29492129; 29035641}. Because both types recur, the distinction between circumscribed and infiltrative forms is not clinically relevant. Lipoblastoma can compress adjacent structures and interfere with function, particularly in large abdominal tumours, mediastinal tumours, or cervical tumours {19738456; 20472310; 26214680; 27097392}. Colonic and mesenteric occurrences have been associated with intussusception and volvulus {28420321; 28034384}.

Ultrasonography can detect tumours with high fluid content and vascularity in superficial tissues {29035641}. MRI reveals a nodular mass with an intensity similar to or lower than that of lipoma or subcutaneous fat on T1-weighted images {21092875; 10663508; 10398794; 29035641; 33097826}.

A subset of patients with lipoblastoma have developmental delays or abnormalities, seizures, or familial lipomas; these are potentially related to larger chromosome 8q alterations that include the PLAG1 gene {19738456; 9331284; 20055849}.

Epidemiology

Lipoblastoma occurs predominantly in infancy and early childhood (75–90% of cases occur before the age of 3 years), less frequently in older children and adolescents, and rarely in adults {4353020; 19738456; 18269579; 18639685; 29035641}. There is a slight male predilection {19738456; 9331284; 8314236; 13583102; 29035641}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Lipoblastoma often exhibits a simple, pseudodiploid, or hyperdiploid karyotype that features a structural alteration (translocation, inversion, insertion, or ring chromosome) of 8q11-q13 leading to the rearrangement of PLAG1, a developmentally regulated zinc finger transcription factor gene, in about 60% of cases {18474299; 19738456; 11549588; 23751806; 26319758; 29897891; 32692992}. The most common numerical change is one or more extra copies of chromosome 8, with or without concurrent rearrangement of 8q11-q13 {18269579; 19264235}. PLAG1 fusion gene partners in lipoblastoma include HAS2 (8q24.13), COL1A2 (7q21.3), RAD51B (14q24.1), COL3A1 (2q32.2), RAB2A (8q12.1-q12.2), BOC (3q13.2), DDX6, KLF10, and KANSL1L {10987300; 15642402; 22488564; 23890983; 24700772; 26701195; 30857637; 32692992}. PLAG1 transcriptional upregulation leading to ectopic PLAG1 expression is the result of replacement of the PLAG1 promoter element by constitutively active promoter regions from one of the partner genes. PLAG1 rearrangements may be difficult to detect, requiring multiple types of testing. Lipoblastoma PLAG1 fusion partners are distinct from those reported in pleomorphic adenoma; however, RAD51B::PLAG1 has been identified in a subset of uterine myxoid leiomyosarcomas {30489320}. Alterations of HMGA2, an upstream regulatory activator of PLAG1, are also described in tumours with lipoblastoma morphology, including fusions of HMGA2 with EP400 and FGD6 {32692992}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lipoblastomas are typically 20–50 mm in diameter, although they can exceed 100 mm {4353020; 19738456; 9331284; 29035641}. The soft, lobulated masses are yellow, white, or tan and may display myxoid nodules, cystic spaces, or fat nodules separated by fine white fibrous trabeculae on the cut surface.

Histopathology

Lipoblastoma is a lobulated tumour comprising sheets of adipocytes showing a spectrum of maturation, ranging from primitive stellate or spindled mesenchymal cells to multivacuolated or small signet-ring lipoblasts through to mature adipocytes, separated by fibrovascular septa {7377463; 4353020; 19738456; 9331284}. Myxoid areas display a plexiform vascular pattern with primitive mesenchymal cells. The proportion of these cell types varies, and there is sometimes a zonal pattern of maturation, with more immature myxoid cells at the periphery (adjacent to fibrous septa) and mature adipocytes in the centre of the lobule. Myxoid regions can occasionally show pooling of matrix similar to myxoid liposarcoma. Mature areas resemble lipoma or fibrolipoma with sparse lipoblasts {19738456; 9331284; 18798558}. Tumour maturation is evidenced by reports of cases diagnosed by biopsy as lipoblastoma, in which later resection showed a fibrolipomatous appearance without lipoblasts {28111780}. Other histological findings include chondroid metaplasia, extramedullary haematopoiesis, chronic inflammation, and sparse multinucleated or floret cells {19738456; 9331284; 17116489}. Hyperchromasia and mild nuclear atypia may be observed, but mitoses are very rare and abnormal mitoses are absent {26943407}. Mast cells are common.

The adipocytes of lipoblastoma demonstrate reactivity for S100, CD56, and CD34 {23519855}. PLAG1 nuclear immunohistochemical staining can be useful, especially in less differentiated tumours, but its concordance with molecular studies is < 60% and sensitivity is about 50% {32692992}. Nuclear p16 expression, which is frequently seen in liposarcoma, is observed in only a small subset of lipoblastomas, without correlation to macroscopic appearance or clinical behaviour {26514741}. The primitive mesenchymal cells are often reactive for desmin {19738456}.

The differential diagnosis may be challenging and require molecular confirmation in lipoblastomas characterized by a predominant fibroblastic proliferation with collagen deposition, which are more frequent in older children and may mimic a low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma {32692992; 33097826}. Diffusely myxoid lipoblastomas with predominant primitive mesenchymal stellate cells may simulate a primitive mesenchymal myxoid tumour of infancy {32692992}.

Cytology

Cytological material from lipoblastoma varies according to the cellularity and the extent of the immature adipocyte, mature adipocyte, and myxoid matrix components {28902468; 30848523; 30322346}. Variation in adipocyte size is usually observed. Lipoblasts, fibrovascular network, and primitive spindled mesenchymal cells are also seen.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In selected cases, the finding of PLAG1 rearrangements / copy-number gain can support the diagnosis; HMGA2 alterations are less common {26319758; 32692992}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a lobulated mass in early childhood, composed of sheets of variably mature adipocytes separated by fibrovascular septa.

Desirable: molecular confirmation is recommended in older children and adults to rule out liposarcoma.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Lipoblastoma is benign, with an excellent prognosis after excision {4353020; 13583102; 19738456; 18639685; 29035641; 28083971}. Recurrence (rate: 13–46%) is usually due to incomplete excision. Recurrence can occur as late as 6 years after primary resection, supporting the practice of long-term follow-up with MRI or (for superficial tumours) ultrasound {29243188}. Locally aggressive behaviour with multiple recurrences has been described, but re-excision is effective {29035641}. There is no risk of metastasis.
