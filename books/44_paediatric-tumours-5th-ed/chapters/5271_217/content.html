5.2.7.1 Paediatric gastrointestinal stromal tumour

Definition

Gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) is a mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by differentiation towards the interstitial cells of Cajal; most GISTs in children are succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-deficient.

ICD-O coding

8936/3 Gastrointestinal stromal tumour

8936/3 Succinate dehydrogenase–deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour

ICD-11 coding

2B5B & XH9HQ1 Gastrointestinal stromal tumour, primary site & Gastrointestinal stromal sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: leiomyoblastoma.

Subtype(s)

Succinate dehydrogenase–deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumour

Localization

GISTs in paediatric patients are nearly exclusively located in the stomach {21997692; 21173220; 22804613}, with the antrum being the most common gastric site {16160481}. Rare GISTs in children arise in the small intestine.

Clinical features

Unlike adults, children with GIST are seldom asymptomatic. The most common presentations include symptoms related to gastric mucosal ulceration (e.g. acute and chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal pain, an abdominal mass, and weight loss) {16160481; 22248968}. Multifocal tumours may be seen at presentation.

Rare patients with germline KIT mutations (familial GIST syndrome) may present in childhood with cutaneous hyperpigmentation {11208730; 30280421}.

Epidemiology

Paediatric GIST is rare. Although the exact incidence is unknown, in institutional series 1.5–2.5% of reported GISTs are in children, most in their second decade of life, and there is a marked female predominance {16160481}.

Etiology

Most GISTs in children are sporadic.

SDH-deficient GISTs can be associated with the autosomal dominant Carney–Stratakis syndrome (GIST and paraganglioma) in the context of germline mutations in the SDH genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, or SDHD) {17667967; 19522824}, or with the non-familial Carney triad (GIST, paraganglioma, and pulmonary chondroma) {10377927}. Familial GIST syndrome may also be associated with germline KIT or PDGFRA mutations {11208730}.

Pathogenesis

Unlike GISTs in adults, GISTs in children rarely harbour KIT or PDGFRA mutations {18483389}; instead, alterations of the SDH complex are common {29413424}. Germline KIT mutations (familial GIST syndrome) are extremely rare {11208730}. SDH, a heterotetrameric enzyme in the Krebs cycle, is composed of four subunits (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD) each encoded by a nuclear gene {20226277}. Roughly 60% of all SDH-deficient GISTs harbour inactivating mutations in SDH genes, most of which are germline with a somatic second hit (copy loss or a second mutation); 40% have methylation of the SDHC promoter, leading to silencing of SDHC expression {27011036; 25540324}. SDHA is the most commonly mutated gene overall, with mutations identified in approximately one third of all SDH-deficient GISTs {23060355; 23282968; 23174939; 22955521}; however, although most SDHA mutations are germline with somatic loss of heterozygosity, SDHA-mutant GISTs usually appear to be sporadic and are seen more commonly in adults than in children. In contrast, the familial Carney–Stratakis syndrome is characterized by germline loss-of-function mutations in SDHB, SDHC, or SDHD {17667967; 17804857}. SDH-deficient GISTs in the non-familial Carney triad may have biallelic epigenetic inactivation of SDHC (epimutation) and/or SDH gene mutations {27011036; 25540324}; SDHC epimutation in tumours is more commonly (but not exclusively) seen in this syndrome.

SDH-deficient GISTs have a global genomic hypermethylation profile that is distinct from KIT- or PDGFRA-mutated GIST {23550148}, resulting in altered chromosomal topology through a loss of CTCF insulators, including those between enhancers and the oncogenes KIT and FGF4 {31666694}.

Macroscopic appearance

Children with GIST usually present with a mural mass varying in size from 15 to > 100 mm {21997692; 19499582}. Not uncommonly, multifocal disease (multiple discrete or adjacent tumour nodules in the stomach) may be seen. On sectioning, tumours are yellowish to pale-tan with focal areas of haemorrhage; cystic change or necrosis may also be seen. Surface ulceration is common {21997692}.

Histopathology

Microscopically, SDH-deficient GISTs are characteristically organized in a multinodular architecture separated by bands of muscularis propria, creating a plexiform pattern of mural involvement. The tumour cells are usually epithelioid or mixed epithelioid and spindle cell; the rare KIT-mutant GISTs in children may show spindle cell morphology. Cytoplasmic vacuolation may be seen. Unlike in conventional GISTs, lymphovascular invasion and lymph node metastases are common {21997692; 22804613}.

Immunophenotypically, most show strong and diffuse expression of KIT (CD117), which appears as cytoplasmic, membrane-associated, or sometimes perinuclear dot-like staining. The chloride-channel protein ANO1/DOG1 is an equally sensitive marker. CD34 is also commonly expressed; however, the muscle markers SMA and desmin are only rarely expressed, as is S100 {21997692}.

Loss of SDHB expression by immunohistochemistry is the diagnostic hallmark of SDH-deficient GIST {21997692; 20890271; 20305538}. Irrespective of the specific SDH gene affected, loss of SDHB protein in tumour cells is a sensitive and specific marker for SDH-complex inactivation. SDHA protein loss is specific for SDHA-mutant tumours {23060355; 23282968; 22955521}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Paediatric SDH-deficient GISTs lack the KIT and PDGFRA mutations associated with conventional GIST, as well as BRAF and NF1 mutations and other rare alterations {25540324}. Molecular subtyping to distinguish between SDH-mutant or SDHC-epimutant tumours should be pursued; in the former category, patients with germline SDH-mutant tumours should be referred for cancer predisposition risk assessment and clinical management and surveillance considerations, whereas patients with SDHC-epimutant tumours need screening only for other Carney triad tumours {27011036}. For the rare paediatric GISTs with retained SDHB expression, KIT and PDGFRA mutation testing is recommended.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an intramural, submucosal, or subserosal mass; epithelioid, spindle cell, or mixed morphology; KIT and/or DOG1 immunopositivity; SDHB loss in SDH-deficient GIST.

Desirable: SDH mutations, SDHC epimutation, or (rarely) KIT mutations.

Staging

Risk stratification applied to conventional GISTs in adults does not reliably predict outcome in SDH-deficient GISTs in children {27340750}.

Prognosis and prediction

Although mitotic activity, tumour size, and tumour rupture are useful prognostic parameters for GISTs, SDH-deficient GISTs are more unpredictable. Even with local recurrences, and metastasis to the liver, peritoneum, or locoregional lymph nodes in 15–20% of patients, SDH-deficient GISTs in general have an indolent clinical course and patients often survive for years or decades without specific treatment {16160481; 19935059}.

The tyrosine kinase inhibitors imatinib and sunitinib are largely ineffective for SDH-deficient GISTs {27011036}, although some patients have experienced clinical benefit from regorafenib {27371698}.
