2.1.0.0 Haematolymphoid disorders: Introduction

The structure of this chapter is based on the postulated normal haematolymphoid counterpart as defined morphologically and immunophenotypically, i.e. myeloid neoplasms (including mast cell neoplasia) and histiocytic/dendritic and lymphoid neoplasms. The neoplasms are divided into precursor or immature neoplasms (i.e. acute myeloid leukaemias [AMLs], lymphoblastic leukaemias/lymphomas, and acute leukaemias of ambiguous lineage) and mature neoplasms (myeloproliferative neoplasms, mastocytosis, myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, mature B-cell and T/NK-cell neoplasms, Hodgkin lymphoma, and histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms with myeloid origins). The chapter has been coordinated as far as possible with the forthcoming fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours {35732830; 35732831; 35732829}, but inevitably there will be some inconsistencies due to the fact that the publication of this paediatric tumours volume has preceded the finalization of the haematolymphoid tumours volume. This has precluded the implementation of some major alterations made in the new WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours compared with the 2017 WHO classification. However, some of these differences are of course due to the specific nature of paediatric versus adult haematolymphoid disorders.

Biological and genetic abnormalities are a defining criterion in some entities, whereas in others they are nonspecific but may contribute to the identification of prognostic categories or new and emerging clinicopathological entities (e.g. ALK-positive histiocytosis, recognized as a new entity in the forthcoming WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours). Still other entities should be recognized that are outside the purview of this classification system for tumours/neoplasms. Specifically, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), better termed “HLH disease”, will not be discussed, being a hyperinflammatory syndrome caused by a pathological immune response, with its activation or dysregulation due to genetic defects (i.e. in lymphocyte cytotoxicity, in the inflammasome, in immune regulation) and/or associated with other etiologies (i.e. infection, malignancy, rheumatic conditions). Thus it is neither a distinct pathological entity nor a single clinical entity and does not require the presence of haemophagocytosis {16937360; 31339233}. Its classification should no longer be simplified into primary (i.e. genetic) and secondary (i.e. due to an environmental trigger) because clinical phenotypes may overlap or fit poorly into these categories. Rather, the distinction should now focus on recognizing an HLH pattern that meets the syndromic definition {16937360; 31339233} and then determining whether the etiology is related to immune dysregulation (i.e. HLH disease) as opposed to HLH disease mimics (which may not require or benefit from immunosuppression) {31339233}.

Why focus on paediatric haematopathology? This chapter is not intended to be a new classification, but it is a change in the perspective, aiming to define the landscape of childhood, adolescent, and young adult (CAYA) haematolymphoid neoplasms, with an emphasis on the specific diagnostic and clinical features {12592323; 30729493; 25435117; 30729513}.

Leukaemias constitute one third of all malignancies in children, with 80% being lymphoblastic leukaemia, 15% AML, 3% myelodysplastic syndromes, and 2% chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML). Some myeloid malignancies are so rare in children that we refer the reader to the general WHO classification; these include chronic neutrophilic leukaemia, polycythaemia vera, primary myelofibrosis, essential thrombocythaemia, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia, and acute leukaemias of ambiguous lineage.

Predisposition conditions are being recognized in a growing number of children, especially those with myelodysplastic neoplasms, but also those with AML and lymphoblastic leukaemia. A dedicated section describes the unique myeloid leukaemias of Down syndrome, and an entire chapter is devoted to some of the most common predisposition syndromes.

In children, Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas are the third most common malignancies, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma accounting for approximately 7% of all malignancies in this age group and Hodgkin lymphoma for 6%.

Paediatric lymphomas have several peculiarities. The etiology and predisposing factors are not known, although a subset is associated with inborn errors of immunity congenital immunodeficiencies or EBV infections {25435117; 30729513}.

Most paediatric lymphomas are precursor B-cell or T-cell leukaemias/lymphomas, high-grade B-cell lymphomas, or (among mature T-cell lymphomas) ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma {30809797; 30729513}. Indolent lymphomas account for < 10% of mature B-cell lymphomas in the CAYA age group and include paediatric-type follicular lymphoma (now accepted as a definite entity in the WHO classification), paediatric nodal and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (which is considered a subtype of marginal zone lymphoma), and the provisional large B-cell lymphoma with IRF4 rearrangement {31187530}. Splenic and extranodal marginal zone lymphomas are very rare in the paediatric age group and do not differ markedly from those in adulthood.

High-grade lymphomas and Hodgkin lymphomas in the paediatric population have an excellent prognosis. However, despite the high curability rate, late effects of therapy represent the major cause of long-term overall survival decline in children {30729513}. Therefore in children, even more than in adults, an ongoing challenge lies in gaining a better understanding of biology, with the identification of prognostic parameters contributing to a balanced therapeutic approach.

New perspectives may come from immunotherapy using CD19-directed and/or CD22-directed monoclonal antibodies, conjugated antibodies, inhibitors of specific pathways, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells; however, clinical trials will be needed to determine the best future strategies {30729493}.

Finally, improving risk stratification by identifying patients potentially relapsing or not responding, as well as patients who could benefit from a less aggressive regimen, is the current challenge.
