4.4.1.2 Retinoblastoma

Definition

Retinoblastoma is a malignant neoplasm derived from retinal precursor cells that develops in early childhood.

ICD-O coding

9510/3 Retinoblastoma

9510/3 Unilateral retinoblastoma

9510/3 Bilateral retinoblastoma

9510/3 Trilateral retinoblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2D02.2 Retinoblastoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: glioma retinae.

Subtype(s)

Unilateral retinoblastoma (non-heritable or heritable); bilateral retinoblastoma (heritable); trilateral retinoblastoma (heritable; associated with primary intracranial tumour [pineoblastoma or suprasellar tumour])

Localization

Retinoblastoma is an intraocular tumour originating in the neurosensory retina, commonly with vitreous and subretinal seeding. Extension into the outer coats of the eye or orbit may occur, often associated with recurrence or metastasis.

Clinical features

Leukocoria (white pupillary reflex) and squint, often noted by parents, are the most common clinical presenting signs {9544909}. Testing for leukocoria by dimming the ambient lighting is a more reliable screening method than other types of tests {26019774}. Other causes of leukocoria, such as Coats disease, giant retinal astrocytic hamartoma, cataract, infections (e.g. toxocariasis), persistent fetal vasculature, familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, and retinopathy of prematurity, are in the differential diagnosis, as well as visualizing a normal optic nerve. Less common presentations include iris heterochromia (from iris neovascularization), glaucoma with buphthalmos, pseudohypopyon, hyphaema, and aseptic orbital cellulitis. Extraocular disease extension into the orbit may manifest with proptosis or a large orbital mass, ulcerating through the conjunctiva and eyelids {31173880; 23960917}.

Suspicion of retinoblastoma in the clinical setting requires examination of the eye under anaesthesia to record findings for the accurate diagnosis and classification of the tumour(s). Fundus drawing and retinal photography is needed to chart the number and location of tumours and associated features. Ocular ultrasound reveals a mass arising from the retina, with foci of calcification. The clinical features dictate the tumour grouping for estimating treatment success and prognosis (see below).

Retinoblastoma is a white mass that can be calcified or non-calcified. The tumour(s) may grow predominantly into the vitreous (endophytic retinoblastoma), and floating small tumour seeds may form that appear as dust, spheres, or clouds by ophthalmic examination. The type of seeding is associated with prognosis for successful resolution after intraocular treatment {25795478}. Vitreous seeding was associated with a poor prognosis for eye preservation until the advent of a safety-enhanced technique for intraocular chemotherapy {22694968}. If the tumours grow from the retina into the subretinal space, there is often a retinal detachment associated with subretinal tumour seeds. The vessels of the retina are seen over the mass and feed the tumour, unlike in Coats disease, where there is telangiectasia with areas of capillary dropout. A third pattern of retinoblastoma is the diffuse infiltrating type, a rare pattern that has a predilection for children aged > 5 years {18962920}.

Epidemiology

Retinoblastoma is by far the most common paediatric intraocular tumour. This embryonal tumour of the retina represents < 3% of all malignancies occurring in patients aged < 15 years {28410997}. More than 90% of cases develop before 5 years of age, and exceedingly few are diagnosed in patients aged > 15 years {28410997}. The incidence rate is approximately 5 cases per 1 million person-years {28410997}, with the highest rate in infancy (< 1 year of age) {16919773}.

Retinoblastoma may occur in one or both eyes. Bilateral tumours represent 40% of all cases {1637670}. All bilateral cases and about 15% of unilateral cases are heritable {5279523}. More than 60% of the heritable tumours result from de novo mutations {1637670}. The median age at diagnosis is 24 months for unilateral retinoblastoma, in contrast to 9 months for bilateral retinoblastoma (see section Retinoblastoma syndrome) {27382102}. Children with the heritable form are at risk of a third tumour, a Ewing sarcoma of the brain (pineoblastoma) {28674118}; the risk is probably increased by external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) {24238207}. In 1.4% of retinoblastoma cases MYCN is amplified in the absence of RB1 mutation {23498719}.

There is no sex predilection {32432168; 16919773}. Five-year survival is > 95% in high-income countries {24314616}, although survivors of the heritable form are subject to an increased risk of second primary tumours {23674091}. In lower-income countries, the overall survival rate is < 50% {18324630}, mostly due to late diagnosis {32105305}.

Etiology

Retinoblastoma tumorigenesis is nearly always initiated by biallelic inactivation of the RB1 tumour suppressor gene {5279523; 2877398; 6694706; 15264269}. Fewer than 2% of retinoblastoma tumours are associated not with RB1 gene sequence variants but with MYCN amplifications. These have specific histopathological features and occur as unilateral tumours {23498719}.

In non-heritable (sporadic) retinoblastoma, mutations of both RB1 alleles occur in the somatic cells, and children usually develop unilateral retinoblastoma. In heritable retinoblastoma, the first pathogenetic variant is inherited from one parental germ cell (heterozygous carrier) or is the result of a mutation that occurred during early embryonal development (mutational mosaicism). About 10% of children with heritable retinoblastoma have familial retinoblastoma defined by having relatives with retinoblastoma. Retinoblastoma without a family history of it is referred to as sporadic retinoblastoma.

Pathogenesis

Retinoblastoma originates from retinal precursor cells, most probably of cone origin {25252974}. Pathogenic RB1 gene variants (mutations) are heterogeneous and include single base substitution, large deletions, hypermethylation of the promoter region, and chromothripsis {15264269; 24509483}. Most RB1 variants lead to the complete loss of RB1 (pRb) or expression of a truncated form. When phosphorylated, RB1 controls entry into the cell cycle, among other functions {27401552}. RB1 inactivation alone is not enough for tumorigenesis and further genetic changes are required to result in retinoblastoma formation {10197588}. Recurrent somatic genetic mutations beyond RB1 are rare but have been described {27126562; 22237022}. Conventional and molecular cytogenetic analyses show that most retinoblastomas have one or more additional recurrent aberrations (gains of 1q or 6p, loss of 16q, or MYCN gene amplification) {17437278; 28211617}. A minority of retinoblastomas have, instead of RB1 inactivation, an amplification of the MYCN oncogene (28–121 DNA copies); these cases typically arise in early infancy {23498719}.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumour has a tan-white, fluffy appearance and is encephaloid when solid. It arises from the retina, and when invading the vitreous it shows endophytic growth and may have vitreous seeding (showing as dust, spheres, or a cloud) {28528011}. Tumour seeds may involve the anterior chamber. Posteriorly, the seeds may land on the retina or optic nerve head and form new tumours. Exophytic retinoblastomas grow into the subretinal space from the retina, producing a serous retinal detachment, pushing the retina into the posterior surface of the lens, and producing anterior displacement of the lens-iris with a secondary closed angle. Subretinal tumour deposits may invade the retinal pigment epithelium and through the Bruch membrane into the choroid. An indeterminate growth pattern may be seen in large tumours. Rarely, retinoblastomas grow diffusely, thickening the retina, in a growth pattern that can be difficult to recognize because a distinct tumour is not formed {1800925}.

Many tumours have areas of tan-yellow necrosis alternating with small bright-white specks of dystrophic calcification. In tumours that have been previously treated, the calcification is chunky and may be the main feature of the mass, or it may be associated with gliotic scars (tissue decalcification is recommended for histology). The tumour cells may grow over the pars plana and zonules into the anterior chamber, giving a pseudoinflammatory appearance. These eyes show no signs of inflammation. In septic orbital cellulitis presentations, the eyes are often soft (hypotonic) and have tan-red or orange friable and extensible necrotic tumours with blood and pieces of pigmented tissue (retinal pigment epithelium) {1928248}. There is usually neovascularization of the iris associated with angle closure (glaucoma). Glaucoma or pressure of the tumour on the central retinal vessels can cause infarction with extensive necrosis of the tumour and/or intraocular structures. In large tumours, there may be involvement of anterior segment structures or massive invasion of the posterior uvea (> 3 mm) seen as tan-white solid infiltrates. In the late stages of the disease, the tumour destroys the sclera and invades the orbit, forming one or more orbital mass(es). If the optic nerve is massively involved, the orbital portion of the nerve may be enlarged.

Adequate macroscopic examination and histopathological sampling are essential to assess high-risk features in retinoblastoma. The eye should be oriented and the optic nerve margin obtained before opening the eye (to avoid contamination by the tumour). The eye is cut into three pieces in a posterior to anterior cut to obtain the central pupil–optic nerve section, which includes the entire thickness of the optic nerve (not cutting into the nerve), the centre of the eye, the lens, and the pupillary region. The other two pieces are the caps (calottes) resulting from this sectioning, and they are further sectioned (anteroposteriorly) in a bread-slicing fashion to increase the choroidal surface examined microscopically. The entire eye is submitted for histological examination in four cassettes (often a deeper cassette is necessary to fit the eye sections): (1) the pupil–optic nerve section, (2 and 3) the calottes, and (4) the optic nerve margin. Histological sectioning of the pupil–optic nerve segment includes early sections before the centre of the optic nerve is reached, and then sections that show the centre of the optic nerve connected to the retina/tumour to evaluate invasion. Incomplete sections may alter interpretation, preventing adequate adjuvant treatment {19653709}.

Histopathology

Retinoblastoma is composed of primitive neuroblastic cells with basophilic nuclei and scant cytoplasm, with many mitoses, apoptoses, and geographical necrosis of the tumour away from the blood supply (90–110 µm away from the vessels) {2211000}. The viable tumour forms basophilic sleeves and cuffs that are tightly associated with the vessels, with eosinophilic necrotic tumour in between the viable tumour sleeves. The necrotic tumour may contain foci of dystrophic calcification. DNA released from the necrotic tumour may form basophilic deposits in basement membranes (vessels, lens capsule, anterior chamber angle) and as globules in the subretinal fluid {7006579; 3684233}.

Retinoblastomas may be poorly differentiated, moderately differentiated (rosettes), or well differentiated (fleurettes). Differentiation is seen in younger children {19653710}. Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes with central lumina are characteristic of retinoblastoma and resemble retinal differentiation, but they may also be seen in medulloepithelioma. Homer Wright rosettes, seen in other neuroendocrine tumours (e.g. neuroblastoma), represent neuroblastic differentiation; their lumina are filled by cytoplasmic tangles (neuropil) {5357720}. In 15–20% of retinoblastomas there are foci of photoreceptor differentiation (fleurettes, resembling bouquets of flowers) with elongated cells, bland nuclei, powdery chromatin, and no mitosis or apoptosis, arranged in short rows resembling photoreceptors {4190798; 19653710; 18211953}. At low power, these areas appear eosinophilic and lack necrosis. A retinocytoma or retinocytoma is a tumour that is entirely composed of photoreceptor differentiation and considered to be a benign precursor or derivative of retinoblastoma {18211953}. Calcification is present in the viable tumour. After chemotherapy, the retinoblastoma may show retinocytoma-like differentiation. Varying degrees of anaplasia are found in retinoblastoma; severe anaplasia is a risk factor for metastasis {25528954}. These tumours are poorly differentiated, with large hyperchromatic cells, pleomorphic nuclei (angular, rhomboid, or fusiform), cell wrapping, and numerous mitotic figures.

Retinoblastoma may invade the space beneath the retinal pigment epithelium, but if the Bruch membrane is still intact, the tumour is considered intraretinal. If the tumour breaks through the Bruch membrane into the choroidal stroma, it has access to vessels that serve as a route for haematogenous metastasis. In tumours with extensive choroidal invasion (≥ 3 mm in any dimension), the tumour may reach the sclera and track the emissary vessels and nerves exiting the eye to invade orbital tissues (see Prognosis and prediction, below) {31539297}. Retinoblastoma characteristically invades the optic nerve, and the tumour may reach the brain via neural stroma or cerebrospinal fluid. If the invasive tumour passes the lamina cribrosa, there is an increased possibility of CNS recurrence (see Prognosis and prediction). Invasion of the anterior segment structures may be seen. Iris neovascularization and occlusive anterior synechiae of the chamber angle are associated with large tumours.

Retinoblastoma is a friable tumour that may disperse during macroscopic examination or sectioning, mimicking histological tumour involvement of the choroid, meninges, or even extraocular tissues. However, this has an admixture of necrotic and viable tumour cells that form irregular aggregates (instead of infiltrative sheets of viable cells) that help to separate this artefact from true invasion.

Immunohistochemistry is seldom used in retinoblastoma but, if there is a need to exclude tumour invasion in the optic nerve or to differentiate choroidal inflammation or extramedullary haematopoiesis from choroidal tumour invasion, synaptophysin or NSE antibodies highlight the tumour cells. Frequently (in ~87% of tumours), retinoblastoma does not express RB1 (pRB), so immunostaining for RB1 protein will be negative in these tumours.

The pathology report in enucleated eyes with retinoblastoma must include confirmation of the diagnosis; degree of differentiation; presence or absence of calcification; percentage of tumour necrosis; tumour invasion status in the choroid (and if present, the depth in millimetres) and in the optic nerve (location and depth of invasion in millimetres, measured either from the inner limiting membrane of the optic nerve or from the Bruch membrane to the deepest point of invasion at the centre of the optic nerve); status of the optic nerve margin; whether the tumour in the optic nerve encroaches on the meninges; the site of extraocular extension if present; and other findings such neovascularization of the iris, extensive necrosis of intraocular structures, or treatment effect {19653709}.

Cytology

Retinoblastoma should not be biopsied routinely because it may produce extraocular recurrence and metastasis. These cytological samples often have pitfalls (sampling of retinal neurons, scant material, and others) that may be misleading. Consultation with a trained ophthalmic cytopathologist is recommended if this is considered to be necessary.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In about 40% of children with retinoblastoma, there are predisposing mutations in the RB1 gene. Tumour tissue (when available) and blood should be sent for genetic/molecular analysis. Genetic counselling of family members and screening of children at risk of developing retinoblastoma is recommended {29056300}.

Molecular identification of minimal residual disease in cerebrospinal fluid or blood may be performed to predict recurrence. Anterior chamber sampling has identified tumoural genomic material that may help in predicting treatment success or identifying high-risk tumours {32434859} Approximately 2% of patients with unilateral retinoblastoma have amplification of MYCN; these retinoblastomas have distinctive histopathological features {23498719}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a patient aged < 5 years; an intraocular tumour (unilateral or bilateral); histology showing a small blue round cell tumour with frequent mitoses, rosettes, and necrosis.

Desirable: germline alteration of the RB1 gene.

Staging

Each eye is separately classified according to the International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB), which groups eyes according to intraocular tumour size, location, extent of serous fluid, and the presence of vitreous or subretinal seeding {15763190}.

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) also developed a clinical TNM classification system for retinoblastoma, and in the eighth edition hereditary factors were added, making it the clinical TNMH scheme (see section Retinoblastoma syndrome) [[Amin MB, Edge S, Greene F, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017.]]. It includes the clinical ocular findings of cellulitis, extraocular extension (clinical or radiographic evidence), and proptosis. The AJCC classification evaluates the extent of the intraocular and extraocular tumour.

The International Retinoblastoma Staging System (IRSS) {15763190} also stages the patient based on tumour extension as follows: eye salvage with no disease dissemination; enucleation with complete resection; orbital, systemic, and/or CNS disease.

MRI of the orbits and brain is performed on each child to exclude extraocular extension and brain involvement {26690907}. Pineoblastoma can develop in heritable retinoblastoma {25126964}. MRI is still not sensitive enough, and histopathology remains the gold standard for assessing high-risk features after enucleation {28302322; 25841975}. The pathological stage pT2 is applied to tumours showing focal local intraocular invasion; pT3 tumours show significant intraocular invasion, and pT4 tumours show extraocular extension.

Prognosis and prediction

Early diagnosis of retinoblastoma (a small tumour without significant choroidal invasion or extraocular extension) has the best outcome when treated, with a cure rate of > 95% {28399338}.

Treatment

The treatment of retinoblastoma is directed first to preserve the child’s life; then to preserve the eye; then to preserve vision; and finally to minimize the risk of late effects, including secondary cancer. Treatment decisions are based on tumour characteristics, the laterality and severity of the disease, the patient’s age, known constitutional RB1 genetic abnormality, and potential toxicity {26378747}. For advanced unilateral retinoblastoma in eyes with no visual potential, enucleation provides the possibility of complete cure and assessment of histopathological risk features {28659391}.

A variety of eye-preserving therapies are available, especially for preserving at least one eye in bilateral retinoblastoma, but also for treating unilateral retinoblastoma. Treatments include focal therapies (laser therapy, cryotherapy, brachytherapy) and different types of chemotherapy regimens {28589646; 31122912}. Delivery of chemotherapy may be systemic (neoadjuvant treatment or concomitant with local treatments) {15747743; 29703164}, intra-arterial {29791475; 18342944}, or intravitreal {22694968; 26630319}. Treatment regimens vary among retinoblastoma centres, with each achieving similar efficacy. Most regimens include carboplatin and at most two other drugs (mostly vincristine and etoposide), based on recommendations by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and the European Retinoblastoma Group (EURbG) (https://www.eurbg.org). Systemic adjuvant chemotherapy is indicated in children undergoing enucleation with histopathological risk factors {23460706; 22084213}. Extraocular retinoblastoma requires multimodal therapy regimens (systemic chemotherapy plus high-dose chemotherapy plus autologous stem cell transplant) {12799253; 2160290} and radiotherapy to the involved sites. Metastatic retinoblastoma to the CNS has an especially poor prognosis {20486181; 20486171}.

Clinical prognostic factors

Extraocular extension of the tumour carries an adverse prognosis for systemic metastasis. This is usually associated with delayed diagnosis, atypical presentation, or iatrogenic metastases due to intraocular surgery. Neovascular glaucoma and buphthalmos are associated with a high risk of metastasis {31539297}.

Histopathological prognostic factors

Histopathology is the most powerful tool for estimating metastatic risk. Retinoblastoma with ≥ 3 mm of choroidal invasion or with extraocular invasion reaches vessels and results in lymph node or systemic metastasis. Retinoblastoma with significant invasion of the optic nerve (postlaminar, meningeal, or at the optic nerve resection margin) may result in local recurrence in the orbit, and in CNS recurrence, which carries a dismal prognosis for survival {31539297; 19653709}. The COG prospective trial ARET0332 evaluated histopathological features that may predict metastasis and examined the role of adjuvant chemotherapy in unilateral retinoblastoma. The most significant predictive factor for recurrence and death from disease was extensive retrolaminar optic nerve invasion concomitant with a peripapillary location and > 3 mm choroidal invasion. Anterior chamber invasion alone showed no predictive value for metastasis {31539297}.

Other histopathological factors associated with metastasis include iris neovascularization and extensively necrotic retinoblastoma (necrosis of > 90% of the tumour) {17076529}. Severe anaplasia is a potential risk factor for metastasis {25528954}.

Genetic and molecular factors

For the first time, in the eighth edition of the AJCC classification, the patient’s genetic status (H, for “heritable trait”) has been added, to address the prognosis of heritable retinoblastoma (H1) versus sporadic retinoblastoma (H0). In patients with H1 tumours, closer follow-up is recommended to exclude the development of new tumours including bilateral retinoblastoma and secondary malignancies (see section Retinoblastoma syndrome). The specific mutation found in the intraocular tumour may be used to confirm metastasis and exclude a second primary malignancy {27318443}. Molecular identification of minimal disease in cerebrospinal fluid or blood may aid in predicting recurrence and possibly implementing aggressive treatment {25928893}. Chromosome 6p amplification in aqueous humour cell-free DNA has shown to be a prognostic biomarker for ocular survival in retinoblastoma {32434859}.
