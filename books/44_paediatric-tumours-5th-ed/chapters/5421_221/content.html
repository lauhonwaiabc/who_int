5.4.2.1 Osteochondroma

Definition

Osteochondroma is a benign cartilaginous neoplasm arising on the surface of bone and consisting of a bony projection covered by a cartilage cap. The cortex and medulla of the osteochondroma are continuous with those of the underlying bone.

ICD-O coding

9210/0 Osteochondroma

ICD-11 coding

2E83.Y & XH5Y87 Other specified benign osteogenic tumours & Osteochondroma

LD24.20 Multiple osteochondromas

Related terminology

Not recommended: osteocartilaginous exostosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most common sites include the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus. The craniofacial bones are generally not affected, except for the mandibular condyle.

Clinical features

Osteochondroma can be solitary or multiple as part of the hereditary multiple osteochondromas syndrome. They develop and increase in size during the first decade of life, and they stop growing at around puberty, when the growth plates close. Osteochondromas are usually found incidentally. Symptoms are often size- and site-related {7702095}.

Radiographs show a bony mass, sessile or pedunculated, located at the diaphyseal-metaphyseal interface. MRI is useful to show the corticomedullary continuity and confirm the thickness of the cap, which should be homogeneous and < 20 mm {10671608; 20392983}. A bulky cap may be present in children, but it should decrease with ageing.

Epidemiology

Osteochondroma is one of the most common bone tumours. The average age of patients with solitary osteochondroma is 18 years, and there is a male predominance [[Picci P, Manfrini M, Fabbri N, et al., editors. Atlas of musculoskeletal tumors and tumorlike lesions. The Rizzoli Case Archive. Cham (Switzerland): Springer International Publishing; 2014.]]. Approximately 15% of patients with osteochondroma have multiple lesions characteristic of the autosomal dominant hereditary multiple osteochondromas syndrome.

Etiology

Osteochondromas are caused by biallelic inactivation of the EXT1 or EXT2 gene. In patients with multiple osteochondromas, EXT1 or EXT2 heterozygous germline alterations are combined with somatic loss of the remaining wildtype allele within the cartilage cap {10441575; 20080592; 11169766; 20813973; 20418910}. In solitary osteochondromas, homozygous deletions of EXT1 can be found in 80% of tumours {17341731; 20418910}.

Pathogenesis

The cell of origin is considered to be either a chondrocyte within the growth plate {20080592} or a progenitor cell from the perichondrium {23458899}. Most of the EXT1 or EXT2 mutations result in a truncated or non-functional protein {19810120}; EXT1 and EXT2 are glycosyltransferases involved in heparan sulfate biosynthesis and the cap is composed of wildtype and mutated cells {20534475; 20080592; 21506131}. These proteoglycans are key modulators of endochondral ossification {16072037}: loss of heparan sulfate may give a proliferative advantage to the mutated cells, leading to loss of polarity {18654627; 20421870}. Because defective heparan sulfate affects multiple signalling pathways {15177029; 29277722; 29545125}, this causes EXT-mutated cells to grow out of the bone and recruit normal cells to form an osteochondroma.

Macroscopic appearance

Osteochondroma may be sessile or pedunculated {24706308}. The size of the glassy bluish-white cartilaginous cap is important and should be documented. It should be measured perpendicular to the bone–cartilage interface, at its thickest portion. The cartilage cap is usually thin (and thickness decreases with age). A thick (> 20 mm) and irregular cap may be indicative of progression. Within the underlying stalk, a medullary cavity can be recognized.

Histopathology

The tumour has three layers: perichondrium, cartilage, and bone. The perichondrium is continuous with the periosteum of the underlying bone. The cartilage cap mimics the growth plate, with disorganized endochondral ossification: the superficial chondrocytes are clustered, whereas the ones close to the bone interface become larger, like the hypertrophic cells of the growth plate. In children, the cartilaginous cap can be highly cellular, and cellularity decreases with age, sometimes with extensive calcification. Binucleated cells, focal necrosis, and degenerative cystic/mucoid changes can be seen {22555180}. Mitoses are absent. The distinction between osteochondroma and peripheral chondrosarcoma is difficult and should be made by a multidisciplinary team, taking into account the radiology (especially the size of the cap) and the patient’s age.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are not required for the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: compatible imaging; a cartilaginous cap (< 20 mm) with a growth plate–like architecture; underlying stalk, the cortex and medulla of which are continuous with underlying bone.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Excision is curative, although recurrence is seen after incomplete removal. Multiple recurrences should raise suspicion for progression to malignancy (estimated to occur in 1% of solitary osteochondromas and 5% of multiple osteochondromas) {7702095; 9272707; 8027127}.
