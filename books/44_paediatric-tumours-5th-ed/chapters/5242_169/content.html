5.2.4.2 Glomus tumour and glomuvenous malformation

Definition

Glomus tumour (GT) is a mesenchymal neoplasm composed of cells resembling the perivascular modified smooth muscle cells of the normal glomus body. Glomuvenous malformation (GVM), previously considered a subtype of GT, is best classified as a peculiar type of venous malformation with glomus cells in the walls of the malformed vessels.

Related terminology

Not recommended: for glomus tumour (the following terms are previous names for lesions now referred to as glomuvenous malformation): glomangioma; glomangiomyoma; glomangiomatosis; for malignant glomus tumour: glomangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Classic glomus tumour; symplastic glomus tumour; glomus tumour of uncertain malignant potential; malignant glomus tumour

Localization

Classic GTs (cGTs) typically affect the distal extremities, particularly the subungual region, but tumours have been reported in different superficial locations, deep soft tissues, and viscera {11145243; 11859201; 10935651; 28438616; 3036410}. Malignant GTs (mGTs) tend to be large (> 20 mm) and deeply located {11145243; 18422692}.

GVMs are almost always limited to the skin and subcutaneous adipose tissue and rarely involve the mucosa or muscle {15313813}.

Clinical features

cGTs are benign, but well-documented cases of mGT have been reported in adults and (rarely) in children {28294349; 11696178}. Cutaneous cGTs manifest as single small blue-red nodules with paroxysmal pain, which is elicited by cold exposure or even by minor tactile stimulation. In deeply located tumours, symptoms are related to compression of surrounding structures.

GVMs are extremely tender and non-compressible cutaneous lesions of variable size; usually they have a cobblestone-like surface. Spontaneous or trauma-evoked paroxysms of pain can occur. GVMs are frequently inherited and multiple {23801931}. Multiple GVMs were previously referred to as glomangiomatosis {12376927}. Plaque-type GVMs are congenital, show extensive distribution, and are difficult to diagnose and treat {16847206}. In newborns with large, thoracic, plaque-type GVMs, pleural effusion and ascites may have already occurred in utero {16847206}.

Epidemiology

cGTs and mGTs are very rare in children. Most paediatric lesions containing glomus cells are GVMs. cGTs and GVMs have no sex predilection, except for subungual cGTs, which are more common in women {8732413}.

Etiology

GTs are usually sporadic. An association between digital GT and neurofibromatosis has been reported, with a biallelic inactivation of NF1 as a potential mechanism of tumour formation {19738042; 22250039}.

In contrast, GVMs can either be sporadic or (most commonly) have autosomal dominant inheritance. GVMs are caused by mutations in GLMN, located on chromosome 1p22.1 {11845407}.

Pathogenesis

GTs harbour recurrent NOTCH-family gene rearrangements. MIR143 fusions with NOTCH1, NOTCH2, or NOTCH3 are present in > 50% of cases {23999936}. A low rate of BRAF and KRAS mutations has been detected in sporadic soft tissue GTs {22317887}. BRAF mutations have been associated with malignant histology {28834810}.

GVMs are caused by loss-of-function mutations in GLMN. The expressivity is variable, and penetrance is incomplete. Somatic second hits explain the wide phenotypic variability of GVMs in patients with inherited GVMs. The most common second hit appears to be an acquired uniparental isodisomy of chromosome 1p {11845407; 23801931; 23375657}. Superimposed mosaic (linear or plaque-type) lesions can appear in this setting {16847206}.

Macroscopic appearance

cGTs are circumscribed nodules with a solid or cystic haemorrhagic surface, usually measuring < 10 mm. GVMs are composed of dilated vascular channels, containing blood, that tend to aggregate and form nodules.

Histopathology

cGTs are composed of sheets, nests, and trabeculae of monomorphic glomus cells in a hyalinized or myxoid stroma with branching capillary-sized vessels. Symplastic GTs display prominent nuclear pleomorphism, probably representing a degenerative phenomenon, but have very low mitotic activity.

mGTs can be either spindle cell type or round cell type. Features frequently seen in mGT include atypical mitotic figures, increased mitotic activity, high cellularity, necrosis, infiltrative growth, and moderate to high nuclear grade {11145243; 28834810}.

GVMs show vein-like dysplastic channels of variable size surrounded by one or more layers of glomus cells. Some of the vessels can be invested by smooth muscle of variable thickness. GVMs with smooth muscle cells have sometimes been designated as glomangiomyomas {12383095}. Organizing thrombi and phleboliths can be seen.

Glomus cells in cGTs and GVMs stain positively for α-SMA, MSA, h-caldesmon, and vimentin. Abundant pericellular collagen IV is also present.

Cytology

FNAB of cGT shows clusters of uniform round cells with homogeneous granular chromatin, scant cytoplasm, and indistinct cell borders in a haemorrhagic, focally myxoid background {11726106; 21187877}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Most GTs are diagnosed by morphology and immunohistochemistry. GLMN mutations can be detected in the lesional tissue of GVMs. In the extremely rare event of a GT with atypical or malignant features arising in children, detection of NOTCH gene rearrangements and BRAF mutations can be helpful.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for cGT: a solid proliferation of monomorphic glomus cells; for GVM: dilated vein-like channels surrounded by one or more layers of glomus cells.

Desirable: expression of SMA, MSA, and h-caldesmon by the glomus cells; pericellular collagen IV deposition.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

cGTs and symplastic GTs are benign, and most cases are cured by simple excision. mGTs have an increased risk of recurrence and metastatic potential, but they are extremely rare in paediatric patients. GTs that do not meet the minimum definition of mGT but display some atypical features should be regarded as GT of uncertain malignant potential and warrant follow-up {11145243}.

GVMs are benign, but new lesions can appear in patients with germline mutation of GLMN.
