5.4.2.5 Chondromyxoid fibroma

Definition

Chondromyxoid fibroma is a benign lobulated neoplasm with a zonal architecture composed of chondroid, myxoid, and myofibroblastic areas.

ICD-O coding

9241/0 Chondromyxoid fibroma

ICD-11 coding

2E82.Z & XH89S0 Benign chondrogenic tumours, site unspecified & Chondromyxoid fibroma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Chondromyxoid fibroma most frequently arises in the long bones, with preferential involvement of the proximal tibia and distal femur. Approximately 25% of cases occur in the flat bones, mainly the ilium. When involving bones of the feet, chondromyxoid fibroma often involves the metatarsals. Other sites of involvement include the craniofacial bones, ribs, vertebrae, and short tubular bones of the hand {29324473}.

Clinical features

Pain, usually mild, is the most common symptom and is sometimes present for several years {9596266}. Swelling is infrequent and is most commonly observed in the bones of the hands and feet.

Imaging

Radiographically, chondromyxoid fibroma in a long bone is typically a well-demarcated, oval-shaped, lobulated, osteolytic, eccentric, and metaphyseally located lesion with attenuation and expansion of the cortex. Lesions of the rib or ilium may be an incidental finding {9596266}. Less frequently, the tumour is juxtacortical {18059222}. The longitudinal axis of the lesion corresponds to that of the involved bone, and the average size is 30 mm (range: 10–100 mm). In the small bones, fusiform expansion of the entire bone is typical. Approximately 10% of cases may show focal calcified matrix, more often detectable with CT. There may be cortical destruction, but it is contained by the periosteum {19700940; 9596266}. On MRI, chondromyxoid fibroma has an intermediate to high signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences, with strong homogeneous or peripheral contrast enhancement {27226218}. Reactive high-signal-intensity changes may be present on fluid-sensitive sequences.

Epidemiology

Chondromyxoid fibroma is a rare tumour, accounting for approximately 2.3% of cartilaginous tumours in children {14569799; 19700940; 18157043}. Although it may affect a wide age range (first to seventh decades of life), it occurs most often in the second and third decades of life (80% in patients aged < 36 years), with a slight male predominance {6337162; 9596266}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Recombination of GRM1 with several 5′ partner genes through promoter swapping and gene fusion events leading to the upregulated expression of transcripts encompassing the entire GRM1 coding region, with or without non-coding 5′ partner remnants, is considered the driver event for chondromyxoid fibroma development {24658000}. These canonical gene fusions were shown to be caused by complex rearrangement processes – predominantly chromoplexy, but also chromothripsis {30166462}. GRM1 expression is absent or very low in other cartilaginous neoplasms, emphasizing the high level of specificity of this finding in chondromyxoid fibroma {24658000}. However, approximately 10% of chondromyxoid fibromas do not exhibit upregulation of GRM1 expression, suggesting that this small subset may develop via a different genetic pathway.

Macroscopic appearance

Chondromyxoid fibroma is grossly well marginated and lobulated, blue-grey or white with a semitranslucent appearance, and lacks obvious necrosis, cystic change, or liquefaction. Typically, there are scalloped margins.

Histopathology

Chondromyxoid fibroma is sharply demarcated from the surrounding bone. Lobules of tumour may be separate from the main lesion. A lobular pattern with stellate or spindle-shaped cells in a myxoid background is evident {9596266}. Lobules demonstrate hypocellular centres with hypercellular peripheries. The centre of the lobules shows morphological features similar to those of hyaline cartilage, in terms of both extracellular matrix and cell composition {15880456; 11488075}. Slit-like and thin-walled haemangiopericytoma-like vessels may be seen at the periphery of lobules, with occasional thick-walled hyalinized small arterioles {29324473}. Microscopic cystic or liquefactive change is uncommon and usually focal when present. Hyaline cartilage is present in 19% of cases {9596266}. Calcification, when present, is usually coarse, and it occurs more frequently in older patients, in flat-bone tumours, and in rare craniofacial lesions {9840392; 29324473}. Individual cells within lobules have oval to spindled nuclei and indistinct to densely eosinophilic cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic extensions, often bipolar or multipolar, are frequent. Enlarged, hyperchromatic, and pleomorphic nuclei are noted in 20–30% of cases. Mitoses are uncommon, although atypical mitoses have been noted {9596266}. Entrapment of surrounding bone trabeculae by tumour is very rare. Osteoclast-like giant cells are often present at the periphery of the lobules. There may also be haemosiderin deposition and inflammatory cells, usually lymphocytes. Aneurysmal bone cyst–like areas are noted in approximately 10% of long- and flat-bone lesions {9596266}.

Immunohistochemistry is not required for routine clinical practice. The cartilaginous nature of this tumour is reflected by immunopositivity for S100 {2263591; 21078438; 19801163; 2793160}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analysis is generally not needed for diagnosis. In selected cases, identification of the exceedingly specific upregulation of GRM1 expression in chondromyxoid fibroma could be a strong diagnostic adjunct in distinguishing this entity from its mimics {24658000}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a lytic, metaphyseal, eccentric, lobulated lesion with sharp margins on conventional radiography; zonal architecture (chondroid and stellate cells within a chondroid and/or myxoid matrix in the centre of lobules; spindle-shaped myofibroblast-like cells admixed with osteoclast-like giant cells at the periphery).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is excellent, even for recurrent tumours. Recurrence has been reported in approximately 9–15% of cases treated locally {25217119}.
