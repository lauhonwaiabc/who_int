5.1.0.0 Soft tissue and bone tumours: Introduction

Mesenchymal tumours in children include a broad array of neoplasms, hamartomas, and complex conditions characterized by a combination of hamartomas and tumours, such as Proteus syndrome and Klippel–Trénaunay syndrome {22375909}.

Benign soft tissue tumours largely outnumber sarcomas by a factor of at least 100 in the general population; however, owing to difficulties in the collection and interpretation of data, information about the relative frequency of the histotypes in adults and children is limited {7282321}. Selected benign tumours or hamartomas in children may signal the presence of tumour predisposition syndromes or other genetic diseases.

The overall incidence of soft tissue sarcomas is approximately 5–6 cases per 100 000 person-years, and it increases with age, from 0.9 cases per 100 000 person-years in children aged < 10 years to 18.2 cases per 100 000 person-years in individuals aged > 70 years. However, the relative share of total malignancies accounted for by soft tissue sarcoma is higher in the paediatric population than in adults; it constitutes 7–8% of all cases of childhood cancer (making it the fifth most frequent paediatric cancer) and < 1% of all malignancies in adults {28271871; 20231056; 22375909}.

Bone sarcomas have an overall annual incidence of approximately 1 case per 100 000 individuals. More than 90% of bone sarcomas in children aged < 15 years are either osteosarcoma (50%) or Ewing sarcoma (41%) {20231056}. Paediatric chondrosarcoma is extremely rare. Bone metastases as the first clinical manifestation are infrequent, mostly deriving from neuroblastoma or kidney tumours {24390452}.

The vast majority of sarcomas arise de novo. Environmental factors, immunodeficiency, and genetic predisposition are associated with the development of mesenchymal tumours in about 10% of cases. Several types of benign mesenchymal tumours may occur on a familial or inherited basis. Various heritable familial genetic cancer syndromes are associated with sarcoma development: Li–Fraumeni syndrome (germline TP53 mutation), RASopathies (neurofibromatosis type 1, Costello syndrome, Noonan syndrome), Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, and DICER1 syndrome (germline DICER1 mutations). The inherited form of retinoblastoma (germline RB1 mutation) may also be associated with sarcoma {27498913}. Viral infection and immunodeficiency may play a key role in the development of Kaposi sarcoma (KSHV/HHV8) and smooth muscle tumours (EBV) {10643499; 16330945}. Osteogenic sarcomas, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas, and angiosarcomas can at times be associated with previous irradiation, with a latency period of about 10 years {15800318}. The risk of radiation-associated osteosarcoma is higher in patients with germline RB1 and TP53 mutations, as well as in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 {27498913; 21059199; 16735710}.

Appropriate classification of paediatric soft tissue tumours is crucial in treatment strategy planning. Advancements in molecular studies have enabled a more sophisticated understanding of tumour biology, contributing to a refinement of the diagnostic criteria in the fifth-edition WHO classification of soft tissue tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.fr/588.]]. Numerous histotypes are now defined by a combination of classic morphological criteria and recurrent genetic alterations. Moreover, new entities, identified by characteristic molecular alterations and homogeneous clinical features, are emerging from the pool of unclassifiable sarcomas [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.fr/588.]]. Furthermore, mounting evidence for the role of tumoural DNA methylation analysis in improving the diagnostic accuracy of soft tissue and bone tumours is leading to the implementation of this tool in clinical practice {26857854; 29572501}.

In children younger than 15 years, rhabdomyosarcoma is the most frequent sarcoma, to the point that paediatric oncologists refer to the remaining histotypes as “sarcomas other than rhabdomyosarcoma” {15877527; 28271871}.

The rhabdomyosarcoma family includes four types (embryonal, including the anaplastic subtype; alveolar; spindle cell / sclerosing; and pleomorphic) driven by different molecular mechanisms. Pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma typically occurs in adults and is extremely rare in children; it is not to be confused with the anaplastic subtype of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma. Rhabdomyosarcoma is a highly aggressive tumour with a strong tendency to metastasize, but it is responsive to conventional chemotherapy, with an overall 5-year survival rate of > 70% for localized disease {19770373; 22665534}. Key to this survival rate are treatment strategies based on cooperative international clinical trials, and risk-adapted multimodal treatment including surgery, radiotherapy, and, particularly, intensive multiagent cytotoxic chemotherapy {15877525; 11408506; 15728225}.

Sarcomas other than rhabdomyosarcoma account for about 3–4% of all paediatric cancers and include low-grade tumours (locally aggressive or rarely metastasizing) and high-grade tumours (with a high propensity to metastasize, particularly to the lung) {10561182; 10577841; 15767645; 15767644; 21145727}. This group encompasses histotypes typical of the paediatric age, as well as adult-type sarcomas that are also seen in children. Infantile fibrosarcoma is an example of a low-grade tumour occurring exclusively in the first few years of life, with a clinical behaviour characterized by initial rapid growth and possible metastatic spread, but an overall favourable outcome {26849118}. Despite the unifying clinical approach, adult-type sarcomas are a heterogeneous group and single entities may show clinical differences according to patient age, the outcome being reported as generally better in children than in adults. Current treatment approaches vary according to the chemosensitivity of the different histotypes. For example, standard chemotherapy (e.g. ifosfamide and doxorubicin) has markedly more efficacy in synovial sarcoma than in alveolar soft part sarcoma or clear cell sarcoma {28391003; 29286582; 28271871}.

The overall cure rate in soft tissue sarcomas other than rhabdomyosarcoma is about 70%, but it varies according to different prognostic factors, with young age being frequently associated with a better treatment response. Risk variables such as the extent of disease at diagnosis, histological grade, and histotype dictate whether chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy are advisable {28271871}.

The role of tumour grade remains debated, although the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) system is widely used in the stratification of cases and is progressively replacing Pediatric Oncology Group (POG) grading. The emerging role of molecular grading systems (such as the complexity index in sarcomas [CINSARC], which correlates with clinical outcome in adult sarcomas) might in the future contribute to redefining prognostic categories in children as well {28710396}.

Among bone sarcomas, osteosarcoma typically arises in teenagers and young adults, occurring at the sites of bone growth. It is a highly malignant disease, with a tendency to develop lung metastases. The use of combination adjuvant and neoadjuvant multiagent chemotherapy and limb-sparing surgery enables long-term survival in approximately 60–70% of cases.

Ewing sarcoma can arise both from bone and, less frequently, from soft tissues or visceral sites. It is characterized by gene fusions involving ETS genes (in 85% of cases) or non-ETS genes (less frequently). Ewing sarcomas are treated by intensive multiagent chemotherapy (with high-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue in high-risk cases), surgery, and/or radiotherapy for local control, with a 5-year survival rate of 60–75% for localized disease and 20–40% for metastatic disease (depending on metastatic sites and burden). In recent years, entities with new genetic abnormalities (e.g. BCOR-rearranged sarcoma) have been identified primarily in the bone, and their treatment strategies are still a matter of debate.

Paediatric patients affected by soft tissue and bone sarcomas should be referred to tertiary care centres (where the full spectrum of care and age-specific expertise are accessible) and enrolled in cooperative multicentre trials {17250970; 19224858}.
