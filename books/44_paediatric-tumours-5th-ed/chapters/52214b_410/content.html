5.2.2.14B Tenosynovial giant cell tumour

Definition

The term “tenosynovial giant cell tumour” encompasses a group of lesions most often arising from the synovium of joints, bursae, and tendon sheaths and showing synovial differentiation.

ICD-O coding

9252/0 Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, NOS

9252/1 Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, diffuse

ICD-11 coding

2F7Z & XH0HZ1 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of unspecified site & Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, NOS

2F7Z & XH6911 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of unspecified site & Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, localized

2F7Z & XH52J9 Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour of unspecified site & Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, diffuse

Related terminology

Acceptable: giant cell tumour of tendon sheath.

Not recommended: pigmented villonodular synovitis.

Subtype(s)

Tenosynovial giant cell tumour, diffuse

Localization

The knee is the most common large joint affected by tenosynovial giant cell tumour; the hand and wrist are the next most common joints affected by the localized type, and the ankle and hip are the next most common joints affected by the diffuse type {28787222; 10757395; 29494352; 31993359; 29252637; 24676801}.

Clinical features

Patients with these tumours generally present with swelling and pain, as well as a reduced range of motion in intra-articular disease or a soft tissue mass in extra-articular disease {29494352; 16949531; 17600720; 10725067; 21455469; 1776897}. Radiographically, most tumours appear as poorly defined periarticular masses. On MRI, these tumours show decreased signal intensity in both T1- and T2-weighted images, with artefacts from haemosiderin deposition {18794322; 26440548}.

Epidemiology

The estimated incidence rates in localized-extremity and diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours are 2.42 and 1.09 cases per 1 million person-years, respectively {28787222}. In children, the ratio of localized to diffuse cases is 1:1 {29494352}. The median patient age is 15 years (range: 3–18 years), and there is a female predominance (M:F ratio: 0.7:1) {28787222; 15605054; 10613228; 17195801; 17907437}. No malignant tenosynovial giant cell tumour has been reported in the paediatric population.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Cytogenetic studies have demonstrated relatively simple structural changes that most often involve a translocation of CSF1, encoding colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) {30152874}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, most localized-type tenosynovial giant cell tumours are small (5–40 mm) yellow-white to pale-brown nodules with a smooth surface. Diffuse-type tenosynovial giant cell tumours are usually large (often > 50 mm) and firm or sponge-like {18794322; 24676801}.

Histopathology

The microscopic appearance of tenosynovial giant cell tumours is variable and depends on the proportions of mononuclear cells, multinucleated giant cells, foamy macrophages, inflammatory cells, and haemosiderin, as well as the degree of collagenization of the stroma. Two principal cell types are identified within the mononuclear component: small histiocyte-like cells with pale cytoplasm and round or reniform nuclei, and larger epithelioid cells with amphophilic cytoplasm and rounded vesicular nuclei. The larger cells often contain a peripheral rim of haemosiderin granules. Osteoclast-like giant cells are less common in the diffuse form than in the localized form, and they may be absent or extremely rare in as many as 20% of cases. Cleft-like spaces are common and appear either as artefactual tears or as synovial-lined spaces. Blood-filled pseudoalveolar spaces are reported in approximately 10% of cases. Mitotic activity may be brisk, and necrosis can be present {11328844; 10757395; 18794322}.

Immunohistochemistry

The larger mononuclear cells express clusterin, and in 45–80% of cases a small subset of these cells stain for desmin, which highlights their dendritic processes. The smaller, histiocyte-like cells are positive for CD68, CD163, and CD45 {19542874; 7628850}.

Differential diagnosis

Correlation of histological features with immunohistochemistry and (in selected cases) molecular characterization can help in the distinction of tenosynovial giant cell tumour from juvenile xanthogranuloma (the latter of which has prominent xanthomatous cells and eosinophils, factor XIIIa-positive); giant cell tumour of soft tissue (absence of clefts and presence of metaplastic bone formation); soft tissue aneurysmal bone cyst (desmin-negative, USP6 fusion); and tendon sheath fibroma (absence of mononuclear histiocytoid and epithelioid cells) {22420728}.

Cytology

The presence of large mononuclear cells with eccentric nuclei, finely granulated cytoplasm, and a cytoplasmic rim of haemosiderin is distinctive {28324880}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an intra- or extra-articular location; varying proportions of small histiocytic cells, large amphophilic cells, foam cells, and multinucleated giant cells.

Desirable: demonstration of CSF1 rearrangement (in selected cases).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumour of localized type is a benign lesion with a capacity for local recurrence (rate: 15%) after excision {28787222; 29494352}. Recurrences are more common in diffuse-type tumours (estimated recurrence rate: 50%), and repeated recurrences may severely compromise joint function {29494352; 31029509}. Adjuvant radiotherapy could be used for incomplete surgical resection {22806927}. Molecular targeted therapy targeting the CSF1/CSF1R pathway has shown promising results when complete excision is not feasible {31229240}.
