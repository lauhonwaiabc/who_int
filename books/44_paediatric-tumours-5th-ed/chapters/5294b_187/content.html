5.2.9.4B Synovial sarcoma

Definition

Synovial sarcoma (SS) is a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm showing variable epithelial differentiation and characterized by a specific SS18::SSX fusion gene.

ICD-O coding

9040/3 Synovial sarcoma, NOS

9041/3 Monophasic synovial sarcoma

9043/3 Biphasic synovial sarcoma

9040/3 Poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B5A.Y & XH9B22 Synovial sarcoma, other specified primary site & Synovial sarcoma, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: synovial cell sarcoma; synoviosarcoma; synovioma.

Subtype(s)

Monophasic synovial sarcoma; biphasic synovial sarcoma; poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma

Localization

Most cases (70%) of SS arise in the deep soft tissues of the extremities, often near large joints. About 15% arise in the trunk and 7% in the head and neck region {19514087}, whereas presentation in visceral organs, retroperitoneum, mediastinum, bone, CNS, and peripheral nerve is rare {15330802; 15725802; 21295819}.

Clinical features

SS may occur as a longstanding and slow-growing mass, with or without pain. Initial presentation as a small, circumscribed tumour may give the erroneous clinical impression of a benign lesion {20858835}.

Imaging

MRI is the preferred modality for diagnosis and staging, typically showing a large mass near a joint with alternating areas of isointense, hypointense, and hyperintense signal {23169746; 22826423}. CT may highlight stippled or spiculated matrix calcifications. SS with aggressive growth may erode or invade adjacent bone {1334066}.

Epidemiology

SS is most common in adolescents and young adults. About 15% of soft tissue sarcomas in children aged 10–18 years are SS {19514087}. Sex distribution is approximately equal.

Etiology

There are no known predisposing factors. In exceptional cases, SS is associated with a history of previous radiotherapy.

Pathogenesis

SS bears a unique chromosomal translocation resulting in the formation of an oncogenic SS18::SSX fusion gene. Otherwise, SS in children has a very low mutation burden relative to other sarcomas and to its adult counterpart {29100075; 23319690; 29533008}. SS is characterized by a unique t(X;18)(p11;q11) translocation {11782370}, in which SS18 on chromosome 18 is fused to an SSX gene (SSX1, SSX2, or SSX4) on the X chromosome {11107170}. SS18::SSX functions as an oncogene, and its expression is necessary to maintain the transformed phenotype of SS cells {26947017; 29202462}. SS18L1 is implicated in a small percentage of adult cases {12696068}. Evidence suggests that the translated fusion protein disrupts the epigenetic control of mesenchymal differentiation by binding and displacing native SS18 in the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex {23540691}. The prognostic relevance of the SS18 partner gene (SSX1, SSX2, or SSX4) does not appear to significantly alter the prognosis {11782370; 15364967}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumour size ranges from < 10 mm to as large as 200 mm (median: 70 mm). Tumours in the hands and feet are usually small {16723849}. Tumours may be yellow-tan to grey on cut section, and they may display areas of cystic degeneration, necrosis, calcification, nodularity, haemorrhage, and softening {6256705}.

Histopathology

There are three main histological patterns of SS: monophasic (most common), biphasic (second most common, accounting for as many as a third of cases), and poorly differentiated.

Monophasic SS is composed of dense cellular sheets and fascicles of uniform spindle cells. The tumour cells have high N:C ratios and ovoid, hyperchromatic nuclei with regular granular chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. Collagen is usually scant, but there may be areas of dense fibrosis or hyalinization. Focal myxoid change may be present. A staghorn vascular pattern is occasionally seen. Foci of calcification and/or ossification are found in as many as one third of cases and may be abundant {6282441}.

Biphasic SS contains an epithelial element, juxtaposed with the spindle cell component that is characteristic of monophasic SS. Most commonly the spindle cell component predominates, but the epithelial component may be a major component of the tumour. The epithelial cells are arranged in nests or in glandular structures with a tubular or sometimes alveolar architecture. In glandular areas, epithelial cells are cuboidal or columnar and have ovoid vesicular nuclei and (typically) more abundant and pale eosinophilic cytoplasm than that of the spindle cells. Glandular lumina may contain epithelial mucin.

Poorly differentiated SS is rare in children {12823715}. Poorly differentiated areas may be composed of small round hyperchromatic tumour cells (reminiscent of a small blue round cell tumour) or fascicular spindle cells (reminiscent of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour) {10375108}. Severe nuclear atypia and high mitotic activity (> 6 mitoses/mm2, equating to > 10 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.17 mm2 in area) may be found {15364967}.

Immunohistochemistry using a novel antibody specific for the SS18::SSX fusion protein {32141887} allows a sensitive and specific diagnosis of SS. In addition, TLE1 shows diffuse nuclear staining in most cases of SS, although it is not specific and is also expressed in histological mimics such as malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour and solitary fibrous tumour {12409719; 21571956}. Epithelial differentiation can be demonstrated with EMA and cytokeratins. In most cases of SS there is expression of BCL2 and CD99 (which may be perimembranous) {16528378}. S100 and SMA are variably expressed, whereas desmin and CD34 staining is exceptional {12409719}. SMARCB1 (INI1) staining may be partially or completely lost in a subset of SS {20305614; 23245672}.

Cytology

FNAB samples of SS most commonly display moderate to marked smear cellularity dominated by cohesive clusters of spindle-shaped cells with ovoid, hyperchromatic nuclei and scant, tapering cytoplasm {8980353}. Conclusive diagnosis requires molecular confirmation (e.g. by FISH) {23444168}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Approximately two thirds of SSs carry SS18::SSX1 fusion, one third carry SS18::SSX2, and rare cases carry SS18::SSX4 {11107170; 17334349} or SS18L1::SSX1 fusion {12696068}. Almost all tumours with SS18::SSX2 fusion show monophasic histology, whereas those with SS18::SSX1 fusion show an approximate 2:1 ratio of monophasic to biphasic histology {26947017; 10718206}. It is recommended that confirmation of the SS18::SSX fusion, either by molecular assay or by immunohistochemistry, be performed in all cases of SS where possible {26970672}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: monomorphic spindle cell sarcoma with or without gland formation, showing variable histological and/or immunohistochemical epithelial differentiation; diffuse and strong nuclear immunostaining for TLE1.

Desirable: diffuse and strong nuclear immunostaining for SS18::SSX fusion protein or demonstration of an SS18::SSX gene fusion.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) or Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system may be used.

Prognosis and prediction

In children and adolescents, the 5- and 10-year disease-specific survival rates are 76–90% and 65–75%, respectively; these rates are more favourable than in adults {19514087; 22878620; 28271871}. Tumour size (< 50 mm) and an absence of metastases are the most important indicators of better overall survival {28391003; 22878620}. Other major prognostic determinants include tumour stage at presentation and the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) tumour grade {15364967; 18997619; 20716627; 25488687}. Tumours with poorly differentiated areas show more aggressive behaviour {9888710; 10078916; 10375108}. Metastatic disease occurs in approximately 40% of patients, most commonly to the lungs and occasionally to bone and regional lymph nodes. Most distant tumour recurrences develop within a few years of initial diagnosis, but late recurrences can occur decades later. Higher genomic complexity may be related to the metastatic potential {20716627; 29533008}.
