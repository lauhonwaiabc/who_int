5.2.10.4 Epithelioid sarcoma

Definition

Epithelioid sarcoma (ES) is a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by partial or complete epithelioid cytomorphology, expression of epithelial markers, and loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression in the majority of cases.

ICD-O coding

8804/3 Epithelioid sarcoma

8804/3 Classic epithelioid sarcoma

8804/3 Proximal epithelioid sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B5F.2 & XH4F96 Sarcoma, not elsewhere classified, of other specified sites & Epithelioid sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: conventional or distal epithelioid sarcoma; large cell epithelioid sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Classic epithelioid sarcoma; proximal epithelioid sarcoma

Localization

Classic ES (C-ES) most commonly occurs in the distal upper extremities, predominantly arising in the fingers and hands {4014539; 26645461}. Tumours of the feet and hands affect mainly volar surfaces. The proximal extremities, head and neck, and trunk are less frequent locations. Proximal ES (P-ES) tends to arise in deep soft tissue, most often affecting truncal (pelviperineal, genital, and inguinal) tissue, buttock, or hip {9042279; 11454997}.

Clinical features

C-ES occurs as solitary or multiple slow-growing, painless, firm, superficially located nodules. Lesions often result in non-healing skin ulcers that can clinically mimic ulcerative non-neoplastic dermal processes. Recurrences can involve deep soft tissue, mimicking P-ES {9042279; 11454997}. P-ES is rare in children. It is a multinodular deep neoplasm, mostly at proximal sites. ES commonly metastasizes to lymph nodes.

Epidemiology

ES represents < 1% of all adult soft tissue sarcomas and 2–8% of childhood non-rhabdomyosarcomatous soft tissue sarcomas {16353216; 30954717}; 11% of cases of ES arise in the first two decades of life {31478778}. P-ES predominates in both adults and children, with P-ES representing about 37% of ES cases in a paediatric series {16353216; 22420732; 26645461}. ES shows a male predominance {26645461; 9042279; 11454997; 22420732}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Antecedent trauma, reported in 20–73% of cases, is probably coincidental {4014539; 639005; 6750534}. There is no recurrent genetic predisposition.

Pathogenesis

ES is associated with a loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) nuclear protein expression by biallelic or (less frequently) monoallelic deletions {23060122; 24585572; 27223121}, or, in some cases, by negative regulation of SMARCB1 transcripts by microRNAs {24327545; 24287458; 27223121}. SMARCB1 (INI1) loss disrupts SWI/SNF function, leading to the activation of EZH2, a histone methyltransferase subunit of PRC2. EZH2 catalyses H3 p.K27me3, thus acting as an oncogenic driver {26845405}. ES with retained SMARCB1 (INI1) expression exhibits abnormal expression of other SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex members, such as SMARCA4 (BRG1), SMARCC1 (BAF155), or SMARCC2 (BAF170). Genomic analysis by next-generation sequencing reveals copy-number aberrations in the ES complex (with the most frequent losses at 22q11.2 and 12p13) and a high overall mutation burden, differing from the simple genome of malignant rhabdoid tumour {26365879; 26923874; 26977886}. By RNA sequencing, the transcriptome shows a unique profile that does not cluster with any particular tissue type or with other SWI/SNF-aberrant model systems {26365879; 26343384}.

Macroscopic appearance

C-ES appears as one or multiple indurated, poorly defined, dermal or subcutaneous nodules measuring from a few millimetres to 50 mm. Deep-seated tumours are multinodular masses that extend along nerves and fascial planes. The cut surface is greyish-white to tan, punctuated by necrotic and haemorrhagic foci. P-ES appears in deep locations as solitary or multiple whitish nodules ranging from 10 to 200 mm in size, with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis {9042279; 11454997}.

Histopathology

C-ES consists of cellular nodules of epithelioid and spindled cells with central degeneration and/or necrosis, with a vaguely granulomatous appearance (pseudogranulomatous pattern) {3717528}. Confluent necrotizing nodules result in a serpiginous mass with central geographical necrosis. Deeply situated lesions spread along the fascia as undulating bands of cells punctuated by foci of necrosis. Tumour cells vary from large ovoid/polygonal epithelioid cells to peripheral plump spindle-shaped cells with deeply eosinophilic cytoplasm and mildly atypical nuclei possessing vesicular chromatin and small nucleoli. Scattered rhabdoid cells can be present. Epithelioid cells may exhibit cytoplasmic vacuoles, mimicking a vascular tumour. A pseudoangiosarcomatous pattern and a fibroma-like pattern have been described. Mitotic activity is usually low. Dystrophic calcification and metaplastic bone formation are detected in 20% of cases {4014539}, and aggregates of chronic inflammatory cells are usually present at the periphery of the tumour nodules. Perineural and perivascular diffusion are frequent. Skip lesions are common.

P-ES is characterized by a multinodular and sheet-like growth of large rhabdoid and sometimes pleomorphic epithelioid (carcinoma-like) cells with enlarged vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli {9042279; 11454997}. Spotty foci of tumour necrosis are frequent, without the pseudogranulomatous pattern of C-ES. Occasional cases may have a prominent myxoid stroma {21083605}. Additionally, rare hybrid cases demonstrate the histological features of both C-ES and P-ES. The differential diagnosis of C-ES includes benign granulomatous lesions and pseudomyogenic haemangioendothelioma {28009608}. For rhabdoid cell–dominant ES, malignant rhabdoid tumour should be ruled out.

Both C-ES and P-ES show immunoreactivity for epithelial markers, including low- and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins (CK8 and CK19, whereas CK5/6 is negative or weakly positive) and EMA, in addition to vimentin {10452506; 12946229}. Expression of CD34 (50%) and ERG (40–67%, predominantly in C-ES) can cause confusion with vascular tumours {23774169; 24072183; 25479928}. Loss of nuclear expression of SMARCB1 occurs in the vast majority of cases {19033866; 18973917; 21357725}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: superficial or deep soft tissue mass; epithelioid to spindled cytomorphology; infiltrative growth; loss of SMARCB1 (INI1, BAF47) expression; staining for vimentin, EMA, and keratins.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) or Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system may be used.

Prognosis and prediction

Clinical series of ES cases have shown 5- and 10-year overall survival rates of 45–70% and 45–66%, respectively {24046109; 25663591; 28756018; 28799063; 32307188}. Paediatric ES (in patients aged < 18 years) has a more favourable survival rate {31478778}. In the largest reported paediatric series (63 cases from a joint North American Children’s Oncology Group [COG] and European Paediatric Soft Tissue Sarcoma Study Group [EpSSG] study), 5-year event-free survival and overall survival rates were 60.7% and 63.6%, respectively {30954717}.

Poor outcome is associated with metastatic disease (22–30% of cases are metastatic at the time of diagnosis), deep location and large tumour size, P-ES and high histological grade, axial locations (versus the hand), and inadequate tumour resection {8996162; 8539226; 3543238; 30954717}.

In the COG-EpSSG paediatric series, the 5-year overall survival rate was 86.4% in patients with localized disease and R0 resection, 63.5% in other patients with localized disease, and 0% in metastatic disease at diagnosis.

Most patients who undergo an adequate resection fare well without adjuvant therapy. Radiotherapy may improve local control in locally advanced disease. Response to chemotherapy is reported to be about 50% in children receiving neoadjuvant therapy {30954717}.
