5.4.1.4 Osteosarcoma

Definition

Osteosarcoma is a family of primary bone sarcomas formed from bone-producing tumour cells.

Related terminology

Acceptable: for osteosarcoma: osteogenic sarcoma; for low-grade central osteosarcoma: well-differentiated intramedullary osteosarcoma.

Not recommended: for parosteal osteosarcoma: juxtacortical osteosarcoma; for periosteal osteosarcoma: juxtacortical chondroblastic osteosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

Conventional osteosarcoma; telangiectatic osteosarcoma; small cell osteosarcoma; parosteal osteosarcoma; periosteal osteosarcoma; low-grade central osteosarcoma; high-grade surface osteosarcoma

Localization

Conventional osteosarcomas usually originate from the long bones of the extremities, which contain the most proliferative growth plates. Approximately half of the cases arise from the knee area, specifically from the distal femur (30%) and the proximal tibia (15%), and 15% arise from the proximal humerus {21437228; 9286295}. Although osteosarcomas can originate from any bone, they rarely involve the small bones of the extremities. Osteosarcomas of the jaws, pelvis, and spine tend to occur in older individuals. Small cell and telangiectatic osteosarcomas originate from bone sites similar to those of conventional cases {2804905; 25723116; 27469493}. In long bones, osteosarcomas are frequently centred in the metaphysis (90%), infrequently in the diaphysis (9%), and rarely in the epiphysis.

Parosteal osteosarcomas usually (in ~70% of cases) arise from the posterior surface of the distal femur; alternatively, they involve either the proximal tibia or the humerus {8126042}. Most periosteal osteosarcomas (~80%) arise from the femur or tibia diaphysis/diametaphysis {16290134}. Approximately 80% of low-grade central osteosarcomas occur in the long bone metaphysis, predominantly involving either the distal femur or the proximal tibia {18425560}. High-grade surface osteosarcomas arise from the long bone diaphysis or diametaphysis. They originate most frequently from the femur, sometimes from the tibia, and least frequently from the humerus {18300258}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with a painful mass that has been growing for several weeks or months. The skin overlying the mass may be warm and erythematous. A pathological fracture through the mass heralds about 15% of cases {30685685; 3492261}. Telangiectatic osteosarcomas have the highest fracture risk (~30%) because of massive tumour-associated bone destruction {1884532; 27469493}. Systemic manifestations, such as malaise, weight loss, and cachexia, may occur in advanced cases but are otherwise rare.

Patients with low-grade osteosarcoma usually present with a history of swelling and/or pain that has persisted for several months or years.

Imaging

The radiographic appearance of osteosarcomas is highly variable. They typically appear as poorly defined, large, part-lytic and part-sclerotic masses that transgress into the cortex and often form a large soft tissue component. Images of the soft tissue component may show a cloud-like pattern of immature tumour ossification {17331824}. Telangiectatic osteosarcomas manifest as aggressive-looking masses that are associated with bone destruction, extraosseous extension, and sometimes pathological fracture {14512511}. These tumours are rarely sclerotic and only subtly ossified {14512511}. On MRI, telangiectatic osteosarcomas usually appear as multicystic masses with aneurysmal bone cyst–like fluid-fluid levels, but unlike aneurysmal bone cysts, they have thickened septa and nodular/solid components {14512511}.

Parosteal osteosarcomas appear as mineralized, lobular masses that attach to and usually wrap around the involved bone. The cortex beneath these tumours may be normal, thickened, or destroyed.

Low-grade central osteosarcomas appear as large, intramedullary, expansile, and coarsely trabeculated metaphyseal or diametaphyseal lesions that often extend to the bone end and disrupt the cortex to some degree {15175837}. They can be lytic, sclerotic, or mixed lytic and sclerotic.

Periosteal osteosarcomas are often lucent, fusiform, bone-surface masses that are associated with cortical thickening, cortical scalloping, and a sunburst-patterned periosteal reaction that is perpendicular to the bone’s long axis. MRI reveals a cartilaginous component. Medullary infiltration is rare {15333772}.

High-grade surface osteosarcomas are variably mineralized masses that extend from the bone surface into soft tissue. They often have a fluffy or cloud-like appearance {11810163}. About half of all high-grade surface osteosarcomas erode the cortex and involve the medulla {18300258}.

Epidemiology

In children and adolescents, osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumour. It accounts for about 55% of all bone cancers in individuals aged < 20 years (annual incidence: ~4.4 cases per 1 million individuals aged < 24 years) {19197972; 20213383; 26054913}. The incidence of osteosarcoma in the paediatric age group peaks between 12 and 16 years of age {19197972; 30685685}. Osteosarcomas affect boys more frequently than girls (M:F ratio: 1.35:1) and Black people more frequently than White people {26054913; 19197972}. Most paediatric osteosarcomas (90%) are the high-grade, conventional subtype. The incidence of the telangiectatic subtype (< 4% of osteosarcomas) peaks in the second decade of life and affects boys more frequently than girls (M:F ratio: 1.5:1) {27469493}. The small cell subtype (1.5%) peaks in the second decade of life and affects girls slightly more frequently than boys {2684389; 2804905; 6953993; 9179055}. Craniofacial osteosarcomas tend to occur in older individuals {24246156}.

About 3% of all osteosarcomas occur after cancer therapy for a preceding childhood malignancy, which is most commonly retinoblastoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, or Ewing sarcoma {3475572}. These secondary osteosarcomas occur, on average, 14.5 years after the first malignancy {25022788}.

The periosteal subtype (< 2% of osteosarcomas) affects both sexes equally and has a similar age distribution to that of the conventional subtype {21472720; 30363676; 16290134}. The high-grade surface subtype (< 1% of osteosarcomas) peaks in the second decade of life and affects boys more frequently than girls {10091787}. Both the parosteal and the low-grade central subtypes (~4% and 1–2% of osteosarcomas, respectively) peak in the third decade of life and affect girls slightly more frequently than boys {8126042; 2306687; 18425560}.

Etiology

Most osteosarcomas are sporadic, but certain heritable syndromes increase their risk. Osteosarcoma is the most common sarcoma in older children and adolescents, with the cancer predisposing to Li–Fraumeni syndrome. In survivors of heritable retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma accounts for almost half of the second primary cancers that develop later in life. Patients with the rare autosomal recessive Rothmund–Thomson, Werner, or Bloom syndromes are also predisposed to developing osteosarcoma. Sporadic osteosarcomas commonly have mutations in the genes implicated in these inherited syndromes. TP53 and RB1 inactivating mutations cause Li–Fraumeni syndrome {21837677; 1978757} and hereditary retinoblastoma {2994066; 2877398; 9333268}, respectively, and TP53 and RB1 are the most frequently mutated genes in osteosarcoma. RECQL4, WRN (RECQL2), and BLM (RECQL3) mutations cause Rothmund–Thomson syndrome, Werner syndrome, and Bloom syndrome, respectively {19657341; 24924172}, and RECQ mutations are also recurrent in osteosarcoma. About 18% of paediatric patients with osteosarcoma carry a cancer predisposition gene germline mutation, whereas only about 1% of individuals without a history of cancer have these mutations. The most common germline mutations are in TP53 and RB1 {26580448}. TP53 germline mutations are seen in 9.5–12% of paediatric patients with osteosarcoma {25512523; 25896519}. Osteosarcoma risk is elevated in taller individuals and in individuals who have had rapid adolescent growth spurts {21465145; 30311632}. The etiologies of these correlations are unknown. Osteosarcoma is one of the most common second malignancies that follow childhood solid cancer therapy. Histories of radiation or alkylating agent treatment are risk factors for secondary osteosarcoma {7448699; 3475572}.

Pathogenesis

High-grade osteosarcoma oncogenesis is predominantly driven by structural variants (SVs), chromothriptic events, and tumour suppressor gene inactivation {24703847; 28643781; 30266815}. High-grade osteosarcomas are intratumourally heterogeneous, and they have unstable genomes and complex karyotypes that include numerous copy-number alterations and SVs {9140456; 25319867; 23436697; 24703847}. High-grade osteosarcoma has one of the highest paediatric cancer SV rates {24703847}. The characteristically multiple rearrangements across an osteosarcoma genome are generally accompanied by chromothriptic events (regional chromosomal shattering followed by massive clustered rearrangements) or chromoplexy {24703847; 25512523; 28643781}. Osteosarcomas have few recurrent single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), although half of all osteosarcomas exhibit kataegis (an SV-colocalized hypermutation pattern) {24703847}. Most osteosarcomas (> 90%) have inactivating TP53 mutations. In more than half of these cases, TP53 is inactivated by an osteosarcoma-specific SV that involves intron 1 {24703847; 25762628}, whereas in the remaining cases TP53 is inactivated by missense, nonsense, or insertion/deletion (indel) mutations or a loss of heterozygosity {24703847; 25512523; 28643781}. Many osteosarcomas also have recurrent mutations in RB1 (50%; SVs, SNVs/indels), CDKN2A (10%; homozygous deletions), DLG2 (~50%; SVs), PI3K/mTOR pathway components (~25%), ATRX (30%; SVs, SNVs), MDM2 (5–10%; amplification), MYC (10%; amplification), FGFR1 (18.5%; amplification), and the genes of the IGF1R signalling pathway (14%) {24703847; 25512523; 28643781; 19718709; 26632267; 24861215}.

Parosteal and low-grade central osteosarcomas commonly have a single genomic aberration – one or more supernumerary ring chromosomes containing amplified 12q13-q15 {9162194; 26248895}. This aberration is thought to drive oncogenesis by amplifying MDM2 and CDK4 {20196171; 9989829; 21336260; 30001240}. These amplifications are present in > 85% of parosteal osteosarcoma cases and less frequently in low-grade central osteosarcoma cases {26248895}.

Macroscopic appearance

Conventional osteosarcoma usually manifests as a large (50–100 mm) intramedullary and metaphyseal mass. The tumour grows into the diaphysis and towards the growth plate but usually spares the epiphysis. Except for advanced cases, conventional osteosarcomas rarely involve the joint space. The tumour cut surface appears heterogeneous, owing to a mixture of different components. Heavily mineralized tumours are tan-white and hard, whereas non-mineralized cartilaginous tumours are glistening, grey, and rubbery, and they may be mucinous if the matrix is myxoid. Typically, the tumour extensively involves the medullary cavity, displaces the periosteum peripherally, breaks through the cortex, and usually has an eccentric or circumferential soft tissue component. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma is a haemorrhagic, multicystic, blood- and clot-filled mass, classically described as a “bag of blood” {1069603}. Solid or sclerotic areas are usually absent/minimal. The mass may extensively erode and destroy the cortex and involve neighbouring soft tissues. The gross features of small cell osteosarcoma are indistinguishable from those of conventional osteosarcoma.

Parosteal osteosarcomas manifest as hard, lobulated, exophytic masses that attach to the outer cortex by a broad base. Soft, fleshy areas, if present, suggest progression to a high-grade sarcoma (dedifferentiation). Periosteal osteosarcomas arise from the cortical surface and appear as sharply demarcated, broad-based, ovoid masses, which may involve the entire circumference. The mass typically spares the medulla but generates conspicuous cortical thickening. Periosteal osteosarcomas usually have glistening grey cartilaginous cut surfaces. The tumour base may be heavily ossified and show sunburst-like calcified spicules {1063059; 7096408}. Low-grade central osteosarcomas are expansile and usually well-circumscribed masses within the medullary cavity that have a rubbery, fibrous, and gritty texture. Cortical destruction, if present, may be accompanied by a soft tissue mass. High-grade surface osteosarcomas are well-circumscribed, lobulated, cortical surface masses that bulge into the surrounding soft tissues and often erode the underlying cortex.

Histopathology

Conventional osteosarcomas grow in a permeative pattern, replace the marrow space, surround and destroy pre-existing trabeculae, and fill and expand the Haversian systems. The morphological spectrum of conventional osteosarcoma is broad. The neoplastic cells may be polygonal, round, epithelioid, plasmacytoid, spindled, fusiform or small. The cytoplasm is most often eosinophilic but sometimes clear. The neoplastic cells typically have anaplastic features (i.e. marked pleomorphism, brisk mitotic activity, and abundant atypical mitotic figures). These malignant features are most easily identified in osteoid-spare areas. When enclosed by osteoid matrix, the tumour cells may appear small and normalized.

Conventional osteosarcoma diagnosis requires the identification of neoplastic osteoid/bone. The bone varies in quantity, pattern, and degree of mineralization. It may be inconspicuous, sparse, or abundant. Characteristically, the bone is intimately associated with tumour cells, is qualitatively woven, and is deposited as primitive, disorganized trabeculae that lack osteoblastic rimming. The deposited bone may be finely or coarsely patterned into either a lace or filigreed motif; produce large, broad sheets of coalescing or anastomosing trabeculae; or have a pagetoid appearance due to haphazardly deposited cement lines. Unmineralized bone is eosinophilic, and mineralized bone is basophilic. Unmineralized osteoid may be mistaken for collagen or other eosinophilic extracellular materials. Collagen may be distinguished from osteoid because collagen tends to be more fibrillar than unmineralized matrix, notably lies compressed between the cells, and is frequently deposited in broad aggregates.

Conventional osteosarcomas are traditionally divided according to the predominant matrix and stromal elements into three main histological subtypes: osteoblastic osteosarcoma (76–80% of cases), chondroblastic osteosarcoma (10–13%) and fibroblastic osteosarcoma (10%) {12044509; 16421923}. In osteoblastic osteosarcoma, the principal matrix produced is bone. When bone is prominent, the tumour is designated as the sclerosing subtype. Chondroblastic osteosarcomas contain mostly malignant cartilage, and (in the proper context) a biopsy that reveals a high-grade sarcoma with purely chondromatous differentiation should strongly suggest a chondroblastic osteosarcoma. The cartilage is usually hyaline and high-grade but may be myxoid, particularly in tumours arising in the gnathic bones. In fibroblastic osteosarcoma, the malignant cells are usually spindled, are variably pleomorphic, and grow in a fascicular or storiform pattern. Fibroblastic osteosarcomas produce extracellular collagen, sometimes extensively, but osteoid production is often modest. These histological subtypes are not relevant for either prognosis or treatment considerations {12044509}. In fact, most cases concurrently exhibit features from different subtypes.

Telangiectatic osteosarcoma is composed of blood-filled or empty cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa that resemble aneurysmal bone cysts. Typically, these cases have many osteoclast-type giant cells, and osteoid production is scarce and focal. Telangiectatic osteosarcomas can be distinguished from aneurysmal bone cysts by the presence of pleomorphic cells that have hyperchromatic nuclei and frequent, atypical mitotic figures. Small cell osteosarcomas have small cells with round to oval nuclei, fine to coarse chromatin, and scant cytoplasm, and they invariably produce lace-like osteoid. Particular care must be taken to distinguish these from Ewing sarcoma cells and differentiate osteoid from fibrin deposits that sometimes accompany Ewing sarcoma cells. Other morphological patterns have distinctive histological features that can be difficult to recognize as osteosarcomas. The giant cell–rich pattern has cytologically bland, reactive, osteoclast-type giant cells that are scattered throughout the tumour and may be mistaken for giant cell tumour of bone {12597223}. The epithelioid pattern is characterized by sheets or nests of large, cohesive, polyhedral tumour cells, which may resemble carcinomas {7683966}. In the osteoblastoma-like pattern, the tumour cells line the neoplastic bony trabeculae in a fashion that resembles osteoblastoma, but the osteoblastoma-like pattern is distinguished by its permeative growth pattern and severe cytological atypia {8302813; 10192265; 30152881}. The chondroblastoma-like pattern resembles chondroblastoma but grows in a more infiltrative pattern and has more cytological atypia than its benign counterpart {31389716}.

Parosteal osteosarcoma typically comprises well-formed woven or lamellar bony trabeculae, often arranged in a parallel manner and which may or may not have osteoblastic rimming. Bland-looking, mitotically torpid spindled cells fill the spaces between the bony trabeculae. Approximately half of the cases have either cartilaginous nodules or a cap, and about 20% of cases are more cellular and show moderate atypia. Progression into high-grade sarcoma (dedifferentiation) occurs in 15–25% of cases and is more common at the time of recurrence than at the initial diagnosis {8126042; 15852358}. Periosteal osteosarcoma comprises mostly intermediate-grade malignant cartilage that blends into either neoplastic osteoid or atypical fibroblastic elements {1063059; 7096408}. Marrow involvement, originally thought to preclude the diagnosis, is rarely seen. Low-grade central osteosarcoma has a hypocellular to moderately cellular fibroblastic component with variable amounts of osteoid deposition in a pattern that resembles that of parosteal osteosarcoma or fibrous dysplasia {8422626}. This subtype can be distinguished from fibrous dysplasia by permeative growth and soft tissue extension. About 10–36% of low-grade central osteosarcoma cases dedifferentiate {8542697; 18425560}. High-grade surface osteosarcoma is histologically identical to its high-grade intramedullary counterpart.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical profiling has limited diagnostic value for osteosarcoma because these tumours do not consistently express any specific markers. However, FOS immunostaining may help differentiate osteoblastomas (which commonly express FOS owing to FOS rearrangement) from osteoblastoma-like osteosarcomas (which do not typically express FOS) {29858576; 31490237; 31768625}. Also, immunostaining for SATB2, which is a very sensitive osteoblastic differentiation marker, can help distinguish small cell osteosarcomas from Ewing sarcomas {23701429; 25723116; 27465835}. However it may be deceptive in the differential diagnosis from BCOR::CCNB3 sarcomas, which may express SATB2 {29084055; 28420319}. In order to avoid a misdiagnosis of carcinoma, it is important to note that occasional osteosarcomas can express epithelial markers like keratin and EMA {14514776; 21876652}.

MDM2 and/or CDK4 immunostaining may help to distinguish parosteal or low-grade central osteosarcomas from benign mimics {20601938; 21336260}. In the proper histological context, it may also help to recognize a dedifferentiated tumour from a de novo high-grade osteosarcoma {22301501}. When a tumour is positive for MDM2 and/or CDK4 by immunohistochemistry, molecular testing is often necessary to prove that the gene is amplified.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No specific diagnostic molecular tests are available. FISH for detecting MDM2 amplification may help to differentiate parosteal or low-grade central osteosarcomas from their benign mimics. However, the absence of MDM2 amplification does not exclude the diagnosis. MDM2 amplification is also maintained in high-grade progression from low-grade osteosarcomas.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Conventional osteosarcoma

Essential: a pleomorphic, intramedullary, bone-producing tumour; a permeative/destructive growth pattern; compatible radiographs.

Telangiectatic osteosarcoma

Essential: a bone tumour with empty or blood-filled cystic spaces; fibrous septa containing malignant cells.

Small cell osteosarcoma

Essential: a bone tumour with small blue round cells; mineralized osteoid.

Parosteal osteosarcoma

Essential: a low-grade spindle cell tumour; woven bone formation; surface location; compatible radiographs.

Desirable: MDM2 amplification.

Periosteal osteosarcoma

Essential: an intermediate-grade chondroblastic osteosarcoma; surface location; compatible radiographs.

Low-grade central osteosarcoma

Essential: a low-grade spindle cell tumour; well-formed neoplastic bony trabeculae; intramedullary location; compatible radiographs.

Desirable: MDM2 amplification.

High-grade surface osteosarcoma

Essential: a high-grade osteosarcoma arising on the surface of the bone without a substantial intraosseous component; compatible radiographs.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) established a TNM malignant bone tumour staging system that classifies tumours by their primary site, i.e. (1) appendicular skeleton (including trunk, skull, facial bones); (2) pelvis; (3) spine. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society has adopted the Enneking musculoskeletal sarcoma staging system {8649730; 3456859}, which classifies tumours by histological grade, local extent (extraosseous extension state), and metastatic state. The AJCC/UICC staging system has not been widely adopted for clinical use in paediatric osteosarcoma. For surgical planning, the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society staging system is most often used. Practically, paediatric osteosarcomas are classified as localized or metastatic, and the staging systems are not currently used to stratify patients for treatment purposes.

Prognosis and prediction

High-grade osteosarcoma is an aggressive bone tumour that disseminates rapidly and haematogenously {3520317}. The prognosis of metastatic osteosarcoma is poor, but localized osteosarcomas can be contained with proper management. Between 15% and 20% of patients with high-grade osteosarcoma present with detectable metastases {9286295}. The lungs are the most common sites of metastasis, followed by other bones {16015627}. Fewer than 30% of patients with metastatic disease at diagnosis survive {12743156; 18754698; 16015627}. Patients with multiple foci of osteosarcoma without a known primary tumour, referred to as synchronous multifocal osteosarcoma, also have a dismal prognosis {16877608; 11450892}. Localized osteosarcomas are usually resected with limb-sparing surgery {11118462}, but without any additional treatment > 80% of these cases will relapse. Treatment for high-grade osteosarcoma should include chemotherapy to eradicate occult metastases {3520317}. Combining surgery and chemotherapy results in an overall survival rate of 65–70% for localized osteosarcomas {3520317; 26304877; 21703851}. With multimodal therapy, the prognosis for telangiectatic osteosarcomas is as good as that for conventional osteosarcomas {27469493; 11372948; 17351949}. Small cell osteosarcomas have a slightly worse prognosis than conventional osteosarcomas {2804905; 9179055}. With multimodal therapy, secondary osteosarcoma may achieve a prognosis similar to that for primary osteosarcoma {10561175; 25022788}.

In addition to distant metastases, other variables associated with a poor prognosis in osteosarcoma are axial skeleton and proximal extremity locations, large tumour size, incomplete resection, and poor histological response to preoperative chemotherapy {11821461; 19349163; 21030381; 30685685}. The response to preoperative chemotherapy is important for predicting disease-free and overall survival; therefore, an accurate histological assessment of this response is critical {11821461; 21703851; 21030381}. A good response to chemotherapy is histologically defined as chemotherapy-induced necrosis observed in ≥ 90% of tumour cells {6577010; 6174200; 29200101}.

For low-grade central osteosarcoma and parosteal osteosarcoma, the prognosis is excellent. The prognosis for dedifferentiated tumours is comparable to that for conventional osteosarcoma. Low-grade osteosarcomas are cured with complete surgical resection alone {8542697; 8126042; 16123997}. Only dedifferentiated tumours are treated with chemotherapy. Outcomes for patients with periosteal osteosarcoma are also better than those for patients with conventional osteosarcoma, although periosteal cases do have a metastatic risk of about 15%. Intramedullary invasion may portend more aggressive behaviour. The use of adjuvant chemotherapy for periosteal osteosarcoma is debated {16290134; 21472720; 16123997}. The prognosis of high-grade surface osteosarcoma is similar to that of conventional osteosarcoma {10091787; 18300258}.
