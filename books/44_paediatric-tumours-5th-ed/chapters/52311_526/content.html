5.2.3.11 Capillary malformations

Definition

A capillary malformation (CM) is a defect in vasculogenesis resulting in the dilatation of cutaneous capillaries and/or postcapillary venules, affecting the skin and mucosa with frequent extension to the hypodermis and occasionally to deeper structures.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

LA90.0Z Capillary malformation, unspecified

Related terminology

Acceptable: for naevus simplex: naevus flammeus neonatorum; stork bite; angel’s kiss; salmon patch; butterfly patch; for capillary malformation: port-wine stain.

Subtype(s)

Naevus simplex

Localization

CMs are most commonly located in the head and neck region, but they also occur on the trunk and extremities.

Naevus simplex (NS) occurs in the forehead, eyelids, glabella, upper lip, nape of the neck, occiput, upper back, and lumbosacral region.

Clinical features

NS comes to clinical attention at birth as one or more pink to red macules that fade with time; if it persists, it is called a CM. Those in the central forehead, cervical, and lumbosacral regions may be associated with underlying abnormalities of the CNS or spine. The best-known CM is the facial port-wine stain, which manifests usually unilaterally in one or more trigeminal sensory dermatomes and is sometimes accompanied by overgrowth of adjacent mucosa, soft tissue, and bone. Upper facial involvement extending to ocular tissues, leptomeninges, and the cerebral cortex constitutes Sturge–Weber syndrome {26055853}.

CM also commonly occurs in simple or combined vascular malformations (lymphatic, venous, lymphatic-venous, arteriovenous), in PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum conditions such as Klippel–Trénaunay syndrome and CLOVES syndrome (congenital lipomatous overgrowth, vascular malformations, epidermal naevi, and scoliosis or skeletal/spinal anomalies), and in other clinically and genetically distinct vascular entities {26055853; 27456075; 25557259; 25864701; 31111168; 31693582}.

Epidemiology

NS is present in the majority of newborns, with no sex predilection. CM is usually evident at birth, with no sex predilection.

Etiology

A somatic GNAQ mutation is present in Sturge–Weber syndrome, and germline or somatic mutations, particularly in PIK3CA, are present in many conditions and syndromes with a CM component {23656586; 25557259; 25864701; 27456075}.

Pathogenesis

GNAQ mutations have been shown to constitutively activate MAPK and/or YAP1 signalling, and PIK3CA mutations activate the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway {23656586; 29217067; 27259057}.

Macroscopic appearance

See Clinical features (above).

Histopathology

Involved skin shows dispersed small channels with the appearance of dilated capillaries, postcapillary venules, and sometimes also small veins. At a young age, in the classic facial port-wine stain, only minimal vascular changes are present, but over time in some patients, channels become larger, with thicker (mostly fibrous) walls, and are accompanied by stromal fibrosis and appendageal hyperplasia. Cutaneous stains in lymphatic, venous, or lymphatic-venous malformations consist of dilated capillaries and/or postcapillary venules {21095470; 26055853}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

GNAQ or PIK3CA mutations are the most commonly encountered findings.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: dilated capillaries and/or postcapillary venules in an appropriate clinical setting.

Desirable: detection of alterations in GNAQ, PIK3CA, and occasionally other genes is helpful in selected cases.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Some CMs in the head and neck region progress with cutaneous and soft tissue thickening and nodularity.
