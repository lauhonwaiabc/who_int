6.1.4.2 Lipoma

Definition

Lipoma is a benign neoplasm of mature fat.

Clinical features and imaging

The reported incidence of breast lipoma varies from 2.2% to 4.6% {15454196}. Lipomas are well circumscribed, soft, mobile, and painless, although angiolipomas can be painful, with an average diameter of 20 mm, and are usually solitary and occasionally present with breast asymmetry. Lipomas may be challenging to palpate in large breasts or in postmenopausal patients, and difficult to evaluate when deep. Giant lipomas occur and are defined variously as > 50 mm in one dimension and > 500 g {35350481; 23730601; 10603190; 23904731; 35350481; 23730601}.

On imaging, lipomas may be difficult to differentiate from the usual fatty composition of the breast especially on ultrasound. MRI is most useful, showing an isodense lesion that fully represses when fat saturation sequences are used. The mammographic appearance is that of a radiolucent nodule bounded by a radiopaque capsule {23904731; 15454196; 23730601}.

Histopathology

Lipomas are composed of uniform, mature adipocytes with associated fibrous or vascular tissue surrounded by a thin capsule that may or may not be evident. The nuclei appear bland and the cytoplasm is univacuolated. Areas of fat necrosis may be evident. True lipoblasts are absent. The subtypes of breast lipoma include fibrolipoma and chondrolipoma. Angiolipoma and spindle cell lipoma are thought to be separate diagnostic entities.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The FNAB diagnosis of a lipoma can be challenging as smears may yield only fat cells, which is inadequate in the absence of clinical and imaging findings consistent with a lipoma, or fat cells and ductal cells, which may raise the possibility of an epithelial lesion or normal breast. Correlation with the clinical and imaging findings is required {15454196}.

The differential diagnoses include other lesions containing fat, and careful screening for the presence of lipoblasts to exclude the rare liposarcoma is required. Lipoblasts have multivacuolated cytoplasm and an atypical nucleus that is indented and scalloped by the vacuoles. Fat necrosis can be present in both lipomas and liposarcomas. Lipoblastoma of the breast has occasionally been described mainly in young children and consists of mature adipocytes, lipoblasts, and myxoid stroma {17051354}.

The differential diagnosis of an angiolipoma, particularly those with prominent capillaries, includes vascular lesions such as haemangioma, Kaposi sarcoma, and angiosarcoma. Other cartilaginous lesions should be considered in a chondrolipoma. Spindle cell lipoma of the breast has many entities constituting its differential diagnoses (see section Spindle cell tumours (including myofibroblastoma, pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia, nodular fasciitis, schwannoma, and fibromatosis)) {21932353}.

Ancillary testing

On ICC, lipomas stain positively for S100. Stains for vascular tissue, such as CD34, CD31, and ERG, will be positive in an angiolipoma. Most lipomas demonstrate abnormalities of chromosome 12, including trisomy 12. Most angiolipomas demonstrate low-level PRKD2 mutations and some show chromosome 13 rearrangement. Liposarcomas are MDM2-positive on ICC.

Ancillary tests are useful to exclude other lesions. PLAG1 abnormalities are described in a lipoblastoma, while myxoid liposarcoma shows balanced translocations, usually t(12;16)(q13;p11) {32692992}.
