10.1.5 Category: Malignant

Definition: A cytopathology specimen classified as “Malignant” provides unequivocal features of malignancy.

A categorization as “Malignant” is an unequivocal statement that the material is malignant, and wherever possible the report should provide a specific diagnosis or a differential diagnosis of the malignancy present [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]] {31112942; 36412573}. The WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology has further developed the International Academy of Cytology Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology, which provided five categories – “Inadequate/Insufficient”, “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious of malignancy”, and “Malignant” – and standardized terminology, incorporating a definition, ROM, and suggested management algorithm, and provided flexibility for geographical and resource variations in clinical care settings {31112942} [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]].

For epithelial tumours, the most common diagnosis is invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST), with a smaller percentage of lobular carcinomas and other special types, whose diagnosis can be suggested on well-prepared smears supported by a cell block for ICC or other ancillary testing. But the final diagnosis of a carcinoma as a special type and not as a mixed carcinoma requires assessment of the excised lesion. Other epithelial malignancies in the breast include metastasis to the breast with ovary, lung, gastrointestinal, and urinary tract being the most common primary sites {35447141}. Non-epithelial malignant lesions involving the breast broadly include lymphoma, melanoma, malignant phyllodes tumour, and sarcoma {14965721}. Similarly, accurate classification of uncommon or rare malignancies in the breast requires well-prepared smears and a cell block for ICC, in situ hybridization, and in some cases NGS and flow cytometry.

Most mass lesions of the breast categorized on FNAB as “Malignant” will have at least some component of invasive carcinoma, reportedly as many as 97.7%, regardless of whether ductal carcinoma in situ is also present in the lesion {10794158}. Carcinomas, such as high-grade carcinoma NST with or without a medullary pattern and mucinous carcinoma, can be diagnosed by FNAB in the presence of a clinical mass. Similarly, low- and intermediate-grade invasive carcinoma, when correlated in the triple test with a suspicious mass on imaging, or particularly when axillary lymph node metastatic spread has been shown by FNAB, can be routinely diagnosed. A comment on the nuclear grading is feasible where there is sufficient cytopathology material, but formal grading requires tissue biopsy to assess tubule formation, nuclear grade, and mitotic count. Lobular carcinoma may produce relatively scant smears with subtle atypia of tumour cells and may not allow a “Malignant” categorization, and pleomorphic lobular carcinoma, although clearly malignant on FNAB, is difficult to distinguish from high-grade carcinoma NST. In resource-limited settings, FNAB provides an accurate powerful diagnostic approach and may be the only diagnostic tool, and practices will vary.

For cases in which the breast biomarkers ER, PR, HER2 (ERBB2), and Ki-67 are needed to inform treatment decisions, a CNB should be considered to delineate the extent of invasive carcinoma and carcinoma in situ in the mass and to accurately identify which component is staining with ER, PR, HER2, or Ki-67. Further ancillary testing of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes may be estimated on cytopathology material, but at present these tests are still subject to investigation {35367980; 35129867}.

The “Malignant” category has a ROM of 98.7% based on a literature review of 27 studies, with a sensitivity of 96.3%, specificity of 98.8%, positive predictive value of 98.7%, negative predictive value of 95.3%, and false negative and false positive rates of 3.7% and 1%, respectively {31141809; 34876918; 34703093; 35573260; 34535580; 34866246; 33017520; 34133084; 32749785}. A malignant breast FNAB diagnosis has a very low false positive rate with a positive predictive value bordering on 100% in many articles, and false negative diagnoses can occur, particularly in cases with extensive necrosis, marked desmoplasia, or deep location in the breast, and correlation in the triple test is required {9568124}. A low threshold for recommending CNB or excision of lesions for which the cytomorphology and clinical findings are discordant reduces the incidence of misdiagnosis {15569203}.

FNAB of a breast mass can be adequate for management if clinical, imaging, and cytopathological findings correlate, and if there is a cell block with adequate material, prognostic and predictive markers can be performed for definitive treatment, including surgery and neoadjuvant chemotherapy {27383478}. Some centres and clinical trials require CNB, while in some low- and middle-income countries, CNB is not available. Repeat FNAB preferably with ROSE and cell block preparation or a CNB is required if the FNAB is not concordant with the clinical and imaging findings. Clinical examination of the axilla, complemented by ultrasound evaluation of axillary lymph nodes, is required, and if the lymph nodes are suspicious clinically and/or on imaging, FNAB is recommended, followed by CNB if the FNAB is unable to render a firm diagnosis. A positive finding can obviate the necessity for an intraoperative sentinel node procedure. Discussion and consensus on a treatment approach for individual patients at a multidisciplinary meeting is highly recommended.
