7.0.0.1 Introduction

In breast FNAB cytopathology, the terms “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” reflect the spectrum of uncertainty when a definitive diagnosis of either a “Benign” or a “Malignant” lesion cannot be made. Use of the two categories has been demonstrated to improve the accuracy of the ROM for the “Benign” and “Malignant” categories [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]] {31112942; 31141809}.

The pattern of dispersal of tissue fragments and individual cells, the architecture of the tissue fragments, the nuclear features of the epithelial cells, and the background features should be assessed. The specimen is atypical if the overall appearance of the biopsy suggests a benign, reactive, or reparative process, but some features, such as very high cellularity, prominent dispersal of single intact epithelial cells, plentiful small epithelial cell fragments composed of three to four cells, and moderate nuclear enlargement or pleomorphism in the absence of an explanation such as an inflammatory background, prevent an unequivocally benign diagnosis. Elements that suggest a benign diagnosis, such as bare bipolar nuclei in the background, may be absent or reduced, and other background features such as mucin or necrosis may be of concern. The architecture of tissue fragments, such as the presence of complex micropapillary or cribriform architecture despite relatively bland nuclear features, can be regarded as atypical, as distinction between low nuclear grade ductal carcinoma in situ and epithelial hyperplasia is difficult.

An “Atypical” categorization can also be used for biphasic tumours such as cellular fibroadenomas, when the distinction from benign phyllodes tumour cannot be reliably made, and spindle cell proliferations such as fibromatoses. Technical issues which result in suboptimal preparations may render a smear atypical and include air-drying artefact in alcohol-fixed smears for Pap, slow-air-drying artefact in air-dried smears for Giemsa stains, obscuring ultrasound gel, formalin vapour effect, and crush artefact.

In each case the atypical features should be specified, the differential diagnosis discussed, and the most likely diagnosis suggested. It is always recommended that clinical and imaging findings be reviewed and correlated before categorizing a specimen as “Atypical”.
