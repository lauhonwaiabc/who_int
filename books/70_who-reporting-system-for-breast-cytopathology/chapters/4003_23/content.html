4.0.0.3 Immunocytochemistry

Introduction

ICC allows direct visualization of the staining on the lesional cells, and is widely applied in cytopathological assessment of breast lesions, utilizing all antibodies that can be used in histopathology, assisting the cytopathological diagnosis, differentiation between benign and malignant lesions, classification of breast cancers, and prognosis and prediction of response to treatment.

Methodology of preparation of materials

ICC can be performed on different preparations, such as direct smears, cell blocks, and LBC, using different fixatives, for either diagnostic or theragnostic purposes {24836219}. Antigen retrieval and adequate on-slide controls, of the same preparation type that is being used, are essential for good and consistent results {8599924; 14968416}. There are also possible differences between preparations, which are detailed below. In general, performing ICC on smears may show good results, but published literature has shown inconsistencies, particularly in air-dried samples. LBC and cytospin slides behave more consistently. Cell blocks show the best performance overall {35367980}; however, false positives may occur if these are prepared from alcohol-based solutions. This is particularly important in HER2 (ERBB2) staining, where a higher number of 2+ and 3+ results can be expected if alcohol fixation is used {22220518; 19294006}. Counting Ki-67 with clinical impact may only be possible in cell blocks, although this issue is unsettled {35367980; 10955449}.

Immunocytochemistry on direct smears

Direct smears are suitable for ICC, but require a specifically adapted protocol and not the protocol used in histopathology. Both wet-fixed and air-dried postfixed smears may be used. Methanol, ethanol, acetone, and formalin are all suitable fixatives as well as sequential fixation {23244485; 8872443; 24836219}. Postfixation with neutral buffered formalin is useful. ICC on pre-stained smears is also possible.

However, smears show poorer results than cell blocks. Air-drying of smears may diminish ICC performance {11892015; 10588349}. Moreover, ICC is often not anticipated during smearing, and only a limited number of slides are usually available, which precludes staining of multiple markers on multiple slides. Therefore, smears are used when there are no other materials for ICC staining.

Advantages and disadvantages of direct smears
Limited number of spare slides Adequate control slides with known antigen expression are required Few antibodies can be investigated Unwanted background staining due to necrosis of tumour cells Insufficient blocking of endogenous peroxidase Specific but obscuring staining of disrupted cytoplasmic content Both air-dried and alcohol-fixed unstained smears can be used but require different protocols Pre-stained smears may be used, but quality control with controls is problematic Alcohol-fixed and Pap-stained smears may eventually be stained with the same protocol as histopathological specimens Air-dried and Giemsa-stained slides require a different protocol {22574078}
Immunocytochemistry on liquid-based cytopathology preparations

LBC preparations can overcome most of the disadvantages of direct smears for the performance of ICC. Both commercial and homemade fixatives are suitable, as well as physiological saline with/without calf serum or an equivalent medium. Alcohol-based fixatives with a small amount of formalin should not be used for long-term storage in the fluid, but should be prepared within a week to avoid reduced immunoreactivity due to the formalin component {27994636}. Long-term storage of methanol-based preparations and residual material in liquid is possible for 3 months at −20°C or 6 months at −74°C without loss of antigenicity {21298022}. Stored, residual material is suitable as positive/negative controls.

Common causes of false negative and false positive findings in immunocytochemistry on direct smears and liquid-based cytopathology preparations
Misinterpretation of neoplastic cell populations Insufficient peroxidase blocking Necrotic cells Inappropriate handling during the staining procedure Nonspecific antibodies binding and cross-reactivity Inappropriate fixation: insufficient or prolonged formalin fixation Antigen diffusion, due to antibody concentration
Immunocytochemistry on cell blocks

Cell blocks are prepared from cytopathological materials using the collodion method, thrombin, agar, or other gel materials (see section Cell preparation methods) on cell preparation. They are then transferred for routine formalin fixation and histopathological processing, and made into paraffin blocks for histopathological assessment including ICC.

Advantages and disadvantages of cell blocks
A large number of slides can be cut from the prepared paraffin block Adequate standard control slides with known antigen expression are available Many antibodies can be investigated: the range of ICC tests is the same as for histopathology With a gel/thrombin clot in the background, there is minimal unwanted material, contaminants, or necrotic debris, and thus interpretation is easy All residual materials in the supernatant can be caught after centrifugation and made into the cell block, and thus there is minimal material loss A suboptimal FNAB can be salvaged, which has no material in the smears
Use of immunocytochemistry

Irrespective of the type of cytopathological specimen, it is well suited for ancillary techniques, such as ICC {35367980; 31141801}. The main diagnostic applications are differential diagnosis between benign and malignant epithelial proliferative lesions, including papillary lesions; subtyping of breast carcinomas; diagnosis of spindle cell lesions, metastatic tumours in the breast, and non-epithelial lesions; and confirmation of breast origin of metastases. Myoepithelial markers including p63, 34βE12, SMA, high-molecular-weight cytokeratin, BCL2, CD34, and calponin have been recommended for evaluation of proliferative epithelial lesions, including papillary lesions and spindle cell lesions {10945900; 12811858; 17580273; 27060708}.

Likewise, evaluation of prognostic and predictive markers including ER, PR, and HER2 is feasible in cytopathological preparations from both primary and metastatic breast carcinomas. Reported concordance rates and accuracy compared with frozen sections and paraffin-embedded histopathological materials are 96–99% for ER and 90–96% for PR for smears, LBC preparations, and cell blocks {8599924; 21174117; 22783929; 22807465; 22298467}. The concordance rate for HER2 has been reported as 88–100% for smears, LBC preparations, and cell blocks {22807465; 15048960; 22298467; 27383478}. The evaluation of ER, PR, and HER2 in cytopathological specimens has been endorsed in the current guidelines {20586616}. Test conditions need to conform to the guideline specifications and at least 100 cells should be assessed. Ductal carcinoma in situ may show higher rates of marker expression than invasive carcinoma, particularly for HER2 {1548522}, and there is a risk that marker assessment may falsely be done on ductal carcinoma in situ leading to possible mistreatment, because in cytopathological specimens, ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive cancers may not be reliably distinguished. In addition, American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) / College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines recommend using only formalin fixation for samples for HER2 testing. In this regard, formalin-fixed cell blocks for HER2 testing are more appropriate, and ICC for HER2 can be supplemented by silver in situ hybridization and FISH.

Subtyping of breast carcinomas by ICC includes assessing markers for metaplastic carcinoma, lobular carcinoma, carcinomas with endocrine differentiation, sarcomas, and malignant lymphomas, and will be covered in the respective sections of this book. ICC confirmation of a metastatic lesion originating in the breast includes, but is not limited to, assessment of ER, GCDFP-15, GATA3, and TRPS1 (see section Metastatic tumours). Diagnosis of tumours metastatic to the breast may include a larger diagnostic ICC panel depending on the history and morphological differential diagnoses.
