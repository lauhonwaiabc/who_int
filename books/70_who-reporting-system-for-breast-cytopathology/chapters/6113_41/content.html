6.1.1.3 Lymphoid hyperplasia and related disorders

Definition

Intramammary lymphadenopathy consists of lymph nodes that are surrounded on all sides by breast tissue.

Clinical features and imaging

Intramammary lymphadenopathy is seen in 5% of patients undergoing mammography {36270911}, most commonly in the upper outer quadrant, and can be palpable or impalpable. If palpable, the mass is firm and mobile, and can mimic a fibroadenoma. Causes of prominent benign intramammary lymphadenopathy include diabetic mastitis, infectious processes such as HIV/AIDS and toxoplasmosis, sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy, silicone-induced lymphadenopathy, and Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease {15569209; 19943128; 20572291; 27073709; 21816047; 17880544; 22749923}. Intramammary lymph nodes can be seen with synchronous or metachronous breast malignancies, in addition to breast malignancies spreading to intramammary lymph nodes {36270911}.

Intramammary lymph nodes are seen on breast imaging as a well-circumscribed oval or reniform mass with a fatty hilum and no calcifications, as large as 30 mm but usually 10 mm or less. If imaging shows an intramammary lymph node that is larger, round or lobulated, and not well circumscribed with loss of fatty hilum and calcifications, then they are deemed abnormal and FNAB is required {31322919; 22749923; 36270911; 38424849; 33012115}.

Histopathology

The cortex of an intramammary lymph node comprises variably sized reactive primary follicles without a germinal centre and secondary follicles with a germinal centre. The paracortical areas consists of T lymphocytes intermixed with immunoblasts and dendritic cells, while medullary cords and sinuses containing histiocytes make up the medulla of the lymph node.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate to high cellularity Mixed population of predominantly small lymphocytes, medium and large lymphoid cells, and tingible-body macrophages in a background containing lymphoid cell cytoplasmic fragments Sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy shows emperipolesis with intact lymphocytes or apoptotic debris, sometimes surrounded by a halo in the cytoplasm of large histiocytes Leaked silicone from a prosthesis is seen in large vacuolated multinucleated histiocytes as globular material Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease will contain phagocytic histiocytes with crescentic nuclei, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and necrosis

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB is useful to diagnose reactive lymphoid hyperplasia and exclude other conditions, both benign and malignant.

Granulomatous lesions in a background of lymphocytes have epithelioid histiocytes and giant cells and are distinguished from a lymph node based on imaging. Lymphocytes may also be seen in diabetic mastopathy, which yields scant fibrotic and ductal epithelium. Seromas may develop after breast surgery and show small lymphocytes, histiocytes, and occasional neutrophils with debris in a variably proteinaceous background.

Small cell lymphomas such as extranodal marginal zone and follicular lymphomas can occur in the breast. Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma shows a mix of lymphocytes, small lymphoid cells, monocytoid cells, plasmacytic cells, and plasma cells, and the distinction from reactive lymphoid hyperplasia is challenging and usually requires flow cytometry and CNB or excision biopsy. Follicular lymphoma can present with a predominant small centrocytic cell pattern, but usually will have follicles with dendritic cells and lack tingible-body macrophages.

Lobular carcinoma may present with dispersed small cells, but these are larger than lymphocytes, have mildly atypical and irregular nuclei and eccentric cytoplasm with a variable number of intracytoplasmic vacuoles, and usually include minute tissue fragments, some of which are single file. The medullary pattern of carcinoma of the breast of no special type (NST), can present with numerous lymphocytes, but interspersed large tumour cells are present with high-grade nuclei and scattered syncytial tissue fragments often infiltrated by lymphocytes. Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), which is almost always metastatic, shows intermediate cells with a high N:C ratio, atypical nuclei, and granular chromatin, often in tissue fragments showing nuclear moulding associated with chromatin smearing and apoptotic debris {15569209; 17880544; 20132132; 20132132; 24261323}.

Ancillary testing

Flow cytometry and ICC on cell blocks can be used to confirm lymphoid hyperplasia, which has no light chain restriction and stains positively for a wide range of B, T, and NK lymphocyte markers.

Microbiological investigations can exclude granulomatous mastitis. Correlation of relevant clinical and imaging findings with cytopathology will aid in excluding diabetic mastopathy. The histiocytes in sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy are positive for CD68, CD163, and OCT2, and negative for CD1a. In silicone lymphadenopathy, globular virtually clear material is present, while in Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, abundant T lymphocytes can be demonstrated with more CD8 than CD4 cells. The lymphoid cells in a seroma are predominantly T lymphocytes.

The cells of extranodal marginal zone lymphoma are typically positive for CD19, CD20, CD79a, PAX5, and IgM, while CD5, CD10, CD23, BCL6, cyclin D1, and SOX11 are negative. Follicular lymphoma will be positive for CD10, CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a, BCL6, and BCL2, and negative for CD3, CD5, CD43, and cyclin D1.

Primary or metastatic carcinoma will be positive for pankeratin, and in NEC, synaptophysin, chromogranin, and INSM1 will be positive {35402679; 24261323; 34985232; 19306424; 20132132; 30156771; 34791787}.
