2.0.0.5 Risk of malignancy and management recommendations

The ROM for the diagnostic categories of the WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology (WHO System) are based on published studies assessing the International Academy of Cytopathology Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology (IAC Yokohama System), introduced in 2020. Since its release, many institutions have published their experience with the IAC Yokohama System {30783035; 31524134; 33017520; 30929288; 32749785; 31108486; 33755356; 34133084; 34029453; 33629823; 34321772; 34866246; 34876918; 34515039; 34535580; 34703093; 36516743; 36988122}. The estimated ROM associated with the diagnostic categories and clinical management recommendations are shown in #39313Table 1.01.

These studies based on the IAC Yokohama System utilized a five-tiered schema that translates well into the WHO System. In developing the ROM for the categories of the WHO System, studies were included if patient cohorts consisted of patients presenting with palpable or screen-detected breast lesions and specimens were evaluated using the IAC Yokohama System, either retrospectively or prospectively, and excluded if only a subgroup of patients (e.g. male patients) were examined. Eighteen studies met these criteria and were analysed (#39314Table 1.02). These studies vary in their cohort size, ranging from 118 to 1745 patients, and study nature, as retrospective or prospective. Despite these differences, the reported ROM for the “Benign” category ranged from 0% to 11.7% and the ROM for “Malignant” ranged from 96.8% to 100%. The IAC Yokohama System used two particular retrospective studies to establish its ROM: one study was based on a practice in which the FNAB was performed by an experienced cytopathologist using ultrasound guidance, and the other presented data based on two practices, one in which the FNAB was performed by an experienced breast physician with ROSE and a second in which an experienced radiologist performed the FNAB without ROSE. The combined ROM of these two studies was 2.6–4.8% for the “Insufficient”, 1.4–2.3% for the “Benign”, 13–15.7% for the “Atypical”, 84.6–97.1% for the “Suspicious”, and 99.0–100% for the “Malignant” categories {30783035; 31108486}.

A multidisciplinary approach is paramount in providing accurate appraisal of breast lesions. Clear communication among surgical and medical oncologists, radiologists, and cytopathologists is essential in this diagnostic approach. Clinical, imaging, and cytopathological findings should be correlated, resulting in the triple test. Further diagnostic testing, typically with a CNB, may be required in cases with one discordant component in the triple test. However, in some situations, particularly where clinical and imaging suspicions for malignancy are high and FNAB yields a sample classified in the “Suspicious for malignancy” category, the patient may receive surgical management with excisional biopsy. In other situations, depending on the equipment availability and therapeutic options at treating facilities, patients with a concordant triple test showing malignancy may still need to undergo CNB. Depending on the clinical stage, patient age, and breast biomarker status, that is, ER, PR, and HER2 status, patients may receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy after a “Malignant” categorization with carcinoma present. In such cases, if there is insufficient material in the cell block, CNB may be needed to obtain sufficient tissue with appropriate fixation requirements to assess the tumour’s receptor status.
