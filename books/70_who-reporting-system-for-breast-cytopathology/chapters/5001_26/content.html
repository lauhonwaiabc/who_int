5.0.0.1 Introduction

“Insufficient”, “Inadequate”, or “Non-diagnostic” FNAB samples are unable to provide diagnostic information regarding the lesion undergoing FNAB. The preferred terminology for such unsatisfactory samples may differ among institutions so three options are provided to allow a familiar term for the clinical team. Each laboratory should pick one term and use it consistently for all such reports. Regardless of the term used, the reason for the classification of FNAB material as “Insufficient / Inadequate / Non-diagnostic” should always be provided in the final report.

Determination of a FNAB sample’s adequacy or inadequacy incorporates assessment of the quality and quantity of cellular material relative to the clinical and imaging assessments of the targeted lesion. The nature of the targeted lesion informs the criteria for adequacy. The requirement for a minimum number of epithelial cells to consider a specimen adequate has been proposed in some publications {9100536; 9477100; 9208973; 9915135}. However, strict adherence to such a minimum epithelial cellularity requirement is not appropriate for cysts with their histiocytes in a proteinaceous background, mastitis/abscess with often only acute inflammatory cells, and intramammary lymph nodes with their polymorphous lymphoid material and predominance of lymphocytes, and clinical–imaging correlation is required. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology suggested, as a guide, a minimum of “seven tissue fragments, each consisting of 20 or more epithelial cells” in the setting of a palpable mass or impalpable solid mass lesion seen on imaging {31112942}. This is considered further in the discussion.

If any atypical cytopathological features are identified, the biopsy material should be considered “Atypical” rather than “Insufficient / Inadequate / Non-diagnostic”.

In addition to cellularity, samples may be rendered “Insufficient / Inadequate / Non-diagnostic” owing to technical factors, such as poor cell preservation related to air-drying artefact of alcohol-fixed smears, slow-air-drying artefact of air-dried slides, poor preparation due to poor smearing technique, or obscuring elements such as blood or ultrasound gel. Contributing factors also relate to the experience of the FNAB operator, experience of the FNAB smear-maker, and the intrinsic properties of the targeted lesion.
