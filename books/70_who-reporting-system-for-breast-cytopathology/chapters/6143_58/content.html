6.1.4.3 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a neuroectodermal tumour originating from Schwann cells and is composed of polygonal cells with abundant granular cytoplasm containing lysosomes.

Clinical features and imaging

Granular cell tumours of the breast account for 1 in every 1000 breast tumours and 6–8% of all granular cell tumours, occurring at an average age of 40 years {15569210} and presenting as single, small, palpable lesions that may be gritty or hard on palpation. Granular cell tumour can uncommonly reach as much as 50 mm in maximum dimension. Multicentricity is reported in 12.5–18% of cases {11348331; 15569210}. Most tumours are benign, and only 1–2% are malignant {9669341}.

On mammography, granular cell tumours vary from round and circumscribed to irregular spiculated masses lacking calcification, and can mimic malignancy. Ultrasound shows irregular, hypoechoic, and indistinctly marginated tumours {15569210; 11348331}.

Histopathology

Granular cell tumours are yellow-white and scirrhous, mimicking a carcinoma. They are composed of cells with abundant granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm and small, round, deeply basophilic nuclei with minimal atypia, and usually inconspicuous nucleoli. The cells are arranged in sheets and trabeculae in an infiltrating pattern {15569210; 11348331; 20471560}. The features associated with rare malignant granular cell tumour include spindling, larger nuclei, perinucleolar chromatin clearing, prominent nucleoli, increased mitotic figures and Ki-67 (MIB1) indices, and necrosis {9669341}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate cellularity Cohesive tissue fragments and sheets, and singly scattered polygonal cells and stripped nuclei A low N:C ratio with small, regular, round to oval nuclei with mild to moderate anisokaryosis Abundant, granular cytoplasm due to accumulation of lysosomes that is fragile, resulting in stripped nuclei and a granular background Malignant granular cell tumours show an increased N:C ratio, nuclear hyperchromasia, prominent nucleoli, mitotic figures, and a necrotic background

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes invasive breast carcinoma, particularly the apocrine type, metastatic renal cell carcinoma, and alveolar soft part sarcoma, as well as metaplastic apocrine cells and histiocytes {10607009; 15569210}. Histiocytes have kidney or footprint shaped nuclei, and abundant eccentric foamy rather than granular cytoplasm that may show phagocytosed debris. Apocrine carcinomas usually have higher N:C ratios and a greater degree of nuclear pleomorphism, and form more distinct tissue fragments. Metastatic renal cell carcinoma shows pale to “clear” cytoplasm and more marked nuclear hyperchromasia and pleomorphism. Alveolar soft part sarcoma shows distinct cytoplasmic borders within tissue fragments and large more-pleomorphic hyperchromatic nuclei with prominent nucleoli and perinucleolar clearing.

Ancillary testing

Granular cell tumours are positive for DPAS and in ICC they are positive for S100, CD68, inhibin, calretinin, CD63 (NKI/C3 clone), TFE3, and MITF {15569210; 20471560; 26009539}. Pankeratin markers and AR are negative, excluding apocrine carcinoma, and although histiocytes are also CD68-positive, they are negative for S100. The S100 positivity may raise the possibility of a melanoma, but granular cell tumours are negative for HMB45 and melan-A. Granular cell tumours stain positive for TFE3 on ICC but lack TFE3 gene rearrangement {26009539; 31788112}.
