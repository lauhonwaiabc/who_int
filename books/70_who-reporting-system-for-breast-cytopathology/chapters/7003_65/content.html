7.0.0.3 Discussion and background

Atypical breast cytopathology reflects an indeterminate lesion with predominantly benign features. Subtle abnormalities, such as mild nuclear pleomorphism, cellular discohesion, or a lack of myoepithelial cells, may prohibit a straightforward benign diagnosis. An “Atypical” category has been variably used in breast cytopathology and in one early study, with a five-tiered classification system, made up 3.4% of a large study cohort with a ROM of 30.2% {11488870}. Some past studies have utilized subgroups of “atypical” or merged “atypical, favour benign” with “suspicious, favour malignant” into an “indeterminate” category for analysis. Use of terms such as “dubious”, “inconclusive”, “indeterminate”, or “equivocal” is discouraged. A standardized diagnostic category of “Atypical” creates more clarity for lesions that show mostly benign features but have other features that warrant follow-up {7754536; 10096355; 16570117; 26466750}.

The histopathological correlates of the majority of breast lesions with an “Atypical” diagnosis are benign epithelial proliferative changes, predominantly fibroadenomas, intraductal papillomas, sclerosing adenosis, usual ductal epithelial hyperplasia, columnar cell change, and radial scars / complex sclerosing lesions. In these settings, single epithelial cell dispersal or small tissue fragments of epithelial cells are frequently seen, and if other characteristic or diagnostic features of these lesions are lacking, the specimen is regarded as “Atypical”. Examples include a fibroadenoma in which there are relatively few bare bipolar nuclei and no fibromyxoid stromal fragments, or a radial scar without apocrine sheets and larger benign epithelial tissue fragments.

Other cytopathological features included in the “Atypical” category may be papillary fragments (see section Intraductal papilloma), lobular-type cells, marked nuclear crowding, atypical apocrine cells, and low-grade spindle cell or some vascular proliferations. The spindle cell lesions pose a particular dilemma as these cases are frequently of low cellularity and offer a wide differential diagnosis, encompassing fibromatosis, adenomyoepithelioma, myofibroblastoma, and low-grade and borderline phyllodes tumours, in addition to subcutaneous soft tissue neoplasms, such as myxoma, schwannoma, and neurofibroma.

Malignant lesions which may show atypical rather than suspicious cytopathology are generally low-grade invasive carcinomas of no special type (NST), special type carcinomas such as tubular and mixed tubular-lobular carcinomas, low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, and lobular carcinoma {11488870}.

An “Atypical” categorization may also be operator dependent and related to suboptimal FNAB technique and smear preparation, or inexperience in diagnosis {9626523; 11507700; 31112942}. The availability of ROSE is also likely to influence the atypical rate, with the potential to markedly decrease the number of “Atypical” cases due to technical factors or insufficient material {31108486; 31137023}. Although the standard in this publication is based on patterns of direct smears, consideration should also be given to variation due to LBC preparation techniques if those are encountered in diagnostic practice {25115652}.

Depending on the experience of the cytopathologist and practice pattern, an “Atypical” categorization should ideally be reserved for only a small proportion of cases and should always be correlated with clinical and imaging features. Allowing a degree of uncertainty in a cytopathology diagnosis of “Atypical” provides the advantage of increasing the negative predictive value of a true “Benign” diagnosis and the positive predictive value of a true “Malignant” diagnosis.
