3.1.0.5 Assessment of the axilla

Introduction

The literature supports preoperative axillary node pathological assessment to confirm metastatic tumour in nodes which are suspicious on imaging and provide maximal information for the clinical team {12618878; 22473359} [[Raymond WA, Kleinig P. The value of fine needle aspiration biopsy in the preâ€‘operative assessment of the axilla in breast cancer patients. J Mol Pathol. 2022 Oct 20;3(4):228–242. doi:10.3390/jmp3040020.]]. This may assist in triaging for sentinel node biopsy or axillary dissection, and in determining if neoadjuvant chemotherapy is appropriate. As clinically palpable nodes are uncommon, most lymphadenopathy is detected after routine ultrasound axillary screening or, increasingly, confirmed on second-look targeted ultrasound after abnormal node detection by staging/screening cross-sectional CT or MRI. The criteria defining an abnormal node on ultrasound are based on the relationship of the cortex of the node to the hilum. Metastases are most likely if the cortical thickness is > 3 mm and there is focal cortical lobulation, irregularity of the cortical outline, an absent or displaced fatty hilum, and a rounded nodal shape {34469221}.

Technical factors

The overall accuracy of ultrasound-detected nodal disease is 60%, with as many as 36% of sonographically normal nodes harbouring tumour deposits {34106588}. Several factors affect diagnostic accuracy, including equipment quality, radiologist and cytopathologist expertise, and biopsy technique. Optimally, the axilla should be explored with a high-frequency linear matrix array transducer, with tissue harmonic imaging to increase contrast and resolution, and with Doppler to detect non-hilar blood flow {32315268}. Operator experience will also play a role in detection and in biopsy of the abnormal node. Accuracy of needle placement and stabilizing the node, good smear technique, and avoidance of artefacts, in particular ultrasound gel, are the same factors described as important in optimizing the result of FNAB in the breast (see section Fine-needle aspiration biopsy techniques and specimen management).

Diagnostic accuracy

Most recent studies demonstrate a sensitivity > 70% (63–91%), negative predictive value > 70% (59–83%), and specificity and positive predictive value of 94–100% {31041851; 27051394} [[Júnior WJA, Barra AD, Silva HM, et al. Fine needle aspiration cytology guided by ultrasound of suspicious axillary lymph nodes in breast cancer patients. Hematol Med Oncol. 2018;3(3):1–6. doi:10.15761/HMO.1000163.]].

Reported false negative rates also vary depending on whether true negative nodes and unsatisfactory smears are included in the analysis and range from 8% to 31% {32568448}. The most common cause of a false negative is sampling error which, in turn, is generally due to the small size of the metastatic deposit. Other causes are failure to image all the nodes, missing nodes lateral to the thoracodorsal trunk (lateral level 1) or posterior along the border of the subscapularis muscle (subscapular node), difficulty in interpreting low nuclear grade neoplastic cells (such as a dispersed metastatic lobular carcinoma), smear artefacts, and inexperience of the cytopathologist.

False positive rates are reported at < 1% and potential cytopathological traps are misinterpretation of germinal-centre material, epithelioid histiocytes (which may be aggregated in granulomatous inflammation), and heterotopic benign breast epithelial fragments. Mistaking other metastatic tumours, such as Merkel cell carcinoma, which may cytopathologically mimic lobular carcinoma cells, or lymphoid neoplasms will have clinical impact, even if the diagnosis of malignant cells is correct, and should always be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Several meta-analyses have concluded that although there is slightly higher sensitivity and diagnostic accuracy of CNB of axillary nodes when compared with FNAB, both have a role {32962089; 34357003}. The FNAB advantages of ease in accessing deep-seated nodes and of sampling multiple nodes, as well as the more general lower cost and minimal invasiveness, support the use of FNAB as a primary tool. Both core biopsy and FNAB will potentially miss microscopic metastases (defined as < 2 mm) {18797874}.

Clinical utility

The overall clinical utility of FNAB in reducing the need for a two-stage axillary surgical procedure {25009748; 30893685} is reported to be 20%. Some studies have indicated a higher metastatic burden in patients with a FNAB-positive node. Castellano et al. identified three or more nodes involved at axillary dissection in 61% of patients with a single FNAB-positive node {25207643}.

Despite the argument that preoperative staging by FNAB and intraoperative assessment of sentinel nodes is unnecessary after recent changes to surgical management of the axilla for some patients with only one or two involved sentinel nodes and T1 or T2 primary tumours where axillary dissection is not performed {28898379; 35803776}, the patient population targeted for axillary node FNAB is generally different. In addition, most nodes with microscopic metastases and isolated tumour cells will not be detected on imaging and would not therefore be biopsied. Even if the node is biopsied and the metastasis is “missed”, this is not of clinical significance as these patients would appropriately be triaged to a sentinel lymph node biopsy.

Intraoperative sentinel lymph node assessment

The role of cytopathology in the intraoperative assessment of sentinel lymph nodes has also been widely accepted, predominantly as imprint preparations, or occasionally as scrape smears, with reports of 82.7–88% sensitivity, 98–99.3% specificity, and 94–95.9% accuracy for diagnosing metastases {21487846; 35341111}. Light touch imprints of the cut surface of the fresh sentinel node will allow ready evaluation of the sentinel node without loss of tissue. The interpretative caveats are identical to those for interpreting FNAB preparations.

Summary

In conclusion, ultrasound-guided FNAB of axillary nodes in patients with breast cancer is a highly sensitive procedure and provides useful information to the clinical team, while accepting that it is not an absolute triage for axillary node management. It is of particular value in the setting of multiple enlarged nodes, whereby FNAB of several nodes may be easily performed if required. If the FNAB is negative and the imaging suspicion is high, CNB biopsy would be appropriate, particularly in the setting of known lobular carcinoma, or if a metastasis other than a breast primary is considered.
