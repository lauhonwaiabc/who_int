6.1.3.1 Fibroadenoma and benign phyllodes tumour

Definition

Fibroadenoma is a circumscribed benign neoplasm of the terminal duct lobular unit with biphasic proliferation of epithelial and stromal components.

Benign phyllodes tumour (BPT) is a circumscribed fibroepithelial neoplasm showing a prominent intracanalicular architectural pattern with leaf-like stromal fronds, capped by luminal epithelial and myoepithelial cell layers, accompanied by mild stromal hypercellularity.

Clinical features and imaging

Fibroadenoma commonly occurs in women in the reproductive age group although it can be encountered at all ages. It is the most frequent benign breast neoplasm in female patients. It has been reported in healthy men including those with gynaecomastia {8159950}. Fibroadenoma presents as a painless, solitary, firm, usually slow-growing, mobile, well-defined nodule < 30 mm. Metachronous and synchronous multiplicity and bilaterality can occur. Giant fibroadenomas, defined as those > 50 mm, may occur in adolescent girls. Mammographically, fibroadenoma presents as a nodular density or calcified nodule.

BPT is significantly less common than fibroadenoma, usually occurring in older women, presenting as a unilateral firm mobile mass averaging 40–50 mm that can grow to very large sizes of > 100 mm, distorting the breast and causing skin erosion or ulceration due to pressure ischaemia {22049216}.

Fibroadenomas are usually smaller and ovoid in shape with circumscribed margins in comparison to phyllodes tumours, which are similarly rounded and well circumscribed, but are larger and bosselated, and sometimes clefted, cystic, and calcified. Precise distinction between fibroadenoma and BPT cannot be consistently achieved on imaging {27239174}.

Histopathology

Fibroadenomas show intracanalicular growth with stroma bulging into and compressing the epithelium, and/or pericanalicular growth with stroma surrounding tubular glands. These patterns do not have clinical significance apart from the more frequent association of intracanalicular fibroadenoma with MED12 mutations {25839987; 25855048}, and juvenile fibroadenoma often disclosing a pericanalicular pattern {32461622}. Fibroadenoma can show various other patterns, including cellular, juvenile, myxoid, and complex forms.

Cellular fibroadenoma shows a mild to moderate increase in stromal cellularity with infrequent mitoses (< 1 per mm2), but in contrast to BPT, which it closely mimics, the cellular fibroadenoma lacks stromal atypia, an exaggerated intracanalicular architecture, periductal stromal accentuation, and prominent intratumoural heterogeneity.

Juvenile fibroadenoma shows a predominantly pericanalicular growth pattern with a cellular stroma disposed in intersecting fibroblastic and myofibroblastic fascicles. The epithelium often displays usual ductal hyperplasia of gynaecomastoid type with delicate luminal epithelial tufts. Although the juvenile fibroadenoma pattern is the most common pattern seen in the paediatric and adolescent population {24998513}, it can be seen in adult female patients as well.

Myxoid fibroadenoma shows a loose hypocellular stroma with abundant myxoid ground substance and can be associated with Carney complex.

Complex fibroadenoma shows sclerosing adenosis, cysts > 3 mm, epithelial calcifications, and papillary apocrine metaplasia. It is reported to be associated with a slightly increased relative risk (2.27–3.1 times that of the general population) of subsequent breast cancer, attributed to its epithelial proliferative changes.

There is a spectrum of benign, borderline, and malignant phyllodes tumours based on a constellation of histopathological parameters, which are assessed in combination and include stromal hypercellularity, stromal nuclear atypia, mitoses, stromal overgrowth (where stroma forms the bulk, or in malignant phyllodes tumour almost all, of the tumour), and tumour borders ranging from circumscribed to an increasingly infiltrating margin. BPT shows an exaggerated or pronounced intracanalicular growth pattern with a broad fronded or “leaf-like” architecture in which the fronds bulge into canalicular spaces, accompanied by mildly increased stromal cellularity, no or mild atypia of stromal nuclei, no stromal overgrowth, a low increase in stromal mitoses (< 2 per mm2), an absence of stromal heterologous components, and well-defined borders.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Fibroadenoma and BPT can show heterogeneity in their epithelial and stromal components, and both FNAB and CNB cannot reliably separate these tumours {30284391}. Fibroadenomas usually produce highly cellular smears, but because they also show a wide variation in their stroma from myxoid to sclerotic, some will yield slightly cellular smears due to fibrotic, hypocellular stroma, while others will show abundant myxoid stroma and a finely granular proteinaceous background. Fibroadenomas may also only yield abundant hyperplastic ductal epithelial tissue fragments with minimal or no stroma, although usually there is a large number of bare bipolar nuclei in the background.

The triple approach combining clinical and imaging assessment with cytopathological findings is needed for management, with symptomatic presentation and large size often warranting consideration for conservative excision even when the cytopathological findings are benign.

Fibroadenoma is one of the most common reasons for a false positive diagnosis in breast cytopathology owing to its frequently high cellularity, focal dispersed intact cells, and occasional nuclear atypia. In particular, complex fibroadenoma can show cellular discohesion, nuclear enlargement, nucleolar prominence, and fewer myoepithelial cells than conventional fibroadenoma {26249102}. Myxoid fibroadenoma can produce a finely granular mucinous material, raising the differential diagnosis of mucinous lesions of the breast, including mucinous carcinoma {26104545}.

Infarction can lead to additional atypical cytopathological features with necrotic debris and siderophages and epithelial nuclear atypia.

Hamartomas have similar imaging to fibroadenomas but yield scant cellularity and lobular and ductal epithelial and stromal components showing no nuclear atypia. Tubular adenomas show moderate to high cellularity, fragments that include stroma and tubules resembling normal lobules, tubular epithelial fragments with myoepithelial cells, and limited bare bipolar nuclei and stroma. Adenomyoepithelioma is another difficult differential diagnosis, showing high cellularity with characteristic biphasic tissue fragments featuring epithelioid cells with spindle cells and dispersed spindle cells. Low-grade invasive carcinomas of no special type (NST) can show moderate to high cellularity with often large, sometimes complex epithelial tissue fragments along with smaller crowded epithelial tissue fragments and a variable number of dispersed intact cells showing low nuclear grade, but lack myoepithelial nuclei in the epithelial tissue fragments and bare bipolar nuclei in the background.

Awareness of pitfalls and close correlation with clinical and imaging findings are needed to avoid misdiagnoses. LBC presents additional challenges with loss of pattern recognition features, greater dispersal of intact cells, and nuclear features with prominent nucleoli and atypia that mimic cancer.

Ancillary testing

Some authors have proposed using myoepithelial markers such as p63 and CK5/6 to distinguish fibroadenoma from ductal carcinoma in situ {28685877}.
