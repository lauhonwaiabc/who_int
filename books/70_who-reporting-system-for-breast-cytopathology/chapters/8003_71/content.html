8.0.0.3 Discussion and background

The most common follow-up histopathological diagnosis in tumours categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” on cytopathology is invasive carcinoma, the majority carcinoma of no special type (NST) {33017520; 32749785; 31108486; 33755356; 34029453; 33629823; 34866246; 34515039; 34535580; 34703093}. Ductal carcinoma in situ is also overrepresented in this category {34029453; 32749785; 31108486; 34703093; 33755356}, as is lobular carcinoma {32749785; 31108486}. Fibroadenoma (especially cellular fibroadenoma), benign phyllodes tumours, intraductal papilloma, and sclerosing adenosis account for most of the histopathological benign cases on follow-up of this category {7992576; 34703093; 32749785; 33017520; 33629823; 34535580; 33755356; 10458501}.

Possible reasons for making a “Suspicious for malignancy” rather than a “Malignant” diagnosis include low cellularity and preparation artefact affecting cytomorphological quality and obscuring unequivocal cytopathological features of malignancy {7992576}. Low cellularity may be intrinsic to the lesion, such as a carcinoma with a fibrotic desmoplastic background, which can be seen in many invasive lobular carcinomas or low-grade invasive carcinomas NST, and metaplastic carcinomas {10934957; 9250310; 16798932}. Insufficient cellularity and cellular preservation can also be associated with lack of experience on the part of the operator performing the FNAB {36412573}. Technical limitations include excessive blood or debris obscuring cellular features, crush artefact, air-drying artefact of alcohol-fixed smears, slow-air-drying artefact of air-dried Giemsa-stained smears, and suboptimal staining {7992576; 24021880}.

Sampling from a necrotic area in high-grade carcinomas or high-grade ductal carcinoma in situ will result in low cellularity as well as obscuring cellular elements. There may be excessive blood in papillary lesions or vascular lesions such as angiosarcomas. The nuclear changes in low-grade invasive carcinoma and low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ are often subtle on cytopathology with few abnormal single cells or only mildly enlarged and irregular nuclei, causing difficulty in interpretation. The distinction between invasive carcinoma and carcinoma in situ is not possible based on cytopathology material alone, although most mass-forming lesions with malignant cytopathology have underlying invasive carcinoma, and in some cases FNAB may yield stromal fragments infiltrated by carcinoma.

Fibroadenoma and papilloma may show enlarged nuclei and prominent nucleoli {8291351; 11747216}. Myxoid change in a fibroadenoma may be extensive enough to consider mucinous carcinoma {7992576; 11747216}. Phyllodes tumours may be associated with enlarged epithelial cells and ductal hyperplasia {15743740; 1311889; 33017520}. High cellularity, nuclear atypia, and exuberant ductal epithelial proliferation can be associated with radial scars / complex sclerosing lesions {10458501}.

To reduce the “Suspicious for malignancy” category due to interpretation errors, ICC stains may be applied on cell blocks or suitable smears, with a combination of myoepithelial markers, such as p63 and calponin, and the high-molecular-weight cytokeratin marker CK5/6, which are positive in benign, hyperplastic epithelium and negative in low-grade malignancies {27060708; 25810244}.
