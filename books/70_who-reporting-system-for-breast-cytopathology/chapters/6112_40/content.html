6.1.1.2 Granulomatous inflammation

Definition

Granulomatous inflammation is a chronic inflammatory process characterized by granulomas composed of epithelioid histiocytes with or without necrosis, and histiocytic multinucleated giant cells with a variable admixture of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils.

Clinical features and imaging

Causes of granulomatous mastitis include infections which can be bacterial, mycobacterial, fungal, and parasitic; causes can be non-infectious, such as sarcoid and foreign body reactions; and some cases are idiopathic. In granulomatous lobular mastitis, the granulomas are centred on the lobules, and Corynebacterium spp. infection is often the cause. Predisposing factors include oral contraceptives, lactational changes, particularly with milk stasis, blunt trauma, autoimmune disease, or as-yet-undetected infectious agents. Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis is a diagnosis of exclusion and the granulomas are non-necrotizing {25940456; 35473758}.

Patients with granulomatous mastitis present with a mass, or less commonly pain, nipple discharge, and sinus formation. Granulomatous lobular mastitis and idiopathic granulomatous mastitis affect women mostly in their reproductive years (mean age: 33–35 years) {29971624; 31968055}, presenting with a painful, palpable mass that may mimic a bacterial abscess or malignant neoplasm {31968055}. Systemic involvement with erythema nodosum with oligoarthritis or polyarthritis can be seen.

The imaging features vary on the etiology of the granulomatous lesion, and can be entirely within normal limits. Ultrasound can show an irregular, hypoechoic mass. Mammography shows irregular densities as the main finding {25940456}, while MRI most often shows focal or diffuse signal intensity changes in the absence of a mass effect {27221974; 25940456} [[Hoda SA, Rosen PP, Brogi E, et al., editors. Rosen’s Breast Pathology. 5th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Wolters Kluwer; 2021.]].

Histopathology

Granulomatous lobular mastitis and idiopathic granulomatous mastitis show non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation centred on the lobule with epithelioid histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells surrounded by lymphocytes, plasma cells, and occasional eosinophils. Special investigation to identify an infective agent should be performed, including DPAS, silver stains, Ziehl–Neelsen, and Gram stains and/or PCR. A different pattern, cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis, shows a central lipid cavity that develops which is rimmed by neutrophils and granulomas. Sparse gram-positive bacilli may be found {32094275} [[Hoda SA, Rosen PP, Brogi E, et al., editors. Rosen’s Breast Pathology. 5th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Wolters Kluwer; 2021.]].

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Granulomas consisting of epithelioid histiocytes with elongate, bent, or bean-shaped nuclei, with or without necrosis or suppuration Epithelioid histiocyte nuclei have smooth nuclear borders, finely granular chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli, and the cytoplasm is pale and abundant Cytoplasmic borders of epithelioid histiocytes within granulomas are not well visualized Mycobacterial mastitis will show necrosis, lymphocytes, eosinophils, and variable numbers of neutrophils, with or without Langhans-type giant cells, in addition to epithelioid histiocytes Necrosis can also be noted in idiopathic and fungal granulomatous mastitis Sarcoidosis shows epithelioid histiocytes, lymphocytes, Langhans, and other types of giant cells, and intracytoplasmic inclusions including Schaumann and asteroid bodies in a background that lacks necrosis Eosinophils can be seen in mycobacterial and idiopathic granulomatous mastitis A conspicuous number of plasma cells can be seen in idiopathic granulomatous mastitis Foreign body granulomatous mastitis may include silicone or suture material Causative microorganisms may be identified on smears or in special stains

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for granulomatous mastitis is wide, both clinically and on imaging. FNAB can certainly narrow the differential diagnosis and, most importantly, exclude malignancy. The diagnosis of idiopathic granulomatous mastitis is by exclusion.

An important entity in the differential diagnosis is granulomatous disease caused by mycobacterial infection, particularly because the treatment of idiopathic granulomatous mastitis is often steroids. Caseating granulomas are a hallmark of mycobacterial infection, while necrosis in idiopathic granulomatous mastitis is unusual but has been described {33310826}. Acid-fast stains can be negative in mycobacterial mastitis. Other ancillary tests such as mycobacterial culture or PCR are more sensitive. Fungal infections can also present with necrosis. Fungal stains such as methenamine silver and DPAS are useful in this regard.

Sarcoidosis and cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis do not show necrosis. The features of the latter are nonspecific on cytopathology and include neutrophils, epithelioid histiocytes in granulomas, lymphocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and plasma cells {30353674}.

Squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts with rupture of ducts can induce both a suppurative and a foreign body response to keratinous material with granuloma formation, amorphous debris, plasma cells, foamy macrophages, giant cells, calcification, and cholesterol clefts, leading to recurrent subareolar chronic abscesses.

Ruptured epidermal inclusion cysts of the skin covering the breast may lead to granulomas, with anucleate squamous cells, keratin debris, giant cells, and acute inflammation {34558655}.

Fat necrosis can present with fat cells, histiocytes, and a granular or necrotic background along with granulomas. Foreign body granulomas can be due to material such as suture material. Other causes of granulomatous mastitis, such as Wegener granulomatosis, autoimmune conditions, and IgG4-related sclerosing disease, will produce a nonspecific granulomatous lesion {36503636; 33310826; 35473758; 29404980}.

Ancillary testing

Ancillary testing is useful to determine the cause of granulomatous mastitis. If mycobacterial mastitis is suspected, acid-fast stains, mycobacterial culture, and PCR should be undertaken.

Lipophages in fat necrosis will stain positively with a fat stain such as oil red O, and in ICC will stain positively for CD68 and negatively for epithelial markers.

Appropriate clinical history and examination of the glass slide under polarizing light will assist in the diagnosis of a foreign body granuloma, although silicone is non-birefringent and appears as faintly lucent globules and shards in the cytoplasm of histiocytes {33310826; 29404980; 35473758; 25940456; 29971624}.
