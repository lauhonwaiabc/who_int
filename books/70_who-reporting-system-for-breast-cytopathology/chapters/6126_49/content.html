6.1.2.6 Intraductal papilloma

Definition

Intraductal papilloma is a benign breast lesion arising within a duct composed of papillary projections with fibrovascular cores, covered by an epithelial and myoepithelial layer.

Clinical features and imaging

Intraductal papillomas most typically occur in the retroareolar region, where they can produce a spontaneous, haemoserous nipple discharge usually from a single duct, or they may present as a mass. Uncommonly, they can also present as a palpable mass elsewhere in the breast. On imaging at any site, larger papillomas may produce a round or oval mass, which on ultrasound may lie within a dilated duct and show Doppler blood flow in the stalk.

Histopathology

Intraductal papillomas show a wide range of features. Large papillomas, which are usually retroareolar, can show a single duct containing a single thick fibrovas­cular sclerotic core, or several large dilated adjacent ducts containing an apparently ramifying complex papilloma. The fibrovascular core is covered in varying degrees of epithelial hyperplasia, columnar cell change, and apocrine change, and within some papillomas there is a proliferation of tubules separated by thin fibrovascular strands resembling adenosis [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; forthcoming. (WHO classification of tumours series, 6th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int.]]. Small, sometimes multiple papillomas can be found anywhere in the breast, often as incidental findings and usually associated with fibrocystic change with epithelial hyperplasia on their fibroelastotic cores.

In all of these papilloma types, the epithelium ranges from a low cuboidal or columnar single layer to proliferative epithelial hyperplasia with frequently hyperplastic apocrine metaplasia. In histopathology, intraductal papillomas are usually easily distinguished from low-grade micropapillary or cribriform ductal carcinoma in situ, intermediate-grade papillary ductal carcinoma in situ, solid papillary ductal carcinoma in situ / carcinoma, encapsulated papillary carcinoma, and the rare invasive papillary carcinoma, although the assessment of atypia in the proliferative epithelium associated with papillomas can be difficult; that is, the diagnoses of atypical ductal hyperplasia and low-grade ductal carcinoma show wide interobserver variation.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

There is a range of cytopathological features for the varying histopathological types of intraductal papilloma and these need to be recognized to make possible the distinction from the various benign, in situ, and invasive carcinomas that are raised in the differential diagnosis.

The stellate papillary tissue fragments and complex meshwork tissue fragments are specific in the diagnosis of intraductal papillomas, with the latter representing the intraductal proliferations of small tubules separated by stroma seen in histopathology and variously called “intraduct adenosis”, “sclerosed papilloma”, or “complex intraductal papillary lesions” {17636490}. Apocrine sheets, columnar epithelial cells, haemosiderophages, histiocytes, and a proteinaceous background are often found, but are not specific for the diagnosis of intraductal papilloma {17636490}. Partially crushed lobules of adenosis in FNAB smears can mimic the meshwork fragments but lack the branching fibroelastotic stroma, size, and complexity of meshwork fragments {17636490}. The stroma of the stellate papillary and meshwork tissue fragments varies from fibroblastic to fibroelastotic, in which non-staining negative-image elastic fibrils can be seen, to densely hypocellular and sclerotic collagen.

Bloody nipple discharge may be due to degeneration or even infarction of a papilloma, and produce a small number of rounded papilliform tissue fragments of partially degen­erate, often mildly atypical ductal, apocrine, or squamous epithelial cells in a bloody background with haemosiderophages {7813370}.

It is essential in every case to assess for the presence of epithelial nuclear atypia in the meshwork and stellate papillary tissue fragments, as well as in the dis­persed cells and epithelial hyperplastic tissue fragments. Atypia in the form of nuclear enlargement, anisonucleosis, pleomorphism, and hyperchromasia should lead to a CNB or excision biopsy being recommended.

The differential diagnosis is varied and includes benign lesions, such as fibroadenoma, epithelial hyperplasia in fibrocystic change, radial scar, and adenomyoepithelioma, and papillary ductal carcinoma in situ, solid papillary ductal carcinoma in situ / carcinoma, encapsulated papillary carcinoma, and the rare invasive papillary carcinoma.

In papillary ductal carcinoma in situ, which is usually low- or intermediate-grade, there is moderate to high cellularity with a pattern of often large 3D epithelial tissue fragments and plentiful dispersed single intact columnar cells, with few or no myoepithelial cells in the tissue fragments and few or no bare bipolar nuclei in the background, which may be proteinaceous. The tissue fragments can have slender papillary fronds giving a ramifying appearance at the edges of the fragments. Other true small papillary tissue fragments are present with thin fibrovascular stalks that may not be easily visible due to the covering of usually columnar and multilayered epithelial cells forming 3D tissue fragments. The epithelial cells show nuclear pleomorphism that varies with the grade of the ductal carcinoma in situ. In low-grade micropapillary ductal carcinoma in situ tissue fragments with small papilla which lack fibrovascular cores and have narrow necks and bulbous tips, or low-grade cribriform ductal carcinoma in situ, the nuclear atypia is minimal within these rigid architectures. In intermediate-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, the nuclear atypia can be more pronounced, but the architecture is the clue to the diagnosis. The background may be haemorrhagic with haemosiderin-laden macrophages, and may contain greater numbers of single columnar epithelial cells that show moderate atypia with hyperchromatic, elongated nuclei. Psammomatous calcifications may be present.

Solid papillary ductal carcinoma in situ cannot be distinguished from the relatively low-grade solid papillary carcinoma on FNAB and CNB, unless there is a carcinoma of no special type (NST) arising in the ductal carcinoma in situ. Distinction usually requires excision. Compared with intraductal papilloma, the smears are typically highly cellular with a large number of columnar cells with bland to moderate nuclear atypia seen singly and in small to larger discohesive tissue fragments. The characteristic features are finding papillary tissue fragments with often branching and sometimes complex thin fibrovascular cores with rounded capillary loop ends that are variably covered in multilayered columnar epithelium. The characteristic stellate papillary fibroelastotic fragments of intraductal papilloma are not seen.

The most common benign differential diagnosis for intraductal papilloma is fibroadenoma. Typically, fibroadenomas show 3D branching, antler or staghorn tissue fragments, and monolayered 2D sometimes folded sheets of ductal epithelial cells with plentiful myoepithelial cells, moderate to numerous bare bipolar nuclei in the background sometimes in “kissing pairs” or doublets, and stromal fragments. The stromal fragments can be rounded, scalloped, and myxoid, or fibrillary, or tufted and sclerotic. Occasionally these stromal fragments can be closely apposed to epithelial tissue fragments, but no true stromal cores are seen. The stellate fibroelastotic stroma of intraductal papillomas is not seen. Occasional complex fibroadenomas can have apocrine sheets or a proteinaceous background sometimes with histiocytes, but haemosiderophages are rare. Dispersed columnar cells can be seen in fibroadenomas and in intraductal papillomas.

In fibrocystic changes, the pattern usually includes a small number of hyperplastic ductal epithelial cell tissue fragments, along with apocrine sheets and histiocytes in a proteinaceous background. Radial scars can produce greater cellularity, a larger number of 3D hyperplastic ductal epithelial tissue fragments, and apocrine sheets in a proteinaceous background. Bare bipolar nuclei may be diluted in the proteinaceous background. The epithelial tissue fragments in fibrocystic change and radial scars lack fibrovascular cores, and stellate papillary tissue fragments with fibroelastic stroma are not seen. However, florid epithelial hyperplasia, apocrine metaplasia with papillary hyperplasia that lacks any fibrovascular cores, and tissue fragments of lobules showing adenosis or sclerosing adenosis may mimic intraductal papilloma.

FNAB smears of juvenile papillomatosis are highly cellular with large hyperplastic epithe­lial tissue fragments with often prominent myoepithelial cells, large apocrine sheets, and tissue frag­ments resembling stellate papillary tissue fragments but lacking the elastotic fibrils and thickness of the stromal cores of intraductal papilloma. There is a proteinaceous background with histiocytes.

Nipple adenoma smears are moderately cellular with large, cohesive, 3D hyperplastic epithelial tissue fragments and sheets of ductal cells with myoepithelial cells in a back­ground of haemosiderophages, histiocytes, variable numbers of bare bipolar nuclei, focal necrotic debris, and a vari­able number of dispersed cells showing round nuclei with fine even chromatin {26011103}. The diagnosis is suggested by the site and the FNAB findings, but CNB, punch biopsy, or incisional biopsy may be required.

A cytopathological diagnosis of intraductal papilloma requires careful assessment of the key cytopathological features and consideration of the various differential diagnoses, and then close correla­tion with imaging and clinical findings. Intraductal papilloma can be distinguished from papillary ductal carcinoma in situ in FNAB cytopathology, and benign intraductal papillomas can be diagnosed in as many as 75% cases {17580343; 17636490}. As in other benign pro­liferative lesions, the FNAB diagnosis requires careful assessment for epithelial atypia and correlation with imaging, to avoid a false positive diagnosis {17636490}. Management of intraductal papillomas is changing, and CNB or excision biopsy may not be considered mandatory if the correlation of the findings is consistent. CNB is a sam­pling procedure and has similar problems to FNAB in distinguishing atypical ductal hyperplasia and low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ. Lesions that have characteristic features of papillomas, including stellate papillary tissue fragments, but show high cellularity, particularly in postmenopausal women not on hormone replacement therapy, or nuclear atypia or marked dispersal should be reported as a “papilloma with atypical epithelial features” and biopsy recommended. These lesions potentially may rep­resent papillomas with focal atypical ductal hyperplasia or low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ.

Ancillary testing

Distinguishing benign from malignant papillary neoplasms can be difficult in FNAB cytopathology. Cell blocks can be prepared {27060933} and ICC used to demonstrate the presence or absence of myoepithelial cells {27060933}. Myoepithelial cells are positive for p63, MSA, calponin, SMA, CD10, S100, and basal-type cytokeratins such as CK5/6 and CK14 {7587880; 19126567}. The presence of myoepithelial markers along fibrovascular cores is seen in intraductal papillomas and not in papillary carcinomas, although residual attenuated myoepithelial cells are present around the ductal margins of ductal carcinoma in situ and encapsulated papillary carcinoma.

In epithelial hyperplasia, basal-type cytokeratins are expressed in 88–100% of cells, whereas in atypical to malignant epithelial proliferations, the expression is much more infrequent, ranging from 4% to 23% {1698341; 11745672}. In the setting of intraductal papillary lesions with proliferative epithelium, CK5/6 has been shown to be useful, where it shows a patchy or mosaic cytoplasmic and membranous pattern of positivity in epithelial hyperplasia, and is negative or weak in atypical epithelial proliferations such as atypical ductal hyperplasia, ductal carcinoma in situ, or papillary carcinoma {19126567}. ICC for ER will show a similar patchy weaker staining in epithelial hyperplasia while in low- and intermediate-grade ductal carcinoma it will show a diffuse “clonal” nuclear positivity. Other markers used for this differentiation are 34βE12 and CK14 {25810244; 15832086}. CK5/6 and CK14 have higher sensitivity and specificity than 34βE12 {15832086}. p63 was found to be more useful to demonstrate myoepithelial cells in fibroepithelial lesions such as fibroadenomas. Neuroendocrine markers such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and INSM1 are consistently negative in papillomas, whereas in papillary carcinoma including solid papillary ductal carcinoma in situ / carcinoma, the positive rate was 35% {17587241; 19126567}. A combination of these markers can serve as a useful adjunctive diagnostic tool when applied to cell blocks {18171414; 16861972}.
