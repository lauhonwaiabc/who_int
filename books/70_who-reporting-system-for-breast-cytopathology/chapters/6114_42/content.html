6.1.1.4 Fat necrosis

Definition

Fat necrosis is degeneration with or without necrosis of adipocytes with a variable number of foamy, vacuolated histiocytes and a lesser number of neutrophils and/or chronic inflammatory cells.

Clinical features and imaging

The incidence of fat necrosis < 1% and it represents 2.75% of all benign lesions {25861475; 29987981}. Fat necrosis most often affects perimenopausal patients who are overweight with pendulous breasts. It is also associated with patients who have undergone breast surgery, including breast reduction, augmentation, fat grafting after primary breast reconstruction, excision of breast lesions, and CNB. It can also be associated with malignancy. In a patient who has undergone surgery for breast cancer, fat necrosis may clinically resemble recurrent malignancy {29987981; 16198567}.

Fat necrosis presents as painless, single to multiple, well-defined, firm, nodular or irregular masses. While any region in the breast can be involved, the subareolar and periareolar regions are involved most often. Additional findings include overlying skin induration, discolouration, and retraction, nipple retraction, pain, and regional lymphadenopathy.

The mammographic appearance varies depending on the stage of necrosis, from an early radiolucent mass or cyst with linear to curvilinear calcifications to central calcifications. With fibrosis the cyst may become thickened, irregular, spiculated, and poorly defined. Calcifications although mostly round and smooth may become irregular and branched {25861475; 29987981; 36849280; 31185981; 19234281}.

Histopathology

Initially the fat becomes necrotic with haemorrhage and an influx of inflammatory cells including lymphocytes and histiocytes. Later, multinucleated giant cells and haemosiderin-laden macrophages predominate with the histiocytes, then granulation tissue, fibrosis, and dystrophic calcification and even ossification may occur.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Fat necrosis can masquerade clinically and on imaging as a breast malignancy, either primary or metastatic disease, and FNAB offers a rapid and accurate diagnostic modality that can, in the majority of instances, make this distinction.

Breast carcinoma can be associated with fat necrosis, which has to be distinguished from reactive ductal cells, which are usually found in cohesive sheets associated with myoepithelial cells and exhibit minimal atypia, although after chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the atypia can become more marked. Lipomas do not typically show necrosis but may yield histiocytes, while they have relatively characteristic imaging especially on MRI. Breast prostheses commonly are associated with fat necrosis with or without leaked silicone, which shows a prominent foreign body giant cell reaction with globular silicone. In a benign breast cyst, the cyst fluid is proteinaceous lacking fat globules, and has foamy histiocytes and apocrine cells. A galactocoele has a “bubbly” milky background with fine fat globules, and foamy macrophages rather than lipophages. A breast abscess, particularly in the later phases of organization, will demonstrate tissue fragments of granulation tissue, and there may also be associated fat necrosis.

Ancillary testing

Lipophages in fat necrosis will stain positively with a fat stain such as oil red O, and in ICC CD68 and CD163 are positive.
