Index > WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology

1.3 Foreword with changes from the book, including corrigenda

The classification of tumours can be viewed from many perspectives. To a histopathologist it is all about the histopathological patterns, to a molecular pathologist or geneticist it is all about which genes are affected, and to an epidemiologist it is about the tumours’ incidence. The WHO Classification of Tumours series started in the 1960s as a result of the histopathologist’s need to make diagnoses by grouping tumours according to their microscopic appearance. The programme now includes contributions from many specialties, including, of course, cytopathology. However, the cytopathologist looks at tumours slightly differently to other specialists, and although the main classification is helpful, it has become clear that reporting systems using common terminology and established diagnostic categories that assist communication between cytopathologists and clinicians need to be based on entities defined by key diagnostic cytopathological features. These reporting systems have to be truly international, serving the needs of patients worldwide.

The International Academy of Cytology (IAC), a scientific, non-profit organization with members from 49 affiliated cytopathology societies, therefore approached the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) – home to the WHO Classification of Tumours Programme – to see whether it would be possible to produce a complementary series of WHO Reporting Systems for Cytopathology. The two organizations signed a memorandum of understanding in early 2020 and have produced the first four volumes of the series: the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology; the WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology; the WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology; and the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology. This is the WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology, and three other reporting systems are in progress, for kidney and adrenal, liver, and head and neck cytopathology.

Cytopathology is used in the diagnosis of breast lesions, including neoplasms, and it is particularly powerful in low-resource settings. Special histochemical stains and ancillary methods such as immunocytochemistry and in situ hybridization are applicable to smears and cell blocks, which also provide excellent material for NGS. The avoidance of formalin in cytopathological preparations enhances the application of diagnostic molecular techniques, whether simple PCR or NGS.

Just as the WHO Classification of Tumours series (also known as the WHO Blue Books), published in print volumes and as a web resource (https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int), is an essential tool for standardizing diagnostic practice worldwide and serves as a vehicle for the translation of cancer research into practice, we hope that these first-edition WHO Reporting Systems will fulfil the same role for cytopathology. The key diagnostic cytopathological features listed for each tumour type under the diagnostic categories represent the first international consensus and are described in precise, uniform language. These diagnostic criteria are underpinned by available evidence that has been evaluated and debated by experts in the field. Each entity’s section includes discussion of its differential diagnosis based on its cytopathological features so that the System can be used worldwide, especially in low-resource settings. This is then followed by discussion of the current best-practice application of ancillary testing on cytopathology material to further the diagnosis. More than 40 authors and editors participated in the production of this volume and gave their time freely to this task. We are very grateful for their help; it is a remarkable team effort.

This volume, like those of the WHO Classification of Tumours series, has been led by an editorial board composed of standing and expert members. The standing members include the chair of the WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board and others nominated by the IAC. The expert members, selected on the basis of informed bibliometric analysis and advice from the standing members, represent a broad international expertise, as does the authorship group. The diagnostic process is increasingly multidisciplinary, and we are delighted that several radiology and clinical experts have joined us to address specific needs.

In addition to using the International System of Units (SI) for all measurements, we have standardized the genomic nomenclature by using Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) notation. We have also further standardized our use of units of length, adopting the convention used by the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (https://www.iccr-cancer.org) and the UK Royal College of Pathologists (https://www.rcpath.org/), so that the size of tumours is now given exclusively in millimetres (mm) rather than centimetres (cm). This is clearer, in our view, and avoids the use of decimal points – a common source of medical errors.

The goal of this long-term project is to meet the needs of the cytopathology community and enhance the use of FNAB cytopathology to benefit patient care. Like the WHO Classification of Tumours, the WHO Reporting Systems, both the website and the print books, are published on a non-profit basis, with proceeds ploughed directly back into the series to support the costs involved, including production, printing, and distribution. There are substantial discounts available for residents of lower-income countries and trainees. It is of vital importance that tumours continue to be classified and diagnosed according to the same standards internationally so that patients can benefit from multicentre clinical trials and studies, as well as from the results of local research conducted on different continents.
