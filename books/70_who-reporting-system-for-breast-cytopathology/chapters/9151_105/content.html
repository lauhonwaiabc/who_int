9.1.5.1 Metastatic tumours

Definition

Metastatic tumours to the breast are those that arise from an extramammary primary site.

Clinical features and imaging

Extramammary metastatic tumours represent < 2% of breast malignancies and are usually detected during clinical follow-up of an extramammary carcinoma, but may be the first presenting sign of a non-breast malignancy. Common primary tumours that metastasize to the breast include melanoma, serous and mucinous ovarian carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, gastric and colorectal adenocarcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) from lung, and various sarcomas {35447141; 18446623; 1921396; 23276173; 2342767; 20082359; 11917589; 2750705; 20806071; 8807244; 12873311}. Even though a subcutaneous location, diffuse skin thickening, bilateral masses, and multiple masses form a minority of the presenting signs in a metastatic tumour, these occur in a higher proportion of patients with a metastatic tumour than a primary tumour.

On ultrasound, metastases are typically round, hypoechoic, circumscribed masses compared with the hyperechoic, irregular, often spiculated primary breast carcinomas {26416136; 28485985; 24497788}. On mammography, diffusely increased parenchymal density and skin thickening are often observed {26416136; 28485985}. On colour doppler examination, areas of central vascularity are considered suspicious.

Histopathology

The microscopic features of the metastasis resemble those of the primary tumour. The presence of ductal carcinoma in situ suggests the diagnosis of a primary breast carcinoma rather than a metastasis {35447141; 1921396}. In the absence of carcinoma in situ, histopathological features that are unusual for a breast carcinoma and/or discordance between the morphology and ER status should raise the possibility of metastatic disease.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Triple negativity for ER, PR, and HER2 (ERBB2) with or without morphology which is unusual for a breast carcinoma should prompt consideration of a metastasis, particularly in younger women, as well as those with a known history of malignancy.

Ancillary testing

Carcinomas found in the breast which are triple negative should have CK5/6 or CK14 ICC to confirm or exclude a primary basal-type carcinoma, as well as a breast marker such as TRPS1, GATA3, or GCDFP-15 {35447141; 35790088; 36055933; 33011748}. A panel of ICC markers is useful in distinguishing a metastatic tumour from a breast primary. Caution should be exercised in patients with a history of ovarian high-grade serous carcinomas, as these tumours may metastasize to the breast and have been shown to express TRPS1 as well as ER {36634642}. Inclusion of antibodies specific for an ovarian primary, particularly PAX8 and WT1, is helpful. Other common antibodies that will help to identify metastatic tumours include HMB45 and melan-A (MART1) for melanoma {35447141; 23276173}; thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and napsin A for lung adenocarcinoma; p40 for squamous cell carcinoma; CDX2 and SATB2 for colorectal primaries; synaptophysin, chromogranin, and INSM1 for NEC; and PAX8 and RCCm for renal carcinoma {35447141}.
