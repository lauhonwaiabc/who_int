9.1.4.1 B-cell lymphomas of the breast

Definition

Breast B-cell lymphomas can be primary or secondary with primary breast lymphoma defined as a malignant neoplasm occurring in one or both breasts, in the absence of a concurrent or prior history of lymphoma.

Clinical features and imaging

Primary breast lymphoma is a rare entity accounting for 0.04–1% of malignant breast neoplasms and less than 1% of non-Hodgkin lymphoma {31976067}. Simultaneous regional lymph node involvement can occur with the primary breast lymphoma diagnosis {4555557}. It includes low-grade and highly aggressive  subtypes {4555557; 8333556; 17932394}. Over 90% of reported primary breast lymphoma cases are of B cell origin, of which diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common (over 50%), followed by extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (EMZL) {18636016; 25572809}. A minority of cases are follicular lymphoma (FL) and Burkitt lymphoma (BL) {18636016; 25572809}. Middle-aged and older adults are generally affected {18480397; 19570964; 8333556}. EMZL and BL may also affect younger women, including pregnant or lactating women presenting with massive bilateral breast masses {28376147; 19199367; 11967901}.

Most patients have a painless single lesion {3315180; 18636016,19570964}, with nonspecific imaging. Rare cases are multiple or bilateral, occasionally with spread to ipsilateral lymph nodes {18480397; 19570964; 18636016;19570964; 18636016; 15590406}. Mammography usually shows a solitary oval mass without calcifications, and with circumscribed or indistinct margins. On ultrasonography, primary breast lymphoma appears as an oval, solid, hypoechoic mass with poorly defined margins and evident hypervascularisation. MRI shows a T1 isointense and T2 hyperintense mass with homogeneous or heterogeneous enhancement. PET-CT is useful, especially in the evaluation of response to treatment, staging and follow-up.

Primary breast lymphoma generally behaves differently from its lymph node counterpart. The optimal treatment modality is still under discussion due to the rarity of this disease and therefore the small number of patients. Surgery is not recommended. Currently, chemo-immunotherapy with consolidation radiotherapy has produced the most favourable responses.  The prognosis is poor due to frequent CNS relapses.

Lymphoma metastatic to the breast or secondary breast lymphoma, most frequently DLBCL, is more common than primary lymphoma of the breast. Follicular and mucosa-associated lymphoma have occasionally been described as secondary breast lymphoma. While primary breast lymphoma usually presents as a solitary mass, multiple and bilateral breast involvement is seen in secondary breast lymphoma. Secondary breast lymphoma may show imaging features similar to breast carcinoma. The histopathology and cytopathology of primary and secondary breast lymphoma is identical {30924754; 22665932}.

Histopathology

Primary breast DLBCL is composed of large B lymphoid cells arranged in a diffuse pattern. The neoplasm replaces breast parenchyma, and the lymphoma cells may resemble centroblasts or immunoblasts or be anaplastic. Lymphoepithelial lesions are uncommon {18636016; 8333556; 11815954; 22569408}.

Primary breast EMZL is a low-grade B-cell lymphoma that shows a loosely nodular to diffuse growth pattern, often with an infiltrative margin, and lymphoma is closely apposed to the epithelium of the lobules and ducts with small lymphoid cells focally infiltrating epithelial structures, but these lymphoepithelial lesions are found less commonly than in EMZL arising at other sites {18636016; 8333556; 11815954; 22569408}.

Primary breast FL is a B cell lymphoma that shows follicular architecture with centrocytes and various proportions of centroblasts. The neoplastic follicles can be intimately associated with breast ducts, but lymphoepithelial lesions are usually absent. If an intramammary lymph node is involved without involvement of breast parenchyma, it should not be considered breast FL {18636016; 8333556; 11815954; 22569408}.

Primary breast BL is characterized by a diffuse monotonous infiltrate of intermediate to large lymphoid B cells with  numerous tingible-body macrophages imparting a “starry sky” pattern {18636016; 8333556; 11815954; 22569408}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

High cellularity Relatively monomorphic dispersed large atypical lymphoid cells Round to oval lobated immunoblastic, centroblastic or anaplastic nuclei Frequent prominent nucleoli Scant cytoplasm which can be eccentric Limited or no epithelial component

Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma

High cellularity Predominantly small to medium lymphoid cells with high N:C ratio, admixed with lymphocytes, plasmacytoid lymphoid cells, plasma cells and monocytoid cells Mildly irregular nuclei Dispersed chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli Scant to abundant pale cytoplasm No necrosis

Follicular lymphoma

High cellularity Mixed lymphoid pattern with centrocytes and a variable number of centroblasts Lymphoid follicles containing dendritic reticulin cells  Variably round to centrocyte-like  nuclei in smaller lymphoid cells and larger round centroblast-like nuclei in the more scattered larger cells Scant basophilic rim of cytoplasm in larger cells Lack of tingible-body macrophages

Burkitt lymphoma

High cellularity Monotonous intermediate to large cells Round to more irregular nuclei with finely clumped chromatin and multiple small nucleoli Rim of eccentric deeply basophilic cytoplasm often containing fine punctate lipid vacuoles (Giemsa stain) High proliferation rate, plentiful mitoses and numerous tingible-body macrophages

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Primary breast lymphoma diagnostic criteria include: 1) the breast as the site of presentation, 2) breast tissue closely related to lymphomatous infiltration, 3) absence of disseminated disease beyond the ipsilateral axillary lymph nodes, 4) no previous diagnosis of lymphoma {4555557}.

Primary breast lymphoma is easily misdiagnosed as breast carcinoma since they are characterized by a similar clinical and imaging presentation, but the preoperative diagnosis of primary breast lymphoma is of fundamental importance because their treatment is different to that of other epithelial or mesenchymal tumours.

Moreover, the breast can be affected not only by lymphoproliferative processes, but also by benign lymphoid processes. Benign lesions comprising lymphocytes include: intramammary lymphadenopathy in which a mixed lymphoid population of predominantly small lymphocytes with tingible body macrophages is seen in a background containing lymphoid cell cytoplasmic fragments; and granulomatous lesions in which epithelioid histiocytes and giant cells may be very scanty. Lymphocytes may also be noted in diabetic mastopathy, which yields scanty material including fibrotic fragments, ductal epithelium and lymphocytes. Seromas may develop following breast surgery showing a cystic background with small lymphocytes, histiocytes and occasional neutrophils. Preoperative cytopathological diagnosis by FNAB, combined with ancillary techniques, such as ICC and flow cytometry, is a useful procedure for the diagnosis of reactive and lymphoproliferative processes of the breast preventing unnecessary surgery and allowing adequate and specific therapeutic procedures {29130993; 22378074}.

Ancillary testing

ICC and flow cytometry are the main ancillary techniques used to  confirm the diagnosis of primary breast lymphoma.

In primary breast DLBCL, the neoplastic cells are positive for B cell markers including CD20, CD79A and/or PAX5 and have either a germinal center-like phenotype (CD10 and BCL6 positivity) or a non-germinal centre-like phenotype (CD10 negative and MUM1 positive). The non-germinal centre phenotype is more common in primary breast lymphoma and is thought to be associated with a poor outcome {15492762; 22569408}. BCL2 is often positive, but this is usually not associated with BCL2 translocations. The neoplastic cells are usually negative for CD5 and CD30. The proliferation index detected with Ki-67 is usually high (about 80%). MYC is often expressed in highly proliferative neoplasms, but usually not uniformly or brightly unless MYC is rearranged. There is no evidence of association with EBV.

In primary breast EMZL, the B neoplastic cells are typically positive for CD20, CD79a and BCL2, negative for CD5, CD10 and CD23,  BCL6, BCL2, cyclin D1 and SOX11, and variable in CD43. Rare EBV+ primary breast EMZL have  been reported in solid organ transplant patients {23773403; 29957733}. Recurrent chromosomal translocations that are characteristic of EMZL in other sites and result in activation of NF-κB are uncommon in primary breast EMZL {16155022; 16917511; 21654070}.

Primary breast FL shows B cell markers and germinal center markers CD10 and BCL6, as well as the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 {27016007}. Some cases are CD10 negative {33252276}. The proliferation index by Ki67 positivity varies from 10 to 40%. Primary breast FL commonly contains a high proportion of reactive T cells.

Primary breast BL is positive for CD20, as well as for CD19, CD22, CD79a and PAX5 and for the germinal centre markers CD10 and BCL6 {23853639; 20799767}. Proliferation is very high, with nearly 100% of the cells positive for Ki-67. The presence of a chromosomal breakage and translocation of the MYC locus is observed also in primary breast BL.

Flow cytometry and ICC of a benign intramammary lymph node will be positive for a wide range of B- and T/ NK-lymphocyte markers with no light chain restriction. Ancillary tests such as microbiological investigations can be performed to exclude granulomatous or tuberculous mastitis. Correlation of relevant clinical and imaging findings with cytopathology will aid in excluding diabetic mastopathy while the lymphoid cells in a seroma are predominantly T-lymphocytes.
