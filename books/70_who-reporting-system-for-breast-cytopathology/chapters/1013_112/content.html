10.1.3 Category: Atypical

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Atypical” demonstrates features predominantly seen in benign lesions and minimal features that may raise the possibility of a malignant lesion, but with insufficient features either in number or quality to diagnose a benign or malignant process or lesion.

The terms “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” reflect the spectrum of uncertainty when a definitive diagnosis of either a “Benign” or a “Malignant” lesion cannot be made. Use of the two categories has been demonstrated to improve the accuracy of the ROM for the “Benign” and “Malignant” categories [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]] {31112942; 31141809}. The “Atypical” category reflects an indeterminate lesion with predominantly benign features, and the atypical features present should be specified, the differential diagnosis discussed, and the most likely diagnosis suggested in the report {7754536; 10096355; 16570117; 26466750}. Most lesions categorized as “Atypical” are benign epithelial proliferative changes, predominantly fibroadenomas, intraductal papillomas, sclerosing adenosis, usual ductal epithelial hyperplasia, columnar cell change, and radial scars / complex sclerosing lesions; less commonly, low-grade invasive carcinomas of no special type (NST), tubular carcinomas, low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, and lobular neoplasia may be assessed as “Atypical” rather than “Suspicious for malignancy” {11488870}.

Cytopathological features that lead to an “Atypical” categorization include prominent dispersal of single intact epithelial cells; plentiful small epithelial cell tissue fragments, mild to moderate nuclear enlargement or pleomorphism in the absence of an explanation such as an inflammatory background, complex micropapillary or cribriform tissue fragment architecture despite relatively bland nuclear features, papillary fragments, dispersed small cells with minimal nuclear atypia and eccentric cytoplasm with vacuoles that may raise the possibility of lobular neoplasia, marked nuclear crowding in tissue fragments, atypical apocrine cells, the presence of a mucinous or necrotic background, and a lack of benign features such as reduced or absent bare bipolar nuclei in the background or a lack of myoepithelial nuclei on epithelial tissue fragments.

Biphasic tumours such as cellular fibroadenomas, which are difficult to distinguish from benign phyllodes tumour, and spindle cell proliferations, which are often only slightly cellular and raise a broad differential diagnosis, including fibromatoses, adenomyoepithelioma, and myofibroblastoma, also produce “Atypical” categorizations. Technical issues which result in suboptimal preparations contribute to atypia, including poor FNAB and smear-making techniques, air-drying artefact in alcohol-fixed smears for Pap stains, slow-air-drying artefact in air-dried smears for Giemsa stains, obscuring ultrasound gel and blood, crush artefact, and inexperienced reporting pathologists {9626523; 11507700; 31112942}. ROSE markedly decreases the number of “Atypical” cases that are due to technical factors or insufficient material {31108486; 31137023}, while LBC preparation techniques have their own benefits and significant problems, such as loss of lesional patterns on direct smears and marked reduction in background material {25115652}.

Since the publication of the International Academy of Cytology Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology (IAC System), studies have reported a wide range in the proportion of lesions categorized as “Atypical”, from 1.2% to 34.8% of cases (median: 9.3%, mean: 10.7%) {30783035; 31524134; 33017520; 30929288; 32749785; 31108486; 33755356; 34133084; 34029453; 33629823; 34321772; 34866246; 34876918; 34515039; 34535580; 34703093; 36516743; 36988122}. This wide range reflects the inherent issues of an indeterminate category, in particular a lack of reproducibility and the variable cytopathologist experience and confidence. Depending on the experience of the cytopathologist and practice pattern, an “Atypical” categorization should ideally be reserved for only a small proportion of cases and should always be correlated with clinical and imaging features. Allowing a degree of uncertainty in a cytopathology categorization of “Atypical” provides the advantage of increasing the negative predictive value of a true “Benign” diagnosis and the positive predictive value of a true “Malignant” diagnosis.

The variable use of the “Atypical” category has resulted in a similar wide ROM range from 13.0% to 40.0% (median: 24.1%, mean: 24.0%) {30783035; 31524134; 33017520; 30929288; 32749785; 31108486; 33755356; 34133084; 34029453; 33629823; 34321772; 34866246; 34876918; 34515039; 34535580; 34703093; 36516743; 36988122}. The IAC System ROM was based on two studies representing best practice models in breast FNAB, and had a ROM of 13–15.7% {31108486; 30783035}, and the WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology suggests that a similar ROM should be achieved.

Management of a patient with a breast FNAB classified in the “Atypical” category is initially correlation with the clinical and imaging findings in the triple test. If the clinical and imaging findings are benign with low clinical suspicion, repeat clinical and imaging assessment in 3–6 months is suggested with immediate repeat biopsy if there are any significant changes in the lesion. If the clinical and imaging findings are indeterminate or are suspicious for malignancy, CNB is the preferred next step in management; however, repeat FNAB can be performed in settings where CNB is not available. If repeat sampling is also categorized as “Atypical”, a tissue biopsy is recommended.
