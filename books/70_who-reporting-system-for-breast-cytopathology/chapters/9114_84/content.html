9.1.1.4 Paget disease of the nipple

Definition

Paget disease (PD) of the breast is an intraepidermal proliferation of malignant glandular epithelial cells in the nipple areolar region.

Clinical features and imaging

PD presents as chronic eczematous change or ulceration of the nipple and areolar skin that may be accompanied by itching, crusting, and erythema. PD is unilateral in most cases and occurs in 1–3% of breast carcinomas {11237774; 23929251}. The associated ductal carcinoma in situ or invasive breast cancer in the affected breast determines the clinical and imaging findings, such as mammographic calcifications, architectural distortion, or a mass lesion {22084182}. Isolated PD of the nipple without malignancy in the remainder of the breast occurs in about 15% of cases. Occult PD refers to the presence of Paget cells in the skin of a surgical resection from a patient without skin symptoms. Nipple discharge is not usually a leading symptom of PD but may be present {20715656}. Although PD has been described in a wide age range, and rarely in men, it occurs mostly in women aged > 60 years {16933329}. Extramammary PD can be encountered in accessory nipples or other sites of the milk line.

The differential diagnosis of PD includes inflammatory dermatoses such as eczema and fungal infections, or on occasion itchy scaly lesions associated with breast feeding. PD of the nipple can present with pigmentation, and the rare possibility of melanoma or a squamous cell carcinoma in situ should be considered {23929251; 32816273; 23598853}.

Histopathology

PD is characterized by breast carcinoma cells typically in the basal layers of the stratum corneum of the epidermis in small clusters and as single cells. PD is usually associated with high-grade ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST), and PD cells morphologically resemble the underlying breast cancer with large nuclei and ample cytoplasm, present singly and in groups, infiltrating between the keratinocytes and associated with a dermal lymphocytic infiltrate {23929251; 2580429}. Benign intraepidermal glandular cells (Toker cells) can be present but do not have the morphological characteristics of adenocarcinoma cells {4313654}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Low cellularity in direct smears and scrapings of the nipple Single intact cells and small cohesive tissue fragments Large atypical cells with high N:C ratios, eccentric cytoplasm, and occasional vacuoles Large pleomorphic nuclei with prominent nucleoli Background of keratinous debris and scant squamous cells

References: {7529588; 30484956; 8390930; 8912355}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Direct scrapes of the nipple usually provide limited material, hampering the diagnosis. Punch biopsy may be required to make the diagnosis.

Ancillary testing

In ICC, the Paget cells are positive for cytokeratins, specifically CK7 and CK19. In keeping with the usually concomitant high-grade ductal carcinoma in situ or high-grade invasive carcinoma NST, Paget cells are negative for ER and PR in as many as 60% of cases, and positive for HER2 (ERBB2) in 80–100% cases, which can be used as a diagnostic marker in cell blocks along with GATA3. Lobular carcinomas are infrequently associated with intraepidermal spread and Paget cells are expected to be positive for E-cadherin. ICC melanoma markers such as HMB45 or melan-A are negative in PD cells {23929251; 20955381}
