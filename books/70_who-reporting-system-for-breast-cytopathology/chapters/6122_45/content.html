6.1.2.2 Fibrocystic change (including adenosis, tubular adenoma, apocrine adenoma, apocrine adenosis, and sclerosing adenosis)

Definition

Fibrocystic change is a common benign breast condition that includes cysts often with apocrine metaplasia accompanied by a variable degree of fibrosis, and may be associated with proliferative changes, including adenosis with increased terminal ductules in lobules, sclerosing adenosis with distortion of the terminal ductules by sclerosis within the lobulocentric unit, and apocrine adenosis where the distorted terminal ductules are composed of metaplastic apocrine cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Fibrocystic change, along with adenosis, sclerosing adenosis, and apocrine adenosis, is often clinically unremarkable and an incidental finding in breast biopsies performed for a palpable mass or impalpable imaging abnormality, such as microcalcifications or architectural distortion. Occasionally, these proliferative processes can produce nodules as nodular adenosis or sclerosing adenosis, with or without apocrine metaplasia. Breast pain and prominent breast tissue that fluctuates with the menstrual cycle may be associated with fibrocystic change. Palpable lumps may reflect cysts or adenosis.

Imaging features of fibrocystic change are generally nonspecific and may be associated with increased breast density. Architectural distortions, enhancement, or distinct nodules, including cysts, are seen on various imaging modalities, such as mammography, ultrasound, and MRI {32738557; 33147055; 34156554}.

Histopathology

In addition to cysts, which are often associated with apocrine metaplasia, fibrocystic change may be proliferative and include adenosis and sclerosing adenosis, which are characterized by variable proliferation of terminal ductules of lobules and stromal sclerosis. Adenosis is a general term for a lobulocentric glandular proliferation resulting in increased numbers of terminal ductules, typically without a concomitant intraductal epithelial proliferation. In sclerosing adenosis there is distortion of the proliferation of terminal ductules by the sclerosis, resulting in compressed, distorted tubules, and often associated with prominent myoepithelial cells. Apocrine metaplasia may occur in both adenosis and sclerosing adenosis, deemed apocrine adenosis.

Adenosis may appear as a distinctly demarcated, densely compact proliferation of well-formed tubules lacking a lobulocentric architecture, where it is termed a tubular adenoma, which lacks the stromal proliferation of a fibroadenoma with prominent tubules. Apocrine metaplasia can occur in this setting, and is regarded as the extremely rare apocrine adenoma {18042688; 36516736; 22706864; 18528894}. Microcalcifications can be associated with any of these lesions.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate to high cellularity with lower cellularity in sclerotic lesions Apocrine sheets of uniform cells with central round nuclei and granular well-defined cytoplasm Tissue fragments of ductal epithelial cells with myoepithelial cells Tissue fragments of intact lobules or portions of lobules, showing distortion, sclerosis, and crush artefact in sclerosing lesions Bare bipolar nuclei and histiocytes and some dispersed single apocrine cells in a proteinaceous background

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The cytopathological cellularity and components vary with the lesion. Fibrocystic change and benign sclerosing lesions enter the differential diagnosis of several benign and malignant lesions.

The cytopathology of fibrocystic change has much in common with fibroadenoma. Both predominantly consist of large ductal epithelial tissue fragments that may be branching, in addition to bare bipolar nuclei and stromal fragments. Both may have a cystic component. Some authors have described the subtle cytomorphological differences that can help distinguish these entities {11393066; 36516736}. Adenosis tends to contain smaller epithelial tissue fragments than those encountered in fibroadenoma, with less cohesiveness, and frequently small tubular structures and fragments resembling lobules. Small stromal fragments are more common in fibrocystic change, whereas in fibroadenoma the stromal component is usually represented by abundant bare bipolar nuclei or large fibromyxoid fragments {36516736}. The features of fibroadenomas are very similar to those of tubular adenomas, which produce moderately to highly cellular smears featuring large tissue fragments of abundant tubules with scant stroma {7992587; 36516736}, and this distinction is challenging, as it is in the triple test.

When the background proteinaceous fluid is abundant, suggesting a cyst, the differential diagnosis of lactational change or a galactocoele should be considered in the appropriate clinical setting. Smears from lactational change will show microvesicular and macrovesicular fat globules in the thin proteinaceous milky fluid, foamy histiocytes, and scant dispersed epithelial cells with round nuclei and single small nucleoli, which are frequently stripped of their fragile cytoplasm. Small fragments of vacuolated acinar cells are also seen [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach based on pattern recognition. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017.]].

Benign intraductal papillomas tend to produce cellular smears with hyperplastic large and small ductal epithelial tissue fragments, apocrine sheets, and a proteinaceous background containing foamy histiocytes similar to fibrocystic change. In addition, intraductal papillomas have fibroelastotic stellate papillary stromal fragments and large fibrovascular stalks and siderophages {17580343}.

Apocrine cells usually indicate a benign lesion, typically a cyst, although uncommon cases of apocrine atypia and apocrine carcinoma are well recognized. Benign apocrine cells have mildly enlarged round nuclei with a slight variation in size and prominent single nucleoli. Low-grade atypia in apocrine cells is difficult to diagnose; however, marked atypia, as seen in apocrine subtypes of ductal carcinoma in situ or invasive carcinoma, is characterized by at least a 3-fold variation in nuclear size with nuclear shape irregularity, hyperchromasia, and prominent, frequently multiple, nucleoli {34537861}.

Epithelial hyperplasia found in a background of fibrocystic change must be distinguished from low-grade invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST) and tubular carcinoma. Although nuclear features are usually sufficient to distinguish carcinoma from a benign lesion, these may be subtle in the case of low-grade malignancies. In this setting, emphasis must be placed on architectural features. The epithelial fragments are typically larger and more complex and branching in epithelial hyperplasia than in carcinoma, where the tissue fragments are smaller, reflecting the prominence of tubular architecture and an infiltrative growth pattern, and single isolated cells are more common {25012460}. Other important clues supportive of fibrocystic change include the presence of numerous myoepithelial cells, producing the bimodal tissue fragment epithelial pattern of ductal cells and myoepithelial cells, and bare bipolar nuclei, apocrine cells, and macrophages.

Cells from invasive lobular carcinoma and lobular neoplasia may mimic histiocytes in fibrocystic change owing to their small size, minimal nuclear pleomorphism, and lack of cohesion, and are especially difficult to recognize when admixed with benign epithelial cells in the smear {25678304}. Cytopathological characteristics of lobular tumour cells include a mild increase in nuclear size, eccentric cytoplasm, subtle nuclear shape irregularities, single intracytoplasmic lumina, and an increased N:C ratio, the latter typically lower in histiocytes.

Ancillary testing

One of the hallmarks for the diagnosis of benign lesions of the breast is the identification and quantification of admixed myoepithelial cells, which may be challenging in some types of cytopathological specimens. ICC performed on direct smears, LBC preparations, or cell blocks may prove useful in this context. Myoepithelial cell markers, including p63, calponin, SMMS-1 (an antibody against SMMHC), SMA, and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins such as CK5/6, can be used to demonstrate the presence of myoepithelial cells associated with the ductal epithelial population, supporting the diagnosis of a benign ductal proliferation, that is, usual ductal hyperplasia {31141801}. In contrast, these markers will be rarely expressed in atypical epithelial proliferations and ductal carcinoma in situ where the myoepithelial cells will be scant or usually absent. It should be noted that benign apocrine cell sheets frequently lack admixed myoepithelial cells, and a small number of myoepithelial cells may also be found not only in ductal carcinoma in situ but also occasionally in invasive carcinoma {21263240; 18936688}.
