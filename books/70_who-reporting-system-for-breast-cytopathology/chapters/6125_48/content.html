6.1.2.5 Lactational changes

Definition

Lactational change occurs in the acini of lobules during pregnancy or breast feeding, or in response to various hormones or medications.

Clinical features and imaging

During pregnancy and lactation there is proliferation of the ductal lobular system with expansion of lobules with acini arising from the terminal ductules and a decrease in adipose tissue, while any pathology that occurs in the non-pregnant breast can be seen in the breast and accessory breast tissue during gestation and in the postpartum period {32816256}. While most breast lesions during pregnancy and lactation are benign, breast cancer does need to be excluded. For example, a unilateral, painful, and tender breast may indicate mastitis or breast abscess, while a bloodstained nipple discharge may indicate trauma, papilloma, or breast carcinoma.

The use of imaging in pregnancy is usually related to the diagnostic workup of symptomatic issues such as palpable masses, breast pain, or nipple discharge. Changes in density reduce the sensitivity of mammography {32114749} and ultrasound is preferred {23345354; 2755376; 31019440; 32114749}. Vascularity is also increased {23345354; 32114749; 24327995}.

A lactating adenoma is a nodule, diagnosed during pregnancy or breastfeeding, composed of lobules showing lactational change, and presents as a painless, well-defined mass in pregnant or lactating women, though occasionally it may be painful due to infarction {23345354; 24327995}. Lactating adenomas often disappear spontaneously after lactation ceases {24327995}. They are mostly hypoechoic, circumscribed, wider than taller, and heterogeneous owing to milk on ultrasonography.

A galactocoele is a fluid-filled cyst, presenting almost exclusively in pregnancy and lactation as single or multiple, non-tender, and mobile masses with or without a milky nipple discharge. A galactocoele appears as a simple cyst-like lesion on ultrasound {32816256}.

Fibroepithelial lesions such as fibroadenoma may increase in size and/or undergo ischaemic changes during pregnancy. Mastitis and breast abscess are relatively common in the setting of pregnancy and lactation (see section Suppuration).

Breast cancer diagnosed during pregnancy or in the postpartum period is known as “pregnancy-associated breast cancer” and women are aged between 32 and 38 years. As pregnant or lactating women do not usually undergo breast screening, patients usually present for investigation of palpable masses, lymphadenopathy, or skin changes. The diagnosis of malignancy may be delayed resulting in a poorer prognosis. The cytomorphological features are similar in both pregnancy-associated and non–pregnancy associated tumours {32816256; 32778072; 31041174}.

Nipple discharge in pregnancy and lactation is usually physiological, appearing as bilateral, milky, and white to yellow, but can, on occasion, be pathological, suggested by a unilateral bloodstained or serous discharge that requires further investigation {32816256}.

Histopathology

Glandular tissue becomes more prominent with a relative decrease proportionately in fat {32816256}. Acini develop from the terminal ducts with crowded, irregular plump epithelial cells with cytoplasmic vacuolation. With advancing pregnancy, increased cytoplasmic secretory changes, lipid droplets, and acinar secretions are seen. This glandular overgrowth can become mass-like in a lactating adenoma or lactational changes can occur in a fibroadenoma.

Tissue sections of lactating adenoma show a circumscribed lesion consisting of crowded expanded lobules showing lactational change with eosinophilic material (milk) and lipoid material in the lumen. Myoepithelial cells are present in the acini. Fibroadipose stromal tissue is sparse.

Galactocoeles are unilocular or multilocular cysts surrounded by a fibrous wall, and lined by cuboidal vacuolated secretory epithelium. Apocrine metaplasia can be observed.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Clinically, the differential diagnosis of a breast mass in pregnancy or the postpartum period is the same as that seen in other women, and requires the same approach to diagnosis.

Cytopathologically, most of the benign lesions show their characteristic features, for example, neutrophils in an abscess or mastitis, and epithelial tissue fragments and stroma with admixed bare bipolar nuclei in a fibroadenoma.

The differential diagnosis of a galactocoele includes a cyst in fibrocystic change, which often shows apocrine epithelium and a more serous and granular background than a galactocoele, where the milky background includes fat globules and acinar cells are present. In both cystic lesions, scattered foamy histiocytes and duct cells can be seen.

Lactating adenoma, although cited as a potential diagnostic hazard, is usually identified as benign with its epithelial component showing vacuolation and small nucleoli, which are cherry red (Pap) in round nuclei with a smooth outline and fine chromatin. Most breast carcinomas in pregnancy and the postpartum period show high-grade malignant changes {31041174}, with more pleomorphic nuclei and a high N:C ratio, increased crowding, and overlapping of nuclei {9250309; 8513709; 23860443}. Differentiating a lactating adenoma from a secretory carcinoma is more problematic and may need CNB and/or ancillary testing including FISH.

Ancillary testing

In difficult cases, ICC for p63, CK14, ER, and PR may be useful. Secretory carcinoma is usually triple negative with an ETV6::NTRK3 gene fusion on FISH. Carcinomas lack p63-positive myoepithelial cells.
