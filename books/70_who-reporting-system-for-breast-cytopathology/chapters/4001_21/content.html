4.0.0.1 Introduction: The role of ancillary testing

Breast FNAB and nipple discharge cytopathology specimens, when handled properly, can provide sufficient and adequate material for ancillary testing {33792972; 35367980}. It is beneficial to plan ancillary testing at the time of FNAB collection. This is to confirm that sufficient material is collected, and that the specimen is transported and processed correctly. ROSE is very helpful in ensuring that appropriate material is collected and triaged for ancillary testing. Different types of cytopathological preparations, including direct smears, cytospin specimens, liquid-based samples, and cell blocks, can be utilized for various ancillary tests [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]] {31543473; 33792972}.

Mastitis is a common condition especially in lactating women. If mastitis is suspected clinically or detected on ROSE, material is submitted for microbiological evaluation. Isolation of an organism on FNAB is considered significant as contamination is limited. In addition, special stains such as Gram stain, stains for acid-fast bacilli, and fungal stains can be performed on smears. Other ancillary tests such as ICC, in situ hybridization, and PCR for infectious organisms can be performed on cytopathological material where appropriate.

ICC can be used for both diagnostic and theragnostic uses. For example, p63 and 34βE12 are useful markers to aid in the differential diagnosis between in situ and invasive lesions, as well as to characterize some benign lesions {35367980; 12811858}. In terms of theragnostic markers, ER, PR, and HER2 (ERBB2) can successfully be assessed in cytopathological samples, especially in cell blocks {35367980; 33020218; 27315045; 29763896}. Results for Ki-67 and PDL1 on FNAB specimens still require clinical validation and must be performed on cell blocks {35367980}. ICC can be performed for primary and metastatic sites, with good performance in effusions material {21488175}. Heat-induced antigen retrieval is always recommended regardless of sample type or fixative used {35367980; 8599924}. ICC can also be used to identify breast as the primary site on metastases assessed by cytopathology. Typical markers currently used to identify breast origin include GATA3, GCDFP-15, and mammaglobin {35367980; 29235613}. There is also recent evidence that TRPS1 is a potentially useful breast-specific marker in cytopathological material {35760555}. Classically, PCR and in situ hybridization have been used as go-to molecular techniques for FNAB of the breast {31141801}. In situ hybridization techniques are largely the most used molecular technique, especially for detection of ERBB2 amplification signals {31141801}. However, more recently techniques such as quantitative PCR and NGS have been used routinely on cytopathology samples in many centres around the world. In low- and middle-income countries, the methylation marker–based automated cartridge system has been used to detect breast cancer in FNAB samples, enabling prioritization of patients with cancer for expedited pathological evaluation {31300453}. For metastatic breast cancer, the goal of molecular testing is to find tumour-specific mutations and then target therapy to increase survival and quality of life. Studies have revealed that cytopathological specimens produce adequate substrates for molecular analysis and can provide clinically reliable genomic profiling {32750212}. In breast cancer, PIK3CA and ESR1 mutations as well as NTRK fusions are examples of targets that can be studied in cytopathological material {31141801} [[Souza da Silva R, Schmitt F. Optimal Assessment of Metastatic Breast Carcinoma: The Value of Cytopathology Combined with Molecular Analysis. J Mol Pathol. 2022;3(4):329–38. doi:10.3390/jmp3040028.]].

The breast can be the site of lymphoid lesions. These range from benign lesions, such as follicular hyperplasia, seromas, and granulomatous inflammation, to non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The ancillary techniques that are well described in lymph node FNAB can be applied to a lymphoid lesion in the breast. These include flow cytometry, ICC, and molecular techniques. Both ICC and flow cytometry show similar features in follicular hyperplasia, namely a mixed population of both B and T/NK lymphocytes with polyclonal light chains. The lymphoid cells in a seroma are predominantly T lymphocytes. Extranodal marginal zone and follicular lymphomas are the most frequently encountered small cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas in the breast. Large cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas of the breast include diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, and breast implant–associated anaplastic lymphoma. An example of the utility of ancillary tests for breast lymphoma is provided by diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of the breast; namely, the neoplastic cells are positive for B-cell markers such as CD20, CD79a, and/or PAX5, and have either a germinal centre–like phenotype (CD10 and BCL6 positivity) or a non–germinal centre like phenotype (CD10-negative and IRF4 [MUM1]-positive). The non–germinal centre phenotype is more common in primary breast lymphomas, and is thought to be associated with a poor outcome {15492762; 22569408}. BCL2 is often positive, but this is usually not associated with BCL2 translocations. The neoplastic cells are usually negative for CD5 and CD30. The proliferation index detected with Ki-67 is usually high (~80%) (see section B-cell lymphomas of the breast) {27639613; 27554776; 34791787}.
