4.0.0.2 Microbiology

Inflammatory lesions of the breast are usually benign and can be infectious or non-infectious, such as duct ectasia, granulomatous lobular mastitis, and silicone mastitis. It is important to note, however, that breast carcinoma can be inflammatory on occasion. FNAB is a rapid, accurate, and cost-effective diagnostic modality allowing breast cancer to be excluded and material to be obtained for ancillary tests including microbiology. Microbiological assessment of breast FNAB material in a patient with a suspected infectious or inflammatory lesion is a well-established practice {35168773; 27555237}.

Breast FNAB should be undertaken in the most sterile conditions possible and performed before administration of antimicrobial agents if feasible. The operator wears sterile gloves, and other personal protective equipment such as masks and safety goggles. Before the procedure, the operator and clinician should communicate to ensure additional material is taken for the appropriate microbiological investigations. Suitable transport or culture media must be available at the time of FNAB. The breast lesion is cleaned well with an antiseptic swab before the FNAB procedure. The needle, after the FNAB, is rinsed in culture medium or separate passes are undertaken for microbiological investigation. Equipment for performing the FNAB such as needles and syringes must be disposed of safely and in accordance with local regulations. The date, time, site of specimen collection, and clinical details should be clearly communicated to the microbiology laboratory.

In general, rinsing the needle or a separate pass placed into normal saline is suitable for many microbiological investigations. However, it is highly recommended that the cytopathology and microbiology laboratories liaise closely regarding the most appropriate transport and culture media to optimize diagnosis. Various types of transport and culture media are available depending on the type of microbiological investigation required. Transport to the microbiology laboratory should be as prompt as possible and the specimen kept at an ideal temperature to promote growth of fastidious organisms while preventing overgrowth of more resilient ones. If other microbiological tests such as PCR or in situ hybridization are required in addition to microscopy and culture, the specimen can be separated before microscopy and culture are undertaken {29730224; 32400869; 11884700; 29730224; 28606308} [[Pantanowitz L, Michelow P, Khalbuss WE. Cytopathology of Infectious Diseases. New York (NY): Springer New York; 2011.]].

FNAB aspiration of purulent material can produce smears for Gram stain as well as for culture. Staphylococcus aureus, a gram-positive organism, is the most common cause of breast abscess. If mycobacterial infection is suspected, additional glass slides can be made for acid-fast stains as well as rinsing of the needle in appropriate transport media for PCR tests and culture {26800030}. A modified Fite stain is useful in atypical mycobacteria, Mycobacterium leprae and Nocardia, while a Warthin–Starry stain will highlight spirochaetes and Bartonella spp. Culture, fluorescent stains, serology, and molecular techniques may be required for definitive identification of an organism.

Fungal infections of the breast are uncommon and tend to occur in immunosuppressed patients. Fungal stains such as PAS and Grocott methenamine silver will assist in the diagnosis. Fungal culture, PCR, and loop-mediated isothermal amplification are some of the additional ancillary tests that can be performed on material acquired during FNAB.

Parasitic infections of the breast are occasionally encountered on FNAB, such as cysticercosis, filariasis, schistosomiasis, and hydatid disease. While microscopy remains the mainstay of parasitic diagnosis, special stains can be performed. For example, Schistosoma ova stain positively with Ziehl–Neelsen and Grocott methenamine silver stains. ICC and molecular tests have improved diagnostic sensitivity of parasitic infections.

Viral infection of the nipple may be due to several viruses including herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, and molluscum contagiosum virus. A viral cytopathic effect can be seen on nipple smears; for example, multinucleation, margination, and moulding of nuclei in herpes simplex and waxy, deeply basophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in molluscum contagiosum. ICC and molecular tests are available to confirm herpes infection while molluscum contagiosum stains with a Lendrum phloxine-tartrazine stain.
