2.0.0.1 Background

Fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) of the breast has a long tradition of providing an accurate diagnosis of palpable masses and, with the introduction of imaging guidance, particularly ultrasound, non-palpable breast masses as well [[Cangiarella J, Simsir A. Chapter 7: Breast. In: Orrell S, Sterrett G, editors. Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology. 5th ed. Edinburgh (Scotland): Churchill Livingstone; 2012. pp. 156–209.]] [[Franzen S, Linsk J. Clinical Aspiration Cytology. Philadelphia (PA): Lippincott; 1983.]] [[Zajdela A, Vielh P, Di Bonito L. Manuel et atlas de cytologie mammaire. Padova (Italy): Piccin; 1995.]] [[Field AS. Chapter 5: Breast. In: Field AS, Zarka MR, editors. Practical cytopathology: a pattern recognition diagnostic approach. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2016.]] [[Ducataman BS, Wang HH. Chapter 9: Breast. In: Cibas E, Ducataman B, Van Der Laan P, editors. Cytology: principles and clinical correlates. 5th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2025.]].

FNAB of breast masses is rapid and readily accepted by patients, and has virtually no side-effects. With initial suitable training, the FNAB procedure can be straightforward, but this training is best provided by cytopathologists and interventional radiologists with expertise in FNAB of the breast, because FNAB cytopathology critically relies on the quality of the actual procedure and the making of smears. The advent of mammography and breast cancer screening programmes in the 1990s produced a large number of well women with lesions that required biopsy by FNAB, but radiologists were often poorly trained to perform the FNAB and pathologists were often poorly prepared to report this new diagnostic test. The advent of spring-loaded core needle biopsies (CNB) for breast sampling has led to a decrease in the use of FNAB in high-income countries, particularly in the screened population where mammographically detected calcifications are often the target. However, in many centres in high-income countries and in most centres in low- and middle-income countries, FNAB of the breast is the first biopsy technique utilized.

FNAB offers many advantages, including lower costs, the use of simple equipment, minimal need for medical infrastructure, rapidity of the test and diagnosis, rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE), and the ability of the test to fit into one-day clinics to reduce patient anxiety and assist further management planning {27160231}. CNB offers the advantages of providing information on the histopathological features of lesions, a definitive diagnosis of invasive malignancy, and tissue for prognostic and other markers, such as ER, PR, and HER2 (ERBB2), although cell blocks from FNAB are a proven alternative {22783929; 31141801; 35367980}.

The WHO System for Reporting Breast Cytopathology (WHO System) is a system based on previous systems, especially the International Academy of Cytology Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology, and acknowledges the work of previous cytopathologists in these systems [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]]. The WHO System uses a five-tiered diagnostic category system linked to recommendations for further diagnostic management options to achieve a specific diagnosis, which is the principal aim of all cytopathology. The WHO System aims to standardize the reporting of breast FNAB by standardizing the definitions of the categories and the key diagnostic cytopathological features of each entity, and emphasizing the differential diagnosis of each lesion based on the cytopathology, ensuring that the WHO System can be used globally, before providing details and discussion of appropriate ancillary testing. The WHO System provides a discussion of the role of FNAB of breast and of ROSE; the integration of clinical findings, imaging, and FNAB in breast diagnostics; and a comprehensive guide to specimen handling and preparation, including for cell blocks and other ancillary testing, to help standardize these procedures in laboratories internationally. Nipple discharge and assessment of the axilla are also reviewed. Importantly, the components of a standardized report for breast FNAB are presented and discussed, recognizing that local custom and laboratory information systems will influence the actual format of the pathology report.

The WHO System is based on the sixth-edition Breast tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; forthcoming. (WHO classification of tumours series, 6th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int.]], but differs in that it discusses breast cytopathology utilizing direct smears, reflecting a cytopathologist’s approach to diagnosis. Entities are grouped and discussed to reflect the cytopathologist’s differential diagnosis, for example, fibroadenomas and low-grade phyllodes tumours, intraductal and invasive papillary carcinomas, and borderline and malignant phyllodes tumours. Non-neoplastic entities, including acute and granulomatous inflammation and fat necrosis, are presented because these are common lesions in the breast that are primarily assessed and diagnosed by FNAB, and can mimic neoplasms clinically and on imaging. Some entities are not included because they have not been reported in the cytopathology literature, such as tall cell carcinoma with reverse polarity. Recent developments in the sixth-edition Breast tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series are discussed in the text, including clarification of a current classification for adenomyoepithelioma and malignant adenomyoepithelioma; solid papillary carcinoma in situ, solid papillary carcinoma in situ with invasion, and invasive solid papillary carcinoma; encapsulated papillary carcinoma with high nuclear grade; mucinous carcinomas with high nuclear grade and micropapillary architecture; renaming of acinic cell–like carcinoma; the use of the terms neuroendocrine tumour (NET) and carcinoma in breast pathology; and the diagnosis of malignant phyllodes tumours.

The primary aim of the WHO System is to standardize breast FNAB reporting as well as improving the procedure, specimen handling, and recognition of key cytopathological features, which form a diagnostic checklist for a cytologist scientist and cytopathologist. This reporting system will bring a focus on breast FNAB, including research into its applicability and usefulness, leading to appropriate revisions, and ultimately will improve patient care.
