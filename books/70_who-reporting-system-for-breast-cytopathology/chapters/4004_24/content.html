4.0.0.4 In situ hybridization and molecular testing

In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) is a common test for chromosomal abnormalities, and in solid tumours, ISH is predominantly used to test for amplifications, translocations, and deletions. In breast cancer, ISH is most commonly used to detect amplification of ERBB2. Less commonly, an ETV6::NTRK3 translocation for diagnosis of secretory carcinoma may be also detected by ISH.

ERBB2 (HER2) amplification
Quality aspects

ERBB2 amplification in invasive breast cancer is tested to select patients for targeted anti-HER2 therapy (e.g. trastuzumab). Strict internal and external validation is necessary to assure quality of the results {29846122}. Laboratories should demonstrate concordance of the laboratory results against results from a validated source according to the technical requirements of the accreditation organization before reporting patient samples. For some accreditation organizations, for example CAP, laboratories have to demonstrate concordance of ≥ 90% for 20 validated positive samples and concordance > 95% for 20 validated negative samples if the laboratory is using an approved validated assay. For laboratory-developed tests or laboratory-modified tests, the same concordance needs to be demonstrated using 40 validated positive and 40 validated negative samples {29846122; 20524870}, and a concordance of > 90% needs to be maintained on external quality assurance programmes.

In situ hybridization methods

In ISH, probes bind to target regions of DNA or RNA in intact cells. There are multiple types of ISH depending on the type of probe which binds to the target region. The commonest type of ISH is FISH, in which the probes emit a fluorescent signal on binding to the target region. The nuclei of the cells are counterstained with another fluorescent stain (e.g. DAPI) {27479335; 15791569}. FISH needs fluorescence microscopy and careful visualization so that only diseased cells, that is, invasive cancer cells, are interpreted for ERBB2. This technique requires careful preanalytical steps so that the proper area of a microscope slide containing invasive cancer cells is marked and documented, since it may be difficult to differentiate between cancer cells and benign cells of a similar size under the fluorescence microscope {27479335}.

Other ISH techniques include chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH), in which a coloured dye is added to an antibody–probe–target complex, or silver in situ hybridization (SISH), in which silver is deposited on an antibody–probe–target complex {23570400; 11073807; 31135445; 21273092}. In both these techniques, probes with an attached antigenic label (e.g. Texas red or fluorescein isothiocyanate) are first hybridized to the target, then the probes are coated by an antibody. These antibodies are conjugated with common immunohistochemistry reporter enzymes, such as horseradish peroxidase or alkaline phosphatase. In the case of CISH, there is further incubation with a substrate which the reporter enzymes break down to form a coloured precipitate. In the case of SISH, the horseradish peroxidase is subsequently incubated with silver acetate, which is precipitated to give rise to a black colour {23570400; 11073807; 31135445; 21273092}. Both CISH and SISH can be performed on a brightfield microscope, with haematoxylin counterstaining of the nucleus, and have shown good results when compared with FISH. Proper area demarcation is still required for FNAB specimens since a monolayer is needed so that individual signals are visualized.

Specimen aspects

Air-dried Giemsa-stained FNAB smears and LBC preparations provide excellent material for FISH {26990933; 26091472; 16951391; 31544361; 23225912}, on FNAB smears of the primary tumour, if reasonably certain on imaging that ductal carcinoma in situ is absent, and on axillary metastases, distant metastases, or serous effusions. ISH of cytopathology specimens should play an important role in patient management, since this is a convenient method for tissue sampling from metastasis and recurrences and there is evidence that HER2 receptor conversion is frequent between primary tumour and distant metastasis {29315431}. Cell blocks also provide sufficient material for FISH analysis {31544361; 29944734; 27383478}. The area to be hybridized is first identified on the stained smear or cell block, and the relevant area is destained if necessary {29846122; 24101045} and hybridized using a validated FISH protocol.

CISH and SISH are feasible on cell blocks {31544361; 16951391}. Protocols exist for performing SISH {20976182; 20661016} and CISH {22574078} on FNAB smears. However, large published studies confirming the robustness of these two procedures on FNAB material are lacking.

Technical problems

ISH is limited by some technical factors. There may be an apparent increase of signals due to aneuploidy. There may be background staining or autofluorescence. An apparent decrease in probe counts may result from nuclear truncation and possible loss of either CEN17 or ERBB2 targets on microtome section cutting, improper fixation, selection of a wrong tissue area for hybridization, and overcorrection for background noise during image acquisition {30600323; 27479335; 15791569}. The processing steps include pepsin digestion, excess of which may damage the nuclei, and careful standardization of the process is required for each type of cytopathological preparation or tissue. These factors often result in conflicting interpretation of the ERBB2 and CEN17 signals, necessitating a rule-based diagnostic algorithm.

The quality of DNA is better in FNAB smears, because smears do not have truncation artefact, giving them an advantage over tissue sections or cell blocks for FISH {30600323; 27479335}. However, FNAB smears may have fewer areas of monolayer formation, a problem which is found less in LBC preparations and cell blocks. Cells may get lost during processing of FNAB smears, and hence the use of positively charged adhesive slides is recommended {27479335}. Cell blocks have an advantage in that they may be used for ICC and may not require additional technical validation if FISH on tissue sections is already validated.

Types of probes

For detecting ERBB2 amplification, there are two types of probes: dual probes, which are generally recommended, and single probes. In validated dual probes, differently coloured probes are targeted against ERBB2 on chromosome 17 and a non-amplified region (the centromere of chromosome 17, CEN17) {29846122; 24101045; 15791569}. These probes are hybridized to the complementary genetic regions inside breast cancer cell nuclei. On FISH, a specific fluorescent signal is emitted, which is visualized by fluorescence microscopy. The probes targeting each specific region may be counted easily owing to the specific fluorophore which emits light of a particular colour on hybridization to a particular target; depending on the manufacturer, probes hybridizing to the ERBB2 region may emit a green signal and those hybridizing to the CEN17 region may emit a red signal, or vice versa. Single probes target only the ERBB2 region.

Interpretation of in situ hybridization

For diagnosis of ERBB2 amplification, there should be an increase in the ERBB2-specific signals as well as the ratio of the amplified ERBB2 signals to the non-amplified CEN17 signals. In a stable diploid nucleus, with no amplification, we expect two ERBB2 signals and two CEN17 signals {15791569}. In cases of ERBB2 amplification, the number of ERBB2 signals as well as the ratio should be more than doubled. Often, the interpretation is not as clear-cut owing to technical factors, such as truncation effects, when using tissue or cell blocks, and owing to intrinsic tumour characteristics, such as increased proliferative activity, polysomy, aneuploidy, or centromeric amplification, and a diagnostic rules-based approach based on the ICC is recommended for interpreting ERBB2 FISH {29846122} (#39626Table 2.01). At least 20 cells should be counted {29846122} but another study has recommended assessment of a minimum of 60 cells to reduce error {33980971}.

Single probes target only the ERBB2 region of chromosome 17. When using single probes, visualization of at least six signals per cell is considered positive, less than four signals per cell is considered negative, and visualization of four to six signals needs to be resolved by ICC or dual-probe FISH {29846122} (#39627Table 2.02).

Molecular testing

Molecular testing for breast cancer on tumour samples can be undertaken as either a multigene expression assay for prognostication or treatment prediction, or a mutation profiling assay for targeted therapy.

The multigene expression assays examine the mRNA expression profile on various platforms (e.g. microarray chips, quantitative PCR). Validated multigene assays include MammaPrint, Oncotype DX, Prosigna Breast Cancer Prognostic Gene Signature Assay, EndoPredict, and the Breast Cancer Index {31099622}. Most of these are performed in centralized laboratories on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded specimens. These assays are not validated on FNAB smears or cell blocks.

Somatic mutation profiling by NGS or PCR may be performed on FNAB smears, cell blocks, or circulating tumour DNA in blood. The quality of DNA extracted from FNAB is often equivalent to or better than that from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue {30600323; 32750212; 26682952; 32511899; 31141801}. On NGS, multiple genetic targets may be assessed in a single assay and this is advantageous over PCR. The somatic mutations that may be useful in breast cancer include mutations in ESR1 (predictive of endocrine resistance to aromatase inhibitors) {30205045; 34392831; 31318440}, mutations in PIK3CA in ER-positive tumours (predictive of increased sensitivity to alpelisib) {33246021}, and mutations in NTRK genes and RET. In addition, analysis of tumour mutation burden and microsatellite instability may be useful. Broad-based panels studying multiple genetic pathways may be advisable over single gene panels since multiple genes may be involved in resistance or sensitivity to therapy.

ESR1 mutations associated with resistance to aromatase inhibitors may be acquired during treatment with endocrine therapy; in such cases, mutation profiling may be done on cell-free DNA {31091522; 33893289} or on samples from recurrences after therapy, including effusion samples {31099622}. Mutations in MAPK pathway genes (e.g. EGFR and KRAS) and MYC, CTCF, FOXA1, and TBX3 are also associated with endocrine resistance and are likely to be found preferentially from tissue samples associated with recurrence {30205045}. It is possible that some of these genomic variations were already present in an analytically non-detectable state in the treatment-naïve primary tumour but metastasized preferentially in response to therapy, while some were actually de novo mutations {30205045}. Acquired mutations in PIK3CA and RB1, which are predictive of resistance to fulvestrant with palbociclib therapy, have also been identified {30206110}. These studies demonstrate the role of molecular testing in recurrences, distant metastases, and circulating tumour DNA.

Besides somatic mutation profiling, germline mutation profiling for BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations is recommended for predicting response to PARP inhibitors {31099622}. Such testing is carried out on the blood or saliva.
