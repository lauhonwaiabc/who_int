6.1.2.8 Gynaecomastia

Definition

Gynaecomastia is the excessive, non-neoplastic proliferation of male breast epithelial tissue and/or the associated stroma.

Clinical features and imaging

Gynaecomastia usually presents as a retroareolar, discoid, soft to firm mass, but may also present as a diffuse swelling. It is usually bilateral. Gynaecomastia may be physiological and reversible, occurring during infancy, at puberty, or in elderly people {22534349; 28179453}. It may also be secondary to any underlying condition or drugs causing an imbalance between estrogen and androgen levels, especially excessive estrogen or reduced androgens. Spironolactone, cimetidine, ketoconazole, growth hormone, antiandrogens (such as flutamide), and 5α reductase inhibitors can cause gynaecomastia {22862307}. Cirrhosis, hypogonadism, obesity, and hypothyroidism are some of the common medical conditions causing gynaecomastia {22534349; 22862307}. Gynaecomastia can cause physical discomfort as well as psychosocial distress.

On imaging, gynaecomastia may take three forms: nodular, dendritic, or diffuse. On ultrasound, the nodular form corresponds to a homogeneous, hypoechoic mass {23397020; 32072386}, and the dendritic form has a stellate, hypoechoic appearance with branches extending into the surrounding breast tissue. The diffuse form resembles the female breast {23397020; 32072386; 20375373}. The nodular and dendritic forms correspond to florid and fibrous histopathology, respectively.

Histopathology

There are three different histopathological patterns depending on the time elapsed between the onset of gynaecomastia and biopsy: florid (biopsy at months to 1 year of onset), fibrous (biopsy taken after 1 year of onset), and mixed (biopsy taken at an intermediate time between the florid and fibrous stages).

The florid pattern shows adenosis and epithelial hyperplasia, which is usually micropapillary or flat, with the epithelial elements surrounded by variable fibrous stroma, which increases over time. The fibrous pattern shows predominantly fibrous elements with reduced epithelial complexity as well as cellularity. The mixed phase is a mix of the above two patterns.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Gynaecomastia is the most common breast lesions in male patients, along with a range of other lesions including breast cancer. The distinction between gynaecomastia and breast cancer can usually be made when adhering to cytopathological diagnostic criteria. However, gynaecomastia can exhibit cellular atypia and loss of cell cohesion. A low Ki-67 seen in gynaecomastia may help in this distinction. The triple test will aid distinguishing gynaecomastia from breast cancer but histopathological evaluation may ultimately be required in these cases {33870108; 35814372}.

Other differential diagnoses to consider are pseudogynaecomastia, inflammatory lesions, and lipoma. In pseudogynaecomastia, there is excessive fatty tissue without a palpable mass; ultrasound shows only fat and no glandular or fibrotic density {34033252}. FNAB shows only fat. Lipoma shows an encapsulated radiolucent mass on imaging {28331703}, and again, only fat on FNAB. In both pseudogynaecomastia and lipoma, clinical and imaging correlation is needed.

The incidence of myofibroblastoma of the breast shows no sex predilection, and myofibroblastoma presents with bland spindle cells, some demonstrating nuclear grooves in a collagenous, fatty, or myxoid stroma. The cells are CD34-positive and exhibit deletion of chromosome 13q14 {31117069}.

Ancillary testing

Clinical, imaging, and cytopathological correlation in the triple test is important when evaluating a male patient with a breast mass. Ancillary tests are utilized in gynaecomastia mainly to exclude other lesions, such as microbiological studies in suspected inflammatory lesions, and ICC for CD34 and in situ hybridization for detection of chromosome 13q14 if myofibroblastoma is suspected {31117069}.
