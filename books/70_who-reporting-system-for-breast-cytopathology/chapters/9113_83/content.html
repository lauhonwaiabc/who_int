9.1.1.3 Low grade ductal carcinoma in situ, papillary ductal carcinoma in situ, encapsulated papillary carcinoma, and solid papillary carcinoma (in situ and invasive)

Definition

Low- and intermediate-grade ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a non-invasive proliferation of neoplastic epithelial cells confined to the ductal-lobular unit, exhibiting a range of architectural patterns and a low to moderate nuclear atypia.

Papillary DCIS is composed of filiform arborizing fibrovascular cores lined by neoplastic ductal epithelium, devoid of myoepithelium, and contained within central or peripheral ducts with retained myoepithelium at the periphery.

Encapsulated papillary carcinoma consists of neoplastic cells of low or intermediate nuclear grade, lining fine arborizing fibrovascular stalks, typically present within a cystic space and surrounded by a fibrous capsule. There are usually no myoepithelial cells along the papillae or at the periphery of the lesion.

Solid papillary carcinomas, in situ and invasive, are tumours characterized by a solid growth pattern with delicate fibrovascular cores. They frequently show neuroendocrine differentiation and are biologically indolent.

Invasive papillary carcinoma is an invasive carcinoma with fibrovascular cores covered by neoplastic epithelium.

Clinical features and imaging

Most cases of low- and intermediate-grade DCIS are non–mass forming and typically are found in women in their fifth decade of life by mammography, which shows small punctate calcifications that are fine to granular {2178285; 20956833}. It less commonly presents as a non–mass enhancement on MRI, and is not reliably visualized by ultrasound. Calcifications or enhancements are typically not evaluated with FNAB unless there is an accompanying mass. Prognosis is better than for higher-grade DCIS.

Papillary DCIS is a subtype of DCIS and is similarly detected by mammography owing to associated calcifications. Solid papillary carcinomas may present as a mass potentially with nipple discharge. Encapsulated papillary carcinomas may also appear as a mass and may have a cystic component on imaging {27054753; 18661510}. These lesions typically affect postmenopausal women, and may be associated with other patterns of carcinoma {27438988; 7573685; 36645462; 36999966; 21753694}. Prognosis is generally excellent.

Histopathology

DCIS of low nuclear grade is composed of small, monomorphic cells, typically growing in a cribriform, micropapillary, or less often solid pattern. Low-grade solid DCIS may have microrosettes with small luminal spaces. Micropapillary and cribriform patterns are commonly admixed and micropapillary DCIS in particular may be extensive {2535928}. The nuclei are uniform in size and shape, with regular chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. The nuclei in DCIS of low nuclear grade are 1.5–2 times the size of an erythrocyte {19123730}. Mitotic figures are rare. Microcalcification is commonly seen in secretions within the luminal spaces and may be psammomatous. DCIS of intermediate nuclear grade is composed of cells that show moderate variability in size, shape, and polarization. The nuclei have variably coarse chromatin and sometimes have prominent nucleoli. Mitoses may be present. Necrosis, either punctate or central (comedo), may be seen. Microcalcifications may be present in secretions and/or in the necrotic material.

Papillary DCIS consists of an intraductal neoplastic epithelial proliferation of various patterns that line arborizing thin fibrovascular cores. Myoepithelial cells are absent along these fibrovascular cores, but are present at the epithelial–stromal interface of the periphery of the involved ducts. Most papillary DCIS is low- to intermediate-grade, with occasional cases being high nuclear grade {25471940}. Most lesions consist of a uniform cell population, but dimorphic papillary DCIS has been described {8056421} and contains a population of globoid cells with abundant clear cytoplasm {8056421}. Papillary DCIS typically has a high rate of ER positivity {11342981}.

Encapsulated papillary carcinoma usually consists of a papillary mass growing within a cystic space; less often, it is composed of an aggregate of nodules. There is a rounded, pushing border and usually a surrounding fibrous capsule of varying thickness. Monomorphic neoplastic epithelial cells, usually of low- or intermediate-grade, line arborizing vascular cores. Myoepithelial cells are typically absent along the vascular cores, and are also usually absent at the periphery of the lesion with rare cases showing an incomplete peripheral myoepithelial cell layer {21084964}. Encapsulated papillary carcinomas express ER and usually PR, lack ERBB2 (HER2) amplification, and demonstrate a low to occasionally moderate Ki-67 proliferation index {27054753; 22025283; 21084964}. On rare occasions, encapsulated papillary carcinoma may demonstrate high nuclear grade, and the sixth-edition Breast tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series recommends that these should be managed as DCIS, rather than an invasive carcinoma, as long as a high-grade carcinoma with central necrosis has been fully excluded.

Solid papillary carcinomas (SPC) are characterized by a solid growth pattern within expanded ducts forming distinct nodules. Monomorphic neoplastic epithelial cells, with round to spindle morphology and mild to moderate nuclear atypia, proliferate around inconspicuous delicate fibrovascular cores. Myoepithelial cells may be present along the vascular cores. Extracellular mucin can be seen and raises the possibility of mucinous carcinoma {7573685}. SPC in situ is diagnosed when the nodules have rounded contours compatible with an in situ process, regardless of whether a peripheral myoepithelial cell layer is present {17927587; 40118257}. The sixth-edition Breast tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series has clarified recently that SPC in situ with invasion is diagnosed when SPC in situ is associated with areas of an invasive component of hypercellular mucinous carcinoma, or invasive carcinoma of no special type (NST) {16625097} or of other special type [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Breast tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; forthcoming. (WHO classification of tumours series, 6th ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int.]], while invasive SPC consists of nodules with the features of SPC that lack myoepithelial cells and have ragged contours, creating a geographical jigsaw pattern within a desmoplastic stroma {27017244; 17587241; 40118257}. On rare occasions, invasive lobular carcinoma can show an architectural pattern resembling SPC in situ, but showing negative E-cadherin and β-catenin in the epithelial proliferation, frequently with focal classic invasive lobular carcinoma at its margin {35656761; 40118257}.

SPCs are typically strongly and diffusely positive for ER. The Ki-67 proliferation index is usually low or intermediate {27017244; 16625097; 22025283}. Neuroendocrine differentiation by ICC is frequent, but is not necessary for diagnosis and has no bearing on management {17587241}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

There are fewer studies describing the cytopathology of low- and intermediate-grade DCIS than of high-grade DCIS, reflecting that FNAB is rarely used for these lesions {15593325; 14674064; 1312285; 15452908}. The nuclear atypia varies from low to moderate. In histopathology, the growth patterns are often mixed with solid, cribriform, and micropapillary architecture, and this is reflected in the cytopathological findings. True papillary structures with a central fibrovascular or stromal core, or without a core but having a radiating columnar cell arrangement, can also be found as part of a DCIS with mixed growth patterns. The main differential diagnoses of low-grade DCIS are ductal hyperplasia with and without atypia, as these also show 3D tissue fragments, although they lack a rigid cribriform or micropapillary architecture, show streaming of nuclei around irregular secondary lumina, feature myoepithelial cells in a bimodal pattern, and have bare bipolar nuclei in the background. The cellular and nuclear monotony seen in most cases of low- and intermediate-grade DCIS is in contrast to benign, hyperplastic lesions where mixed ductal and myoepithelial cells usually give a messy, haphazard appearance. Low-grade DCIS may occasionally have scattered myoepithelial cell nuclei on the cell aggregates, and bare myoepithelial cell nuclei are no proof of a benign lesion. A diagnosis of low-grade DCIS can be suspected cytopathologically, but should always have an additional note that the criteria overlap with usual epithelial hyperplasia and atypical ductal hyperplasia, which is defined by histopathological criteria and cannot be diagnosed on cytopathology. CNB should be recommended.

The histopathological subtypes of papillary DCIS and papillary carcinoma cannot reliably be differentiated in cytopathology. There are a number of relatively small studies describing the features of papillary neoplasms. They invariably describe branching 3D papillary tissue fragments with delicate fibrovascular cores {28469321; 16514838; 11985565; 12685198; 11172205; 26329503}. Other criteria described in several studies include moderate to abundant cellular material, small papillae arranged in cell balls, tall columnar cells, isolated naked nuclei, and haemosiderin-laden macrophages, and irregular tissue fragments of predominantly monolayered 2D sheets of epithelium composed of small, polygonal, or cuboidal cells with eosinophilic eccentric cytoplasm and round nuclei {7924806; 2244588; 1468334; 17328487; 10518128; 8160644; 21042528; 33561323}.

The nuclear atypia may be quite mild and lead to false negative benign diagnoses, but a small number of papillary DCIS and papillary carcinomas may show high-grade nuclear atypia. The main differential diagnoses are other low-grade carcinomas and intraductal papilloma. The malignant cells in papillary DCIS and carcinomas, notably those with endocrine differentiation, may show the same monotony (monoclonality) as described above, whereas benign papillomas will reveal a mix of ductal and myoepithelial cells in cohesive hyperplastic ductal epithelial tissue fragments with bare bipolar nuclei in the background and distinctive stellate papillary tissue fragments with fibroelastotic stroma. SPCs with endocrine differentiation typically show high cellularity, discohesive epithelial tissue fragments, abundant single cells, round to mildly pleomorphic nuclei, fine granular chromatin, eccentric cytoplasm, and inconspicuous nucleoli {17580353}. In SPC in situ with an invasive component composed of a typical type of breast carcinoma where the invasive carcinoma is sampled, the SPC component is unlikely to be diagnosed. Similarly, invasive SPC cannot be distinguished from SPC in situ and this requires assessment of the excised lesion. The new entity, solid papillary invasive lobular carcinoma is very rare, and has not been reported in the cytopathology literature, but its diagnosis on FNAB is likely to be problematic {35656761; 40118257}, and the same applies to a provisional entity, invasive lobular carcinoma with extracellular mucin {35477749}.

Intraductal papillomas are commonly associated with epithelial hyperplasia and may show atypia and on rare occasion DCIS, usually low-grade. Subsequently, the cytopathological findings may be mixed, and the differentiation between papillary ductal carcinoma in situ and a papilloma with proliferative changes may be difficult in FNAB and also CNB. Features that favour papillary ductal carcinoma in situ are high cellularity, more complex and frequent fibrovascular papillae with thin disorganized fronds, mild to moderate nuclear atypia, and prominent dissociation {11954026}. CNB can be of assistance, but remains a sampling technique, and simple excision is often required to fully assess the lesion.

The diagnostic accuracy of FNAB for papillary lesions is reported to be 59% in one study, with an atypia rate of 24% and an error rate of 17% (false positives and false negatives) although the range of lesions included is broad {18552172}. In general, the lesions are most often recognized as papillary, but with significant spread in the cytopathological diagnostic category from benign to malignant. The accuracy of the specific diagnosis of intraductal papillomas in contradistinction to papillary lesions has been reported in retrospective and prospective studies as 74% with a low false negative rate {17580343; 17636490}.

In invasive papillary carcinoma, the invasive component is usually a carcinoma NST. For all practical purposes, it cannot be identified in the cytopathological material.

Ancillary testing

A simple but crucial test is assessment of a cell block by H&E and ICC for p63 for myoepithelial cells. The presence of architectural papillary features may assist in the diagnosis.

The main aim in ancillary testing is to differentiate between low-grade carcinomas, including carcinomas in situ, and benign hyperplastic changes. There is significant cytomorphological overlap. In a benign hyperplastic lesion, the proliferative epithelium will show CK5/6 and CK14 positivity that can be patchy or diffuse, and a non-clonal heterogeneous expression of ER. In contrast, low-grade DCIS or invasive carcinoma epithelium will be negative for CK5/6 or CK14 high-molecular-weight cytokeratins and show a clonal homogeneous positivity for ER. The use of ER staining on FNAB material has been extensively practised and to date the reported concordance with histopathology is very high, in the range 95–98% {15048960}, and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins such as CK5/6 show similar concordance {19843143}.

In the differentiation between high-grade and low-grade lesions, apart from morphology, ICC staining of additional/available materials using HER2 or p53 can be helpful. p53 has been reported to be highly expressed in high-grade malignancy compared with low-grade malignancy {8542791; 10692218; 21488175}.
