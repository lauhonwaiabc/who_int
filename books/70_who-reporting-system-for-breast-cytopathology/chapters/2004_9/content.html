2.0.0.4 Diagnostic categories and report structure

Categories

Each diagnostic category generates a risk of malignancy (ROM) that can be directly attached to a recommendation either for further diagnostic testing to achieve a specific diagnosis or to refine a differential diagnosis, or for follow-up clinical management. Specific processes or tumours which have a similar ROM are placed in the same category. The WHO Reporting System for Breast Cytopathology (WHO System) uses diagnostic categories to communicate the ROM to the clinician based on the biopsy, but a specific diagnosis or differential diagnosis further refined by ancillary testing is the aim of all cytopathology reporting and is paramount in the report.

The WHO System has five diagnostic categories for breast FNAB cytopathology, and these are applicable to touch preparations of CNB, LBC, and nipple discharge cases.

The diagnostic categories are:

Insufficient / Inadequate / Non-diagnostic Benign Atypical Suspicious for malignancy Malignant.

An individual cytopathologist and a department with more than one cytopathologist should choose one of the terms “Insufficient”, “Inadequate”, or “Non-diagnostic” for cases where there is no material for assessment or there are technical problems which prevent assessment of material on the slides, such as obscuring blood or ultrasound gel. This term should be used consistently. If there is adequate benign tissue on the smears then this is described and reported as “Benign”. In contrast to other organ systems, such as the pancreaticobiliary system where benign tissue that does not explain a malignant-appearing lesion on imaging can be categorized as “Non-diagnostic”, benign breast tissue may well explain the lesion and be diagnostic. For example, a stellate carcinoma on imaging may be a radial scar on FNAB. However, correlation with imaging and physical examination should be carried out wherever possible and if the tissue on the slides does not correlate with or explain the imaging or clinical findings, then a caveat can be placed in the report that clearly states that the tissue “may not represent the lesion seen on imaging and further diagnostic workup is recommended”. The discrepancy may relate to sampling error particularly with small, non-palpable lesions seen only on imaging, or may reflect the limitations of imaging in accurately distinguishing some benign and malignant breast lesions. This is the approach successfully taken in the IAC Yokohama System for Reporting Breast Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy Cytopathology and is incorporated into the WHO System [[Field AS, Raymond WA, Schmitt F, editors. The International Academy of Cytology Yokohama system for reporting breast fine needle aspiration biopsy cytopathology. 1st ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2020.]].

If any atypical features are seen in a case showing otherwise insufficient material, the case is regarded as “Atypical” and not “Insufficient / Inadequate / Non-diagnostic”.

The “Benign” category includes normal breast components, inflammatory processes, fibrocystic change, epithelial hyperplasia, and benign tumours, such as fibroadenomas. If the features are benign, but a precise diagnosis cannot be made, the features should be described and a differential diagnosis provided if possible. As with all categories, correlation with physical examination and imaging is required.

The “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low ROM and allows for a high negative predictive value for a “Benign” diagnosis, while the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to have a high ROM and provide for a high positive predictive value, approaching 100%, for a malignant diagnosis. The categories stratify the ROM and allow for the development of management recommendations.

The “Atypical” category includes cases where there is scant or poorly prepared but atypical cells, and cases where mainly benign cytopathological features are present, but there are some features, such as dispersal of intact cells, mild nuclear atypia, or necrosis, which may raise the possibility of malignancy.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cases where there is scant, poorly smeared, or poorly prepared and stained tissue suggestive of malignancy, and cases where there is considerable tissue suggestive of malignancy, but not all features of a particular malignancy are present or there may be discrepant features.

The “Malignant” category is reserved for those cases in which there are unequivocal features of malignancy without any discrepant findings. All cytopathology specimen types result from a selective, representative sampling technique and it is not always possible to make a precise diagnosis of a specific carcinoma or other malignancy on direct smears, LBC preparations, and CNB, even with ancillary testing on cell blocks and CNB. For example, the distinction between fibroadenoma and low-grade phyllodes tumour usually requires assessment of the entire resected tumour.

The chapters on the section “Benign” and section “Malignant” diagnostic categories follow the order of the sixth-edition Breast tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series (the WHO Blue Books), but not all tumours found in the WHO Blue Book have been described in cytopathology publications, and so are not presented in this cytopathology reporting system. Spindle cell lesions, which usually behave in a benign fashion but have a low risk of recurrence or rarely metastasize, are described in the section “Benign” chapter with a clear discussion of the cytopathological features and ancillary studies that often allow a specific diagnosis. An example is solitary fibrous tumour.

Structured reporting

Each cytopathology report should have a structured format with a heading of a specific diagnostic category in the diagnostic summary. Structured reporting improves the quality, clarity, and reproducibility of reports within individual pathology departments and between institutions and countries, and will improve patient management and facilitate research and quality assurance measures. Standard guidelines for cell block preparation, ICC, and in situ hybridization for tests of prognostic and predictive markers improve accuracy, reduce cost, and improve patient care. Structured reports are based on key diagnostic cytopathological findings, which are presented in this reporting system and act as a checklist for the reporting cytopathologist.

There are core components in a cytopathology report: the precise site of the lesion using the descriptors of side, o’clock in the breast based on relationship to the nipple, and distance from the nipple papilla; diagnostic category using the defined terminology of the reporting system and not a number; a diagnosis, which may be specific or as part of a differential diagnosis; a microscopic description of the key diagnostic features; and, where required, ancillary testing results. The categorization of a lesion implies inadequacy or adequacy, but an optional component is to include a quality indicator stating whether there is adequate, limited, or inadequate tissue for evaluation, followed by the diagnostic category and diagnosis. No particular format is mandatory and this often relies on the local laboratory information system.

It is not recommended to use a number in defining a diagnostic category at any stage. A category number can be included in the body of the report for audit and research purposes but not in the diagnosis. Reducing a cytopathology report to a number reduces the information that can be given to the clinician and management team.

A microscopic description is optional, but is highly recommended when a specific diagnosis is not provided. It should provide a clear, concise cytopathological description, which includes the degree of cellularity and focuses on the presence or absence of key cytopathological diagnostic features.

A discussion and description of ancillary tests when they have been applied to a case should always be part of the final cytopathology report, and if ICC or in situ hybridization reports, or on rare occasions flow cytometry or molecular pathology reports, have been issued by a different laboratory, it is recommended to refer to these tests as pending, and wherever possible to issue an integrated supplementary report to the original cytopathology report, with a final report incorporating all test results.

The WHO Reporting Systems in Cytopathology are based on cytomorphology for the initial categorization, discussion of a differential diagnosis if required, and reporting of the lesion, and this approach can be applied in any department whether well resourced or in a low- to middle-income country setting. But the systems also incorporate the application of appropriate ancillary testing as current best practice, which may well change the final diagnostic category of a particular case.
