9.1.1.1 Invasive carcinoma of no special type and high-grade ductal carcinoma in situ

Definition

Invasive breast carcinoma (IBC) of no special type (NST) is a large and heterogeneous group of IBCs that cannot be classified morphologically as any of the special histopathology types.

Clinical features and imaging

Most cases of IBC-NST present as a single mass in one breast that is palpable in the non-screened population or as a mammographically detected mass in the screened population. In advanced stages, other common presenting symptoms include nipple discharge, nipple inversion, irregular surface of the skin (peau d’orange), skin ulceration, or an axillary mass. In a minority of cases, incidental imaging for non-breast disease may be the first presenting sign of breast carcinoma. Genetically predisposed patients, such as carriers of alterations in BRCA1 and BRCA2, usually present at a younger age than the more common non–genetically predisposed patients. A minority of the patients have multiple and/or bilateral breast masses.

On mammography, IBC-NST presents as a mass with varying contours that can be spiculated, irregular, round to oval, microlobulated, and asymmetrical {19164116}. There may be microcalcifications. Ultrasonographic features include a hypoechogenic mass with calcifications, posterior shadow, spiculated contour, angulated margins, taller than wider orientation, and punctate calcifications {19881096; 7784555}. On MRI, spiculated or irregular margins and marked internal enhancement are predictive of cancer {22589320}, and contrast-enhanced mammography provides similar information.

The ability to diagnose ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) on mammography is lower than for invasive carcinoma and varies with the grade, with high-grade DCIS being more consistently calcified {28199152}. High-grade DCIS may present as asymptomatic fine calcifications which may be pleomorphic, linear, or branching. Less commonly, DCIS presents as a non-calcified mass with focal asymmetry and architectural distortion. On ultrasound, DCIS may present as a hypoechoic, irregular mass with parallel margins and no posterior shadow or as a lesion having mixed solid and microcystic features. On MRI, DCIS may present with a non–mass enhancement which may be segmental or linear {34931856}.

Histopathology

IBC-NST is characterized by malignant cells infiltrating into the breast collagenous and fatty stroma, forming tubules, cords, trabeculae, and solid sheets. Desmoplasia may be prominent and the stroma has variable and in some cases pronounced tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes. A specific medullary pattern showing a florid lymphocytic infiltrate around the circumscribed tumour borders, a syncytial growth pattern, and high-grade nuclei usually presents as a rounded mass on imaging.

Often, there are mixed patterns with the cancer showing regions of special subtypes, such as lobular, tubular, cribriform, mucinous, micropapillary, neuroendocrine, and apocrine.

The invasive component may be admixed with a DCIS component, which can vary from low- to high-grade and is characterized by expanded ductal structures containing malignant epithelial cells, but bounded by a lining of attenuated myoepithelial cells. The molecular characteristics of DCIS are usually similar to those of the invasive component, but may differ for important predictive markers, particularly HER2 (ERBB2).

Discussion and differential diagnosis

IBC-NSTs make up the large majority of breast carcinomas. These tumours are heterogeneous in terms of nuclear grade and growth pattern, which is reflected in the cytopathological material.

In a setting of mammographic microcalcifications without a mass lesion, finding scattered tissue fragments of high-grade carcinoma cells in a background of necrosis and calcifications is highly suggestive of high-grade DCIS or a high-grade DCIS component with invasive carcinoma {15593325; 1316029; 1334826; 8147202}, but invasive carcinoma cannot be ruled out. A number of studies have identified and tested invasion criteria including the presence of stromal fragments infiltrated by carcinoma {16780019; 23729369; 11284955; 11466818; 9678734; 14674064; 14986299; 9360042}, but they have not been widely acknowledged or used. Microinvasion or an invasive lesion within the mammographic microcalcifications of a high-grade DCIS may not be seen on imaging, and when incidentally sampled will have the same cytopathological features as the high-grade DCIS. CNB, which is a similar sampling biopsy, may miss the small invasive component in a region of high-grade DCIS, but has the advantage of being able to diagnose the invasive component when sampled.

Low-grade carcinomas have more cohesive and often larger tissue fragments and fewer single cells, and the degree of nuclear enlargement, anisonucleosis, and pleomorphism of shape and nuclear envelope may be mild. Good nuclear morphology is essential to recognize low-grade carcinoma, as are the lack of myoepithelial cells on the tissue fragments and lack of stripped bare bipolar nuclei in the background. The main differential diagnosis of low-grade carcinoma is fibroadenoma {1468344; 18627406}.

Some carcinomas have an associated osteoclast-like giant cell population. Most of these carcinomas are low-grade, and finding giant cells, prominent stroma, and mildly atypical epithelial tissue fragments should raise the differential diagnosis of phyllodes tumour {30248671}. Occasional high-grade carcinomas may have malignant multinucleated giant cells.

Several authors have proposed and published cytopathological grading systems for breast carcinomas, mainly based on nuclear grade with some additional features such as discohesion, mitoses, and tubular tissue fragments. But there is no international agreement on the usefulness of cytopathological grading of carcinoma and the correlation with histopathological grading reported as 65% to 90% {36304360; 7909010; 8513710; 37266482; 30089943; 29403164; 23905097; 29333191}.

Ancillary testing

Ancillary testing to differentiate malignant from benign lesions is rarely required since the diagnosis of malignancy can be rendered on FNAB smears alone. In difficult cases, ICC for p63 and CK5/14 demonstrating a lack of positive myoepithelial cells has been shown to assist differentiating benign lesions from carcinomas, which are usually negative but may show a limited number of positive cells in some cases {35367980; 27060708; 25810244}. Both p63 and CK14 may be positive in adenoid cystic carcinoma and metaplastic carcinoma, while CK5/6 and CK14 are positive in basal-like invasive carcinoma {36247506; 22520948; 21076464}. ICC for GCDFP-15, TRPS1, and GATA3 may be required to differentiate IBC-NST from metastases and occasionally from other primary breast malignancies such as angiosarcoma and high-grade lymphomas {33011748; 28574279}.

Ancillary testing for prognostic and predictive markers is possible on cell blocks as well as cytopathology smears for ER and PR but uncertainty may remain regarding whether the ICC-stained cells are from a carcinoma in situ or an invasive carcinoma {35367980}. Correlation with imaging is required in the triple test to largely resolve this issue. ICC for these biomarkers can also be applied to metastatic IBC-NST to confirm consistency with the primary carcinoma. PCR and silver in situ hybridization / FISH for ERBB2 have been used on FNAB material, and FISH techniques on cell blocks for detection of ERBB2 amplification signals have been preferred {31141801}. More recently, techniques such as quantitative PCR and NGS have been used routinely on breast cytopathology samples especially in metastatic sites, and methylation marker–based automated cartridge systems have been proposed as having the ability to distinguish benign from malignant tumours in FNAB breast samples {31300453}.
