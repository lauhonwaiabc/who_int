9.1.1.10 Invasive apocrine carcinoma

Definition

Invasive apocrine carcinoma (AC) is characterized by large cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and enlarged nuclei with prominent nucleoli, resembling apocrine sweat glands.

Clinical features and imaging

Invasive AC is a rare subtype, constituting about 1–4% of all breast carcinomas {30240079; 23245877; 18171412}. A worse clinical outcome was observed in patients with invasive AC than in patients with invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (NST) {28445946; 23245877}. Invasive AC usually presents as a firm, poorly circumscribed mass often with axillary lymph node involvement. Microcalcifications may be detected on mammography, when there is an associated ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS).

Histopathology

Invasive AC is composed of cells with abundant granular eosinophilic or finely vacuolated cytoplasm with distinct cell borders {3717305; 15695073}. The nuclei are enlarged and round to oval, with moderate and often marked atypia and prominent nucleoli. The predominant growth pattern is solid. The mitotic activity is usually moderate to high {18248561}. Invasive AC usually exhibits a specific ICC profile, which is positive for AR, GCDFP-15, and HER2 (ERBB2) and negative for ER and PR {27259012; 23245877; 20208479}.

DCIS with apocrine differentiation is mainly observed associated with invasive breast carcinoma NST and shows intermediate or more commonly high nuclear grade with central necrosis and/or microcalcifications {23771415}.

Invasive carcinoma with histiocytoid apocrine cells, which are GCDFP-15–positive and E-cadherin–negative, has prominent eccentric finely vacuolated or foamy cytoplasm resembling large histiocytes, but with large hyperchromatic nuclei, perinucleolar clearing, and prominent nucleoli {7726365}. Most of these tumours are believed to be invasive lobular carcinoma with apocrine differentiation {12089723}. Pleomorphic lobular carcinoma may also contain apocrine cells {1592388}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Cells showing apocrine differentiation are present in a wide spectrum of breast lesions, ranging from benign cysts, through to atypical apocrine hyperplasia {12146046}, ductal carcinoma in situ, and invasive carcinomas {20734358}. Invasive AC needs to be distinguished from benign apocrine lesions, DCIS with apocrine cells, lobular carcinoma with apocrine cells, metaplastic squamous cell carcinoma of breast origin and squamous cell carcinoma metastatic to the breast, and granular cell tumour {12040645}.

Benign apocrine cells show round to oval nuclei with centrally located nucleoli and abundant granular cytoplasm. Degenerative cysts may be lined with large, atypical apocrine cells, which can lead to a false positive diagnosis [[Zajdela A, Vielh P, Di Bonito L. Manuel et atlas de cytologie mammaire. Padova (Italy): Piccin; 1995.]] [[Kulkarni N. Fine needle aspiration cytology of apocrine mammary carcinoma: Report of five cases. Oncyocytology 2012;2:6–9.]]. Invasive AC smears are usually hypercellular with relatively uniform sheets and crowded tissue fragments consisting of cells with variable but often raised N:C ratios, abundant dense to granular cytoplasm, round to oval overlapping malignant nuclei with marked nuclear pleomorphism, and frequent mitotic figures, which is better appreciated on Pap.

Granular cell tumours are relatively easily recognized on smears, exhibiting abundant granular eosinophilic (Pap) cytoplasm, low N:C ratios, indistinct borders at the periphery, and small nucleoli.

Pleomorphic lobular carcinoma in situ, histiocytoid AC, and “oncocytic” carcinoma of the breast (positive for GCDFP-15 and HER2 and usually positive for ER) {21111455} cannot be distinguished reliably from invasive AC on cytopathology, and ancillary testing is required {17017444}.

The diagnosis of DCIS with apocrine cells may be suggested if slightly cellular smears are composed of predominantly abundant granular necrosis associated with microcalcifications {14986299}, although invasive carcinoma cannot be excluded and these cases are usually diagnosed as high-grade carcinomas with necrosis.

Finally, malignant squamous cells may simulate apocrine cells, but keratin debris is absent in invasive AC {37987613; 11813328; 12585027}.

Ancillary testing

Carcinomas with apocrine differentiation express GCDFP-15 {7850069}, an antigen also found in apocrine metaplasia. They also have a characteristic steroid receptor profile that is ER-negative, PR-negative, HER-2 positive, and AR-positive. An androgen signature can also be identified by gene expression array analysis, showing increased androgen signalling and significant overlap with HER2-positive carcinomas {9521507; 20208479; 23245877; 27259012; 15897907}. Granular cell tumour is ER-negative and strongly and diffusely CD68-positive and S100-positive {20074934; 32523283; 11748580}, whereas invasive AC shows only focal and weak positivity for S100.
