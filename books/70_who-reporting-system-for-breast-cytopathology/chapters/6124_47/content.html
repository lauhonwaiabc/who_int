6.1.2.4 Usual ductal hyperplasia

Definition

p;                        

Usual ductal hyperplasia is an architecturally, cytopathologically, and molecularly heterogeneous benign epithelial proliferation primarily expanding terminal duct lobular units, consisting of small to intermediate mildly pleomorphic cells arranged in architecturally disorganized sheets and streaming around secondary lumina.

Clinical features and imaging

Usual ductal hyperplasia is frequently associated with fibrocystic change and has no typical clinical features. It can present as a palpable mass or be seen on mammography as a nonspecific irregular density, architectural change, or asymmetry, with similar nonspecific changes on ultrasound, although the findings may suggest fibrocystic change with or without epithelial hyperplasia and on some occasions the changes may be regarded as “indeterminate”.

Histopathology

Epithelial hyperplasia is characterized by the expansion of lobules and ducts by a proliferation of small to intermediate mildly pleomorphic epithelial cells showing nuclei that vary from small with no atypia to occasionally large and mildly atypical with occasional indentations, notches, folds, pseudoinclusions, small round nucleoli, and infrequent mitoses. The epithelial cells can form solid proliferations expanding the terminal ductules and ducts, or they may form tufts and stream around small slit-like irregular secondary lumina. There may be residual columnar cells lining the duct lumen and focal apocrine change can occur. No necrosis is present, while calcifications may be seen. Epithelial hyperplasia can occur by itself but usually there is fibrocystic or columnar cell change, or the epithelial hyperplasia occurs in an intraductal papilloma, radial scar, fibroadenoma, or low-grade phyllodes tumour.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Usually the ductal cells show pale cytoplasm that can in some cases have distinct margins and occasional vacuoles, and the nuclei are uniform and round to oval or mildly pleomorphic with indentations, grooves, and occasional pseudoinclusions. Nucleoli are single and small and the chromatin is fine to mildly coarse. When in flat or folded sheets, the cells are arranged in a relatively uniform pattern, but in 3D more-complex tissue fragments the nuclei can be haphazardly arranged, overlap, or stream around slit-like irregular secondary lumina of the duct. And the nuclei in some cases, although uniformly oval, will be larger and more pleomorphic, with coarser chromatin and prominent single nucleoli, and the N:C ratio may vary from low to moderate. When cellular or nuclear enlargement and pleomorphism are present it is important to recognize that these changes are uniformly seen across whole tissue fragments in all the cells, and, supported by the overall slide pattern of predominantly large tissue fragments and the presence of myoepithelial cells and bare bipolar nuclei, a “Benign” categorization can be made. These changes may relate to the pro­liferative phase of the menstrual cycle or to the oral contraceptive pill, or other medications when seen in older women.

Care must be taken when assessing stripped nuclei in the background because the presence of bare bipolar nuclei is a key feature of epithelial hyperplasia and benign lesions that include epithelial hyperplasia. These nuclei must be perfectly oval and lack any irregularity of the nuclear envelope and coarse or irregular chromatin or nucleoli, to distinguish them from the stripped nuclei of ductal carcinoma in situ, lobular carcinoma, and invasive carcinoma. Similarly, the presence of myoepithelial cells on epithelial tissue fragments is a crucial feature of benign proliferations, although technically they may be present in ductal carcinoma in situ. They are small, perfectly oval dark nuclei with even chromatin and no nucleoli, and frequently are found in a different focal plane to the larger ductal nuclei, which is better seen in alcohol-fixed Pap-stained smears. This imparts the “bimodal” tissue fragment pattern of benign epithelial proliferations. Special attention should be paid to avoid confusing myoepithelial nuclei in tissue fragments with the apoptotic debris visible in tissue fragments of ductal carcinoma in situ and invasive carcinomas, particularly when their nuclear grade is low. In some instances, these fragments may resemble myoepithelial cells.

Epithelial hyperplasia and columnar cell change are often associated. Columnar cell change involves dilated terminal ductules of lobules with a spectrum of changes from simple columnar epithelium with apical apocrine snouts, through multilayering, to tufting and hyperplasia of the epithelium. Immediately adjacent ducts may show epithelial hyperplasia {12717115}. FNAB of columnar cell change shows bulbous, hyperplastic epithelial tissue fragments, which are 3D and ballooned with a central lumen that can be demonstrated by focusing up and down on the fragment. Myoepithelial nuclei are seen on the outer surface, and there is columnar cell orientation at the margins of the fragments, and columnar cells are usually present in the background [[Field AS. Chapter 5: Breast. In: Field AS, Zarka MR, editors. Practical cytopathology: a pattern recognition diagnostic approach. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2016.]]. Calcifications are often present in a proteinaceous background.

The histopathological distinction on CNB and even larger excision biopsies of the spectrum of flat epithelial atypia, where there is a layer of at least two nuclei in the thickened epithelium and the relatively monotonous nuclei lack orientation to the lumen, to atypical ductal hyperplasia and low-grade cribriform and micropapillary ductal carcinoma in situ can be difficult, with high interobserver variability, and these changes are often intermingled. Their definitive diagnosis on FNAB is generally recognized as problematic, but overall a change in pattern to more frequent smaller tissue fragments along with the larger tissue fragments, fewer or no myoepithelial cells and bare bipolar nuclei, more plentiful dispersed single epithelial cells, and increasing rigidity of micropapillary and/or cribriform architecture are indicators that the FNAB should be categorized as “Atypical”. The aim is not to make these specific diagnoses but to provide a cytopathology report that does not underreport these changes as “Benign”, does not overreport them as “Malignant”, and can maximize the information to correlate with imaging findings in the triple test [[Field AS. Chapter 5: Breast. In: Field AS, Zarka MR, editors. Practical cytopathology: a pattern recognition diagnostic approach. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2016.]] {8143551}.

The features should be clearly described and a differential diagnosis provided that emphasizes the most likely diagnosis. The cytopathologist should assess if the features are consistent with epithelial hyperplasia or if they are atypical, in which case a diagnosis of “epithelial hyperplasia with atypia” is appropriate {8143551}. The specific diagnosis of “atypical ductal hyperplasia”, which is based on histopathological features, should not be attempted {8143551}.

Epithelial hyperplasia can show collagenous spherulosis with magenta (Giemsa) or pale green (Pap) globules derived from basement membrane material within hyperplastic epithelial tissue fragments, often in a background of fibrocystic change [[Field AS. Chapter 5: Breast. In: Field AS, Zarka MR, editors. Practical cytopathology: a pattern recognition diagnostic approach. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2016.]] [[Ducataman BS, Wang HH. Chapter 9: Breast. In: Cibas E, Ducataman B, Van Der Laan P, editors. Cytology: principles and clinical correlates. 5th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2025.]]. The collagen balls need to be distinguished from the larger and more numerous hyaline globules seen in adenoid cystic carcinoma in which the background is usually clean and there is no evidence of fibrocystic change. Stripped myoepithelial nuclei are frequent. If a definite distinction cannot be made, the smears should be categorized as “Atypical” and CNB recommended.

Epithelial hyperplasia can be seen in radial scars, which are usually an incidental mammographic finding mimicking stellate carcinomas, with central and radiating sclerosis containing small distorted tubules, ductal epithelial hyperplasia with or without atypia, apocrine change, adenosis, and sclerosing adenosis. At excision, the number of these cases that are upgraded to atypical ductal hyper­plasia, low-grade ductal carcinoma in situ, or tubular carcinoma varies in the literature. Radial scars in FNAB have moderate to high cellularity and show the features of fibrocystic change with epithelial hyperplasia and apocrine metaplasia {10458501}. Rarely, stromal tufts and small tubules may be seen {10458501}. If the imaging features are suspicious and the cytopathological findings are benign, CNB is recommended.

Fibroadenomas usually show moderately to highly cellular smears with large sheets and branched often 3D hyperplastic ductal epithelial cell tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells, plentiful bare bipolar nuclei, and distinctive stromal fragments, which may be myxoid, large, and rounded, or fibrillary, or small, sclerotic, and tufted. Fibroadenomas may in some cases produce only the epithelial hyperplastic tissue fragments without stroma, and large numbers of bare bipolar nuclei may be a supportive feature for their diagnosis [[Field AS. Chapter 5: Breast. In: Field AS, Zarka MR, editors. Practical cytopathology: a pattern recognition diagnostic approach. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2016.]] {11747216; 2292223} (see section Fibroadenoma and benign phyllodes tumour for further discussion).

Intraductal papillomas frequently are associated with epithelial hyperplasia, and FNAB shows large epithelial hyperplastic tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells in a proteinaceous background with apocrine sheets and haemosiderophages, but the specific diagnosis requires stellate papillary and sometimes more-complex meshwork tissue fragments featuring a fibroelastotic branching stroma {17580343}.

Ancillary testing

ICC for p63 and other markers such as calponin can be used on cell blocks and directly on smears to locate and confirm myoepithelial cells in tissue fragments in epithelial hyperplasia. The ductal cells are CK7-positive and a combined CK7/p63 stain can be utilized. In practice, CK5/6 stains the ductal cells in a variable pattern, and ER staining is patchy in contrast to the uniform staining of atypical ductal hyperplasia and most ductal carcinomas in situ. It should be noted that columnar cell change is usually CK5/6-negative. In practice, ICC is rarely applied to make or support a diagnosis of epithelial hyperplasia.
