2.0.0.3 The integration of clinical, imaging, and key FNAB cytopathological features with ancillary testing in a diagnostic approach

The integration of clinical, imaging, and key FNAB cytopathological features with ancillary testing occurs at two critical points in successful breast FNAB practice: at the time of ROSE and again at the time of final cytopathological diagnosis, when ancillary studies can be incorporated into the interpretation. Interventional cytopathology is a term which has been used to refer to the integration of clinical findings with cytopathology for diagnosis {35725678}. In the ideal setting, FNAB of the breast is performed by an interventional cytopathologist or with an interventional cytopathologist present during the procedure, who can correlate the ROSE of the FNAB with the physical examination findings as well as any available breast imaging studies. The interventional cytopathologist can then adjust the approach taken for the FNAB, and triage the FNAB sample to specific slides and media needed for ancillary testing.

At the time of ROSE, the primary goal of correlating clinical findings with the rapid cytomorphology is to ensure adequate sampling of the targeted lesion. ROSE can significantly reduce the incidence of false negative diagnoses that result from missing the target lesion {11507700}. Physical examination findings that correlate with a benign diagnosis include a lesion that is small or poorly defined, mobile, and soft or cystic with smooth borders. Physical examination findings that favour malignancy include a lesion that is large, fixed, immobile, and irregular in shape, and possibly demonstrates overlying skin changes including ulceration, oedema, or erythema. Similarly, some ultrasound findings that correlate with benign breast lesions include small size and smooth borders, and lesions that are compressible or hypoechoic with posterior enhancement. Some ultrasound findings which suggest malignancy include large size, irregular borders that disrupt the tissue planes, lesions with greater depth than width (taller than wide), heterogeneous echogenicity, and posterior shadowing. Mammography and MRI typically are not used to direct FNAB needles; however, mammographic and MRI findings can help inform the clinical impression at the time of ROSE and may be confirmed with ultrasound examination to then direct the FNAB.

Once the clinical impression of a benign, malignant, or indeterminate lesion has been established, the FNAB is performed and smears should be prepared and examined by ROSE. If the clinical impression is benign, and the ROSE is also benign with sufficient quantity and quality of well-preserved benign ductal cells and stroma or cyst fluid, then the FNAB is adequate. If the clinical impression is benign and an insufficient quantity of quality ductal cells are present on ROSE, then an additional FNAB pass should be considered, possibly targeting a different area of the lesion. In the case where the clinical impression is malignant, and ROSE demonstrates malignant or atypical ductal cells, then the FNAB is adequate. Additional FNAB passes or a CNB are recommended to obtain adequate material for cell block for ICC staining or for other ancillary testing as indicated. In some cases, the clinical impression by physical examination and imaging will be that of a benign lesion but the FNAB-ROSE will demonstrate malignancy {27160231}. Similarly, the clinical and imaging impression may be malignant, and the FNAB-ROSE may demonstrate benign material. In these cases, additional FNAB passes and cell block preparation and/or a CNB are recommended for histopathology and ICC or other ancillary tests that may help confirm the diagnosis at final review.

The triple test, combining physical examination, breast imaging, and cytopathological diagnosis for final diagnosis results in a negative predictive value of nearly 100% for benign breast lesions {15569203}. Without using the triple test, for example when imaging is not performed or when the physical examination and/or imaging findings are not communicated to the cytopathologist, the negative predictive value decreases to 93% {15569203}. At the time of final cytopathological diagnosis, the cytopathologist should correlate the cytomorphological findings with all available clinical and imaging data to reduce false negative and false positive diagnoses. This includes the physical examination as well as mammogram, ultrasound, and MRI findings. In cases where the cytomorphology is benign but the clinical and imaging impressions are suspicious or malignant, the cytopathology report should be descriptive with a recommendation for correlation and review of clinical and imaging findings and a statement that the material may not represent the target lesion. The multidisciplinary team can then decide on the need for additional evaluation by FNAB, CNB, or excision as clinically indicated.

Ancillary testing also plays an important role for the final breast FNAB diagnosis. The most common ancillary study is the cell block, which is often incorporated in the final cytopathology report. Cell blocks can provide suitable material for breast biomarker studies including ER, PR, and HER2 (ERBB2). Given the strict fixation requirements for these assays, cell blocks can be immediately fixed in formalin or placed in a suitable medium such as RPMI or Hanks. These media can be centrifuged, the supernatant removed, and serum and thrombin added to form a clot fixed in formalin, or preferably a histogel/agarose system or the colloidal bag system can be used for cell block formation, which can provide core biopsy equivalents {27105161}. Cell blocks can provide material for ICC for the prognostic indicators and to confirm cell type, such as ductal, lobular, mesenchymal, or lymphoid. Silver in situ hybridization and FISH for ERBB2 can also be performed on the cell block. Additionally, cell blocks can provide material for molecular testing including NGS. In the case of a metastasis to the breast, ICC on the cell block should be used to identify the primary site. The most common metastatic tumours to the breast include melanoma, lymphoma, and lung, ovarian, and contralateral breast carcinomas {14965721}. Other ancillary tests that should be considered at the time of ROSE include flow cytometry for possible lymphoma and microbiology studies for acute suppurative or granulomatous inflammation. It is the role of the interventional cytopathologist to perform ROSE and identify these often unexpected diagnoses at the time of FNAB and ensure adequate material is collected in appropriate media for ancillary studies.
