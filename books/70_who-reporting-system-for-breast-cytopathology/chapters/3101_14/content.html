3.1.0.1 FNAB techniques and specimen management

Methods for obtaining and handling of breast cytopathological samples are similar to those derived from other superficial anatomical sites. Cytopathological sampling techniques are minimally invasive, straightforward to perform, time and cost efficient, very safe, and well tolerated by patients {27160231; 33839072; 27160231}. FNAB material is complementary to histopathological biopsy in many scenarios.

Guidance

While sampling is relatively simple to perform, it remains important to have training from experienced cytopathologists and gain experience in order to minimize non-diagnostic rates {11507700}. Accurate diagnosis relies on obtaining representative samples and creating well-made smears free from artefacts. Depending on the size and anatomical location of the lesion, FNAB can be guided by palpation or by ultrasound. Palpable breast lesions may be sampled using palpation, which simplifies the procedure; if palpation-based sampling is insufficient for diagnosis, ultrasound guidance may then be incorporated if available. Non-palpable or deep lesions are sampled under ultrasound guidance to optimize targeting, support targeted sampling of multiple areas within the lesion, and visually confirm drainage of any cystic components. In addition, the use of ultrasound enhances characterization of lesions < 10 mm {28505308; 10993966}. Palpable lesions are preferably sampled by a cytopathologist utilizing ultrasound to assess the lesion, who can then perform ROSE and assess whether ancillary studies are needed. Non-palpable lesions visible on ultrasound may be sampled by both a cytopathologist and a radiologist in a four-hands approach to obtain FNAB and CNB, if such resources are available.

Breast lesions weakly visualized by ultrasound, such as microcalcifications, should not be sampled using FNAB {10993966}.

Anaesthetic cream is viable for FNAB. Injectable local anaesthesia (e.g. 1% or 2% lidocaine) does not need to be used routinely for FNAB, but is required for CNB to ensure patient comfort {33839072}.

Materials and methods for fine-needle aspiration biopsy, nipple scraping, and nipple discharge evaluation

Fine-needle aspiration biopsy for mass lesions

The needles used are generally 23 gauge and 25 or 30 mm in length, although some cytopathologists prefer 22 gauge. Cystic lesions can be sampled with 22 gauge needles to facilitate evacuation of fluid. Fibrotic (e.g. fibroadenoma, scar), highly vascular, or painful lesions may be sampled with thinner 25 or 27 gauge needles {9237187}.

The application of negative pressure with a syringe may shorten the amount of needle dwell time required to obtain an adequately cellular sample, but may induce bleeding in vascularized lesions, which may hamper subsequent sample interpretation. Sampling without negative pressure may cause less haemorrhage, and the cellular yield is satisfactory {3815294; 1111924}.

A combined approach may be used as appropriate. The physician performing the FNAB can assess the surface and internal texture of the lesion, including its resistance to the needle. After sampling, the material is distributed over one to three smear slides, depending on local practice. A good quality smear is minimally haemorrhagic, centres the material on the slide, and spreads cells in a thin monolayer distributed over an ovoid area. It is advantageous to generate both air-dried and alcohol-fixed smears for diagnostic evaluation. Air-dried smears can be stained with Giemsa and enable ROSE. Alcohol-fixed smears should be immersed in fixative immediately. These may also be utilized for ROSE by staining with toluidine blue; otherwise Pap or H&E staining is typical. Samples may also be deposited into various media or fixatives such as saline, 50% ethanol, or formalin for preparation into cytospins and cell blocks. Preparation of LBC slides and technique using proprietary technologies should follow manufacturers’ guidelines.

Rapid onsite evaluation

The quality of diagnosis can be improved by performing ROSE. Air-dried smears are stained with Giemsa after the smear has completely dried, which can be accelerated by use of a hairdryer, and can then be examined microscopically. Alcohol-fixed slides can also support ROSE if stained by rapid H&E or toluidine blue. ROSE enables assessment for adequacy of the FNAB, reducing the need for expensive and time-consuming repeat studies, enables triaging of material for appropriate ancillary testing, informs physicians as to whether CNB should be pursued immediately for additional evaluation, and allows for immediate discussion and efficient planning for treating the patient {31515971}. In most cases, ROSE and its provisional report, when correlated with clinical and imaging assessment, can immediately reduce patient anxiety regarding breast lesions.

Ancillary techniques

FNAB material can be utilized for the full range of ancillary testing, which closely matches that of surgical biopsy material [[Layfield LJ. Biopsied material from abscesses may be sent for microbiological culture and/or PCR testing. Samples may be reserved in media (e.g. saline, RPMI, Hanks) for flow cytometry to evaluate for lymphoproliferative disorders, particularly low-grade lymphomas {10993966}. Identifying carcinoma or other malignancy at the time of ROSE may prompt preparation of a cell block, which in many clinics is routine, and is very reliable for routine biomarker testing by ICC and silver in situ hybridization / FISH, including E-cadherin, ER, PR, and HER2 (ERBB2) {27383478}. In the case of sarcoma or other rare tumours, the specimen may be handled for specific molecular studies. If available, cryopreservation of tumour cells may allow material to be available for future studies.
