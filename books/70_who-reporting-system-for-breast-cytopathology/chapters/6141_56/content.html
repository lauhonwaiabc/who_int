6.1.4.1 Spindle cell tumours (including myofibroblastoma, pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia, nodular fasciitis, schwannoma, and fibromatosis)

Definition

Spindle cell lesions of the breast consist of spindle cells without an epithelial component.

Myofibroblastoma is a benign tumour of the stroma composed of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts.

Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia (PASH) is a stromal myofibroblastic proliferation lining anastomosing slit-like spaces.

Nodular fasciitis is a self-limited, benign clonal proliferation of fibroblastic/myofibroblastic cells.

Schwannoma is a benign, typically encapsulated peripheral nerve sheath tumour composed entirely of well-differentiated Schwann cells.

Desmoid fibromatosis is an infiltrative spindle cell neoplasm characterized by fibroblastic/myofibroblastic differentiation and activation of the WNT/β-catenin pathway.

Clinical features and imaging

Myofibroblastoma usually presents as a slow-growing, painless, non-tender mass {18976021; 26328916; 35976671} with nonspecific imaging features of a solitary, well-circumscribed, oval or irregular, lobulated, homogeneous, solid mass lacking microcalcifications {27936420; 29420368; 33332985}. Infrequently, it may be occult or present as a focal or developing asymmetry. Rare cases of bilateral or multicentral lesions have been reported {8832563}.

PASH occurs in male and female patients and is frequently incidentally detected in routine biopsies performed for possible benign or malignant breast lesions. It may present as nodular or tumour-forming PASH, with a palpable, painless, firm to rubbery mass; it may coexist with other lesions; or it may present in the form of unilateral or bilateral breast enlargement {27019818}. When detected on a mammogram, PASH appears as a non-calcified, circumscribed or partially circumscribed mass, rarely with irregular margins {8539361; 8553945}. It is a hypoechoic mass on ultrasound and a non-enhancing mass on MRI {26949063}. In male patients, PASH usually appears as a palpable mass associated with gynaecomastia {9659260}.

Nodular fasciitis typically presents with a sudden onset over weeks, then rapid progression, followed by spontaneous regression over months unless the tumour is excised {27721715; 26698206}. It presents as a well-defined painful or painless subcutaneous nodule, with an average size of 20 mm, and a range of 6–60 mm {27721715}. On ultrasound, it is a hypoechoic mass and can create a false impression of malignancy {24349717}.

Schwannomas occurring in the breast represent only 3% of all schwannomas occurring in male and female patients and are rare. They present as solitary, painless, palpable masses occurring over a period of time and mimic fibroadenomas. Ultrasound reveals a well-demarcated hypoechoic mass {17679472}. Multiple schwannomas are associated with neurofibromatosis syndrome, and have a propensity to undergo malignant transformation.

Desmoid fibromatosis is extremely rare in the breast, more common in female than in male patients, and usually arises from the pectoralis fascia and subsequently involves the breast {2920364; 17896146}. Some tumours have been documented in association with saline breast implants, trauma, and previous surgery {2920364}.

Histopathology

Myofibroblastoma is circumscribed and consists of bland, oval to spindle cells with pale to eosinophilic cytoplasm, arranged haphazardly or in short intersecting fascicles with interspersed variably hyalinized collagen bundles. Focal areas with storiform or neural-like growth patterns can be seen. The nuclei are round to oval with indistinct or sometimes small nucleoli, along with grooves and uncommon pseudoinclusions. Mitoses are absent or rare. Interspersed breast ducts or lobules are usually not seen. Unusual features include focally infiltrative margins, high cellularity, focal nuclear pleomorphism, multinucleated floret-like cells, intracytoplasmic and extracellular hyaline globules, extensive myxoedematous stroma with blood vessels containing fibrinoid material, and focal smooth muscle and cartilaginous or osseous metaplasia {18976021; 26328916; 9199625; 3037930}. Significant mitotic count, tumour necrosis, and extensive infiltrating margins are not seen. Various morphological patterns have been described: lipomatous, myxoid, fibrous/collagenized, epithelioid/deciduoid, and palisading/Schwannian-like {18976021; 26328916; 35976671}.

PASH is a myofibroblastic proliferation in the form of slit-like pseudovascular spaces, lacking red blood cells, within a lobule or between one or more breast lobules. Individual cells are spindle and lack significant nuclear atypia. The adjacent epithelium may resemble gynaecomastia {18084246}. PASH may coexist with fibroepithelial tumours, including fibroadenoma and benign phyllodes tumour, and rarely with carcinoma of no special type (NST) {18084246; 22583194}.

Nodular fasciitis is well circumscribed and consists of plump fibroblastic/myofibroblastic spindle cells arranged in intersecting fascicles. Mitoses may be seen but atypical mitotic figures are not identified. The intervening stroma is variable, depending upon the age of the lesion, ranging from microcystic to myxoid to collagenous, and includes extravasated red blood cells. Infiltrative growth into fat or lobules has been reported. Early lesions are typically more cellular and more mature lesions are more collagenous {27721715; 28751104; 34099872}.

Schwannomas are composed of alternating hypercellular (Antoni A) and hypocellular (Antoni B) loosely textured areas, composed of spindle cells with minimal atypia, wavy and tapering nuclei, and nuclear palisades. Hyalinized, thickened blood vessels along with lymphocytic aggregates are usually seen {28647116; 23326032}. Various other morphological forms of schwannomas, mostly reported at various other sites, include epithelioid, plexiform, and rarely melanotic schwannoma {28368924; 18439936; 24145644}.

Desmoid fibromatosis shows intersecting fascicles of uniform spindle cells containing nuclei with tapering ends, fine chromatin, inconspicuous or faintly discernible nucleoli, and poorly defined cytoplasm, separated by fibrillary strands of collagen. The tumour cells infiltrate into lobules, fat, and skeletal muscles. A variable amount of collagen is present along with myxoid change in some cases. Mitoses are rare to absent. Tumour cells lack significant nuclear atypia. Lymphoid aggregates are seen towards the periphery, which constitutes a diagnostic clue {28751104}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of myofibroblastoma includes a descriptive term, “benign spindle cell tumour”, low-grade phyllodes tumour, and malignant tumours. Most cases lack epithelial cells, excluding a phyllodes tumour, despite the presence of metachromatic stroma in both the tumours. In occasional cases with epithelial cells, fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumour are close differentials. In some cases with collagenized stroma, a solitary fibrous tumour enters the differential diagnosis and ICC is helpful {23075890}. Myofibroblastoma and solitary fibrous tumour express CD34. In such cases, STAT6 immunostaining is useful, as it is positively expressed in a solitary fibrous tumour. Rare cases including cellular patterns have been mistaken for malignancy {11992369; 20518427; 9622694; 23075890}.

PASH can be mistaken for fibroadenoma or phyllodes tumour owing to the bimodal epithelial tissue fragments, bare bipolar nuclei, and stromal fragments, but in most cases ductal epithelial cells are far less prominent than those seen in a fibroadenoma {12789917; 15880710; 16758690}. Borderline phyllodes tumour shows increased cellularity within the stromal fragments, along with atypia, mitotic figures, and occasionally necrosis. Myofibroblastomas show cells with intranuclear grooves and sometimes pseudoinclusions and lack the benign bimodal epithelial cell tissue fragments and myoepithelial cells {23075890; 12789917}. Schwannomas show elongate, indented, wavy nuclei and have distinctive ICC.

The differential diagnosis of nodular fasciitis includes most spindle cell tumours, including fibromatosis, phyllodes tumour, myoepithelioma, and reactive lesions, namely scar, granulation tissue, and inflammation, as well as metaplastic carcinoma, especially fibromatosis-like carcinoma and sarcoma {26838327}. A careful assessment of morphological features along with clinical and imaging correlation and judicious application of ancillary tests is essential for differentiating these various entities. Unlike adenomyoepithelioma and phyllodes tumour, nodular fasciitis does not include epithelial and myoepithelial cells {16544319}. The distinction between fibromatosis and nodular fasciitis can be challenging on FNAB. However, nodular fasciitis is relatively hypercellular in contrast to fibromatosis, and displays a granular to myxoid background, in contrast to collagenous stromal fragments in fibromatosis against a relatively clean background. Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours show intranuclear grooves along with plasma cells. Metaplastic carcinomas, including fibromatosis-like, show positive expression of epithelial markers, along with p63, unlike nodular fasciitis {22849737}. Sarcomas, although rare in the breast, will display unequivocally malignant cells with mitotic figures and tumour necrosis {15187996}.

The differential diagnosis of a schwannoma includes fibroadenoma and phyllodes tumours, fibromatosis, metaplastic carcinoma, including fibromatosis-like, myoepithelial tumours, and neurofibroma. The fibroepithelial tumours have an epithelial cell component and myoepithelial cells {23326032}, while fibromatoses, metaplastic carcinoma, and myoepithelial tumours require ancillary testing.

Desmoid fibromatosis has a wide differential diagnosis ranging from scar, to nodular fasciitis, fibroepithelial tumours, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours, and myofibroblastoma, to fibromatosis-like metaplastic carcinoma. The presence of foamy and haemosiderin-laden macrophages suggests reactive scar. Nodular fasciitis has a typical sudden onset clinically, and shows a granular background with myxoid to collagenous fragments, depending upon the clinical stage of the lesion, whereas fibromatosis displays collagenous fragments.

Ancillary testing

Myofibroblastoma coexpresses desmin and CD34, along with ER, PR, and AR {18976021; 26328916}, while SMA, calponin, CD10, BCL2, and CD99 are variably expressed, and cytokeratins, EMA, S100, STAT6, ALK, and β-catenin are negative {29940288; 29413661}. Deletion of 13q14 can be tested and confirmed by FISH in as many as 80% of tumours, which results in loss of expression of RB1 {29940288}.

PASH is positive for CD34, SMA, desmin, ER, and PR, and negative for keratins, S100, and endothelial markers, such as CD31, D2-40, and ERG {7872425; 15108236; 1989462}.

Nodular fasciitis is positive for SMA and negative for pankeratin, CD34, desmin, and nuclear β-catenin. USP6 gene rearrangement including MYH9::USP6 fusion along with other fusion partners is present {34099872} as shown by FISH {26698206}.

Schwannomas are positive for S100P and SOX10 {8050328; 23326032; 22495377}, while nearly half of epithelioid schwannomas display loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) {28368924}. Melanotic schwannomas are positive for HMB45 and melan-A {24145644}.

Nuclear β-catenin is positive in fibromatosis, differentiating it from other spindle cell tumours of fibroblastic/myofibroblastic type {11823972; 15832090}, and can be confirmed by CTNNB1 mutations {21076461}.
