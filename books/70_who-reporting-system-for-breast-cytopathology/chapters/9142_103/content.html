9.1.4.2 Breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma

Definition

Breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) is a mature CD30-positive T-cell lymphoma that arises in association with a breast implant as an effusion confined by a fibrotic pseudocapsule, and less commonly forms an invasive mass.

Clinical features and imaging

BIA-ALCL is uncommon, with a risk of 1:300 to 1:30 000 among patients with textured breast implants {28157769; 32008941; 30817554; 29302687; 37289985}, detected at a median of 8–9 years (range: 2–32 years) after implant insertion {24323027; 33358076}. Approximately 70% of patients present with a peri-implant effusion manifested by a large and/or painful breast, and about 30% of patients present with a tumour mass. Local invasion of the chest wall is more common than skin involvement, but both are uncommon. Approximately 5% of patients have bilateral disease, where usually one side is symptomatic, and the contralateral tumour is detected upon capsulectomy. About 20% of patients have regional lymphadenopathy at diagnosis, more commonly involving the axilla than the infraclavicular or supraclavicular regions {24323027; 29194092}. BIA-ALCL can rarely disseminate to distant sites, including bone marrow {29635424}. Using Ann Arbor staging, 86% of patients have stage I and 14% have stage II disease {26628470}.

An effusion surrounding breast implants is common in the immediate postoperative period, but an effusion occurring > 1 year after implant placement requires workup. Ultrasound is most helpful for detecting an effusion, with a sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 75% in one study {25073777}. After detection of a late effusion, FNAB should be the first diagnostic procedure. The effusion fluid is often referred to as a seroma because it is considered to arise from serum, but the effusion in patients with BIA-ALCL is strictly an exudate, which is rich in proteins and commonly includes necrotic cells {30206414}. CT, PET-CT, or MRI are optimal methods for detecting and following a mass {30715173}. Mammography has a low sensitivity and specificity for detecting BIA-ALCL but is useful to detect other associated breast abnormalities {33358076; 25073777}.

Histopathology

Removal of the breast implant and its surrounding capsule en bloc is optimal treatment of patients with resectable tumours {31383966}. The only evidence of neoplasia may be a layer or clusters of neoplastic cells adherent to the luminal surface, often with minimal infiltration of the capsule {22613996; 24323027}, or in some cases only as dispersed cells in the effusion {26628470}. Extensive capsular infiltration may be microscopic or may form a larger tumour mass. The neoplastic cells are generally large with pleomorphic or anaplastic lobated nuclei, prominent nucleoli, generally pale chromatin, and abundant variable cytoplasm. Hallmark cells, with a horseshoe-shaped nucleus and paranuclear hof, are present in about 70% of cases. Necrotic ghosts of neoplastic cells may be present within the effusion fluid or adherent to the luminal surface.

Infiltration of the capsule is staged using the TNM system: T1, no infiltration of capsule; T2, minimal infiltration of capsule; T3, cell aggregates or sheets infiltrating the capsule; T4, infiltration beyond the fibrous capsule {26628470}. In regional lymph nodes, the neoplasm typically involves the sinusoids, but more extensive involvement can also occur {29194092}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

BIA-ALCL is diagnosed by correlation of imaging and FNAB cytopathology, followed by surgery to remove the implant and capsule, which is curative in most cases {26628470}. In approximately 12% of cases, the neoplastic cells are confined to the effusion without involvement of the capsule, so cytopathological evaluation of the peri-implant breast effusion may be the only evidence of the disease {31383966}.

The main differential diagnosis is reactive peri-implant breast seroma, which can be highly cellular, composed of foamy histiocytes, small lymphocytes, and sometimes granulocytes and immunoblasts, with a dense eccentric or rim of cytoplasm and a large, rounded nucleus and central nucleolus. Pseudosynovial cells that line the luminal aspect of the capsule comprise another possible source of atypical cells present in a reactive seroma. These are intermediate round or polygonal cells with abundant dense cytoplasm and a small, round nucleus {29633403; 36634640}.

The differential diagnosis in patients with breast cancer who have undergone reconstructive surgery also includes recurrent breast carcinoma. Poorly differentiated breast carcinoma can present with discohesive and pleomorphic tumour cells and a pancytokeratin should be added to an ICC panel to exclude this possibility {29633403; 36634640}.

The most common B-cell lymphomas that arise in the breast are diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma {18636016}, and these neoplasms have been reported rarely in association with breast implants {32315816}.

Ancillary testing

ICC studies can be performed on cytospins or a cell block.

The neoplastic cells of BIA-ALCL uniformly and strongly express cytoplasmic and membranous CD30, and one or more T-cell antigens, such as CD4 (80%) and CD43 (80%). About 70% express cytotoxic markers, such as TIA1, perforin, and/or granzyme B. These neoplasms are often negative for CD3, CD5, CD7, and T-cell receptors. BIA-ALCL is negative for B-cell markers, ALK, and EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER). The expression of CD4 and lack of expression of CD20 and EBER distinguish BIA-ALCL from breast implant–associated EBV-positive large B-cell lymphoma {34226673}.

On flow cytometry, the cells often have higher forward and side scatter than normal lymphocytes {32525554; 32045544; 37149362}. Although flow cytometry is recommended in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for diagnosis of ALCL and is helpful in excluding B-cell lymphomas and confirming a T-cell proliferation, it may not be required if a cytopathological specimen is definitive {30715173; 37149362}.

Most cases of BIA-ALCL carry clonal TRG and/or TRB rearrangements. Conventional cytogenetic analysis of a few cases has shown complex karyotypes. Loss of 20q13.13 has been detected in 66% of cases of BIA-ALCL {33331925}. No rearrangements of ALK, DUSP22, or TP63 have been detected {30715169}.
