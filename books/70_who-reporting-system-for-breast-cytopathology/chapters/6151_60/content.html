6.1.5.1 Nipple adenoma

Definition

Nipple adenoma is a benign epithelial proliferative lesion, featuring ductal/tubular glands involving the superficial ducts of the nipple, surrounding stroma, and often the epidermis.

Syringomatous tumour is a locally infiltrative neoplasm of the nipple and areolar region, composed of epithelial cells with glandular and squamous morphology.

Clinical features and imaging

Nipple adenoma, also known as florid papillomatosis or erosive adenomatosis, usually presents as a nodule in the nipple which may be associated with discharge and epithelial erosion. The condition is benign but can be longstanding and painful, and sometimes leads to effacement or destruction of the nipple {27206635; 31537599}. Mammography and ultrasound may not reveal a mass lesion in the nipple/subareolar region but are helpful to exclude other breast lesions. MRI or dermoscopic methods can be useful {35915844; 25639650}. Surgical excision is curative, and newer, minimally invasive procedures may preserve the nipple {33935461; 27045746}.

Syringomatous tumour, also known as syringomatous adenoma or infiltrating syringomatous adenoma, usually presents with a palpable mass or occasionally with nipple retraction and discharge. The imaging findings can be suspicious for malignancy. Although the growth pattern is infiltrative, the lesion is considered benign and does not metastasize; complete excision is usually curative with possible local recurrence if not excised completely. It typically presents as a unilateral mass but rarely can be bilateral {32596979; 36128591; 15327500}.

Histopathology

Nipple adenoma consists of an aggregate of benign tubules in an adenosis pattern in the papilla of the nipple and may be associated with epidermal erosion. The glands are lined by epithelial and myoepithelial cells and separated by stroma, and depending on the prevailing histopathological pattern, four types of nipple adenoma are described: adenosis, papillomatosis, pseudoinfiltrating, and mixed types {14352171; 27206635; 27206635; 37272622}.

Syringomatous tumour of the nipple shows compressed duct-like structures with dual glandular and squamous differentiation. The growth pattern is infiltrating with irregular comma or teardrop shapes in a dense stroma, located in the nipple or subareolar breast {6660349; 2919717; 24713747}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Nipple adenoma can be diagnosed by FNAB and by scraping the lesion, particularly when it is ulcerated. Clinically, the main differential diagnosis is Paget disease of the nipple, which shows mainly single large cells with large, highly atypical nuclei and prominent nucleoli usually admixed with squamous cells. The characteristic features of intraductal papilloma, including stellate fibroelastotic tissue fragments and epithelial hyperplasia in a background of apocrine cells and siderophages, are not seen. Fibroadenoma has epithelial ductal tissue fragments and myxoid or sclerotic stroma but enters the differential diagnosis. Coincidence with invasive carcinomas of no special type (NST) has been described but there is no correlation with breast carcinoma {3006526; 27206635; 37272622}.

The differential diagnosis of a syringomatous tumour includes low-grade adenosquamous carcinoma, which is a metaplastic carcinoma with glandular and squamous components, and some authors emphasize the morphological and molecular overlap between these two tumours, while tubular carcinoma and grade 1 NST carcinomas must also be considered and show variably prominent tubule formation and single cell dispersal {15327500; 15327500}.

Ancillary testing

In nipple adenoma, the epithelium shows irregular staining with CK5/6 and CK14, and p63-positive and SMM-positive myoepithelial cells.

Syringomatous tumour tissue fragments show outer cells positive for p63, p40, and SMM, while the inner cells are negative for ER and PR {37346881; 24713747}.
