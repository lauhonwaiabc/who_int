6.1.1.1 Suppuration

Definition

Acute inflammation is a suppurative inflammatory pattern of predominantly neutrophils, with or without necrosis.

Clinical features and imaging

Suppurative inflammation presents as mastitis or a more localized breast abscess, most frequently in lactating women. Mastitis is described in as many as 25% of lactating patients while breast abscess develops in 4–10%. Nipple damage such as cracks and dryness are associated with mastitis and breast infection. In non-lactating patients, those who have undergone trauma to the breast, patients with diseases causing immunosuppression, such as diabetes mellitus and HIV/AIDS, and those receiving immunosuppressive therapy are predisposed to mastitis and breast abscess {34991976; 23170038; 35168773; 28606308}.

Patients may present with generalized symptoms such as fever, chills, and malaise while the breast will be painful, diffusely erythematous, and oedematous. A tender and warm mass is noted in breast abscess. A purulent nipple discharge and reactive axillary lymphadenopathy can be seen. The most common organisms are Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus viridans, and then Corynebacterium spp. Actinomycosis has been described in patients with nipple rings. Melioidosis can be seen {32978864}, and mother-to-child transmission via lactation has occasionally been described. Fungal infections of the breast are uncommon and tend to occur in immunosuppressed patients while parasitic infection of the breast is occasionally encountered on FNAB, such as cysticercosis, filariasis, schistosomiasis, and hydatid disease.

Imaging is not usually performed in lactating patients with mastitis, but ultrasound is the preferred imaging technique {29350290; 34991976; 34012369; 36651859; 30123878; 28606308}.

Histopathology

An extensive, predominantly acute inflammatory infiltrate with necrotic debris is present, often obscuring lobules and ducts, with central necrosis in abscesses. Surrounding granulation tissue and chronic inflammation with multinucleated giant cells will be seen as the abscess resolves.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Extensive population of inflammatory cells, predominantly neutrophils Fibrinous and necrotic debris in the background In the later stages, chronic inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes and histiocytes, and granulation tissue Reparative or atypical changes in ductal cells Specific infectious organisms may be seen on routine and special stains

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Breast FNAB is a simple, rapid, and cost-effective procedure to investigate a patient with an inflamed breast lesion, and as far as possible, drainage by FNAB is the current recommended management for abscesses, particularly lactational abscesses.

Granulomatous breast disease may present with collections of neutrophils, but epithelioid histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and chronic inflammatory cells should be looked for, and microbiological studies are required. Periductal mastitis can present with acute inflammatory cells, but usually shows amorphous debris, plasma cells, foamy macrophages, giant cells, calcification, and cholesterol clefts. Numerous neutrophils are seen in chronic recurring subareolar abscess, along with mature and anucleate squamous cells. Skin epidermal cysts can rupture and show neutrophils, anucleate squamous cells, keratin debris, and multinucleated often foamy giant cells. The site of the lesion is required for diagnosis.

Inflamed reactive ductal and terminal ductular cells occur in cohesive tissue fragments with myoepithelial cells, in a background of pus, and nuclear changes of enlargement and prominent nucleoli can be seen with uniformly oval nuclei across any particular tissue fragment.

Inflammatory breast carcinoma presents with clinical features of inflammation, including skin oedema and redness due to dermal lymphatic obstruction, and FNAB shows usually high-grade malignant cells dispersed and in small tissue fragments and few if any inflammatory cells. Necrosis can be seen in both acute inflammation and inflammatory carcinoma. However, the presence of neutrophils and lack of malignant cells favour acute inflammation rather than breast carcinoma. On nipple discharge in a patient with a breast abscess, acute inflammatory cells and reactive duct cells may be seen {24450694; 32816262; 32352648; 29350290; 30123878}.

Ancillary testing

Breast FNAB and nipple discharge provide material for microbiological studies, including culture and sensitivity studies for Gram-positive bacteria, tuberculosis, and fungi. Isolation of an organism on FNAB is considered significant as contamination during specimen collection is limited. Blood cultures are performed in patients who exhibit signs of sepsis. Special stains such as Gram stain, stains for acid-fast bacilli, and fungal stains can be performed on direct smears and cell blocks. Other ancillary tests such as ICC, in situ hybridization, PCR, and serology are used when appropriate {30123878; 30156776; 35168773; 29350290; 32352648}.
