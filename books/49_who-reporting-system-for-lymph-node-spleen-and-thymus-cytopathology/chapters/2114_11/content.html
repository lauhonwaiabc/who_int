2.1.1.4 Cell preparation methods

Sample preparation can be carried out in several ways, provided that adequate evaluation occurs and there is material for eventual ancillary techniques. Direct smears are the simplest way to place cells on the slide to ideally produce a monolayer preparation with excellent cytomorphological detail. But the apparent simplicity of direct smears is misleading, because the technique is operator-dependent and care must be taken, particularly when dealing with fragile cell populations such as lymphocytes. The FNAB material must be rapidly transferred from needle onto the glass slide to avoid clotting within the needle.

The material required for smears should be limited to a tiny drop, about 3–5 mm in diameter, and with the touch-off technique divided into two smears: one air-dried Giemsa-stained smear for ROSE and subsequent assessment, and one quickly dipped in 95% alcohol for H&E or Pap stain for later examination. The surplus material can be rinsed from the needle (and the syringe if blood has inadvertently been aspirated into the syringe) and can be used to prepare a cell block or cytospins, or to send directly for molecular techniques {29989244; 27081878}.

Any ancillary diagnostic technique currently used for diagnosis and therapy selection can be performed on smears, but it requires additional validation {27081878; 28475299}. Sample optimization is best achieved on cell blocks. Lymph node direct smears usually display stripped nuclei and cytoplasmic debris hampering immune assessment, in particular when dealing with CD immunocytochemistry.

The cytospin technique is a quick and cost-effective method of increasing cell yield in hypocellular samples, preserves cell morphology, and provides a basis for excellent immunostains, although there are difficulties in working with validated controls in smears and cytospins.

Cell blocks are optimal for ancillary studies when prepared from highly cellular fluids, and they are processed as formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks, with the advantage that numerous sections can be prepared. Cell blocks provide reasonable cytomorphology and useful tissue architecture, and immunocytochemistry can be easily applied, using validated techniques for histopathology. There are several methods of performing cell blocks and all are suitable for morphological evaluation, immunocytochemistry, and molecular techniques {25113785}.

FISH studies are better achieved on cytospins and on direct smears than on cell block sections because they provide monolayer preparations and avoid truncating artefacts associated with tissue sections {20862704; 29989244}.
