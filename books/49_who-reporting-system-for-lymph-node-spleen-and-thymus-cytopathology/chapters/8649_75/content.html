8.6.4.9 Follicular lymphoma

Definition

Follicular lymphoma (FL) is a neoplasm of germinal-centre B cells with varying proportions of centrocytes and centroblasts or large transformed cells, with an at least partially follicular growth pattern or in rare cases an entirely diffuse growth pattern of cells showing the same germinal-centre B-cell morphology and immunophenotype.

Clinical features and imaging

FL accounts for approximately 5% of all haematological malignancies and 20–25% of all new non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), being the second most common type of NHL, after diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS). In the fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, FL with a follicular growth pattern is classic FL (cFL) [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Rare cases of cFL do present with cFL grade 3A cytopathology and a diffuse pattern, and if the cells are large, then this is categorized as DLBCL-NOS. Two new additional subtypes, FL with diffuse growth pattern and FL with blastoid and large centrocytic cells await elucidation in the cytopathology literature. The M:F ratio is 1:1.7, with a median age range of 60–65 years [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Patients with FL often present with painless lymph node enlargement and widespread lymphadenopathy (stages III–IV) at diagnosis, detected by staging studies. About 20% of patients have localized disease. Patients are often asymptomatic, and abnormal laboratory findings are uncommon; anaemia is not frequent. An increase in serum LDH and β2 microglobulin levels occurs in approximately 15% of patients.

The most common ultrasound features in FL are enlargement > 20 mm, round shape, absent or eccentric hilum, and hypoechoic pattern; calcifications and necrosis are generally absent. Power Doppler ultrasound may show increased and abnormal vascularization, but these findings are not specific. However, similar ultrasound findings may be observed post-vaccination or in HIV infection, infectious mononucleosis, and other reactive lymphadenopathies, mainly in children or adolescents {22351626; 33290901}. If more than one lymph node is involved at the same site, a tendency to aggregate can be observed, as well as moulding. PET-CT is the standard imaging modality for lymphoma staging and prognosis, but not for diagnosis.

FL usually has a chronic and relapsing clinical course. The relapse rate after treatment is high [[Medeiros LJ, Miranda RN, editors. Diagnostic pathology. Lymph nodes and extranodal lymphomas. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2018.]]. FL cases have a risk of undergoing transformation to an aggressive lymphoma, most often DLBCL-NOS or less often a DLBCL / high-grade B-cell lymphoma (HGBCL) with MYC and BCL2, or DLBCL-NOS where BCL2 and BCL6 rearrangements are present. The risk of FL undergoing transformation to DLBCL-NOS is 2–3% per year for the first 10 years after diagnosis {25499449; 29452666}. Transformation is most frequently observed in patients in the fifth to sixth decades of life.

Clinical features associated with a poorer prognosis in FL patients are also associated with high-risk of transformation, and include older age, male sex, elevated serum LDH level, advanced-stage disease, and high age-adjusted Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (FLIPI) score {25499449; 17485708}.

Histopathology

Most cases of typical cFL have a complete or partial follicular (nodular) pattern, but rare cases can be entirely diffuse (see Discussion and differential diagnosis, below). The follicles are closely packed and replace the nodal architecture. Follicles appear monotonous and have thinned, incomplete, or absent mantle zones. The neoplastic cells are centrocytes (small to medium, cleaved cells) and centroblasts (medium to large, nucleolated cells), intermingled with a variable number of non-neoplastic lymphocytes, histiocytes, and dendritic cells. The prevalence of centroblasts, typically counted manually, has been traditionally used for grading. Macroscopically, affected lymph nodes are round to oval, sometimes bulky, and nodular, with a greyish cut surface.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Highly cellular smears Dispersed mixed pattern of lymphoid cells of different sizes, intermingled with a variable number of small reactive mature lymphocytes Follicular architecture demonstrated by denser regions of follicular nodules is frequently observed on direct smears at low magnification; however, diffuse areas are present. Follicular nodules are seen as dense cellular lymphoid tissue fragments consisting of dendritic cells with smoothly outlined oval nuclei with fine beaded chromatin and small or inconspicuous nucleoli and no discernible cytoplasm; small to medium round centrocytes with irregular, indented, or angulated nuclei with dense or more dispersed chromatin with small nucleoli; and larger round centroblasts with a rim of cytoplasm and round to slightly oval nuclei with fine dispersed chromatin and one or two visible nucleoli, often on the nuclear margin. There may be small capillaries associated with the nodules. Tingible-body macrophages are generally absent. Pseudoaggregation of lymphoid cells can be seen. Mitoses are occasionally observed in grade 1 and 2 FLs, but when > 0.4 mitoses/mm2 (two or more mitoses per high-power field) are detected, high-grade FL should be considered. Variable numbers of reactive T lymphocytes and dendritic cells are present in the background. In cases of signet-ring cell FL, a rare pattern, smears show a variable number of signet-ring cells with cytoplasmic vacuoles and marginated nuclei. A diagnostic clue is the similarity in nuclear details between the vacuolated signet-ring cells and non-vacuolated lymphomatous cells. A sclerosing pattern of FL has been reported in retroperitoneal, mediastinal, or deep-seated inguinal lymph nodes, producing less cellular smears with crushed nuclei and cell distortion caused by the fibrosis (correlates with the histopathological partial or complete fibrotic effacement of the lymph node), but the fibrous component is often scant or undetectable in smears. In paediatric-type FL, smears tend to be highly cellular and contain numerous “blastic” cells of intermediate size between centrocytes and centroblasts, associated with plentiful tingible-body macrophages, which may lead to misinterpretation as reactive.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Grading is no longer mandatory for FL because of concerns about the reproducibility of grading and the common clinical outcomes produced by current treatment protocols for grades 1, 2, and 3A [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Recent studies have highlighted the lack of reproducibility in centroblast counting {29472351}. FL grades 1, 2, and 3A are biologically related entities with similar genetic profiles, are clinically managed in a common fashion with similar outcomes following current treatment protocols, and have been merged in the new WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours (WHO-HAEM5) classification under the umbrella of cFL [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {27117536; 34334687; 28976863; 30184451}. cFL represents the vast majority of FL and shows a follicular growth pattern, centrocytes and centroblasts admixed, and the t(14;18)(q32;q21) translocation with the IGH::BCL2 fusion. In WHO-HAEM5, grade 3B FL has been renamed as a subtype of FL, follicular large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), which can often show a diffuse pattern of infiltration {33704790} and shares biological and molecular features with DLBCL-NOS. . This new classification has also introduced a new subtype, FL with uncommon features, which includes two subsets: FL with “blastoid” or “large centrocyte” cytopathology shows more variable immunotype and genotype and worse prognosis and must be distinguished from LBCL with IRF4 rearrangement; FL with a predominantly diffuse growth pattern usually presents in the inguinal region with prominent lymphadenopathy and shows CD23 positivity, absence of IGH::BCL2 fusion, and frequent STAT6 mutations and 1p36 deletion or TNFRSF14 mutation {37561599}.

FNAB generally produces dense whitish material that is very smooth to smear. The lymphoid material and needle rinses can be suspended in specific media according to the selected ancillary technique, such as flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and molecular tests. Part of the sample can also be used for liquid-based cytopathology, Flinders Technology Associates (FTA) cards, and cryopreservation {16937377; 29989244}.

The nuclear shape, chromatin pattern and nucleoli, and cytoplasmic features can be assessed in the traditional haematologists’ Giemsa-stained smears, whereas Pap-stained smears emphasize nuclear shape and nucleoli and are less useful for chromatin and cytoplasmic assessment. But many cytopathologists use both stains in all cases, and most have personal preferences.

The differential diagnosis includes, most importantly, reactive follicular hyperplasia, which also often shows a low-power pattern of follicles and at higher power a mixed lymphoid population, but with predominantly small lymphocytes, a variable (sometimes large) number of tingible-body macrophages, and many mitotic figures, as compared with FL. Large “blue cells”, which are large lymphoid cells with circumferential dense and well-demarcated cytoplasm, are often observed in lymph node cortical and paracortical hyperplasia and absent in FL.

Specific reactive processes such as progressive transformation of germinal centres and the hyaline-vascular subtype of Castleman disease cannot be confidently diagnosed on FNAB samples. FL should be considered and excluded through ancillary techniques when available {22351626; 30194715}, or by excision biopsy.

Mantle cell lymphoma, marginal zone lymphoma, and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma can be difficult to distinguish from particularly low-grade FL, and it is vitally important to have well-made direct smears with well-fixed Giemsa-stained and Pap-stained smears to assess. Often, the assessment of “small-celled” lymphomas requires flow cytometry and cytogenetics {12672094; 19247181; 10884795; 29131043} or excision biopsy with appropriate ancillary tests.

Evolution over time from a predominantly centrocytic pattern to a predominant centroblastic population with increased mitotic figures should raise the possibility of transformation of FL to DLBCL-NOS. Correlation with clinical presentation and elevated LDH is essential.

HGBCLs represent a complex and challenging differential diagnosis with “high-grade” FL (grade 3A), and the diagnosis of “high-grade” FL mainly depends on the correlation of cytopathological features with immunophenotype and clinical setting {26414904; 31042638}. Grade 3B FL, now known as follicular LBCL [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]], cannot be specifically diagnosed on cytopathology and requires ancillary testing and usually excision biopsy.

Paediatric-type FL presents as localized lymphadenopathy in children, adolescents, and young adults. Highly cellular smears containing numerous medium-sized “blastic” cells, which show rounded nuclei with fine chromatin, and tingible-body macrophages and small lymphocytes can mimic reactive follicular hyperplasia. The presence of numerous tingible-body macrophages may lead to misinterpretation of smears as reactive {33273959; 33290901}. Flow cytometry and molecular analysis are recommended in clinically suspicious cases {27325104; 27592067}.

LBCL with IRF4 rearrangement presents as an isolated painless neck mass in children, adolescents, and young adults, similar to paediatric-type FL. FNAB smears can be equally cellular and contain a monomorphic population of centroblast-like cells or intermediate-sized “blastic” cells more suggestive of a high-grade lymphoma. In the right clinical setting and age group, when such a neoplasm is in the differential diagnosis, biopsy is recommended {21487109}.

Ancillary testing

The most common differential diagnosis, based on the cytopathology smears with a mixed pattern, is follicular hyperplasia, which shows on immunophenotyping by either immunocytochemistry or flow cytometry no evidence of light chain restriction or aberrant immunophenotype, and molecular studies show no evidence of clonal immunoglobulin gene rearrangements, t(14;18)(q32;q21), or BCL2 rearrangements {22351626; 12672094; 19247181; 10884795; 19306424; 22517675}. Despite the high reliability of FNAB in the diagnosis of FL when good-quality direct smears are available stained with both Giemsa and Pap stains, well-known pitfalls must be considered. The distinction on FNAB of low-grade FL with a population of predominantly small to intermediate centrocytes from other small-celled lymphomas relies on excellent-quality smears and is often not possible. Flow cytometry is most useful in this setting. HIV infection or autoimmune reactive conditions may cause immunoglobulin light chain restriction in a percentage of B cells {26414904; 30194715}. In this clinical context, integration of multiple diagnostic criteria is advisable.

The diagnostic accuracy of FNAB for FL is high when FL is diagnosed as a “B-cell NHL unspecified” and is relatively high for a specific diagnosis of FL. The latter may be achieved when ROSE is available and adequate material is obtained for cytomorphology and for flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, FISH, and/or molecular testing {29989244; 21411774; 25611108; 16961657; 11353059; 19247181; 22517675; 26482649}. Flow cytometry can provide clonality analysis directly by light chain assessment or can detect an aberrant immunophenotype indirectly suggesting clonality.

Using a cell block for immunocytochemistry, several markers expressed in FL can be assessed, such as CD10, BCL2, and BCL6, together with CD20. Other markers, including MUM1/IRF4, cyclin D1, SOX11, and T-bet, can be used to exclude other entities {26414904}.

A small percentage of high-grade FLs do not express CD10 and do not show t(14;18)(q32;q21) {19465899}. In these cases, the differential diagnosis with LBCL is not possible, and excision biopsy is required. Therefore, FNAB diagnosis of FL should be rendered according to the cytopathological and immunophenotypic evidence in combination with specific clinical settings.

The standard immunoprofile of FL includes CD10+, CD19+, CD20+, CD22+, CD79a+, BCL6+, BCL2+, CD23−/+, CD3−, CD5−, CD43−, and cyclin D1− {11353059; 20132132; 19247181; 16961657; 15669024; 10884795; 19306424; 30194715}. CD10 positivity should be evaluated with CD19 coexpression by flow cytometry. CD10/CD19 coexpression in > 50% of the gated cells is almost always indicative of an NHL such as FL, LBCL (including DLBCL-NOS), or Burkitt lymphoma {20132132}, and CD10/BCL2 coexpression is more specific for FL {15669024}. By flow cytometry, the neoplastic cells in FL are often dimmer for CD19 than the non-neoplastic B cells {15624204}.

Light chain restriction is detectable by flow cytometry in a high percentage of FL cases, being more common in “low-grade” than in “high-grade” FL {11353059; 15669024; 19247181; 10884795; 29131043}. Light chain restriction assessed as monoclonal on immunocytochemistry by semiquantitative visual estimate can also be detected on cytospins or direct smears in a high percentage of cases, with a polyclonal population appearing as an admixture of CD3, kappa, and lambda expression in equal proportions. Various cut-offs for light chain restriction have been proposed in the literature. Cut-offs employed using flow cytometry are more precise based on the quantitative assessment of numerous cells. Cut-offs for light chain restriction assessed by immunocytochemistry should be more conservative. Cell block preparations can be visually estimated for the presence of clonal restriction by immunocytochemistry, chromogenic ISH (CISH), or FISH {34221096; 8178762; 2293872; 2111624} [[Koss LG, Melamed MR, editors. Koss’ diagnostic cytology and its histopathologic bases. 5th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Lippincott Williams & Wilkins; 2006.]].

Monoclonal immunoglobulin heavy and light chain gene rearrangements can be detected by PCR on smears, cell blocks, and cell suspensions {17397497; 19306424; 26482649; 28008668}. IGH::BCL2 fusion, resulting from t(14;18)(q32;q21), is present in 80–90% of FL cases and can be detected by PCR or FISH {14681948; 20340097}. 3q27/BCL6 rearrangement or amplification {30156771} can be detected by FISH. MYC rearrangement is negative in most FL; when it is present, the possibility of transformation into DLBCL or HGBCL must be considered {21560251}.
