8.6.4.14 Thymic carcinomas

Definition

Thymic carcinomas are rare malignant epithelial neoplasms of the thymus encountered in the anterior mediastinum of adults, with multiple subtypes, including squamous carcinoma, basaloid carcinoma, lymphoepithelial-like carcinoma, clear cell carcinoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, carcinoma with adenoid cystic carcinoma–like features, undifferentiated carcinoma, and NUT carcinoma.

Clinical features and imaging

The most frequent symptoms of thymic carcinoma are related to mediastinal compression, such as chest discomfort, cough, and shortness of breath {33847631; 23866799; 33415074}. Other symptoms may include swelling of the face and neck, syncope, conjunctival suffusions, and headache due to superior vena cava syndrome. One third of patients are asymptomatic, and < 5% present with myasthenia gravis. On imaging, thymic carcinomas are most frequently seen as irregular anterior mediastinal masses. Carcinomas are more likely than thymomas to show greater internal heterogeneity, which is related to necrosis {33389148}, along with lymphadenopathy, pleural effusions, pericardial effusions, and metastases.

Histopathology

Squamous cell carcinomas show infiltrative nests and cords of large polygonal cells with associated desmoplastic stroma. Traversing blood vessels may be seen. The neoplastic cells have moderately abundant and usually eosinophilic cytoplasm with large hyperchromatic nuclei. Basaloid carcinomas show an admixture of cystic papillary and nested morphologies, with the nested components containing compact groups of monotonous small to intermediate cells with peripheral palisading and background sclerotic stroma. Adenocarcinomas of variable nuclear grades may be papillary, mucinous, or not otherwise specified with acinar to solid architecture. NUT carcinomas comprise sheets of monomorphic round to oval cells with scant cytoplasm, high N:C ratios, and often focal squamoid features. Other less common specialized types of thymic carcinomas, including lymphoepithelial carcinomas, clear cell carcinomas, sarcomatoid carcinomas, undifferentiated carcinomas, and salivary gland–type carcinomas, are histopathologically similar to tumours of the same types in the lungs and salivary glands.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The diagnosis of thymic carcinoma requires at least three necessary parameters: cytopathological nuclear features of malignancy, absence of thymic microanatomy, and correlative clinical data and imaging studies. Caution should be exercised when considering a diagnosis of a primary thymic carcinoma in a patient with a known history of a potentially similar carcinoma at another body site, because FNAB features of thymic carcinomas are essentially identical to those of epithelial malignancies encountered in other organs, such as the lungs.

Squamous cell carcinomas are the most common thymic carcinomas and may show pearl formation with concentrically wrapped malignant cells, as well as spindle, tadpole, and tapered cells, with dense cytoplasm and well-demarcated cell borders, and in keratinizing tumours cytoplasmic eosinophilia (Pap stain) or light-blue cytoplasm (Giemsa stain). Coarse chromatin is a feature of most squamous carcinomas, with nucleoli seen in non-keratinizing tumours. Basaloid thymic carcinomas show tightly cohesive tissue fragments, sometimes with minute trailing fragments, and with discrete tissue fragment borders and peripheral palisading in some cases, along with single cells. The cells of basaloid carcinomas are small to intermediate, with high N:C ratios, coarse chromatin, and small nucleoli. Adenocarcinomas have varied cytomorphologies, including acinar, papillary, and solid architecture, with usually large cells with variable, often moderately abundant and sometimes vacuolated eccentric cytoplasm and a background of mucin or intracellular mucin, and variable nuclear atypia and pleomorphism, ranging from bland to pleomorphic with large nucleoli.

The other types of primary thymic carcinoma, such as lymphoepithelial-like carcinoma, clear cell carcinoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, and salivary gland–type carcinomas, are rare. Organ-specific markers and cell lineage markers may be helpful and should be tailored to the FNAB cytomorphology, keeping the patient’s history and imaging in mind.

Distinguishing thymic carcinomas from thymomas can be challenging. Definitive diagnoses of carcinomas should be reserved for cases in which there is no question as to malignancy. In some instances, a diagnosis of “thymic epithelial neoplasm with atypia” may be prudent if a carcinoma appears possible but the available material does not allow for definitive classification by cytopathology and ancillary testing {33389148}.

Ancillary testing

Immunocytochemical studies may prove valuable in differentiating between carcinomas of thymic and non-thymic origin. When a non-small cell carcinoma of possible thymic origin is seen on FNAB, a limited panel of tests to define cell type and to separate the disease from a carcinoma of lung origin can include pankeratin such as cytokeratin AE1/AE3, CD117, TTF1, and p40. Other markers to aid in refining this differential diagnosis might include CK7, CK5, p63, napsin, PAX8, and CD5 {23958552; 17592270; 26643918; 33389148}. Recurrent loss of chromosome 16p, various other copy-number alterations, and microsatellite instability are found in thymic squamous carcinomas {29438696; 33915954}. MAML2 gene fusions and NUTM1 gene fusions, respectively, are demonstrated in thymic mucoepidermoid carcinomas and NUT carcinomas {24134933; 30362654}.

Immunocytochemical markers such as CK5, p40, and p63 can confirm a squamous phenotype but cannot determine the organ of origin, and not all thymic squamous carcinomas are positive for all markers; for example, approximately only 80% are positive for p63. Some authors have reported that CD5, CD117, FOXN1, and/ or CD205 may be helpful in confirming primary squamous carcinomas of the thymus, as these markers are less commonly expressed in non-thymic squamous carcinomas {23958552; 17592270; 26643918; 33389148}.

Most basaloid thymic carcinomas express p63, p40, and CD117. Other small blue cell malignancies, such as small cell carcinomas and NUT carcinomas, may enter the differential diagnosis for basaloid carcinomas. Small cell carcinomas originating in the lungs and other body sites have higher N:C ratios than basaloid carcinomas and show different cellular features, with more pleomorphic nuclei featuring finely granular chromatin and nuclear moulding. Small cell carcinomas are non-reactive with p63 and p40 and instead mark positively with TTF1 and markers of neuroendocrine differentiation, such as INSM1.

Primary thymic adenocarcinomas are most often negative for organ-specific immunocytochemistry such as TTF1. When positive, CD5 and CD117 have been used to support a diagnosis of primary squamous thymic carcinoma, but negative results with these markers do not exclude the possibility of a thymic primary {23958552; 26643918; 33389148}, and CD117 is positive in adenoid cystic carcinomas from other body sites such as the tracheobronchial tree {26261681}.
