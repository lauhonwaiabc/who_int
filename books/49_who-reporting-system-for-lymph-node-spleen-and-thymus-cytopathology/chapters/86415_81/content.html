8.6.4.15 Thymic neoplasms: Introduction

Primary tumours of the thymus are rare, accounting for about 0.2–1.5% of all neoplasms [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Thoracic tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 5). https://publications.iarc.who.int/595.]]. They account for 20% of mediastinal tumours, and about half of the tumours are located in the anterior mediastinum {16236967}. They tend to appear around the fourth to fifth decade of life, without sex predilection {1550889}. Approximately 25–30% of patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally by chest CT {26818050}. When symptomatic, 40% of patients report local symptoms associated with the presence of the mass (chest pain, cough, and dyspnoea), 30% report systemic symptoms (weight loss, fever, and night sweats), and 30–50% have paraneoplastic symptom syndromes {1550889; 21524480}. #40948Box 7.02 shows the primary thymic entities according to the 2021 WHO classification.

Thymic tumours include neoplasms that arise from cellular components of the thymus, including thymic epithelial tumours (thymomas, thymic carcinomas, neuroendocrine tumours), germ cell tumours, lymphoid and haematopoietic tumours, and mesenchymal tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Thoracic tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 5). https://publications.iarc.who.int/595.]]. Thymoma and lymphoma are the most common primary tumours of the thymus. Non-neoplastic proliferations consist primarily of thymic cysts and thymic hyperplasia.

The differential diagnosis of primary tumours of thymus is complex. In individual cases, it is often difficult, if not impossible, to determine from which mediastinal component the neoplasm originated, in particular for neuroendocrine neoplasms and lymphoma. Another differential diagnosis is germ cell tumour, because treatment depends on tumour type, and this possibility should be always considered when the diagnosis of thymic carcinoma is not fully satisfied due to the cytoarchitectural and immunophenotypic characteristics.

The histopathological characterization and staging of the lesion (i.e. the degree of local invasion, the presence of lymphadenopathy, and the presence of distant dissemination) are important because they allow clinicians to distinguish patients who will benefit from surgical management from those who require other types of treatment {9436541}. The US National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines classify thymic tumours into three clinical categories: localized and resectable, locally advanced and unresectable, and metastatic. Surgery is sufficient to treat the first category. For the other two categories, a combination of surgical, radiotherapy, and/or chemotherapy protocols is appropriate.

The diagnosis and classification of thymic neoplasms can be challenging on FNAB specimens because of the morphological variations and overlap between the subtypes and with other neoplasms. In addition to cytomorphological evaluation, ancillary tests on FNAB specimens can be used for accurate pathological diagnoses and to determine appropriate treatment strategies {37510144}. CT and endoscopic ultrasound–guided FNAB (EUS-FNAB) are generally the sampling techniques used.
