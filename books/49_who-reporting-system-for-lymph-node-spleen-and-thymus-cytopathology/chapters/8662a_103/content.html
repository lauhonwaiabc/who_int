8.6.6.2A Classic Hodgkin lymphoma

Definition

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL) is a neoplasm derived from germinal-centre B cells, characterized by a low fraction of large neoplastic Hodgkin and Reed–Sternberg cells, which have a defective B-cell expression programme, embedded in a reactive microenvironment rich in immune cells including lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, neutrophils, and histiocytes.

Clinical features and imaging

CHL has nodular sclerosis CHL, mixed cellularity CHL, lymphocyte-rich CHL, and lymphocyte-depleted CHL as subtypes. CHL accounts for 90–95% of all Hodgkin lymphomas, with nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma making up the rest. CHL has a bimodal age distribution, with one incidence peak at 15–35 years and another around 55 years. It presents with painless lymphadenopathy, most commonly in the cervical and supraclavicular lymph nodes and the mediastinum. CHL may present with B symptoms, which are fever, weight loss, and night sweats. There is strong association with EBV infection and increased risk after infectious mononucleosis. Cervical lymph nodes are most frequently affected (found in 75% of all cases), while involvement of non-axial lymph nodes and primary extranodal sites is rare. About 60% of cases present with early-stage disease (stage I or II). Spleen is involved in 60% of cases and bone marrow involvement is seen in 5% of cases {32703953; 22867814; 17465262; 32384177; 16896892; 21374695}.

Histopathology

The CHL subtypes share a similar immunophenotype but differ in their clinical features, EBV association, and probably prognosis – although modern treatment protocols are blurring the prognostic differences. Diagnosis is based on identifying the histopathological pattern and the presence of Hodgkin cells (HCs) and Reed–Sternberg cells (RSCs), which make up 0.1–10% of the whole cell population. The reactive inflammatory cell background varies according to subtype. The RSCs are large (40–70 μm), with basophilic to pale (amphophilic) abundant cytoplasm, and they have a bilobed nucleus with prominent single or multiple red nucleoli, sometimes with a clear halo around them (“owl-eye nucleoli”). The two lobes of the nuclei are usually mirror-image. HCs have single nuclei with similar features. Both HCs and RSCs, collectively referred to as Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg (HRS) cells, are derived from germinal-centre B cells and are unable to produce immunoglobulin, but they escape apoptosis and release different mediators, which induce recruitment of mixed inflammatory cells and degrees of surrounding fibrosis. Other cells can also be seen, such as mummified cells and lacunar cells, and are subtype-dependent. In extranodal sites or CHL relapses, classic histopathological features may be absent and only atypical CD30+ cells may be identified.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

CHL subtypes vary in the number of diagnostic HCs and RSCs found in a background of small lymphocytes and a lesser number of eosinophils and plasma cells and variable number of histiocytes. RSCs are large (40–70 μm), with bilobed or two distinct but similar nuclei and prominent single or multiple large nucleoli in each nucleus. HCs are smaller than RSCs and have single, round to more pleomorphic nuclei with one or more prominent nucleoli. Both RSCs and HCs have blue-green cytoplasm and bright-red nucleoli (Pap stain), or the cytoplasm is basophilic and the nucleoli are light blue (Giemsa stain). In some cases, only HCs are seen in the same mixed inflammatory cell background. Granulomas can be seen in association with CHL, especially when mediastinal. HCs within collagen fibres may be present, representing tissue sclerosis.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The diagnosis is based on identifying the HCs and/or RSCs in the characteristic background. However, there are mimics of HCs and RSCs, including immunoblasts in reactive lymphadenopathies, particularly infectious mononucleosis (#29920Table 7.11). The other major differential diagnoses include T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma and anaplastic large cell lymphoma, and at times immunocytochemistry is required (#29616Table 7.12). Cells of other non-haematopoietic malignancies, such as carcinomas, melanomas, and even sarcomas, may mimic HRS cells {19373880; 21472876; 32418255; 27735994; 30089946; 30802809; 14600125; 16680774; 26107683}.

Ancillary testing

For newly presenting cases of CHL, confirmation of the characteristic immunophenotype of HRS cells is required to establish the diagnosis where available. The HRS cells are usually positive for CD30, MUM1, PAX5, and CD15, while CD45 and T-cell markers are negative. CD20 may be weakly positive in HCs and RSCs, and CD79a is usually negative, while PAX5 characteristically shows weaker staining than the surrounding small B cells. EMA may occasionally be expressed in HCs and RSCs, but other epithelial cell markers are negative (#29616Table 7.12). Flow cytometry can rule out monoclonal B cells of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and demonstrates increased CD4 cells and a resultant increase in the CD4:CD8 ratio {30350336}. No consistent cytogenetic or molecular changes are present in HRS cells {10556196; 34293084; 30350336}.

Major differential diagnosis of CHL

Cytopathological clues to help make the distinction

Immunophenotypic features by immunocytochemistry

Reactive lymphadenopathies with increased numbers of atypical large immunoblasts, such as infectious mononucleosis and other viral infections

In reactive lymphadenopathies, a spectrum of background lymphocytes and large lymphoid cells is usually noted, and tingible-body macrophages are often prominent. Immunocytochemistry will resolve the differential diagnosis (see ancillary testing details).

In reactive lymphadenopathies, the large cells express CD45 and many mature B-cell markers (e.g. CD20, CD19, and PAX5). In CHL, the HCs and RSCs lack CD45 (LCA) and CD20 in most cases. PAX5 will show characteristic weaker staining than surrounding mature B-cells in CHL, a very helpful feature of HRS cells. CD15 and CD30 are usually positive in the majority of the HCs and RSCs in CHL, and negative in immunoblasts and other mimics.

T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma (THRLBCL)

THRLBCL can mimic CHL when occurring in lymph nodes, especially when large atypical pleomorphic cells are present, and it may be very difficult to make the distinction from NLPHL. Immunocytochemistry is usually needed.

Almost all LBCLs express several B-cell markers (e.g. CD20, CD79a), as well as CD45, which are usually negative in the RSCs and HCs of CHL.

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL)

ALCL may contain a background of polymorphous lymphoid cells in addition to the large neoplastic cells, but the presence of numerous large atypical cells favours ALCL.

The large atypical cells of ALK-positive ALCL express ALK and/or multiple T-cell markers, such as CD2, CD3, and CD5. B-cell markers, such as CD20, CD79a, and PAX5, are usually negative in ALCL. CD30 expression is positive in both and is therefore not helpful in this distinction.
