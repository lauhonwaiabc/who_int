8.6.4.6 Plasma cell neoplasms

Definition

Plasma cell neoplasms arise from the monoclonal expansion of post–germinal-centre, clonal terminally differentiated B cells that usually secrete a monoclonal immunoglobulin called M proteins or paraproteins.

Clinical features and imaging

Likely clinical scenarios where cytopathological evaluation will be employed for patient management are extramedullary involvement by plasma cell myeloma and plasmacytoma.

Plasmacytoma is a solitary neoplasm of clonal plasma cells without evidence of plasma cell (multiple) myeloma or end organ damage due to plasma cell neoplasia.

Plasma cell myeloma is a bone marrow–based, multifocal neoplastic proliferation of plasma cells, defined by the combination of plasma cell myeloma (usually associated with a serum and/or urine monoclonal immunoglobulin) and evidence of organ damage related to the disease, including so-called CRAB features: hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, anaemia, and bone lytic lesions {12528874; 22549792; 25439696}. Patients with myeloma may also have recurrent infections, bleeding diathesis, and signs and symptoms attributable to amyloid deposition {15180858}.

The forms of plasma cell neoplasia are defined by clinical, laboratory, pathological, and imaging findings. Symptoms and signs in myeloma can be attributed to tumour growth, with mass-like effects that replace normal structures, as well as the secretion of monoclonal immunoglobulin (i.e. an M protein), which is found in the serum and/or urine of most patients with a plasma cell neoplasm {15794860; 35103762}. Levels of M protein tend to be highest in patients with myeloma. Importantly, an M protein is not specific for plasma cell neoplasms and can also be seen in other conditions, such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome and various types of small B-cell lymphoma.

About 10–15% of patients with myeloma have smouldering myeloma. These patients have appreciable levels of M protein and monoclonal plasma cells in bone marrow but lack CRAB features {33067414}. Patients with plasmacytoma can have bone or extramedullary disease {33145987; 33270992}, and their symptoms relate to the location and size of the tumour mass, while CRAB features are absent {21437886}. Patients with bone plasmacytoma are further divided into those with only bone disease versus those with minimal bone marrow involvement (by < 10% monoclonal plasma cells). The latter group has a higher risk of progression to myeloma {25439696; 31412808}.

Histopathology

Neoplastic plasma cells diffusely replace the tissue architecture and can exhibit a spectrum of differentiation from small and closely resembling normal plasma cells (which is the so-called Marschalko type); to cells with lymphoplasmacytic features; to moderately or poorly differentiated plasma cells with nucleomegaly, a high N:C ratio, and prominent nucleoli; to pleomorphic, plasmablastic, or anaplastic cells with marked nuclear atypia and a high mitotic rate {18580096; 25842207; 29388036}. Plasma cells may have pale intranuclear pseudoinclusions (Dutcher bodies), cytoplasmic eosinophilic globules (Russell bodies), multiple grape-like cytoplasmic globules (Mott cells), immunoglobulin crystals, or abundant IgA with glycogen and appear red (flame cells). Dutcher bodies are more frequent in malignant cells than reactive plasma cells, while the converse is true for Russell bodies. Amyloid deposition can be associated with plasma cell neoplasms {14560572; 33687521}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate to high cellularity Plasma cells tend to lie singly or in aggregates in some cases. Plasma cells are usually of intermediate size with eccentric cytoplasm, perinuclear clearing (hof), and blue cytoplasm (Giemsa stain). Binucleation and multinucleation are clues to the presence of a plasma cell neoplasm. Mature plasma cell neoplasms have condensed, clock-face chromatin with indiscernible nucleoli. Immature plasma cell neoplasms have finer chromatin, with prominent nucleoli, a high N:C ratio, and mitotic figures, and substantial pleomorphism can be present.

Reference(s): {10086234; 20518407; 11190797}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of plasmacytosis in lymph node FNAB specimens includes benign and malignant entities {11190797; 18580096}. Benign lesions with conspicuous plasma cells include syphilis, drug reactions, some viral infections, and connective tissue diseases – particularly rheumatoid arthritis. Benign lesions tend to present with a mix of plasma cells, eosinophils, histiocytes, and a range of maturing lymphocytes without atypia. Non-Hodgkin lymphomas that have a mix of cells and may include conspicuous plasma cells include lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma and marginal zone lymphoma, while plasmablastic lymphoma shows a monomorphous atypical plasmacytoid population and may be a difficult differential diagnosis requiring flow cytometry. Reactive plasma cells can be prominent in classic Hodgkin lymphoma and some non-haematological malignancies, including neuroendocrine neoplasms and metastatic melanoma and adenocarcinoma. Multicentric Castleman disease, either KSHV/HHV8-associated or idiopathic, is another cause of plasmacytosis in lymph nodes, and the plasma cells can be polytypic or monotypic. Detection of a serum or urine paraprotein supports the diagnosis of a plasma cell neoplasm and helps to exclude reactive plasma cell lesions.

Ancillary testing

Immunocytochemistry and flow cytometry are helpful for distinguishing benign lesions from plasma cell neoplasms. Immunophenotypic and molecular features are similar in all plasma cell neoplasms, but they may differ in frequency in different clinical manifestations of disease.

Neoplastic plasma cells express monotypic cytoplasmic immunoglobulin heavy chains (IgG>IgA>IgD>IgE>IgM) and light chains in > 98% of cases {15080299; 24462038; 24876564}. They are positive for CD38 and CD138 in almost all cases; often express MUM1/IRF4 and Blimp1; and are usually negative for T-cell antigens, CD19, CD20, CD27, CD45, and CD81. Neoplastic plasma cells often have an aberrant immunophenotype; for example, plasma cells may be positive for CD20 (10–20%), CD28, CD52 (~10%), CD56, CD117 (20–30%), and CD200, and in rare cases for CD3, CD10, and myeloid or monocytic markers.

Neoplastic plasma cells carry clonal immunoglobulin heavy and light chain rearrangements and lack clonal T-cell receptor rearrangements. Plasma cells carry mutated immunoglobulin heavy and light chain variable genes, indicating passage through the germinal centre {1421403}. Conventional karyotyping shows an abnormal karyotype in one third of cases. However, FISH analysis on sorted or enriched plasma cells shows that abnormalities are present in almost all cases {10673522}, and plasma cell neoplasms can be divided into hyperdiploid (~40–50%) and non-hyperdiploid (~40%) groups {15920009; 24434212}. Hyperdiploid cases have gains in three or more chromosomes, usually involving 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 19, and/or 21. Ploidy is an early event in pathogenesis that is present in monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance and plasma cell neoplasms. A number of chromosomal translocations that involve IGH occur in myeloma, including translocations involving CCND1 on chromosome 11q13 (~15%), FGFR3::NSD2 at chromosome 4p16.3 (~15%), and MAF / chromosome 16q23 (~5%), as well as rare translocations (1–2%) involving CCND3 / chromosome 6p21, MAFB / chromosome 20q11, MAFA / chromosome 8q24, and CCND2 / chromosome 12p13. Other abnormalities in plasma cell neoplasms include monosomy 13 or partial deletion of 13q14. Mutations involving KRAS, NRAS, or BRAF occur in about 40% of cases. MYC abnormalities occur late in pathogenesis and correlate with transformation {29967379}. TP53 mutations, gains of chromosome 1q, and loss of chromosome 1p, as well as mutations of CDKN2C, RB1, TENT5C (FAM46C), and DIS3, occur as a part of disease progression {24434212; 34885058}.
