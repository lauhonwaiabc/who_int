8.6.8.4 Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma

Definition

Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (FDCS) is a malignant neoplasm showing cytomorphological and phenotypical characteristics of follicular dendritic cells, which are stroma-derived cells normally found in germinal centres.

Clinical features and imaging

FDCS was previously divided into two types, the conventional FDCS and the EBV-positive inflammatory FDCS. EBV-positive inflammatory FDCS was previously known as “inflammatory pseudotumour-like follicular/fibroblastic dendritic cell sarcoma” and is now regarded as a separate entity defined as an indolent malignant neoplasm characterized by neoplastic follicular dendritic cell proliferation, a prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, and a consistent association with EBV, with its own distinctive clinicopathological features {11395549} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. It presents almost exclusively in the liver and/or spleen, and rarely in the colon, tonsils, bronchus, pancreas, and mesentery.

FDCS is a clinically indolent, slow-growing neoplasm. However, distant metastases and local recurrence can occur, with aggressive clinical course. There is no sex predilection and a wide age range, with adult predominance and a median age of 65 years {12121233}. It affects lymph nodes, with a propensity for the cervical region, and a variety of extranodal sites, such as the head and neck, soft tissue, gastrointestinal tract, omentum, liver, breast, and chest. In rare cases, paraneoplastic pemphigus can be the first clinical manifestation {26722384}. FDCS may arise in the setting of hyaline-vascular Castleman disease, either concurrent with or preceded by Castleman disease. CT and MRI may show necrosis and calcifications in addition to hypervascularity and progressive enhancement {33405946}.

Histopathology

Conventional FDCS is composed of spindle to ovoid to polygonal cells, which often exhibit prominent nucleoli, with a variable number of small lymphocytes in the background. At low power, the tumour cells often show sheet-like, fascicular, storiform, or whorled patterns. The mitotic rate varies from 0 up to 4 mitoses/mm2.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Two cell type pattern of spindle to ovoid tumour cells in tissue fragments in a background of mature small lymphocytes, often with scattered single intact tumour cells Lymphocytes may be scant or numerous and are occasionally admixed with plasma cells. Oval nuclei with finely granular chromatin with prominent nucleoli and often perinucleolar clearing (“vesicular”, Pap stain) Occasional intranuclear pseudoinclusions and nuclear grooves may be identified. Moderate amount of cytoplasm Binucleated cells reminiscent of germinal-centre follicular dendritic cells as “kissing pairs” and multinucleated large cells resembling Hodgkin and Reed–Sternberg cells are often identified. Small foci of necrosis and frequent mitoses can be seen.

Reference(s): {15584048; 29275417}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

In the recent fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, FDCS has been moved from the category of “histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms” to a newly proposed group, “stroma-derived neoplasms of lymphoid tissues”, and classified as a “mesenchymal dendritic cell neoplasm”, because FDCS originates from non-haematolymphoid mesenchymal cells in the germinal centre. Based on this fact, EBV-positive inflammatory FDCS has been separated from FDCS and established as a distinct entity within the category of “mesenchymal dendritic cell neoplasms”. It has similar features in FNAB cytopathology: cellular smears show a dual population of spindle cells as single cells and in small syncytial tissue fragments showing whorls and fascicles in a background of lymphocytes and plasma cells. Nuclear atypia is variable, as are often prominent nucleoli, perinucleolar clearing (“vesicular” nuclei in Pap stain), and multilobated nuclei; multinucleated cells can be seen {18064686}. The distinction from FDCS relies on ancillary testing, including EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) positivity, and clinical presentation.

A wide variety of neoplasms of spindle to ovoid cells forming small tissue fragments with or without small lymphocytes in the background are included in the differential diagnosis, and FNAB can suggest the diagnosis, but the specific diagnosis relies on ancillary testing of cell block material with or without excision biopsy. Metastatic undifferentiated carcinomas should be firstly excluded, especially at nodal sites. Other neoplastic processes yielding cellular smears of spindle to ovoid to polygonal cells with scattered small lymphocytes include ectopic meningioma, intrathyroidal thymic carcinoma showing thymus-like differentiation, large cell lymphomas with pseudoaggregation such as ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma and anaplastic large cell lymphoma, classic Hodgkin lymphoma, amelanotic melanoma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, ectopic thymoma, extragonadal seminoma, and soft tissue sarcomas {17718144; 34351024}.

Ancillary testing

Immunocytochemistry on cell block sections is highly recommended. The tumour cells are positive for the three conventional follicular dendritic cell markers CD21, CD23, and CD35. Although not highly specific, expression of CXCL13, podoplanin (D2-40), and clusterin is also usually observed. Vimentin is positive, and S100, CD68, and EMA are variably expressed. FDCS is negative for cytokeratin, CD1a, CD3, CD20, CD34, CD45, CD79a, CD163, lysozyme, MPO, and HMB45. A rare case with aberrant coexpression of CD4 and CD30, misdiagnosed as anaplastic large cell lymphoma, has been reported {32547956}. Flow cytometry can play an important role in excluding other haematolymphoid lesions.
