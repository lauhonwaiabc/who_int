9.2.3 Category: Atypical

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Atypical” demonstrates features seen predominantly in benign lesions and minimal features that may raise the possibility of a malignant lesion, but with features insufficient, either in number or in quality, to diagnose a benign or malignant process or lesion.

In most instances, smears categorized as “Atypical” will show features of a benign lymphoid process, but there are some features that raise the possibility of a neoplastic lesion. This category predominantly encompasses indeterminate lesions with essentially benign features but in which there are some concerning features.

The causes of an “Atypical” categorization will most frequently be:

Benign reactive hyperplasia but scant or no germinal-centre material or scant tingible-body macrophages; Rare bland epithelioid cells representing benign inclusions (epithelial or naevoid); Poor-quality smear preparation resulting in artefactual monotony or nuclear atypia; Scant material only, without typical benign features.

An “Atypical” categorization should be reserved for only a small proportion of cases and should always be correlated with clinical and imaging features. Allowing a degree of uncertainty when the categorization of “Atypical” is used provides the advantage of increasing the negative predictive value (NPV) of a true “Benign” case, as well as the positive predictive value (PPV) of a true “Malignant” or “Suspicious for malignancy” diagnosis. The availability of ROSE has the potential to decrease the number of “Atypical” cases when they are due to technical factors or limited material, as well as the number of cases categorized as “Atypical” where more cytopathology material on cellular slides can reduce uncertainty and provide material for ancillary testing. If ancillary testing is available, a preliminary report of “Atypical” may be modified to “Benign” or “Malignant” in a subsequent final report, such as after confirmation of lymphoma with flow cytometry.

The “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low to intermediate ROM. A review {39097796} of 10 recent retrospective and heterogeneous studies, based on the similar Sydney System, found that the “Atypical” category accounts for a range of 0.33–14.9% of cases, with a ROM ranging from 29% to 77% {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657}. There is a ROM of 65% in a recent meta-analysis {38096796}.

Management of a patient with an “Atypical” lymph node, spleen, or thymus FNAB is initially correlation with the clinical and imaging findings, as well as discussion in a multidisciplinary setting if at all possible. If the clinical and imaging impressions are benign (i.e. there is a low clinical suspicion), then repeat clinical and imaging examination, with a repeat FNAB if necessary, is appropriate. If the clinical and/or imaging features are of concern, then a repeat FNAB, with or without a CNB, may be performed. Triage of the case with ROSE will allow optimal use of material for ancillary studies. Further management will depend on the likely clinical diagnosis, the site of the lesion, and the status of the patient.
