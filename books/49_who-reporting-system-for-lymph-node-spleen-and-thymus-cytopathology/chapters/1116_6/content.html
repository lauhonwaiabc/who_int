1.1.1.6 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

A primary goal of FNAB in the proposed WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology (WHO System) is to assess and categorize each case using a five-tier system of well-defined categories: “Insufficient/Inadequate”, “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, and “Malignant”. The ultimate goal is to make a specific diagnosis of the lesion, but this will often require full knowledge of the clinical and imaging findings and in some cases ancillary testing. A ROM for each category is derived from the literature and will help guide clinicians in their management of individual patients in terms of further diagnostic steps to establish a definitive diagnosis.

The recently published proposal for an international system for reporting lymph node cytopathology, the Sydney System, aimed to provide a standardized diagnostic system and diagnostic categories based on an integration of clinical, imaging, and cytopathological findings together with ancillary studies linked to management recommendations {32454496}. The editors and authors of the WHO System acknowledge the authors of the Sydney System and the role they have played in the development of reporting FNAB of lymph nodes. The Sydney System has five categories: inadequate/insufficient, benign, atypical cells of uncertain significance / atypical lymphoid cells of uncertain significance, suspicious, and malignant. These are very similar to the definitions and applicability ultimately chosen for the WHO System.

Recent studies based on categorization of lymph node FNAB using the Sydney System have shown a broad range of performance indicators and ROM {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657}. A review of these 10 studies showed that the ROM varied greatly: for the “Insufficient/Inadequate” category it ranged from 17% to 67%, for the “Benign” category from 2% to 16%, for the “Atypical” category from 29% to 77%, for the “Suspicious” category from 88% to 100%, and for the “Malignant” category from 98% to 100% {39097796}. A recent meta-analysis of nine of these studies showed a pooled ROM for “Insufficient/Inadequate” of 34%, “Benign” 5%, “Atypical” 65%, “Suspicious” 93%, and “Malignant” 100% {38096796}.

These studies represent retrospective cross-sectional studies that reflect the probability that the patient has malignancy at the time of the FNAB, perhaps better regarded as the “rate” of malignancy, rather than the probability or “risk” that the patient will develop malignancy over a certain period of time {38126672}. If the current approach is applied as in these studies, there is a wide range of ROM, which suggests that the performance data are questionable at best {39097796}.

The studies are starkly heterogeneous {39097796}. They have marked variation in case numbers and are based on different population cohorts, who may have different rates of malignancy and in whom the role and timing of the use of FNAB in the diagnostic workup of a wide range of lymph node lesions varies greatly. The studies reflect a broad range of approaches in the performance and reporting of lymph node FNAB, with potential variation arising from different interpretation of key diagnostic criteria and categories and confidence in the diagnosis. They vary markedly in the use of ultrasound guidance and ROSE; the site of the lymph node; the percentage of cases biopsied by cytopathologists skilled in FNAB; the percentage of infectious, reactive, metastatic, and lymphoma cases; the use of ancillary tests; and the statistical analysis.

Most of these studies calculated their ROM for each diagnostic category dependent on histopathological correlation and malignant outcomes. However, the ROM can also be calculated by dividing the number of confirmed malignant cases by the number of cases with a histopathological or clinical follow-up within specific diagnostic categories. If only “Benign” cases with surgical pathology follow-up are included, this introduces verification bias, because most benign diagnoses are managed by clinical follow-up and only cases with high clinical or imaging suspicion go on to surgical biopsy. This risk of verification bias varies through these retrospective studies. For example, when Caputo et al. recalculated their ROM including “Benign” cases without surgical excision and only subject to clinical follow-up as true negatives, they showed that the “Benign” category ROM dropped from 9.38% to 0.84%, and the “Atypical” category ROM dropped from 28.6% to 8.7% {34817104}. Similarly, Vigliar et al. calculated the ROM for each category by dividing the number of cases with a confirmed malignant lesion by the total number of cases with a histopathological or clinical follow-up within each diagnostic category and showed the “Benign” category ROM was 1.92% {34441249}. These studies suggest that the ROM of a “Benign” FNAB of lymph node is at the lower end of the range in #39638Table 1.04.

The use of ancillary tests, in particular flow cytometry, also varied greatly between the studies, as they are sourced from a range of high income and low- and middle-income countries. The techniques used varied greatly because they were impacted by the availability of flow cytometry and the use of direct smears, cytospins, liquid-based cytopathology, and cell block sections, as well as the varying fixatives used, such as methanol, alcohol, and formalin. The most fundamental information as to whether the FNAB report categorization was made on the cytopathology findings alone, or whether it was made based on the cytopathology along with all the ancillary tests available, is not reported {39097796}. Further, the ROM for metastatic disease and haematolymphoid tumours in lymph node FNAB cytopathology is not reported separately in all the studies {39097796}, and in future studies this essential information on these subcategories will need to be assessed with its resultant impact on ROM.

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that FNAB of lymph nodes has excellent specificity and sensitivity and high diagnostic accuracy, that using a standardized and reproducible reporting system can assist reporting, and that a hierarchical system of categories with increasing ROM can be achieved and will be useful to clinicians in managing their patients {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657}. The review of these 10 studies shows that the sensitivity of the Sydney System lies between 46% and 100%, the specificity lies between 92% and 100%, the positive predictive value is between 53% and 100%, the negative predictive value is between 69% and 99%, and the accuracy is between 67% and 99% {39097796}. These performance indicators vary with the definition of a “positive” result, as demonstrated in the meta-analysis {38096796}.

In addition, the studies that included ROSE show its value in reducing inadequate cases, in reducing the number of indeterminate reports, and – most importantly – in triaging material for appropriate ancillary testing to further reduce atypical and suspicious cases and make specific diagnoses of metastatic and lymphoma cases.

The range of ROM as reported in the review of the 10 published studies is tabulated for the five categories in #39638Table 1.04 {39097796}. The pooled ROM is presented from the recent meta-analysis of nine of these studies {38096796}, but as detailed above covers over the marked variation in these studies. The concept of “risk of malignancy” as applied to cross-directional studies and currently used in non-gynaecological reporting systems requires discussion, and these studies, the meta-analysis, and the recent review provide what is best regarded as an estimate of rates of malignancy in the WHO lymph node reporting system’s categories {38126672; 39097796}. Longitudinal prospective studies with large case numbers are required, recording a broad range of parameters of the cohort studied, to elucidate the actual ROM for each of the categories of this first edition of the WHO System with regard to lymph nodes {39097796}.

The use of diagnostic categories for the reporting of FNAB of the spleen and the thymus and for the calculation of the ROM for each diagnostic category in the WHO System is currently a complex objective, and further study is needed to assess the role of this reporting system and to establish the ROM for each category. In the international literature, relatively few studies have evaluated the applicability of FNAB and its accuracy in the thymus and the spleen {31742902; 20859120; 18514125; 34546006; 33403241; 28746730; 21953852; 18181188; 17573764; 17115435; 12704692; 9143823; 8191816}. Moreover, some of these publications include other mediastinal or subdiaphragmatic pathological processes.

Nonetheless, in the available series, FNAB specimens were generally classified as inadequate, benign, indeterminate, suspicious, and malignant, although the cytomorphological criteria used to distinguish the various categories, particularly the indeterminate categories, were not reported. Furthermore, in some series the authors excluded inadequate and indeterminate cases, and the results of the histopathological follow-up were reported as a summary, lacking detail or tabulation {31742902; 20859120; 18514125}.

Therefore, it is currently not possible to define the expected ROM for FNAB cytopathology of the thymus and spleen, and it is hoped that further research and specific studies critiquing this WHO System will establish ROM and other performance indicator measures for FNAB not only of spleen and thymus but also of lymph nodes.

The management for each category in a particular FNAB report will vary with the clinical situation, the availability of other pathology test results such as a full blood count and serology, imaging findings, and the cytopathological features. For example, an “Insufficient/Inadequate” categorization due to an absence of diagnostic material may lead to a repeat FNAB, while an “Atypical” categorization of cellular lymphoid smears favouring a follicular hyperplasia but lacking flow cytometry, resulting in an inability to exclude a follicular lymphoma, may lead to a recommendation for core or excision biopsy with flow cytometry. Alternatively in the situation of an “Atypical” categorization, the clinician could adopt a “watch and wait” approach, for instance with a young patient with the typical clinical presentation of infectious mononucleosis. The suggested diagnostic management for each category is presented in #39638Table 1.04 . It is imperative that the clinical and imaging findings, as well as any other available pathology testing, are reviewed in association with the cytopathology report. Further information on the recommended diagnostic management is presented in the chapters on each category, with a summary to assist clinicians presented in the final chapter.
