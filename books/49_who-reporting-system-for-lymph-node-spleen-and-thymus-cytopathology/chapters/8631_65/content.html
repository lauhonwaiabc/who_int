8.6.3.1 Lymphoblastic lymphoma

Definition

B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma (B-LBL) and T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma (T-LBL) are neoplasms of precursor lymphoid cells committed to either B-cell or T-cell lineage that are indistinguishable on cell type (small or medium blasts with scant cytoplasm, finely dispersed chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli) and as B-lymphoblastic and T-lymphoblastic lymphomas involve nodal or extranodal sites.

Clinical features and imaging

Because the FNAB features of B-lymphoblastic and T-lymphoblastic lymphomas are indistinguishable, the two lesions are presented together in the WHO System. Ancillary tests are required to make the specific diagnoses.

B-LBL can present with skin, soft tissue, and localized lymphadenopathy in addition to bone marrow involvement. Mediastinal involvement is uncommon. Patients with B-lymphoblastic leukaemia, who are most often children less than 6 years of age, frequently present with lymphadenopathy [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

T-LBL can affect patients of any age but is more frequent among adolescent males. Advanced-stage disease is common at presentation, and mediastinal involvement is typical, with some patients presenting with respiratory distress due to rapid tumour growth or pleural effusions. Any nodal site may be involved, as well as many extranodal sites, particularly tonsils, spleen, liver, skin, CNS, and testis.

Histopathology

The morphological features of B-lymphoblastic and T-lymphoblastic lymphomas are indistinguishable. There is a monotonous proliferation of medium-sized blasts with round to oval, indented or slightly convoluted nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli and scant cytoplasm. Partial involvement of a lymph node with paracortical proliferation may occur with sparing of the germinal centres. Mitotic figures are often easily found, along with many tingible-body macrophages producing a so-called starry-sky pattern. Sometimes, a multinodular pattern mimicking follicular lymphoma can be observed.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

High cellularity Intermediate cells with high N:C ratio Nuclei display fine chromatin, often with small nucleoli. Nuclear clefting and indentations are frequent. Scant basophilic cytoplasm and sometimes cytoplasmic clearing at the site of nuclear indentation (Giemsa stain) Coarse azurophilic granules can be seen in a subset of B-LBL. “Hand mirror” morphology can be seen. Frequent mitotic figures are often seen.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

B-LBL is much less common than T-LBL and represents approximately 10% of all lymphoblastic lymphoma and may present as a tumour mass. There are several recurrent genetic abnormalities observed in B-LBL that define WHO-recognized distinct entities. Each of them has their own characteristic clinical, phenotypical, biological, and prognostic features. The genetic abnormalities include balanced translocations and an aberrant number of chromosomes (hyperdiploidy or hypodiploidy).

T-LBLs also display recurrent genetic lesions, although these currently do not define disease entities recognized by WHO. However, the rare early T-precursor lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma has a characteristic phenotype and prognostic features, justifying its recognition as a separate entity in the WHO classification [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Typically, the neoplastic cells display immunophenotypic features of a very early T cell combined with the expression of one or more myeloid and/or stem cell antigens suggestive of a cell of origin retaining the potential for myeloid differentiation but without irreversible commitment to the T-cell lineage. Early T-cell precursor neoplasms may present as leukaemias [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] or lymphomas {33921074}. Importantly, none of these WHO-recognized entities display unique blast cytomorphological features. The distinction between lymphoblastic lymphoma and lymphoblastic leukaemia is somewhat arbitrary, as there is no agreed threshold of blasts in the bone marrow defining leukaemia. Many treatment protocols use the cut-off of 25% blasts in the bone marrow for leukaemia.

A mediastinal mass is a typical presentation in young adults with T-LBL because of the thymic origin of the T cells, in contrast to B-LBL. Non-haematopoietic neoplasms such as Ewing sarcoma and thymoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Ewing sarcoma can present in the chest wall and can be distinguished from T-LBL by the absence of haematopoietic markers such as CD45. Thymoma must be considered in the differential diagnosis of T-LBL because both frequently occur in the anterior mediastinum and share some phenotypical findings such as expression of CD99. Also, immature thymocytes seen in thymoma may resemble the immature cells in T-LBL. The cytomorphological distinction between T-LBL and thymoma is difficult if the epithelial cells of thymomas are not recognized. The presence of a homogeneous population of medium-sized cells is more typical of T-LBL, because thymomas often display variable-sized cells including epithelial cells {11842377; 8964175; 26243719}. In contrast to T-LBL, thymoma is more common in adults.

The differential diagnoses of both T-LBL and B-LBL include Burkitt lymphoma and the blastoid subtype of mantle cell lymphoma. Lymphoblastic lymphomas can be distinguished from Burkitt lymphoma cytomorphologically because they do not contain the characteristic cytoplasmic lipid vacuoles that are usually seen in Burkitt lymphoma, and they often demonstrate a finer chromatin pattern with indentations of the nuclei along with cytoplasmic clearing at the site of indentation and less basophilic cytoplasm. As opposed to lymphoblastic lymphoma, the blastoid subtype of mantle cell lymphoma often displays irregular nuclei with 1–4 small nucleoli {9664186}. Lymphoblastic lymphoma must also be distinguished from myeloid sarcoma, because they share similar morphological features and some immunophenotypic markers. In particular, early T-cell precursor lymphoblastic lymphoma shows immunophenotypic features overlapping with those of myeloid sarcoma.

If a significant number of eosinophils is found among the lymphoblasts, consideration should be given to the possibility of a lymphoid neoplasm with eosinophilia and PDGFR gene or FGFR1 rearrangement, or with PCM1::JAK2 [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Ancillary testing

Immunophenotyping with flow cytometry or immunocytochemistry is critical for the diagnosis of lymphoblastic lymphoma. Flow cytometry is the preferred method, because several antigens can be analysed simultaneously. In addition, flow cytometry can readily distinguish intracellular from cell-surface antigens, such as cytoplasmic CD3 versus surface CD3, which may facilitate interpretation. B-LBL is characterized by a B-cell phenotype with the expression of CD19, CD79a, CD22, CD24, and CD10. Additional markers such as PAX5 and TdT are expressed in most cases, while the expression of CD20 and the stem cell antigen CD34 is variable, and CD45 may be absent. The typically elevated expression of CD58 seen in B-LBL may be useful to rule out Burkitt lymphoma, if light chain restriction or TdT analyses are ambiguous {26679753; 21451365; 33340851}.

T-lymphoblastic lymphoma can be classified into four subtypes, reflecting the stages of thymocyte differentiation: (1) pro-T, (2) pre-T, (3) cortical T, and (4) medullary T. Currently, the pro-T and pre-T subtypes constitute the early T-cell precursor lymphoblastic lymphoma category, and the cortical T and medullary T subtypes the T-LBL entity in the WHO classification. T-LBL usually expresses cytoplasmic CD3, CD7, CD2, and TdT, often along with strong coexpression of both CD4 and CD8. Tandem expression of CD4 and CD8 defines the cortical subtype, while expression of either CD4 or CD8 characterizes the medullary subtype. Expression of CD1a, sCD3, CD4, CD5, and CD8 is variable. CD34, CD10, and CD99 expression may be seen, which may help to identify the T lymphoblasts. Assessment of TRBC1 may be used to demonstrate monoclonality {34302330}. T-cell receptor γ or β chain gene rearrangements are seen in a significant number of cases {26243719; 17369128; 21273093; 15160915}. Early T-cell precursor lymphoblastic lymphoma shows the expression of cytoplasmic CD3 and CD7 but lacks CD8 and CD1a by definition. CD2 and CD4 may be expressed, surface CD3 is usually absent, and CD5 is dim on < 75% of blasts or absent. Lack of CD2 seems to be associated with a particularly dismal outcome {33921074}. By definition, early T-LBL also expresses one or several stem cell or myeloid markers, including CD34, CD117, CD13, CD33, CD11b, and CD65 {26747249}.

Recurrent chromosomal abnormalities of B-LBL and T-LBL are prognostically important and can be demonstrated by FISH on cytospins or cell blocks.
