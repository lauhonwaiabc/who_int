3.1.1.5 Molecular testing

Introduction

With the rapidly evolving technology supporting the diagnosis of specific genetic aberrations that are related to diseases, the use of molecular testing of cytopathological material is increasingly assisting cytopathologists and clinicians to make accurate diagnoses and identify prognostic and specific treatment targets. There is a range of cytopathological specimens that can be used, including fresh FNAB material, direct smears, liquid-based cytopathology (LBC) slides, cytospins, supernatants, cell blocks, and body-cavity fluids. Problems that can occur include scant genetic target material being available, which also entails a risk of false negative results {27333361; 31021538}. Careful and resource-intensive validation of the molecular methods is necessary for each type of cytopathological specimen, including sensitivity, limit of detection, specificity, accuracy, and precision. Massive parallel sequencing (NGS), including targeted panel sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, and transcriptome sequencing, requires careful consideration of optimal quality metrics such as amplicon coverage, sequencing depth, and suitable allele frequency cut-offs, which are critical to the reliability of results {27333361; 29154853}.

An advantage of cytopathological samples that are not exposed to formalin is the high quality of DNA, devoid of strand fragmentation and formalin-fixation–induced deamination mutations {32980613}. Therefore, despite their possible scant nature, cytopathological specimens are often equivalent, or even superior, to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) small biopsies and cell blocks, for example for assessment of tumour mutation burden {32980613; 29981866}. Numerous studies have shown the usefulness of various types of cytopathological material, including archived slides, for molecular testing {31021538; 20959611; 26921541; 32980613}. These are important aspects to consider, because FNAB or other cytopathological specimens can easily be made available for molecular testing after the initial evaluation of patients with malignancies. This has set a new stage for diagnostic cytopathology, in which close cooperation between cytopathologists, surgical pathologists, and molecular pathologists is crucial, and institutional strategies for the optimal use of the various cytopathological specimens need to be discussed and developed {27588332}.

Technical requirements

Any PCR or NGS-based technique that is used for assessment of standard tissue biopsies can be used for cytopathological samples. However, there are specific technical considerations that must be addressed when using these samples, particularly the performance of limited material on different testing platforms and using different assays. PCR-based clonality tests, for example, are common assays used for the assessment of suspected lymphoid neoplasms. Restricted IGH and T-cell receptor clonal rearrangements may support the diagnosis of a neoplastic process in challenging cases, particularly cytopathological samples where diagnosis relies on cytomorphological features alone, largely without accompanying tissue architectural features. In case of a small neoplastic clone within a prominent polyclonal background, there may not be enough DNA template of the monoclonal gene rearrangement because of competing targets from the DNA of the polyclonal cells during the PCR reaction, thus producing a false negative result. Another problem is the occurrence of false positive results entailing a risk of overdiagnosis of lymphoma. This situation is common in all paucicellular specimens where few targets are present, which may lead to random selective amplification of one rearrangement, thus giving a false impression of clonality, so-called “pseudoclonality” {19669208}. This problem can be circumvented by running the assays in duplicate, which is highly recommended in routine practice. In cases of pseudoclonality, the suspected monoclonal peak will not be reproducible. The overall performance of clonality assays can also vary widely depending on the sample preparation. When samples are formalin-fixed, fragmentation of the DNA template may render the samples unsuitable for testing with leader and FR1 framework region primers, which yield longer sequences, while FR2 and FR3 primers provide better performance. In cases with high fragmentation, tests may yield failures or pseudoclonality due to the combination of very scant material and low effective template {30590126}.

In the context of highly multiplexed and NGS assays, preanalytical processing of cytopathological samples to ensure template conservation becomes a critical point. While FFPE cell block preparations are generally preferred across laboratories for their versatility and resemblance to surgical FFPE samples, these sample preparations suffer from considerable nucleic acid losses during de-paraffinization, extraction, and purification processes. Depending on the specific protocol used for fixation, high levels of formalin-induced nucleic acid damage and fragmentation may interfere with the performance of the NGS assay, particularly when amplicon-based, which relies on the intactness of the template for primer annealing {28341590}. In this regard, hybrid capture assays may be more suitable for assessment. DNA isolated from cytospins or LBC is generally of higher quality, but the yields may be lower, particularly if the DNA is isolated from one or few smears. Key preanalytical requirements for reliable biomarker analysis in cytopathological samples are that the specimen is of sufficient quality and quantity to meet adequate amplification of the genomic region of interest, acceptable sequencing depth and quality metrics, and sufficient tumour fraction to allow detection of variants in accordance with technology used for NGS. Both tumour proportion and nucleic acid input requirements vary across different NGS assays and the specific platform. Therefore, familiarity with these requirements is important to ensure reliable results. A tumour fraction of 10–20% is often needed for reliable NGS analysis in routine diagnostic testing. Management of types of cytopathological specimens

A variety of cytopathology specimen types can be used to perform molecular testing. Most specimens received in the cytopathology laboratory are received fresh or in collection/transportation media such as saline, RPMI medium, or LBC medium, according to standard operating procedures supervised by the cytopathology laboratory. The specimen can be processed as direct smears, cytospin preparations, LBC, and/or cell block preparations (which are typically FFPE). A portion of the cytopathology specimen can be triaged to flow cytometry and for molecular diagnostics, prior to fixation in alcohol or formalin. DNA and RNA can be extracted from all routine cytopathological specimens, and molecular testing can be performed, provided the specific specimen preparation has been adequately validated for the specific assay.

Cytomorphological assessment of the specimen for adequacy and appropriate triage and selection of specimens for molecular testing are crucial for reliable results. Cell counting can be performed to aid in assessing the adequacy of the specimen for ancillary studies, including flow cytometric analysis and molecular diagnostics. Haemolytic agents such as Carnoy’s solution can be used to process haemorrhagic specimens for cytomorphological evaluation.

Fixation

Fixation of cytopathological specimens is one of the most important preanalytical factors impacting the success and validity of molecular assays. In general, non–cross-linking fixatives using alcohol (as wet or spray fixatives) or air-drying provides higher-quality nucleic acids than specimens fixed in formalin. Formalin fixation is known to result in higher C>T or G>A sequence artefacts {10550302}. Additionally, FFPE tissue is known to yield poor-quality RNA due to chemical modifications and partial degradation, making RNA extraction and reverse transcription more challenging {23936242}. Air-dried and/or alcohol-fixed specimen preparations and the use of fresh cytopathological specimens for nucleic acid extraction can provide superior-quality DNA and RNA for a wide range of molecular diagnostics assays.

Controls

The use of control material is an integral component of any type of molecular testing, including NGS. Control samples are a requirement for clinical testing and are used to readily detect sources of error and monitor the overall performance of the assays. For this purpose, positive, negative, and no template controls are generally used. Each run should also include a sensitivity control to ensure the detection of the target of interest at the lowest limit of detection established for the assay {28341590}. Clinical laboratories typically derive a set of controls needed to match the specifics of the methods used with respect to the intended clinical use. In this context, the incorporation of controls that reflect the quality of cytopathological samples, depending on differences in preparation, is also an important consideration. For example, for FFPE cell blocks, controls should be prepared, using similar procedures, from a well-characterized sample with variants near the limit of detection of the assay.

Procedures

FNAB specimens for molecular testing can be obtained by free hand (often by a cytopathologist) or by various image-guided FNAB techniques, generally performed by an interventional radiologist. Image-guided procedures include any ultrasound-guided FNAB, such as endobronchial ultrasound–guided FNAB (EBUS-FNAB) and endoscopic ultrasound–guided FNAB (EUS-FNAB). Image-guided FNAB can also be done under CT, MRI, or PET-CT monitoring. All techniques have their advantages and disadvantages {31367090}, but the procedure type does not correlate with the NGS success rate {27333361}.

Numerous extraction protocols and commercially available kits are available for DNA extraction. All methods incorporate the three basic steps of lysis, precipitation, and purification of the nucleic acid. In the lysis step, the nucleus and the cells are broken to release the DNA. This process involves the mechanical and enzymatic disruption of the cells and tissue to free the DNA. This is followed by a precipitation step, in which the freed DNA is separated from the cellular debris and finally purified and resuspended in water for storage and handling. For samples that are prepared in cell blocks, a de-paraffinization process precedes these common steps and generally involves the use of xylene or mineral oil and a series of graded alcohol washes. Each step in the entire process may affect the quality and quantity of the DNA that is isolated, as well as the overall performance of the assay. It is, therefore, important to optimize all steps to minimize losses and maintain the highest quality possible.

For NGS, the DNA is fragmented following isolation and then converted into a library by the ligation of sequencing adapters to each fragment, with patient-specific barcodes. Sequencing adapters are designed to interact with the NGS platform, for example the flow cell or bead surface, to enable amplification and sequencing. Several considerations are important in library preparation, depending on the sequencing platform and the planned analysis. Among these, the quality and quantity of the DNA input are the most critical and the most highly affected in cytopathological samples given their often scant nature. The ultimate goal of library preparation is to obtain the highest complexity level possible, to reduce the amount of sequencing bias. Library complexity refers to the number of unique DNA fragments in the library that would most comprehensively reflect the starting material. Reductions in complexity are common in very small and highly degraded samples.

Diagnostic application

Lymphoid tissues consist of many B-lymphoid and T-lymphoid cells that in normal situations vary enormously in composition of the antigen receptors they carry. This contrasts with lymphomas, in which the antigen receptor of the tumour cells is the same on all malignant cells, reflecting the clonal nature of the neoplastic process. Because antigen receptors are coded for by rearranged DNA segments, the difference between an enlarged lymph node due to a polyclonal, reactive process and a lymph node involved by a clonal neoplastic proliferation can be determined by clonality testing at the DNA level. This can be achieved by PCR using the variation in length of the DNA segments that code for components of the antigen receptors, like the heavy and light chain of B lymphocytes and the gamma and beta receptors of the T cell {22918122; 19669208}. Cytopathological specimens, even with low numbers of cells, can be used for clonality testing {27169754}, but there are risks in interpretation more than in histopathological material. Enlarged lymph nodes due to a reactive process often contain enlarged germinal centres in which B cells proliferate to combat antigens. In a germinal centre often only a few clones expand, sometimes only one. Detecting a restricted clone, therefore, does not directly indicate that there is a neoplastic process. And conversely, because of hypermutations that may affect primer-binding sites, not all neoplastic clonal sequences can be detected by a clonality test, although this pitfall can be avoided in almost all cases by using multiple targets. The recognition of lymphoma cells may be challenging by morphological examination of the cytopathological specimen, particularly for those forming indolent B-cell lymphomas. Therefore, high levels of expertise from the cytopathologist, haematopathologist, and molecular pathologist/biologist, with close communication between them and clinical correlation, are critically important to correctly determining whether a sample is neoplastic or reactive {32270299}.

Clonality testing on cytopathological specimens has an especially helpful role in situations of a potential recurrence of a lymphoma, particularly when the lesions are difficult to access for histopathological evaluation. In these cases, when the sequence of the antigen receptor of the primary lymphoma is known, detection of the same sequence in a clonal result proves that the same disease is present. It is critical to report the result of a clonality test carefully. It is not sufficient just to state that a clone is present or absent. The presence of a polyclonal background, the type of clone and comparison to previously detected clones, and the amount of it are all information needed to be present in the report. Furthermore, it is always important to correlate the result to the cytopathological findings for proper interpretation.

Clonality testing may also be performed by NGS and is a highly valuable approach for high-sensitivity detection of a known clone in lymphomas, as well as in metastatic disease. Using NGS, it is possible to determine the presence of a known clone even when it is hidden deep in a polyclonal background. Due to the unique nature of the neoplastic clones, which are patient-specific, NGS may be used even on very scant samples to specifically search for a predetermined sequence without the major concerns of false positivity regarding clonality testing of lymphoid cells by PCR {30590126}. This approach may facilitate the assessment for involvement of a known malignancy at another site and establish the clonal relationship, including for non-lymphoid tumours {30963573}.

NGS testing is generally used to determine genomic alterations for diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic purposes {30590126; 31197258; 30679319}. Determining a broad mutation profile may also facilitate future monitoring of a patient for assessment of circulating tumour cells or measurable residual disease. For NGS-based testing, FNAB of lymph nodes is particularly attractive because of the immediate accessibility and high DNA/RNA quality compared to formalin-fixed specimens. Caveats include the sometimes-sparse material, which may lead to inconclusive or false negative results. The power of NGS resides in the ability to simultaneously map many types of genetic aberrations in lymphomas or other malignancies. For instance, in addition to investigating a selected number of genes for single nucleotide variants and insertions/deletions, it is also possible to analyse copy-number changes, i.e. deletions and amplifications, as well as structural variants such as translocations, and gene fusions that involve RNA sequencing. Gene panels that target the most common and recurrent genetic aberrations in both haematological and solid tumours are often preferred in diagnostic laboratories to provide higher-quality sequencing of genomic regions that are clinically relevant and could be difficult to target using broader sequencing approaches. Currently, whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing, which are more expensive and have longer turnaround times, are primarily performed on selected cases (depending on the clinical setting) or for research purposes. Provided there is adequate validation, all tests can be performed on cytopathological material.
