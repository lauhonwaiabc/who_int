1.1.1.5 Diagnostic categories and report structure

Diagnostic categories

Each of the categories of the WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology (WHO System) is associated with a ROM that is directly linked to a recommendation for further diagnostic testing to achieve a specific diagnosis or to refine a differential diagnosis and suggest follow-up management. Specific processes or tumours that have a similar ROM when diagnosed on cytopathology are placed in the same category; for example, inflammatory and benign neoplastic entities are placed in the “Benign” category. The WHO System uses categories to assist communication of the ROM to the clinician, but a specific diagnosis or a differential diagnosis that can be further refined by ancillary testing is the aim of all cytopathology reporting and paramount in the report.

There are five WHO System categories, which can be used for direct FNAB smears, liquid-based cytopathology, touch preparations of lymph node CNB, and effusion cytopathology:

Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic Benign Atypical Suspicious for malignancy Malignant

Currently there are limited publications defining the ROM for these categories in lymph node cytopathology, and there is a need for further research using the WHO System to more clearly define the ROM associated with each category.

Individual cytopathologists, as well as departments with more than one cytopathologist, should choose to use either “Insufficient” or “Inadequate” as the term for cases where there is no material for assessment or when there are technical problems that prevent assessment of material on the slides. If there is adequate benign lymphoid material on the smears, then this is described and reported as “Benign”. Correlation with imaging should be carried out wherever possible, and if the material on the slides shows benign material that does not correlate with or explain the imaging or clinical findings, then it is mandatory to include a caveat in the report conclusion, that the material “may not represent the lesion seen on imaging and further diagnostic workup is recommended”. This discrepancy may relate to sampling error, particularly with small lesions seen on imaging, or it may reflect the limitations of imaging in accurately distinguishing some lesions seen in lymph nodes, thymus, or spleen.

It is recognized that in some cytopathology practices in some parts of the world the term “Non-diagnostic” has been applied to encompass not only cases where there is insufficient or technically obscured material, but also cases where there is benign cytopathological material that does not correlate with or explain the clinical and/or imaging findings. This is the first WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology, and it accepts that in line with current usage, these cases may in some regions of the world be categorized as “Non-diagnostic” and placed in the “Insufficient/Inadequate” category. However, the term “Non-diagnostic” is not encouraged in lymph node FNAB. Research into the impact of this approach on the “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” and “Benign” ROM is required.

If any atypical features are seen in a case showing otherwise insufficient material, the case is regarded as “Atypical” and not “Insufficient”.

The “Benign” category includes normal lymphoid components and inflammatory processes including suppurative, granulomatous, and specific infectious or inflammatory diseases. This wide variety of lymphoid proliferations in response to viral and other infections, autoimmune processes, and other lesions can produce a wide range of lymphoid patterns, which can be predominantly follicular hyperplasia, immunoblastic reactions, prominent histiocytosis, prominent plasmacytosis, and prominent necrosis, and these patterns can also be mixed. In some cases, the distinction from a lymphoma can be difficult, for example follicular hyperplasia versus follicular lymphoma, or a viral immunoblastic reaction versus a lymphoma. If the features are benign but a precise diagnosis cannot be made, the features should be described and a differential diagnosis provided. If the features raise a differential diagnosis that includes lymphoma, then the case is placed in the “Atypical” category. As with all categories, correlation with clinical features and wherever possible imaging is mandatory.

This is an area of debate and current research {39097796}. The ability of individual cytopathologists and their teams to confidently diagnose on cytopathology alone various inflammatory reactive patterns as benign or to place them in the “Atypical” category as possible “lymphoid cells of uncertain significance” varies with local practice and expertise, as well as the expectations and support of their clinical colleagues. FNAB of lymph nodes in any setting is a powerful tool that is minimally invasive and inexpensive and can diagnose a broad range of the many pathological processes that involve lymph nodes, such as infections and metastatic malignancies, leaving a proportion of cases that show lymphoid material. The number of such lymphoid cases varies depending on the nature of the practice; for example, compare a more general community practice versus an oncology-focused hospital service, and it also depends on the country in which the cytopathologist practices. The crucial areas of debate are how many of these lymphoid cases can be specifically diagnosed by FNAB alone as “Benign”, how many need to be categorized as “Atypical” or “Suspicious for malignancy”, how many can be diagnosed on cytopathology supported by CNB and/or ancillary testing, and how many require excision biopsy for surgical haematopathology.

It should be noted that a specialist cytopathologist who also reports haematopathology and works within a team of haematopathologist diagnosticians and oncologists will more easily develop the expertise to interpret and exploit the diagnostic attributes of FNAB cytopathology of lymph nodes consisting of lymphoid material, more so than a cytopathologist who sees fewer cases and works without this supporting environment. This will enable the cytopathologist-haematopathologist to interpret clinical and laboratory findings, interpret the FNAB material and ancillary tests, and issue a final integrated report.

The “Atypical” category includes cases where there is scant or poorly prepared cellular material, as well as, importantly, cases with considerable lymphoid material showing mainly benign cytopathological features but with some features that may raise the possibility of malignancy, for example highly cellular lymphoid smears showing a mix of lymphoid cell types with a relative lack of tingible-body macrophages and small lymphocytes, which raises the differential diagnosis of follicular hyperplasia and follicular lymphoma.

The “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low to intermediate ROM and allows for a high negative predictive value (NPV) for a “Benign” diagnosis, while the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to have a higher ROM and provide for a high positive predictive value (PPV) approaching 100% for a “Malignant” categorization. The categories stratify the ROM and allow for the development of diagnostic management recommendations.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cases where there is scant, poorly smeared, or poorly prepared and stained material suggestive of malignancy, as well as cases where there is considerable material, some of which appears malignant, but not all features of a particular malignancy are present or there may be discrepant features. This category includes cases suspicious for lymphoma and cases suspicious for metastatic carcinoma. The report should specifically describe the features that are suspicious and provide a differential diagnosis of the lesions suspected.

The “Malignant” category is reserved for those cases in which there are unequivocal features of malignancy without any discrepant findings. FNAB is a sampling technique that yields a small biopsy, and it is not always possible to make a precise diagnosis of a specific carcinoma, lymphoma, or other malignancy, even with ancillary testing. The precise diagnosis of carcinoma and lymphoma subtypes usually requires ancillary testing.

The presentation of tumours in this WHO System generally follows the order found in the fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, but tumours that have not been described in the cytopathology literature are not included.

Structured reporting

Each cytopathology report on a lymph node FNAB should have a structured format, with a heading of a specific diagnostic category using the WHO System’s standardized descriptive terminology and a diagnosis. Structured reporting improves the quality, clarity, and reproducibility of reports within individual pathology departments and between countries, and will improve patient management and facilitate research and quality assurance measures. Standard guidelines for cell block preparation, immunocytochemistry, ISH, and flow cytometry, as well as molecular pathology for tests of diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive markers, will improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce cost, and improve patient care. Structured reports are based on key diagnostic cytopathological findings for each lesion, which act as a checklist for the reporting cytopathologist and will also improve reporting.

The essential or core requirements in a cytopathology report are presented here. This is not intended to make any particular format mandatory, but some components are required: the precise location of the lesion; the diagnostic category using the defined terminology of the reporting system; a microscopic description of the key diagnostic features; and a diagnosis, which may be specific or descriptive with a differential diagnosis. An addendum outlining any ancillary tests and their results is also required wherever possible. The diagnostic summary must be clearly delineated and stand out in the report so that it can be easily seen by the patient care team.

It is not recommended to use a number in defining a category at any stage. A category number can be included in the body of the report for audit and research purposes but not in the diagnostic summary. Reducing a cytopathology report to a number reduces the information that can be given to the clinician and management team and increases the risk of error.

The diagnostic category states whether there is inadequate material and an “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” categorization is applicable, or whether there is adequate material and a “Benign”, “Atypical / Atypical lymphoid cells of uncertain significance”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant” category can be applied. But it is recognized that some cytopathologists prefer to include a statement in the report that “there is adequate material for assessment”. Other cytopathologists require a statement to be included regarding the degree of cellularity of the material from the target lesion.

The microscopic description should provide a clear, concise cytopathological description, which includes the degree of cellularity and focuses on the presence or absence of key diagnostic cytopathological features. Some cytopathologists prefer to provide a full description of these features; others may prefer, in cases where there is a specific diagnosis, to provide minimal or no description.

The aim of cytopathology is to provide as specific a diagnosis as possible in the vast majority of cases, and this remains the priority. Categories allow the grouping of lesions, including those that are “Atypical / Atypical lymphoid cells of uncertain significance” or “Suspicious for malignancy”, and the establishment of a ROM and subsequent diagnostic management recommendations as part of an algorithm to aid communication with the clinician and further diagnostic management of the patient.

The ancillary tests should always be part of the final cytopathology report, and if immunocytochemistry, flow cytometry, ISH, or molecular pathology reports are issued by a different laboratory, then it is recommended that they should be added as a supplementary report to the original cytopathology report, in the interest of having a final all-inclusive integrated report for optimal patient care. The WHO reporting systems for cytopathology are based on the cytomorphology findings for the initial categorization and reporting of the lesion, which can be applied in any well-resourced or low- and middle-income country (LMIC) setting, but they also incorporate the application of appropriate ancillary testing as current best practice. Subsequent ancillary testing may well change the final diagnostic category and provide a specific diagnosis of a particular case.

Ancillary testing
Depending on local practice, the cytopathology report (including its conclusion) can be issued as a final or provisional report, with supplementary information or addenda on ancillary studies to follow and to be added to a final integrated report, or in some cases the cytopathology report can be withheld until ancillary reports are available. Includes all cell block H&E and special stains, immunocytochemistry, ISH, and molecular techniques performed on cell block or direct smear slide scrapings and further stains; the report should state which type of specimen was used for each ancillary test to facilitate continuous quality assurance If flow cytometry results are available for cases of lymphoproliferative diseases, and are issued by the same laboratory, then these results should be entered here; or if flow cytometry results are issued by a different laboratory, their results should be added as a supplementary report when available under ancillary testing in the original report. If a CNB or other surgical biopsy was performed at the same time as the FNAB, the supplementary report should recommend correlation with the surgical report, and its accession number should be stated.
