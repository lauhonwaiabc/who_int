1.1.1.3 Integration of clinical, imaging, and key FNAB cytopathological features with ancillary testing in a diagnostic approach

Providing a correct pathological diagnosis on lymphoid cells procured by FNAB is complex and requires integration of clinical and imaging data with cytopathological features and the results of ancillary tests. Factors to consider before the FNAB is performed include the clinical history and the location and size of the lymphadenopathy, as well as the patient’s age, sex, and country of residence. Imaging features, including those seen on ultrasound, CT, MRI, and PET, are useful indicators of potential disease processes. The results of haematological peripheral blood screens and basic serological tests should also be correlated. This preanalytical integration of all the available clinical and imaging features is mandatory before the FNAB.

Ultrasound, in addition to guiding the FNAB, also produces information similar to that provided by the macroscopic examination of surgical excision biopsy. Grey-scale and Doppler ultrasound are valuable in evaluating enlarged cervical lymph nodes where the differential diagnosis encompasses benign reactive lymphadenitis (including infectious mononucleosis and tuberculous lymphadenitis, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, and Rosai–Dorfman disease) as well as malignant entities (most commonly metastatic carcinomas and lymphoma). Ultrasound features that help to identify abnormal nodes include a round shape, absent hilus, intranodal necrosis, reticulation, calcification, matting, and peripheral vascularity.

The FNAB will then provide a cytopathological diagnosis to be integrated with the clinical and imaging features; for example, cervical lymph nodes are more likely to be involved by reactive processes in children and by lymphoma or metastatic malignancies in elderly patients, or by infectious diseases such as infectious mononucleosis or tuberculosis or as a reaction to therapeutic drugs such as cephalosporins, phenytoin, or sulfonamides across a broader range of ages.

If the clinical and imaging findings suggest a possible lymphoma at the time of FNAB, then the proceduralist will be guided to procure an adequately cellular specimen for cytopathology and for the appropriate ancillary testing, including flow cytometry, cytospins, immunocytochemistry on a cell block, and cytogenetics.

ROSE of the cytomorphology at the time of the FNAB will facilitate the decision to carry out additional FNAB passes to procure adequate material for a cytopathological diagnosis, and very importantly to triage the material for the cost-effective and patient-centred selection of appropriate ancillary tests. Ancillary tests usually require additional passes and the placement of the material in various containers and fluid media. These ancillary tests include microbiological culture, flow cytometry, cytospins, preparation of a cell block for tissue immunophenotyping, FISH, and molecular studies. The application of this approach optimizes the handling and triage of lymph node material of whatever type; for example, an extra slide made at ROSE for a Ziehl–Neelsen stain when a direct smear shows caseous necrosis or granulomatous features may confirm tuberculosis by demonstrating acid-fast bacilli. As further examples, the diagnosis of metastatic non-small cell carcinoma of lung can be made on direct smears confirmed by immunocytochemistry on a cell block with follow-up molecular pathology for genetic studies; malignant lymphoma can be sent for flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and FISH to make a specific lymphoma diagnosis; and a reactive inflammatory lymphoid process such as infectious mononucleosis can be confirmed by negative flow cytometry. However, judicious and selective use of ancillary tests is required to conserve tissue and resources, and in many parts of the world ancillary tests are restricted or not even available. The results of each ancillary test must be carefully assessed against the cytopathological findings, because each may provide misleading information; for example, flow cytometry may be falsely negative in Hodgkin lymphoma because the diagnostic Reed–Sternberg cells are too few to assess, and in some large cell lymphomas the cells have fragile cytoplasm and do not survive the flow cytometry process.
