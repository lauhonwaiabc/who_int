5.2.1.1 Acute inflammation

Definition

Acute inflammation of lymph nodes or suppurative lymphadenitis and acute inflammation of spleen yield purulent material consisting of neutrophils and variable numbers of lymphocytes and histiocytes with cellular debris in a fibrinous background.

Clinical features and imaging

Typically, tender lymph node enlargement has reddened overlying skin in a patient, with or without fever. Abscess formation may have a local mass effect. Imaging demonstrates a central necrotic area surrounded by a fibrotic wall. #29520Box 4.01 shows some infectious causes of acute lymphadenitis {19698282; 34304774} [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. (Pattern recognition series).]] {27555237}.

FNAB yields purulent viscous material with a large number of neutrophils in varying stages of degeneration, with a varying amount of admixed blood and particulate material. FNAB of a splenic abscess yields similar material. Significant or even complete drainage of an abscess may assist with early healing.

Histopathology

The dilated sinuses are filled with numerous neutrophils with a varying amount of thin proteinaceous fluid, and the paracortex contains plentiful neutrophils and foamy histiocytes. The capsule may be oedematous and inflamed.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Plentiful dispersed neutrophils and histiocytes admixed with nuclear debris and a variable number of lymphoid cells Fibrinous proteinaceous background, which can be haemorrhagic or have necrotic particulate debris Tissue fragments of granulation tissue composed of branching anastomosing capillaries with a corona of inflammatory cells may be present, particularly later in the inflammatory process. Histiocytes, including multinucleated histiocytes, become more prominent, along with a variable number of tingible-body macrophages containing nuclear debris. Specific infectious agents may be seen in routine and special stains, including filariasis, cocci, bacilli, rods, and fungal elements.

Reference(s): [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. (Pattern recognition series).]] {27555237}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses in acute lymphadenitis are neutrophil-rich haematological and non-haematological malignancies. A suppurative background can sometimes hide Reed–Sternberg and mononuclear Hodgkin cells. Anaplastic large cell lymphoma can occasionally present in smears with predominantly neutrophils and scattered large blasts. Similarly, metastatic squamous carcinoma showing cystic degeneration and keratinization can be accompanied by numerous neutrophils and histiocytes obscuring the malignant cells. Myeloid sarcoma smears can show abundant neutrophils and some eosinophils in various stages of maturation together with blasts {28701837; 34304774}.

Ancillary testing

Special stains, including the Gram stain, acid-fast stains such as the Ziehl–Neelsen stain for mycobacteria, and PASD and Grocott methenamine silver stain for fungi, can be used routinely. Immunocytochemistry can be used for viral infections where indicated. Correlation with microbiological cultures and PCR and serology is required.

·     Bacterial (e.g. Gram-positive cocci) and fungal infections

·     Early stages of mycobacterial infection (differential diagnosis: cold abscess)

·     Lymphogranuloma venereum

·     Cat-scratch disease

·     Tularaemia (caused by the bacterium Francisella tularensis)

·     Yersiniosis (mesenteric lymphadenitis)

·     Lymph nodes draining infection / abscess formed in adjacent site (e.g. appendicitis)

·     In many cases, the etiology remains unknown
