5.2.2.4 Prominent plasmacytosis

Definition

Prominent plasmacytosis is defined as an increased number of reactive plasma cells in a mixed lymphoid background.

Clinical features and imaging

Prominent plasmacytosis is often seen in reactive lymphadenopathy associated with immunologically mediated conditions {27721273}, where it is associated with generalized reactive lymphadenopathy on imaging. An increased number of intranodal plasma cells can be observed in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases, mainly rheumatoid arthritis {22706532} and systemic lupus erythematosus. Prominent plasmacytosis can also be due to medication-associated lymphadenopathies, such as those seen during methotrexate therapy. Some infections can cause prominent plasmacytosis, not only in the affected tissues but also in the associated enlarged lymph nodes, such as syphilis, HIV, EBV, and other viral infections. Reactive plasmacytosis is also a component of subtypes of Castleman disease {33148595}, Rosai–Dorfman disease, and IgG4-related lymphadenopathy {29738364; 23068302}.

Histopathology

The architecture of the lymph nodes is partially effaced by prominent plasma cells in the interfollicular and/or paracortical compartments, with a variable proportion (1–50%) of polyclonal plasma cells, often associated with less prominent follicular hyperplasia {27721273} and sinusoidal and capsular expansion by plasma cells. Binucleation, multinucleation, and Russell bodies may be observed.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Mixed lymphoid cells with plentiful benign plasma cells Plasma cells are small to intermediate in size, with eccentric cytoplasm that may contain a perinuclear clearing (hof). Nuclei are round, with coarse chromatin and small or inconspicuous nucleoli. Occasional binucleation of reactive plasma cells may occur. Occasional Russell bodies representing condensed immunoglobulin may be present in the eccentric cytoplasm of cells and in the background. There are no granulomas, Hodgkin cells, or other malignant cells. No necrosis is generally observed.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Reactive plasma cells may be a component of the inflammatory background in some T-cell lymphomas and classic Hodgkin lymphoma, with predominantly intermediate to large atypical large lymphoid cells and diagnostic Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells, respectively, as distinguishing features. B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma with plasmacytoid differentiation or a plasma cell myeloma must also be excluded. Clinical correlation, immunocytochemical stains, and molecular studies will help make the correct diagnosis.

Awareness of a history of rheumatoid arthritis or other autoimmune disease or of medication profile is essential.

Ancillary testing

The results of microbiological studies, cultures, and PCR; the results of serological studies for specific infections (most notably syphilis, HIV, or EBV); and the IgG4 level will provide essential information for interpretation. Castleman disease, particularly in HIV-positive patients, must also be excluded. EBV infection can be confirmed by ISH on cell blocks or direct smears.

The polyclonal reactive nature of the plasma cells can be confirmed by immunocytochemistry, flow cytometry, ISH, or molecular clonality analysis.
