8.6.4.18 Primary effusion lymphoma

Definition

Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) is a large B-cell lymphoma presenting as a pleural, pericardial, and/or peritoneal serous effusion in the absence of lymph node involvement or an extranodal mass lesion (except for the rare presence of a body-cavity–related tumour mass) and consisting of terminally differentiated B cells, consistently associated with Kaposi sarcoma–associated herpesvirus / human herpesvirus 8 (KSHV/HHV8) and usually coinfected with EBV. Extracavitary PEL (EC-PEL) is a related entity presenting with a tumour mass, often at extranodal sites.

Clinical features and imaging

PEL usually arises in pleural and pericardial effusions and ascites, and the affected sites are often isolated or unilateral. An extremely rare case of PEL developing in cerebrospinal fluid has been reported. Most patients with PEL are severely immunocompromised due to HIV infection or after organ transplantation with immunosuppressive agents, or rarely due to immunosenescence without HIV. Coexistence with other KSHV/HHV8-related diseases, such as Kaposi sarcoma, multicentric Castleman disease (MCD), and MCD-derived large B-cell lymphoma, can occur. EC-PEL occurs as solid tumours in sites such as lung, gastrointestinal tract, and CNS {7700311; 22682793; 23899073}, and PEL may form serosal masses, already apparent at the time of presentation or arising during the course of the disease. The clinical course is generally aggressive, with a poor prognosis. PEL does not display distinguishing features on imaging, apart from the effusion and possible serosal masses; however, PET-CT presentation with hypermetabolic body-cavity fluid in the absence of lymphadenopathy is regarded as a hallmark of PEL. The clinical features and imaging of EC-PEL depend on the involved site {23899073; 29988764; 28399009; 10839567; 35158997; 33906629; 22682793}.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of classic PEL is usually based on cytopathology, and the histopathological features of EC-PEL are similar to those seen in the cytopathological evaluation of effusion fluid. Malignant lymphoid cells vary in morphology from large immunoblasts to plasmablasts to more anaplastic cells. Tingible-body macrophages and mitotic figures are conspicuous and result in a starry-sky appearance. There may be involvement of lymph node sinuses, as well as lymphatic and vascular channels. The striking pleomorphism seen on cytopathology is not always seen histopathologically, and ancillary tests are very useful to confirm the diagnosis.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The integration of cytomorphology, clinical history of immunodeficiency, and KSHV/HHV8 positivity confirmed by ancillary tests usually ensures a correct diagnosis of PEL. Secondary body-cavity involvement of a known pre-existing lymphoma should be excluded. True body-cavity–based large cell lymphomas, which are KSHV/HHV8-unrelated and more frequent than PEL, have been named “fluid overload–associated large B-cell lymphomas” [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. These effusion-based lymphomas are of mature B-cell origin, are CD20-positive, and often demonstrate centroblastic cytomorphology similar to that of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS. Most fluid overload–associated large B-cell lymphomas develop in elderly patients with no history of immunosuppression except immunosenescence due to ageing. Some cases with hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection have been reported. The clinical course can be aggressive or indolent, and indolent cases may show regression without any treatment.

Rare KSHV/HHV8-related and KSHV/HHV8-unrelated solid lesions with similar cytopathological features must be differentiated from EC-PEL. These include KSHV/HHV8-positive multicentric Castleman Disease, KSHV/HHV8-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, KSHV/HHV8-positive germinotropic lymphoproliferative disorder, plasmablastic lymphoma, and ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma {35158997; 24868220; 33906629; 28399009}.

Ancillary testing

Identification of KSHV/HHV8 within the malignant lymphoid cells is key to the diagnosis of PEL. Neoplastic cells usually express a KSHV/HHV8 latency pattern, with LANA1 being the most significant latency protein. Approximately 80% of PELs demonstrate coinfection with EBV and express EBNA1 but usually not EBNA2 or LMP1. PEL is of B-cell lineage but tends to be of “null” phenotype, rarely expressing pan–B-cell antigens including CD19, CD20, CD22, PAX5, OCT2, BOB1, and CD79a. BCL6 and CD10 are usually negative. By flow cytometry, the cells show dim expression of CD45, and expression of CD38 and CD138, but are negative for surface and cytoplasmic light chains {29669178}. The tumour cells often lack T-cell and NK-cell antigens, although aberrant expression may be observed, especially in EC-PEL.

Some atypical EC-PELs can be positive for CD20, CD79a, and CD138, and aberrant expression of CD3 has been rarely observed, in which cases CD30 and EMA may also be positive, potentially leading to a misdiagnosis as anaplastic large cell lymphoma. Burkitt lymphoma may occur primarily in body fluid, the so-called “effusion Burkitt”. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma associated with chronic inflammation (pyothorax-associated lymphoma, KSHV/HHV8-unrelated) should also be excluded {35158997; 24868220; 33906629; 28399009}.

Mutations involving BCL6 are noted in a subset of PEL. Complex karyotypes including trisomy 12, trisomy 7, and abnormalities of 1q21-25, in addition to gains in chromosome 12 and the X chromosome, have been described {35158997; 30154110; 17554754; 23899073; 29988764; 7700311}.
