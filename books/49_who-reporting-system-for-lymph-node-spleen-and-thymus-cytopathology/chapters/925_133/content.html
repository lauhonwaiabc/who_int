9.2.5 Category: Malignant

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Malignant” demonstrates unequivocal cytopathological features of malignancy.

FNAB of lymph nodes has been used to make the initial assessment of lymphadenopathy for more than 60 years, and it provides a minimally invasive and rapid diagnostic test that is highly accurate and can often make specific diagnosis, with or without the need for further testing, for example a lymph node containing metastatic melanoma, which helps to stage a patient with a melanoma but requires no further testing on that particular sample unless NGS has not been performed previously. But the huge range of lesions that can involve lymph nodes, including infections, inflammatory processes, lymphomas, and metastases, makes assessment difficult at times. The clinical context and the imaging findings, along with basic supportive tests such as a peripheral blood film, are vitally important to make the FNAB effective, and assessment of the clinical findings is mandatory before proceeding with an FNAB.

The sensitivity and specificity of lymph node FNAB are variable in the literature, ranging from 75% to 99% and 87% to 100%, respectively, for the diagnosis of malignancy {33830657; 27554776}. Two studies showed higher ranges when they used a uniform cytopathological reporting system {33830657; 34441249}. Malignant cases correspond to about 45% of lymph node FNAB cases, and metastases correspond to 60–90% of the cases {33830657; 34441249}.

A review {39097796} of 10 retrospective studies analysing similar categories used in the Sydney System showed the ROM to be very high, ranging from 98% to 100% {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657}. A recent meta-analysis based on nine of these studies found a pooled ROM of 100% {38096796}. These studies represent different population cohorts and a marked variation in the use of ultrasound to guide the biopsy, the percentage of FNAB procedures performed by cytopathologists, the use of ROSE, the use of ancillary tests, and the statistical analysis {39097796}.

Ancillary testing using flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and molecular tests plays a key role in achieving a specific malignant diagnosis, as well as a classification and subtyping of the malignancy, and it should be used whenever feasible. In the case of metastases, the application of immunocytochemistry panels driven by clinical data and cytomorphological features enables the identification of the site of origin of tumour in most cases {34441249}. However, these techniques do not significantly change the ROM for the category “Malignant”.

The management of cases categorized as “Malignant” depends on the specific diagnosis, which in turn often relies on the availability of ancillary tests, including flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and NGS. In cases of metastatic malignancy, the clinical situation and staging of the carcinoma, melanoma, or other malignancy will determine the management; for example, a cervical lymph node containing metastatic squamous cell carcinoma of the oropharynx may lead to a surgical neck dissection, while a cervical lymph node containing metastatic lung carcinoma will assist in staging and provide material for NGS assessment and potential chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and therapy targeted at a specific genetic abnormality.

In cases of the initial presentation of a lymphoma, specific subclassification may be possible by using ancillary testing when available, but surgical excision of the lymph node is usually required if ancillary testing is not available and in cases of Hodgkin lymphoma, T-cell lymphomas, and any situation where a specific diagnosis is not possible {32454496}. Repeat FNAB with or without CNB may be an option in cases where only cytopathology is available and there is no material for flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, or NGS, and where access to the lymph node for surgical excision or the clinical status of the patient make surgery difficult.
