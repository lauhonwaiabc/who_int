7.1.1.4 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

The main reason for incorporating the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is to increase the positive predictive value (PPV) of the “Malignant” category while maintaining the high sensitivity for detection of malignant lesions. Whereas the “Atypical” category should be reserved for cases where the cytopathologist is inclined towards a benign diagnosis but finds it difficult to entirely exclude malignancy, the “Suspicious for malignancy” category by contrast should reflect a situation where the cytopathologist is close or very close to rendering a definitive malignant diagnosis but for some reason is hesitant.

The published ROM for the “Suspicious for malignancy” category, based on a review of 10 recent studies {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657} analysing the very similar categories used in the Sydney System, is very high, ranging from 88% to 100% depending on the use of ancillary techniques {39097796}. A recent meta-analysis based on nine of these studies found a pooled ROM of 93% {38096796}. These studies represent different population cohorts and a marked variation in the use of ultrasound to guide the biopsy, the percentage of FNAB procedures performed by cytopathologists, the use of ROSE, the use of ancillary tests, and the statistical analysis.

If flow cytometry is used along with FNAB cytopathology, the reported ROM is very high (96–100%), reflecting the strength of flow cytometry to exclude the most common types of B-cell lymphomas {34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249}. Notably, ROSE was performed in all reports where flow cytometry was applied in the clinical routine, and a 100% ROM was reached. ROSE plays a decisive role in the workup of neoplastic lymphadenopathy, allowing efficient and cost-effective triage of the sample, and thus reduces the need for subsequent procedures {30195266}. Without flow cytometry, cases that would otherwise have been categorized as “Atypical” or even “Benign” may fall within the “Suspicious for malignancy” category, and consequently, when flow cytometry is not available, the published ROM has a lower specificity and a broader range, from 82% to 100% {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 33830657}.

It is important to realize that the ROM reflects the cytopathologist’s risk assessment within the unique clinical context. This means that without access to adequate clinical information, or without ROSE, the accuracy of the assessment may be jeopardized. In most publications, the ROM for metastatic disease and haematolymphoid tumours is combined, making it difficult to assess specific differences. This will be an area of future research, and this first WHO edition of a reporting system does not attempt to discuss possible tumour-specific dissimilarities with respect to the ROM.

The consequences of a “Suspicious for malignancy” report depend on the locally available facilities and the clinical context. A repeat FNAB to obtain a diagnostic cell block or material for flow cytometry is one management option, if certain lymphoma subtypes are suspected. An excisional biopsy is ideal for the initial classification of some lymphomas, such as follicular lymphoma, suspected peripheral and other subtypes of T-cell lymphomas, and Hodgkin lymphoma, when an FNAB suggests their diagnosis, particularly in the absence of flow cytometry and other ancillary tests. A CNB may be a suitable alternative for many lymphomas, particularly if flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and NGS are available. If no ancillary testing is available, an excisional biopsy with histopathology is required. For suspected metastases, repeat FNAB with cell block and immunocytochemistry on CNB may be sufficient for definitive diagnosis. The site of the lymph node will often determine which biopsy technique is used.
