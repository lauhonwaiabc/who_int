8.6.2.7 Myeloid sarcoma

Definition

Myeloid sarcoma (MS) is a tumour mass involving any anatomical site other than bone marrow (i.e. extramedullary) that effaces tissue architecture and is composed of myeloid blasts, with or without maturation.

Clinical features and imaging

The most frequently affected sites are lymph nodes, skin, gastrointestinal tract, bone, soft tissue, and testes, but almost any site can be involved. Lymph node enlargement is particularly common {27631510}. Less than 10% of cases present at multiple sites {17443224; 17170724}. MS can precede or coincide with overt leukaemia and should be considered equivalent to acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) even in the absence of leukaemic presentation, given the similar prognosis and ultimate involvement of the bone marrow in most patients {27631510}. In about one quarter of the cases, MS presents without underlying AML or other myeloid neoplasm. In some cases, MS represents blastic transformation of myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms {17443224; 17170724}. In other cases, MS is the initial manifestation of relapse in patients treated for AML, including those who have undergone allogeneic stem cell transplantation. MS can also occur secondary to chemotherapy {17170724}.

On imaging, MS can be circumscribed or diffusely infiltrating. Sonography, CT, MRI, and PET findings are nonspecific, being indistinguishable from those seen with other malignancies, and MS is frequently mistaken for lymphoma {17443224}.

Histopathology

MS most commonly consists of diffusely infiltrating myeloid blasts, with or without variable degrees of granulocytic differentiation. In many cases, it displays myelomonocytic features, often with a prominent population of monoblasts {17443224; 17170724}. Tumours composed predominantly of erythroid precursors or megakaryoblasts are rare and most often seen in the setting of blastic transformation of myeloproliferative neoplasms {17170724}. At extranodal sites, the neoplastic cells may form cohesive sheets or single files surrounded by fibrous septa, mimicking carcinoma. In lymph nodes, there is a paracortical proliferation of the neoplastic cells, sometimes with preservation of germinal centres, but often with complete effacement of the architecture and/or extension into perinodal fat {19687319}. The cytomorphology is variable, ranging from blastoid cells mimicking lymphoblastic lymphomas to large cells reminiscent of large cell lymphomas {17170724}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Myeloid sarcoma without maturation
Monotonous intermediate or large blasts with round to oval nuclei with blastic fine chromatin and inconspicuous or prominent nucleoli Blasts have scant to moderate, pale-blue to blue-grey cytoplasm (Giemsa stain), rarely with granulation or Auer rods. Large blasts may be seen with delicate nuclear chromatin and one or more large prominent nucleoli, with more ample greyish or bluish cytoplasm reminiscent of monoblasts. Fragments of myeloid cell cytoplasm (“lymphoglandular bodies”) may be seen.
Myeloid sarcoma with maturation
Significant number of blasts, as in cases without maturation Most cases exhibit promyelocytic-myelocytic or myelomonocytic cytomorphology, with azurophilic granulation in a sparse or moderate amount of cytoplasm, sometimes with the presence of eosinophilic myelocytes or mature eosinophils. Rarely binucleated or multinucleated cells with abundant cytoplasm and nuclear lobations resembling megakaryocytes, together with blasts that may show granular cytoplasm and cytoplasmic blebbing reminiscent of megakaryoblasts Rarely, dominance of erythroid precursors

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Diagnosis of MS by cytomorphology alone is virtually impossible in the absence of maturation. Without ancillary testing or the clinical setting of a known myeloid neoplasm, it can easily be mistaken for other round cell tumours, in particular high-grade lymphomas {11156520; 10231412}. Other mimics include plasmacytoma, blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm, and aggressive NK-cell leukaemia, as well as metastases of undifferentiated carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, melanoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma, medulloblastoma, and seminoma. To differentiate these tumours effectively, immunophenotypic analysis of fresh FNAB material by flow cytometry or immunocytochemistry on cell blocks or cytospins is mandatory.

In MS with maturation, a tentative MS diagnosis can be reached on Giemsa stains in the presence of myeloid differentiation with granulation, whereas Pap stain alone often leads to misinterpretation in the absence of ancillary testing because granulation is not seen.

Myeloblastic MS is the most common type, but monoblastic or myelomonocytic forms occur frequently {8629407; 11156520; 10838454; 18181186; 9850672; 14587051}. Megakaryoblastic cases and tumours with dominance of erythroid precursors are rare {11015986; 22022337}.

Because myeloid metaplasia often arises in the same clinical settings as MS and may form tumour masses, i.e. myeloproliferative neoplasms including chronic myeloid leukaemia, it can be mistaken for MS {11156520; 2338034; 17115434}. This lesion, however, is composed of granulocytic precursors, nucleated erythroid cells, and mature-looking large megakaryocytes in various proportions, with small numbers of myeloid blasts. Nevertheless, the distinction from MS is somewhat arbitrary, as no threshold of blasts has been defined.

Ancillary testing

The nature of the blasts can be determined by immunocytochemistry on cell blocks or cytospin preparations, or by flow cytometry. Myeloid blasts typically express CD34 and CD117, as well as one or more of the markers CD13, CD33, and MPO {27631510}. Monoblastic tumours are often positive for CD68 and CD163 and lack CD34 and MPO. By flow cytometry, staining for CD4, CD11b, CD11c, CD13, CD14, CD33, CD36, CD64, and HLA-DR is useful to reveal monoblastic or monocytic features [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Notably, aberrant expression of the T-cell markers CD3 and CD5 can be seen in up to one third of all MS cases, and CD7 in almost half {27631510}. Megakaryoblasts can be revealed by staining for CD61 {22022337}.

With FISH and/or karyotyping, chromosomal aberrations can be detected in more than half of cases, including the specific gene rearrangements of core-binding factor leukaemias, i.e. t(8;21) and inv(16)/t(16;16), or acute promyelocytic leukaemias t(15:17). The mutation spectrum can be established by NGS, for example by targeting recurrently mutated genes of AML with a customized gene panel.
