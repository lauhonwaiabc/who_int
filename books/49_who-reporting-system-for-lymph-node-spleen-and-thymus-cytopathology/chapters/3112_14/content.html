3.1.1.2 Flow cytometry

Introduction

Flow cytometry is a sophisticated assay in which intact, unfixed cells that have been stained with fluorochrome-labelled antibodies are examined as they pass single-file through laser light. The instrument assesses each cell for size and granularity through forward and side scatter, respectively, and for fluorochrome emission to determine the intensity of expression of the target of each antibody {26767547}. Most antibody targets have been classified according to the cluster of differentiation (CD) system, and the CD markers commonly assessed in FNAB of lymphoid tissues are listed in #29189Table 2.02 . It is important to include CD45 in at least one tube and display CD45 versus side scatter, so that both the lymphocyte gate–specific events (i.e. bright CD45 and low side scatter) and other areas of the plot can be checked for neoplastic cell populations.

Where resources are available, cytometers that assess eight or more fluorochromes (“colours”) on a single cell are widely used in clinical cytometry {31655051}. Such instruments are effective in assessing abnormal cells in the presence of normal counterparts. If resources are constrained, sound flow cytometry can be performed on instruments that assess four colours {31968576}. Regardless of instrumentation, flow cytometry requires highly skilled technical staff, as well as experience in interpretation.

Flow cytometry has become widely established as an important ancillary test for FNAB of lymphoid tissue, especially when ROSE is performed {30156771; 32454496; 21438908; 27554776; 31968576}. Even samples with little apparent material may have sufficient cells for flow cytometry analysis, which is most valuable in assessing samples for lymphoproliferative disorders.

Samples for flow cytometry must not be placed in fixative, because the antibodies used in flow cytometry detect unfixed antigens. If samples can be analysed as soon as practicable, they may be placed in tissue culture medium, or if such medium is not available, in saline {32606494}. Tissue culture medium may be supplemented with fetal bovine serum {24022851; 33456333}. A variety of commercial or in-house stabilizing solutions are particularly useful if flow cytometry is performed 18–24 hours or more after sample collection {23674509; 32479677}, although stabilizers may reduce light scatter and fluorescence intensity {23674509}. Some protocols recommend storage at 4 °C pending analysis, while others recommend ambient temperature {32606494; 33456333; 24022851}.

B cells

Each B cell expresses a single type of surface immunoglobulin light chain (sIg LC), restricted to either kappa (κ) or lambda (λ). Normal B cells, which express CD19 and CD20, usually have a κ:λ ratio between 1:1 and 3:1, although the ratio may vary between specimen types, between analysis techniques, and following therapy {30549186; 30194715} (see #29192Table 2.03, for other unusual situations). Mature B-cell lymphomas usually demonstrate light chain restriction at the population level, where all the neoplastic cells express either κ or λ.

Identification of B-cell lymphomas is often complicated by admixture with polyclonal normal B cells. When these are present, analysis software is used to select subpopulations of B cells and display their light chain expression. Subpopulations are selected based on light scatter and altered expression levels of surface markers, especially CD5, CD10, CD19, and CD20. Careful analysis usually reveals any aberrant population, made up of cells that are essentially all κ or λ {30549186}. Such analysis is preferably performed on a flow cytometer that can determine at least kappa, lambda, CD5, CD10, CD19, and CD20 simultaneously, if resources permit.

In some cases, monoclonality cannot be determined by sIg LC because κ and λ are not expressed, or because both κ and λ are detected on the same cells. In such cases, assessment with different pairs of sIg LC antibodies may reveal a monoclonal population {22095382}. Absence of sIg LC expression on mature peripheral B cells (CD19+, CD20+) is an aberrant finding that can be used to support a lymphoma diagnosis.

The characteristic immunophenotypic features of major categories of mature B-cell lymphomas are summarized in #33280Table 2.04 [[Axler O, Porwit A. Chapter 7: Immunophenotyping of mature B-cell lymphomas. In: Porwit A, Béné M, editors. Multiparameter flow cytometry in the diagnosis of hematologic malignancies. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2018. pp.105–27.]] and described in detail in the fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Flow cytometry detects nearly all cases of mature B-cell lymphomas, except for some cases of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma {11488065; 26352428}. Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) account for the majority of CD5-positive cases. Typical CLL/SLL has a characteristic immunophenotype and can be diagnosed without further ancillary testing, even in cases negative for surface light chain. Other CD5-positive B-cell populations, including cases with atypical CLL phenotype, should be tested for MCL by immunocytochemistry and/or FISH. CD10-positive mature B-cell lymphomas typically have germinal-centre (GC) cell origin, but categorization of CD10-positive lymphomas is difficult by flow cytometry alone. Likewise, categorization of other types of B-cell lymphoma is difficult by flow cytometry, and further ancillary testing should be used where available.

Different strategies are needed to identify B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma (B-LBL) and plasma cells. B-LBL is dim or negative for CD45 and usually expresses CD10, CD19, and CD34, as well as intracellular TdT and BCL2; sIg LC and CD20 are usually negative {30549186; 31146399}. Plasma cells are also dim CD45 and negative for sIg LC. They have bright expression of CD38 and CD138, and intracytoplasmic immunoglobulin light chain should be assessed for restricted expression of κ or λ {26100534}.

T cells

Flow cytometry immunophenotyping is valuable in detection of mature T-cell neoplasms. However, an aberrant population may be difficult to identify due to the background of normal and reactive T cells. Good knowledge of the phenotype of such background cells is mandatory [[Tierens A. Chapter 9: Mature T-cell neoplasms and natural killer-cell malignancies. In: Porwit A, Béné M, editors. Multiparameter flow cytometry in the diagnosis of hematologic malignancies. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2018. pp. 140–60.]] {30729667}. Reactive conditions may cause a predominance of some T-cell subpopulations and an abnormally high or low CD4:CD8 ratio. Elevated CD4:CD8 ratios are often seen in reactive T cells in Hodgkin lymphoma. Nevertheless, a very markedly high or low CD4:CD8 T-cell ratio may be a clue to the presence of a monoclonal population. Other clues include the reduced, absent, or increased expression of one or more of the commonly used pan–T-cell markers (CD2, CD3, CD5, and CD7). Ungated data plots should be reviewed, because aberrant T-cell populations may have abnormal light scatter properties {30729667; 32947231}. CD10, a marker routinely checked on B cells, should also be checked on T cells, because it is often positive in nodal T follicular helper cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic type (formerly called angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma) and T-lymphoblastic lymphoma {27105079; 26243719}. Immunophenotypes of the more common mature T-cell neoplasms are given in #33281Table 2.05 , and these neoplasms are described in detail in the WHO classification [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Recently, assessment of the T-cell receptor beta chain constant domain 1 (TRBC1) has emerged as an effective way to identify monoclonal T-cell populations. Each α/β T cell uses either the TRBC1 or the TRBC2 gene, but not both. In polyclonal T cells, slightly fewer than half express TRBC1, whereas in monoclonal populations all cells are either positive or negative {30715093; 32333725}. TRBC1 should be tested in combination with standard T-cell markers, to select a suspicious population and display its TRBC1. Assessment of TRBC1 can be used to assess all α/β T-cell lymphomas with surface T-cell receptor (TCR), in a single flow cytometry tube. By contrast, an earlier method using a panel of 24 TCR Vβ antibodies positively detects only about two thirds of α/β T-cell neoplasms and requires 8 tubes {21571962}.

Other cells

Myeloid sarcoma, a tumour of acute myeloid leukaemia cells outside the bone marrow, commonly occurs in lymphoid tissue. It is important to distinguish myeloid sarcoma cells from abnormal lymphoid cells {17170724}. A suspicious population in the blast gate (dim CD45 and moderate side scatter) should be tested for myeloid markers {28574302}.

Mastocytosis and its subtypes may involve lymph nodes. By flow cytometry, mast cells are located in the granulocyte gate in standard side scatter and CD45 plots, and they are very bright for CD117 {22912359}.

Natural killer (NK)-cell disorders may be found in lymph nodes. NK cells are CD3−, CD16+, and/or CD56+, and flow cytometry screening should include these markers. If NK cells in tissue are > 10% of lymphocytes, they should be further investigated {31313887}.

Pitfalls

Flow cytometry is sometimes unable to confirm clonality or even determine the immunophenotype of the neoplastic population. This is most likely when tumour cells are fragile or infrequent, as in Hodgkin lymphoma, or very large, as in anaplastic large cell lymphoma. When the initial cytomorphological evaluation favours Hodgkin lymphoma or its mimics, or when there is a significant possibility of a non-haematological neoplasm, preparation of a cell block for phenotyping by immunocytochemistry should be prioritized over flow cytometry.

ROSE usually establishes one of two major cytomorphological categories with implications for subsequent gating strategy and potential sources of error. In the first category, large cells (usually with cytopathological features of malignancy) are present, and the differential diagnosis includes large cell lymphomas, myeloid sarcoma, and undifferentiated non-haematological neoplasms. In the second category, small or intermediate-sized lymphoid cells are present, and the differential diagnosis includes reactive lymphoid tissue and indolent B-cell lymphomas. These and other pitfalls are described in #29192Table 2.03 .
