8.6.4.10 Mantle cell lymphoma

Definition

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a mature B-cell neoplasm derived from the mantle zone of lymphoid follicles; it is typically composed of small to medium monomorphic cells expressing CD5, SOX11, and cyclin D1, and it is associated with CCND-family rearrangements, most commonly of CCND1.

Clinical features and imaging

MCL usually occurs in middle-aged and older patients. It is generally an aggressive neoplasm, albeit with heterogeneous behaviour, and recent advances in treatment have produced an overall better survival [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {19188674; 21782061; 25499451}. The median age at presentation is approximately 60 years, with a male predominance of 2:1 [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Apart from in situ MCL, which is a rare and incidental finding of replacement of the mantle zones of lymphoid follicles by B cells overexpressing cyclin D1 and not diagnosable in FNAB cytopathology, there are two molecular subtypes that differ in their clinical and biological features: conventional and leukaemic non-nodal {32584970; 20124476; 22425896; 23023712}.

Classic MCL, which expresses transcription factor SOX11, usually presents with stage III or IV disease, displaying generalized lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and bone marrow involvement, often with peripheral blood involvement. Extranodal sites such as gastrointestinal tract, lungs, and pleura can also be involved [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. In contrast, the leukaemic non-nodal MCL is negative for SOX11 and is genetically more stable, with a more favourable prognosis. These patients present with involvement of peripheral blood, bone marrow, and spleen, but may progress over time with secondary genetic lesions including TP53 mutations, resulting in a more aggressive disease with nodal engagement {30154113}.

Histopathology

The histopathological findings in classic MCL are those of a monomorphic lymphoid proliferation with a vaguely nodular, diffuse, mantle zone or rarely follicular growth pattern. Most cases are composed of small to medium lymphoid cells with markedly irregular nuclear contours and usually inconspicuous nucleoli. A subset of cases present with blastoid or pleomorphic morphology, and along with a high Ki-67 proliferation index, p53 expression and TP53 mutation are markers of high-risk MCL. Histopathological transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS) does not occur, but loss of a mantle zone growth pattern with acquisition of blastoid or pleomorphic features and increased mitotic activity and Ki-67 proliferation index can be seen at relapse {11707830; 22615102; 30154113}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The accuracy of FNAB to diagnose MCL depends to a degree on the subtype of MCL present. Classic MCL can usually be identified, but the other subtypes may be difficult to recognize. According to one study, FNAB correctly identified 77% of the blastoid subtype and 53% of the pleomorphic subtype cases when compared with paired CNBs {30452126}. The inability to assess lymph node architecture is a limitation of FNAB, but the histopathological pattern has little or no correlation with prognosis and outcome {26926679}.

MCL is difficult to separate from other low-grade lymphomas and some types of T-cell lymphoma composed of small or intermediate cells without immunophenotypic studies, and as with all the small cell lymphomas, flow cytometry is very useful. Typical cases of low-grade follicular lymphoma differ from MCL because of the presence of centroblasts. However, MCL with a mantle zone pattern can show a variable number of centroblasts. Archetypal examples of small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) may be distinguished from MCL by their monotonous round nuclei and coarsely granular chromatin, but the nuclei of some MCL can look very similar to those of SLL. Nodal marginal zone lymphoma can be very difficult to distinguish from MCL, particularly cases dominated by centrocyte-like cells. The blastoid MCL subtype can be indistinguishable from B-lymphoblastic or T-lymphoblastic lymphoma, high-grade B-cell lymphomas (including diffuse large B-cell lymphoma / high-grade lymphomas with MYC and BCL2 gene rearrangements), and even Burkitt lymphoma. The pleomorphic MCL subtype may look like DLBCL-NOS, other high-grade B-cell lymphomas, and pleomorphic T-cell lymphomas – including (very rarely) anaplastic large cell lymphoma. Finally, MCL can be mistaken as reactive lymphadenopathy, because benign centrocytes may resemble MCL cells. In the absence of ancillary studies, surgical biopsy with immunocytochemistry and if possible flow cytometry should be recommended.

Ancillary testing

Immunocytochemistry of the atypical lymphoid population demonstrating cyclin D1 expression is required for the diagnosis of MCL, and this can be performed on cell blocks or cytospin preparations, but false negatives can occur, and immunocytochemistry on core or excision biopsies can be recommended if this material is not available {30452126}. However, apart from identifying the architectural pattern as diffuse, nodular, or mantle zone, histopathology adds little to the diagnosis when ancillary tests are available and there is a lymphoid population expressing CD20, CD5, BCL2, CD43, and SOX11 on cytopathological specimens, including cyclin D1 on the cell block. Cyclin D1 is expressed in > 95% of cases, including the rare CD5-negative cases. SOX11 is expressed in > 90% of cases, including cyclin D1–negative and blastoid cases.

Cell blocks or cytospin preparations can be used to determine the Ki-67 index, although it may be higher than on histopathology paraffin-embedded tissue sections. In a recent study, the proliferation index was 15% higher on FNAB (mostly cytospin preparations) than in the corresponding CNBs {30452126}. The blastoid and pleomorphic subtypes often display a high Ki-67 index, but high values are also recognized in some cases of classic MCL. Importantly, Ki-67 values on histopathology are an independent prognostic factor irrespective of cytomorphology and growth pattern. Patients with ≥ 30% Ki-67 index have an inferior prognosis, with adverse outcomes {26926679}. TP53 status is another critical prognostic parameter, and TP53 mutation carries a poor prognosis {31537689; 28819011}.

By flow cytometry, the cells express CD19, CD20, and restricted light chains, with the intensity of light chains and CD20 usually similar to that of normal B cells. Unusually for B-cell lymphomas, lambda cases outnumber kappa cases. The cells are typically positive for CD5 and negative for CD10, CD23, and CD200 {34114641; 22994971}. Several features distinguish MCL from chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / SLL (CLL/SLL), which is also usually CD5-positive. These include CD23 and CD200, which are typically positive in CLL/SLL, as well as the intensity of the expression of light chains and CD20, which in CLL/SLL are typically dimmer than in normal B cells {22994971; 25389338}.

Cyclin D1 antibodies are not routinely used in flow cytometry, and a definitive diagnosis of MCL requires the use of other modalities. Aberrant or less typical phenotypes have been described, especially in the blastoid and pleomorphic subtypes. These include absence of CD5 and expression of CD10, BCL6, CD23, or LEF1 {31051744; 25713417; 28628241}. Expression of CD23 and CD200 is associated with leukaemic non-nodal MCL {31054894}. TP53 mutations can be determined with various techniques, including NGS. Overexpression of p53 in > 30% positive cells with nuclear staining can be used as a surrogate marker for TP53 mutation, although a negative stain does not exclude the presence of the mutation {32748433}.
