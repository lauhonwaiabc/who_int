1.1.1.4 Paediatric cytopathology

Lymphadenopathy is frequent in children, the etiology and pathogenesis are diverse, and a large majority of cases (67%) have an unknown cause {31384630; 7070884} or are due to benign causes, arising in the course of infectious or autoimmune diseases. FNAB is a valid option to distinguish benign from malignant conditions and offers a minimally invasive, rapid, and accurate diagnostic modality, obviating the need for more invasive procedures {33290901}.

Most frequently, children present with cervical or inguinal lymphadenopathies. Unusual locations, including supraclavicular and epitrochlear, carry more suspicion, as do large (> 20 mm in diameter), fixed, and/or tender lymphadenopathies in a child who lacks infectious symptoms, with an elevated LDH and/or persistent elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate / C-reactive protein levels (ESR/CRP) {645650; 6471252; 17097154; 26283585; 7070884}. B symptoms in children are uncommon with non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), except in patients with anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) {28864732; 22867814}.

Infectious diseases due to viral agents, bacteria, or mycobacterial infection are common underlying causes of benign paediatric lymphadenopathies (see #40543Table 1.02). The prevalence of the infectious agent is closely related to socioeconomic factors and geographical location of the patient {31384630}. Other less common causes of benign lymphadenopathies in children are Kawasaki disease, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, and even systemic lupus erythematosus (see #40543Table 1.02).

Whenever malignancy is suspected, FNAB should be performed under general anaesthesia if possible at the same time as any other invasive medical procedures, to spare the child any additional discomfort. However, FNAB is a minimally invasive procedure and can also be carried out in situations where no other procedure is performed [[Monaco SE, Teot LA. Pediatric cytopathology: a practical guide. Heidelberg (Germany): Springer Berlin; 2017.]].

Less commonly, malignant causes, whether primary or secondary, can be the underlying cause of lymphadenopathies in the paediatric age group. Lymphomas account for approximately 15% of all childhood malignancies, and their incidence is highest in adolescents {19087093}. Most of the paediatric lymphomas are either Hodgkin lymphomas (HLs; 6.4% of childhood malignancies) or high-grade NHL (6–8% of childhood malignancies) [[Huang MS, Weinstein HJ. Chapter 21: Non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In: Fish JD, Lipton JM, Lanzkowsky P, editors. Lanzkowsky’s manual of pediatric hematology and oncology. 7th ed. Amsterdam (Kingdom of the Netherlands): Academic Press; 2021. pp. 473–83.]] (see #40599Table 1.03).

Age at the time of diagnosis should be considered (see #40599Table 1.03). In younger children, lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma (LBL) and Burkitt lymphoma (BL) are more prevalent. In children older than 15 years, the diagnoses of large B-cell lymphomas (LBCLs), ALCL, and primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) prevail {33723759; 27913533; 21030984; 32570974}. The body site of presentation of lymphomas in children is also valuable information (see #40599Table 1.03).

In adults, B-cell lymphomas are much more frequent than T-cell lymphomas, but in children the proportion of T-cell versus B-cell lymphomas is balanced, and immature cell lymphomas prevail {30809797; 30729513}.

In paediatrics, small cell lymphomas like small lymphocytic lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, and marginal zone lymphoma are rare. More recently, some indolent new entities have been confirmed in the latest WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours (WHO-HAEM5), including paediatric nodal and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma and LBCL with IRF4 rearrangement {31187530} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Apart from the fact that some lymphomas are more frequent in the paediatric population than in adults, there are minor epidemiological and even histopathological and molecular differences in the entities when they occur in the adult and paediatric populations.

Classic HL (CHL) accounts for half of childhood lymphomas. Most cases occur in adolescents {32134194; 23585445}, and CHLs are the most common malignancy among adolescents aged 15–19 years in high-income countries. The annual incidence is 1 case per 1 million population in children aged 1–4 years and 30.5 cases per 1 million population in adolescents. Cytomorphologically, there are no differences distinguishing CHL in adults from CHL in children. In the overall paediatric age group, approximately 90% of HL cases are CHL of the nodular sclerosis subtype, whereas in early paediatric ages, the mixed cellularity subtype has a higher prevalence (accounting for 50.8% of HL cases), especially in children with EBV or HIV infection. Only 10% of paediatric HL cases are of the nodular lymphocyte-predominant HL (NLPHL) subtype {32134194; 32570974}.

NHL in children is most frequently one of the high-grade lymphomas, namely LBL, BL, LBCL, and ALCL. Regardless of the subtype, males are more commonly affected.

In childhood, T- and B-precursor lymphomas account for about 20% of NHLs, and unlike in the adult population, the T-cell subtype (T-lymphoblastic lymphoma) is more prevalent (70–80%), while only 20–25% of cases are B-lymphoblastic lymphoma {30809797}. Clinical presentation is usually related to the presence of a mediastinal mass, although the first diagnosis is usually made on a peripheral enlarged lymph node, most commonly cervical.

BL is an aggressive B-cell lymphoma and one of the most common lymphomas in children, especially in equatorial Africa {24932211}, where it accounts for 30–50% of all childhood cancer, with an estimated incidence of 3–6 cases per 100 000 children per year {24932211}. In its endemic form, BL is frequently associated with EBV infection, mostly in adolescents and young adults. There appear to be no molecular differences between paediatric and adult BL (see section Burkitt lymphoma). Although the diagnosis of BL does not pose great difficulties either cytomorphologically or immunophenotypically, its diagnosis should be confirmed by flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and FISH (when available) to exclude differential diagnoses including LBCL, paediatric follicular lymphoma, and high-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberrations.

LBCLs account for about 15–20% of childhood NHL. They are classified according to the cell of origin, and the two main subtypes are the germinal-centre B-cell (GCB) and the activated B-cell (non-GCB or ABC) subtypes, although there is no evidence that this subclassification has an impact on survival in the paediatric population. Most LBCLs in this age group are of the GCB subtype. In the paediatric population, some specific types of lymphoma should be excluded by ancillary testing. Unlike in adults, BCL2 gene rearrangement is infrequent; it is associated with older age and infers a poorer outcome. Similarly, MYC rearrangements in LBCL are also infrequent, and their occurrence should always raise the presumption of BL. LBCL with IRF4 gene rearrangements and LBCL with chromosome 11q abnormalities should also be excluded before rendering a diagnosis of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS).

PMBCL in children accounts for about 1–2% of NHL, and it mainly affects adolescents. PMBCL is an LBCL located in the mediastinum, with a probable origin in the thymus. Mediastinal lymphomas are common in children and account for 50% of paediatric mediastinal masses, whereas in adults they represent only 12% of all mediastinal tumours {28566711}. Besides PMBCL, other mediastinal lymphomas diagnosed in paediatrics are CHL, T-lymphoblastic lymphoma, and (more rarely) mediastinal grey zone lymphomas. In adults there is a greater variability of lymphoma subtypes diagnosed in this location {28566711}; DLBCL-NOS and MALT lymphomas can also be found in addition to LBL, CHL, and PMBCL. In children, unlike in adults, secondary mediastinal involvement by lymphomas is unusual.

Apart from T-lymphoblastic lymphoma, peripheral T-cell lymphomas are infrequent in children and differ from those in adults. They are found in association with congenital immunodeficiencies or autoimmune disorders, and they are less complex from a molecular standpoint.

ALCL accounts for 10–20% of all childhood NHL. Unlike in adults, most ALCL in childhood is ALK-positive [[Monaco SE, Teot LA. Pediatric cytopathology: a practical guide. Heidelberg (Germany): Springer Berlin; 2017.]] {22867814} (see section Anaplastic large cell lymphoma).

In children, lymphomas often present as a clinical emergency due to the fact that there is a higher prevalence of high-grade lymphomas with an enhanced threat of tumour lysis syndrome, and due to the involvement of vital structures in the mediastinum and the abdominal cavity. About 70% of children present with advanced-stage disease. It is not at all uncommon to have a clinical presentation with superior or inferior vena cava syndrome, airway obstruction, or an acute abdomen due to intestinal occlusion.

The etiology and predisposing factors of paediatric lymphomas are generally not known, although a subset of cases are associated with congenital immunodeficiencies, DNA repair syndromes, and EBV infections {25435117; 30729513}.

High-grade lymphomas and HLs in the paediatric population have an excellent prognosis (see #40599Table 1.03).

Other malignancies can also cause enlarged lymph nodes in the paediatric age group. Histiocytic disorders, including Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH), and secondary tumours, namely small round cell tumours, should always be considered in the differential diagnosis in this age group.

Although craniofacial lesions are the most frequent clinical presentation of LCH in children, lymph nodes can also be involved – particularly cervical nodes {17669469}. The peak occurrence is between 1 and 4 years of age. On cytopathology, no differences exist compared with adult cases, and the diagnosis is made with the identification of the characteristic cells and the presence of markers, such as CD1a and/or CD207 (langerin). However, the clinical presentation in children is more aggressive and LCH is often a multisystem and multiorgan disease.
