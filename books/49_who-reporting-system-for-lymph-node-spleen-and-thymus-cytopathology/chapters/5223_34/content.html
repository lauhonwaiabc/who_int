5.2.2.3 Prominent histiocytosis

Definition

Prominent histiocytosis is defined by an increased number of histiocytes; it encompasses benign inflammatory reactions such as sinus histiocytosis, infections, and non–Langerhans cell histiocytoses.

Clinical features and imaging

Histiocytosis may result in lymphadenopathy or an extranodal mass. Sinus histiocytosis is a common cause of lymphadenopathy in regional lymph nodes draining specific infections, biopsy sites, or a malignant neoplasm, following effective chemotherapy or from postsurgical drainage. Histiocytic lymphadenopathy can be associated with prosthetic joint replacement as “particle disease”. Accumulation of melanin-laden macrophages due to skin diseases causes dermatopathic lymphadenopathy with persistent painless superficial nodal enlargement {3178430}. Prominent diffuse histiocytosis can be a manifestation of mycobacterial and fungal infections. Less common causes include a reaction to foreign material, storage diseases, haemophagocytic syndrome, Rosai–Dorfman disease (RDD), and Whipple disease.

In the fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, RDD has been reclassified into the “histiocytic neoplasms” group under the “histiocyte/macrophage neoplasms” heading. RDD is a histiocytosis characterized by nodal or extranodal accumulation of large S100-positive histiocytes/macrophages that commonly exhibit emperipolesis, i.e. lymphocytes and other inflammatory cells within the cytoplasm. It usually presents in cervical lymph nodes but can involve other lymph nodes and extranodal sites in the head and neck, skin, and CNS, with painless slow-growing lymphadenopathy or symptoms related to a mass. The clinical course varies, ranging from self-limiting to resolution only after specific therapy {11764080; 31004029}.

Histopathology

Histiocytes, derived from circulating monocytes, are normally found in sinuses or diffusely located within the paracortex of lymph nodes. The sinuses can become distended when filled with a diffuse collection of bland histiocytes, as occurs with reactive sinus histiocytosis. Histiocytes can also form small focal clusters, as found in lipid lymphadenopathy with cholelithiasis, or can be scattered throughout a node, as in toxoplasmosis lymphadenopathy. Histiocytes within reactive germinal centres contain apoptotic debris, forming tingible-body macrophages. Macrophages can contain melanin in dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, anthracotic pigment in thoracic nodes, and inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes constituting emperipolesis in RDD. Necrosis may be present with infections. Atypical mycobacterial infections can result in a lymph node being completely replaced by foamy round or spindle histiocytes.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Usually moderate to high cellularity in background of lymphocytes Histiocytes may vary in shape from epithelioid to spindle. Histiocytes have a low N:C ratio, with abundant cytoplasm. The cytoplasm is typically pale, may be foamy, and is usually eccentric, and it can contain endogenous or exogenous materials such as fat droplets, microorganisms, polarizable prosthesis particles, silicone, or tattoo or anthracotic pigment. In haemophagocytosis and RDD, histiocytes are enlarged and can harbour engulfed intact or fragmented inflammatory cell components. Engulfed lymphocytes can be surrounded by a halo. The nuclei are round or indented by the cytoplasm, reniform, or “footprint”, with smooth thin nuclear contours, fine chromatin, and variably prominent nucleoli. Multinucleated histiocytes are often present, with nuclei and cytoplasm similar to those of uninuclear histiocytes. Mitoses are infrequent. Background can contain aggregates of lymphocytes and histiocytes, with a variable number of lymphocytes, larger lymphoid cells, tingible-body macrophages, and other inflammatory cells.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Benign histiocytoses involve lymph nodes more frequently than their malignant equivalents and represent a diagnostic challenge, especially in the later stages of specific disorders in which the number of histiocytes is high and mild cytopathological atypia in the form of nuclear enlargement and prominent nucleoli can be present (#28279Table 4.02).

This is the case in RDD, where Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is in the differential diagnosis. It is important to recognize that large macrophages in RDD lack the nuclear grooves of LCH cells and that the latter generally do not show emperipolesis. Rarely, RDD and LCH may coexist {11393062; 20729813}.

The absence of Reed–Sternberg cells, mononuclear Hodgkin cells, eosinophils, and plasma cells is helpful in excluding classic Hodgkin lymphoma, which can be seen with plentiful histiocytes. Histiocytes displaying ingested red cells may be associated with primary (familial) or secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytic syndrome, and the latter is frequently associated with viral infections, autoimmune diseases, and malignancies such as T-cell lymphomas. Numerous foamy macrophages triggered by an underlying infection must be differentiated from metabolic storage diseases such as Gaucher disease or crystal-storing histiocytosis {29150219}. Iron-containing laminated Michaelis–Gutmann bodies may be identified within histiocytes in malakoplakia. Bizarre pleomorphic histiocytes are the hallmark of histiocytic sarcoma. Finally, the differential diagnosis should include T-cell/histiocyte–rich B-cell lymphoma, where scant scattered malignant B cells are obscured by abundant histiocytes.

Ancillary testing

Special histochemical stains, particularly acid-fast stains, PASD and methenamine silver, or PCR for microorganisms, are helpful to exclude an infectious etiology. Immunoprofiling the histiocytic population is helpful in the differential diagnosis. Histiocytes are generally positive for CD68, CD163, and CD15, with variable expression of S100 (which is intensely positive in RDD). They are negative for markers defining follicular dendritic cells (CD21, CD23, CD35), Langerhans cells (CD1a and langerin), interdigitating dendritic cells (CD4), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (CD123). Unlike in LCH, histiocytes in RDD are positive for S100, CD68, OCT2, and cyclin D1 but negative for CD1a, langerin, and BRAF p.V600E in most cases. LCH is a diagnostic pitfall in dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, where subsets of dendritic cells may exhibit an immunoprofile (S100+, CD1a+, langerin+) similar to that of LCH cells {31896812}. In these cases, the absence of BRAF p.V600E can help confirm dermatopathic lymphadenopathy. In certain clinical settings, it may be useful to demonstrate negative staining for pancytokeratin, HMB45, MART1, and SOX10 to exclude metastatic carcinoma and melanoma.
