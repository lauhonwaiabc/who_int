5.1.1.1 Introduction

The analysis of a sufficient sample from a lymph node FNAB in lymphadenopathies includes as a first step the confirmation of “lymph node” origin, followed by the assessment of a “benign versus malignant” diagnosis. The second step implies a fundamental familiarity of the reporting cytopathologist with reactive conditions that can mimic a neoplastic process, including infections as well as non-infectious and nonspecific changes, such as follicular hyperplasia, affecting primarily lymph nodes [[Field AS, Geddie WR. Lymph node and spleen cytohistology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2014. (Cytohistology of small tissue samples series).]]. A final diagnosis requires close correlation with clinical history, microbiology, serology and special stains for microorganisms, immunocytochemistry, and in some cases molecular tests [[Jaffe ES, Arber DA, Campo E, et al., editors. Hematopathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2016.]]. Similarly, assessment of FNAB of spleen and thymus requires a thorough awareness of the normal features and the features of infections and benign neoplastic lesions that can occur.

The role of FNAB in the diagnosis of benign lymphadenopathies and processes in the spleen and thymus is of paramount importance, because FNAB, together with ultrasound, may be the only investigation the patient might experience and when integrated as necessary with appropriate ancillary techniques can allow management and treatment without further follow-up {22351626}.

This chapter includes infectious and non-infectious reactive lymphadenopathies and challenging differential diagnoses with neoplastic conditions encountered in routine cytopathology reporting.
