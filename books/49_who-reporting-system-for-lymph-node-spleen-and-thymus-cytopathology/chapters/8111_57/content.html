8.1.1.1 Introduction

Cytopathology specimens, including FNAB specimens, represent one of the safest and most cost-effective methods to distinguish benign from malignant lesions involving lymph nodes. Most cytopathology specimens can be procured in an office setting with minimal complications. The most common malignancies involving lymph nodes include non-Hodgkin lymphoma (B-cell and T-cell lymphomas), Hodgkin lymphoma, stromal neoplasms, and metastases. Among these, large B-cell lymphoma, most particularly diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS, is the most common lymphoma.

The diagnosis of malignancies involving lymph nodes requires a combination of cytomorphological, clinical, and ancillary studies to obtain a definite diagnosis. In most cases, a primary diagnosis of a haematopoietic malignancy should not be based on cytomorphological findings only. ROSE is recommended for optimal triage for ancillary tests if these studies are available. In the absence of ROSE, additional material should routinely be collected for cell block preparation for immunocytochemical and molecular studies, and in preservative media for flow cytometry. Immunocytochemical studies may assist in the subclassification of non-Hodgkin lymphomas and in the diagnosis of Hodgkin lymphoma or metastasis. The ancillary studies should be interpreted with caution, because the presence of a clonal population detected by either flow cytometry or molecular studies is highly suggestive but not diagnostic of a malignant process. Clonal T-cell and B-cell proliferations have been previously reported in reactive conditions and represent a potential pitfall {15080297; 15563664; 21546505}. Also, clonal B-cell populations have been demonstrated in in situ follicular neoplasia {11964306; 23831923} and monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis. These entities are not currently categorized as malignant, although they might suggest a higher ROM. The presence of selected molecular alterations detected by cytogenetics or genomic analysis, such as MYD88 alterations, can be helpful to confirm a malignant diagnosis, although correlation with other ancillary studies is recommended.
