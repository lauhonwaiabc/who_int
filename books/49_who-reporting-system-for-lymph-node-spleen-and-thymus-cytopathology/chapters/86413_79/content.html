8.6.4.13 Thymomas

Definition

Thymomas are malignant thymic epithelial neoplasms composed of bland epithelial cells with a variable mix of immature T lymphocytes.

Clinical features and imaging

Although rare, thymomas are the most common primary neoplasm of the anterior mediastinum in adults {12712448}. Most patients are asymptomatic, but symptoms can be associated with a mass lesion or autoimmune or immunodeficiency syndromes [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Thoracic tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 5). https://publications.iarc.who.int/595.]]. Imaging shows thymomas as well-defined lobulated masses that may show intrinsic cystic components. The majority of thymomas are encapsulated and behave in an indolent fashion.

Thymomas are classified by the type of epithelial cells present, i.e. spindle cells (type A thymoma) or polygonal cells (type B thymoma). The histopathological classification is not relevant for patient management, which depends on tumour stage {20699490; 23769581; 27114832}.

Histopathology

Thymomas generally show a lobulated architecture with cellular lobules and intersecting fibrous bands, and they are usually at least partially surrounded by a fibrous capsule. Cystic changes may be present, and in some instances, thymomas may be largely necrotic. Haemangiopericytoma-like vascular pattern, rosettes/pseudorosettes, and glandular and glomeruloid structures are possible growth patterns in type A thymoma. Medullary islands are observed with varying frequency in type B thymoma. Sheets of neoplastic spindle or polygonal epithelial cells are mixed with variable numbers of lymphocytes also known as thymocytes.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Type A thymoma
Cohesive, large tissue fragments or sheets of spindle epithelial cells Epithelial cells have elongated or wavy nuclei with clear or “vesicular” (Pap stain) chromatin. Cytoplasm is evident but lacks clear cell borders Thymic lymphocytes in the background or permeating the sheets
Type B thymoma
Cohesive 3D tissue fragments or flat sheets of epithelial cells admixed with lymphocytes Epithelial cells are polygonal. Smooth nuclear contours with fine or “vesicular” (Pap stain) chromatin and a small nucleolus B1 thymomas show abundance of lymphocytes over epithelial tissue fragments, which may be difficult to identify and misinterpreted as “lymphoid tangles”. B2 and B3 thymomas are usually moderately to highly cellular, with the characteristic dual population of neoplastic epithelial cells infiltrated by lymphocytes and small lymphocytes, and the epithelial cells show prominent nucleoli and delicate cytoplasm.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

In several published series, the accuracy of FNAB for the diagnosis of thymoma ranges from 70% to 100% {30797131; 24455533; 33110833; 31742902}. The hallmark for the diagnosis of thymoma in cytopathology is the presence of two cell populations: neoplastic epithelial cells and lymphocytes {18514125; 9684567; 9684568}. The epithelial cells have bland morphology, and they are best seen in the Pap stain or in cell block preparations {18514125}.

The accurate diagnosis of thymoma in cytopathology is essential to differentiate thymoma from other tumours of the mediastinum with greatly differing clinical management. Thymoma must be included in the differential diagnosis of any anterior mediastinum lesion to avoid misdiagnosis or misinterpretation {18514125}. Endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB) {26521902; 25502247} is generally used for diagnosis of locally advanced thymoma in the mediastinum, as well as for metastatic disease {23769581; 27114832; 33447450; 27618984} and ectopic thymomas {26622659; 25502247}. For surgically resectable thymoma, FNAB is used less commonly but is preferable when a lymphoma is to be ruled out {33447450; 20699490}. Thymoma should enter the differential diagnosis of FNAB of any intrathoracic site, especially a pleural-based lesion, if there is a history of an anterior mediastinal mass.

The presence of necrosis and cytopathological atypia cannot differentiate a B3 thymoma, which can also show pleomorphic nuclei, from a thymic carcinoma, as these features can be encountered in both tumour types {18514125}. Type AB thymoma and other rarer types of thymomas, such as micronodular thymoma with lymphoid stroma or metaplastic thymoma, may be very difficult to classify correctly on FNAB samples. Like for the more common types, the diagnosis of thymoma can be made by the presence of a dual population of cells, namely bland epithelial tissue fragments and immature thymic T cells.

Each histopathological subtype produces a different cytopathological pattern and has different differential diagnoses (#29078Table 7.21). The elongated nuclei and cohesive epithelial tissue fragments may resemble those of a low-grade mesenchymal tumour. Most thymomas retain open or “vesicular” (Pap stain) chromatin, while thymic carcinomas have hyperchromatic nuclei with dense chromatin. Metastatic carcinoma to mediastinal lymph nodes and extragonadal germ cell tumour such as a seminoma are in the differential diagnosis.

Ancillary testing

All thymomas have a similar immunocytochemistry: the epithelial cells are positive for pancytokeratin, p63, p40, and PAX8, and the lymphocytes show the characteristic immunocytochemistry of thymic T lymphocytes, positive for CD3, CD5, CD99, CD1a, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) {26295375; 24722150}. The use of one pancytokeratin marker and TdT is sufficient to diagnose thymoma in the appropriate setting.

Thymic carcinoma can show reactivity to CD5 and CD117 in 60–79% of cases, and the associated lymphocytes are negative for TdT and other immature lymphocyte markers {26295375}. In situations of limited cellularity and lack of material for ancillary tests, a diagnosis of “thymic epithelial neoplasm” may be appropriate, to provide enough information to the clinician/surgeon to plan appropriate therapeutic decisions, for example in cases in which benign entities such as thymic cysts or other cysts are in the clinical, imaging, and FNAB differential diagnoses, as well as at the other end of the spectrum in which higher-grade neoplasms such as carcinomas may be relevant differentials.

Flow cytometry does not detect the thymoma epithelial cells but does detect the developing T cells, which are intracellular CD3-positive and mostly double positive for CD4 and CD8, the phenotype of cortical thymocytes. Small subsets of more mature lymphocytes are positive for either CD4 or CD8. The T cells also express CD1a, CD2, CD5, and CD7, with CD10 on less mature cells. A key distinction between thymoma and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma is that in the latter the neoplastic T cells have a uniform staining intensity of surface CD3, CD4, and CD8 {14983942; 30467242}.
