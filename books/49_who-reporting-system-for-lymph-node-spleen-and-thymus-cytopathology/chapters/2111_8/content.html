2.1.1.1 FNAB techniques and specimen management

Background

The performance of the FNAB, application of smearing techniques, and management of the lymph node samples are operator-dependent procedures that require well-developed skills. The effectiveness of these procedures and the management of preanalytical issues determines the quality of the cytopathological smears and the success of ancillary techniques. Accurate sampling, proper smearing, and good management of the diagnostic material are essential to obtain an adequate and effective lymph node, spleen, or thymus FNAB {29989244}.

FNAB procedure

Superficial and deeply located lymph nodes, as well as deep organs and masses, can undergo FNAB supported by imaging using ultrasound, CT, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), and endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS). Generally, no local anaesthesia is needed, and the procedure is usually well tolerated. FNAB may be performed by physicians of various specialties, including interventional radiologists or endoscopists, usually with the assistance of cytotechnologists. The best results are generally obtained by cytopathologists who evaluate clinical and imaging data, execute the FNAB, prepare the smears, perform ROSE, manage residual material and/or additional passes, choose the appropriate ancillary techniques, and handle the material accordingly.

Thin 22, 23, 25, and 27 gauge needles are recommended, used alone for superficial lesions or mounted on a 10–20 mL syringe, and optionally additionally on a holder. Wider-bore needles (21 gauge or larger) are not regarded as fine needles for aspiration. Through delicate repetitive back and forth movements, with or without concomitant aspiration, cells are retrieved from the FNAB target. Care should be taken to stop the procedure when material appears in the needle hub. Samples obtained by “capillarity without aspiration” (the French or Zajdela technique) {3815294}, in which the needle is used alone without a syringe, may contain less material than those obtained by aspiration, but they are generally less contaminated by blood. Two or more passes can be carried out to obtain enough cells. The quantity and quality of the sample will depend on the setting and may be checked by ROSE. Air-dried and alcohol-fixed smears are recommended on every case; the remaining material is triaged according to the ROSE indications and the clinical context.

Smearing technique

The material should be flushed gently onto the slides and divided into small drops, to obtain multiple slides. The specific smearing technique depends on the quality of the material: if there is a large amount of blood or fluid, the haematological technique of smearing with the edge of another slide can be used, or a second slide can be placed onto the wet slide and the two slides can then be pulled apart. If the material is denser and in the centre of the slide, a second slide can be tipped at a right angle onto the material and dragged down the slide. Smearing should be rapid to prevent clotting and air-drying of slides intended for alcohol fixation for Pap-stained slides. The pressure exerted on the slides should be gentle but firm and uniform, to obtain a teardrop-shaped thin and uniform monolayer while avoiding excessive mechanical trauma to preserve the morphology of the cells, which may be fragile and easily crushed by excessive pressure (refer to section Cell preparation methods).

Specimen management

Different vials and cytopathological preparations may be used to effectively use the residual material and additional passes on the basis of the procedures available in each laboratory {30156771}. Additional direct smears, cytospins, cell blocks of rinsed buffered-formalin–fixed or alcohol-fixed material, and liquid-based cytopathology slides represent the most effective bases for histochemical and immunocytochemical stains, FISH, or molecular analysis (including PCR and NGS), and they are prepared according to the ancillary techniques necessary in the specific diagnostic context {30156776; 26620694; 32606494; 25545197; 22085281; 21771093; 24836219; 20862704; 22517675; 20340097}. PBS (Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline), RPMI medium (Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640), EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid), and buffered normal saline are cell collection media into which the needle sample can be rinsed. Cell suspensions may be used for flow cytometry, liquid-based cytopathology, and cytospins {27554776; 27639613}. Cell stabilization reagents can also be used, to preserve samples for 2–3 days to maintain membrane antigenicity {15627612}. Cell blocks, additional smears and cytospins, or cell suspensions at −80 °C and FTA cards are suitable options for cell storage.
