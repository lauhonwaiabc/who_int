8.6.6.3B Metastases from melanoma

Definition

A metastasis from a malignant melanoma from the skin or mucosa involving a lymph node or occasionally spleen

Clinical features and imaging

The general worldwide incidence of malignant melanoma, a highly aggressive neoplasm with a high propensity to metastasize to regional lymph nodes as well as skin, and then to viscera and bones, has increased considerably in the past 40 years. The mean age at diagnosis varies according to sex, and in young patients the prevalence is higher in female than male patients (F:M ratio = 10:4) {34310059}. Despite greater awareness of risk factors and prevention, as well as the development of targeted therapies and immune therapies, the rate of metastatic melanoma remains high. Currently, therapy for patients with stage III or IV melanoma includes surgery and/or systemic therapy, where it depends on the presence or absence of activating BRAF mutations.

Histopathology

The commonest location of early melanoma metastases in the lymph node is the subcapsular sinus – as singly dispersed cells or a few small groups or sheets. Diffuse metastases involve the deeper sinuses and eventually the paracortex and entire lymph node. Extracapsular extension is infrequent.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Heterogeneous cytomorphology and architectural patterns Epithelioid, plasmacytoid, and spindle cells are more frequent. Discohesive pattern of single cells more frequent with epithelioid or plasmacytoid cells Syncytial or 3D tissue fragments more frequent with spindle or mixed cell types Small, rhabdoid, signet-ring, myxoid, and balloon cells are other rare patterns. Nuclei can be round to oval to moderately pleomorphic and may have prominent nucleoli or pseudoinclusions. Pleomorphic and binucleated or multinucleated cells are frequent. Mitotic figures are usually readily identified. Cytoplasmic vacuoles are often observed in air-dried smears. Melanin pigment appears yellow-brown (Pap stain) and brown-black (Giemsa stain) in the melanoma cells, in macrophages, and free in the background. Necrosis can be present with a peritheliomatous pattern with residual viable cells around small blood vessels.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB can diagnose metastatic melanoma in suspicious enlarged lymph nodes or visceral metastases in patients with a known previous melanoma or metastases of an unknown primary, as well as in sentinel node evaluation. The reported sensitivity and specificity ranges are 86.5–100% and 92.3–100%, respectively {34310059}. The reported false negative range is 0–8% {34310059}. False positives occur at a rate of 0.6–2% {34310059}, most frequently due to misinterpretation of pigment-containing macrophages as melanoma cells. The macrophages have oval to irregular “footprint” nuclei, eccentric cytoplasm, distinct nucleoli, dispersed granular chromatin, and pigment in the cytoplasm. Fibroblasts with plump nuclei may also be a problem, but mainly in hypocellular samples {3464794}; in hypocellular scenarios, a recommendation for repeat FNAB or tissue sampling for further evaluation is appropriate. False negatives generally relate to inexperience of the operator performing the lymph node FNAB {10775847} and poor quality of the FNAB sample, rather than interpretation errors {17276948; 7431919}.

The primary melanoma may be unknown in 3% of FNAB-positive cases, when melanoma is present in the subcutaneous tissue or lymph node, or with visceral localization, in the absence of a known primary skin, ocular, or mucosal lesion {21502902}. The cytomorphological, immunocytochemical, and molecular findings are the same as those observed in cases with a known primary melanoma.

Sentinel lymph node evaluation is recommended for staging at the time of excision of all melanomas {30392755}. There are conflicting opinions regarding the use of FNAB in sentinel lymph node evaluation, and the diagnostic performance of FNAB in sentinel lymph node evaluation is highly variable. FNAB may play a role in sentinel lymph node evaluation in specific clinical settings. Indeed, FNAB could be performed as presurgical screening, especially in a subgroup of patients with suspected larger melanoma metastases, so that patients with positive FNAB could be directly referred for complete lymph node dissection

Ancillary testing

Ancillary techniques, in particular immunocytochemistry, play an important role in confirming the FNAB diagnosis and excluding other poorly differentiated malignancies. The most widely used melanocytic markers include S100, melan-A/MART1, HMB45, and SOX10. S100 is classically considered the most sensitive marker, and HMB45 the most specific. Recently, SOX10 has emerged as a sensitive marker for melanocytic differentiation {33509946}. SOX10 may be superior to all other melanocytic markers in pigmented neoplastic cells because it is a nuclear marker {33509946}.

The use of cell blocks is recommended because it enables panels of immunocytochemistry, and in abundantly pigmented cases where the melanin may make the immunocytochemistry interpretation difficult, Fast Red can be used. Although S100 and SOX10 are the most sensitive markers of melanocytic differentiation, they are not highly specific, because they are also positive in other neoplasms: S100 is expressed by some carcinomas, mesenchymal tumours, and Langerhans cell histiocytosis {33110239; 22495377; 29131006}. SOX10 may be positive in some mesenchymal tumours, diffuse astrocytoma, and pleomorphic undifferentiated sarcomas {22495377; 27327192; 18636017}.

The differential diagnosis of malignant melanoma with malignant nerve sheath tumour and clear cell sarcoma may be particularly challenging, because the immunocytochemical findings of these entities overlap with those of melanoma. HMB45 is the most specific marker, but it may also be expressed by clear cell sarcoma, some pigmented neuroectodermal tumours, perivascular epithelioid tumours, and some renal cell carcinomas {21143519; 27565001}. Melanoma may be HMB45-negative, mainly in the metastatic setting. Melanoma cells may express aberrant non-melanocytic markers such as mesenchymal markers, including vimentin {20856146; 1845482} and cytokeratins {19190520; 29385322}.

Evaluating for BRAF mutations by PCR and NGS is required wherever possible with metastatic melanoma {23086767; 36807248}, while a mutation-specific antibody for BRAF p.V600E mutation is also available for immunocytochemical evaluation {34207125}.
