8.1.1.3 Discussion and background

A comprehensive report should describe the cytomorphological features and the results of ancillary testing (if performed) that led to the conclusion of malignancy. The type of malignancy should be defined whenever possible; otherwise, a range of differential diagnoses should be provided. This category includes haematolymphoid and metastatic malignancies. For haematolymphoid neoplasms in particular, it cannot be overemphasized that knowledge of the clinical context is fundamental and sometimes crucial to reach a malignant conclusion if ancillary testing is unavailable. This is particularly true for indolent (low-grade) lymphomas, for two main reasons. First, lymphoma cells of some common types of indolent lymphomas, such as small lymphocytic lymphoma and classic follicular lymphoma, do not necessarily reveal their malignant nature by cytomorphological analysis alone. In other words, the lymphoma cells may look deceptively bland or very similar to their normal counterparts, including small lymphocytes or centrocytes. Second, mature B-cell lymphomas tend to contain a considerable number of reactive T cells, which might in some cases obscure the neoplastic component. The same problem may be faced with some T-cell lymphomas. For example, in nodal T follicular helper cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic type, the inflammatory component is often significant, with low lymphoma cell burden, making the lymphoma cells difficult to identify, particularly in early-stage disease. The difficulty in recognizing the malignant lymphomatous component by cytomorphology alone is probably one of the reasons FNAB has been regarded in the past as inadequate for lymphoma diagnosis {15284254}. Stripped of the clinical context and ancillary testing, lymphoma cells may easily escape detection. Therefore, ancillary testing, especially flow cytometry, is highly valuable and sometimes indispensable for a definitive malignant diagnosis and for subtyping a haematolymphoid neoplasm. Conversely, in most cases of aggressive large cell lymphomas, the high-grade cytomorphological features leave little doubt about the malignant nature of the lymphoid cells.

The main strength of flow cytometry resides in its ability to confidently identify even small monoclonal populations of lymphoid cells. For a long time, clonality testing by flow cytometry has been restricted to B cells alone, but the technique has recently been developed to also allow clonality testing of T cells {30715093; 30972977; 32333725}. Conclusive evidence for a haematolymphoid neoplasm may also be obtained with immunocytochemistry on cell blocks (e.g. cyclin D1 in the diagnosis of mantle cell lymphoma), although flow cytometry remains a more reliable tool for clonality testing {27554776}. PCR for clonality testing, FISH for deletions/translocations and fusions, and NGS for various aberrations may help further to provide a specific diagnosis of haematolymphoid neoplasms on unfixed material or cell blocks {34703094; 15917402; 25924600}. In cases with clinical history compatible with a haematolymphoid neoplasm but uncertain cytomorphological features and unavailable ancillary testing, the “Suspicious for malignancy” category can be considered, depending on the strength of the clinical suspicion. For example, FNAB of recurrent indolent lymphoma in the appropriate clinical setting but without ancillary testing should be considered in the “Malignant” category, whereas de novo disease with the same prerequisites may be placed in the “Suspicious for malignancy” category.

The cytomorphological features alone are often sufficient to reach a malignant diagnosis in cases of metastatic disease. Clinical and radiological correlation is always necessary for subtyping, and ancillary testing should be considered whenever available. Immunocytochemistry and FISH for specific structural genetic abnormalities on cell blocks are very useful in metastatic solid tumours, as are NGS molecular pathology studies for specific diagnostic or targetable genetic abnormalities.
