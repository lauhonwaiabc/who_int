7.1.1.3 Discussion and background

Cases categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” will have some cytopathological features suggestive of malignancy, but these are not enough to make an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy, usually in a situation where there are insufficient supportive clinical, immunophenotypic, and molecular data.

The report should always describe the features that are suspicious and whenever possible should state the type of malignancy suspected, or provide a differential diagnosis. This category includes cases in which the material is suspicious for haematolymphoid or metastatic malignancies. The cytomorphological features of lymphomas are often subtle, emphasizing the importance of the clinical context and ancillary studies in assigning a case to the “Suspicious for malignancy” category. Ancillary testing may not always clarify cytopathological material as definitively benign or malignant or support a specific lymphoma or other malignancy diagnosis. A lack of relevant clinical information or unavailable ancillary techniques may also preclude a definitive diagnosis of malignancy. However, ancillary testing with flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, PCR for clonality testing, FISH for deletions or translocations and fusions, and NGS for various aberrations can change the final category and provide a specific lymphoma diagnosis. The ancillary findings should be reported in an integrated final cytopathology report.

There are four major determinants of a “Suspicious for malignancy” diagnosis:

1. The expertise of the cytopathologist in interpreting the material. Incorporating the physical examination, particularly the site and the palpation characteristics of the lymph node, as well as the relevant clinical history, influences the experienced cytopathologist in assessing the FNAB material of an enlarged lymph node.

2. The quality of the smear is crucial. Poor FNAB technique or poor handling of the material may result in artefacts prohibiting definitive diagnosis.

3. The available clinical information will influence the categorization. For example, the finding of a few atypical enlarged non-diagnostic cells in an inflammatory smear from a prominent supraclavicular lymph node in an adolescent who also has a mediastinal mass and B symptoms, such as fever and weight loss, may qualify as suspicious for Hodgkin lymphoma. Conversely, the same cytomorphology but without relevant clinical and imaging information will probably be categorized as “Atypical”.

4. The cell smear pattern, predominant cell type, and homogeneity or heterogeneity of FNAB material on slides will influence the propensity to designate a case as “Suspicious for malignancy”. Examples include: the monomorphic predominantly small/intermediate cell pattern, which may mimic benign lymphadenopathy, in a situation with or without a clinical suspicion of low-grade lymphoma, but without clear-cut malignant cytomorphology and without access to or inconclusive flow cytometry; the monomorphic predominantly intermediate/large/pleomorphic/blastic cell pattern, which raises the possibility of Burkitt or lymphoblastic lymphoma, but only scant material, such as when there is partial involvement of a node or when ancillary techniques are unavailable; finding occasional single very large atypical cells in a mixed pattern of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, and histiocytes, raising the possibility that they represent scant Hodgkin or Reed–Sternberg cells, but immunocytochemistry on cell blocks is not diagnostic or not available; and scant atypical cells suspicious for a metastasis are detected but without cell block material available to perform confirmatory immunocytochemistry.
