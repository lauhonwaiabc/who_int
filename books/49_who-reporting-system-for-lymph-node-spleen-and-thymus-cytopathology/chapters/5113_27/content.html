5.1.1.3 Discussion and background

FNAB is a rapid, cost-effective, and safe technique that is particularly helpful in benign conditions, where it can support clinical assessment and clinical management and help to reduce patient anxiety. However, the cytopathological diagnosis of certain non-neoplastic disorders can be challenging, especially when the clinical history and ultrasound appearance are not readily available to the reporting cytopathologist. Therefore, when challenged with the differential diagnosis of benign lymphadenopathies, it is important to follow a systematic approach by recognizing specific patterns {22351626} [[Field AS, Geddie WR. Lymph node and spleen cytohistology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2014. (Cytohistology of small tissue samples series).]] [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. (Pattern recognition series).]] and using appropriate ancillary techniques {30194715; 16222688; 16155944}. These patterns can also be seen in the white pulp zones of the spleen.

The histopathological evaluation of benign lymphadenopathies is based on the predominant architectural pattern, including which lymph node compartment is most affected (i.e. follicular/nodular, sinus, interfollicular/mixed, and diffuse patterns). More than one compartment can be affected simultaneously, depending on the etiology, and the appearance may change over time with the evolution of the process [[Jaffe ES, Arber DA, Campo E, et al., editors. Hematopathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2016.]]. A systematic pattern-based approach is also recommended in the assessment of lymph node FNAB through the combined evaluation of the pattern of smeared material on the slide, the background, the predominant cell type, and the heterogeneity of the lymphoid population {22351626} [[Field AS, Geddie WR. Lymph node and spleen cytohistology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2014. (Cytohistology of small tissue samples series).]] [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. (Pattern recognition series).]].

The evaluation of the FNAB background at low magnification is often very informative in the case of acute inflammatory and suppurative conditions of infectious etiology, where the predominant cell population is neutrophils associated with a variable number of macrophages and necrotic debris. If the necrotic debris is prominent, cat-scratch disease and atypical mycobacterial infection should be considered. A neutrophil-rich background associated with necrotic debris and epithelioid granulomas is more suggestive of mycobacterial infection, but fungal infections (e.g. cryptococcal infections) and other bacterial infections (e.g. yersiniosis and lymphogranuloma venereum) must be excluded using special stains, serology, microbiology, and/or molecular tests where appropriate {8989539; 15184446} [[Kocjan G. Clinical cytopathology of the head and neck: a text and atlas. London (UK): Greenwich Medical Media; 2001.]].

Benign non-infectious lymphadenopathies include disorders ranging from common nonspecific changes like reactive lymphoid hyperplasia to Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease that affects lymph nodes or to lesions that can involve lymph nodes as well as extranodal sites, such as Rosai–Dorfman disease. These encompass several patterns and are generally characterized by a mixed lymphoid population in the background, variably associated with prominent histiocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, or immunoblasts. In specific cases, like Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, a non-neutrophilic, necrotic background can be present {22351626}.

Ancillary techniques are not widely applied or strictly necessary in the diagnosis of benign lymphadenopathies. However, there are specific circumstances where a lymphoproliferative disorder must be excluded or the presence of a certain microorganism must be ascertained by molecular tests {15184446}.

Flow cytometry represents the best ancillary test in association with cytomorphology in cases of reactive lymphoid hyperplasia with limited germinal-centre sampling and abundant small to medium lymphocytes, due to paracortical hyperplasia mimicking a low-grade lymphoma. In different circumstances, even high-grade lymphomas might need to be excluded. This may occur, for example, in cases of necrotizing lymphadenitis or Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease with numerous large plasmacytoid dendritic cells and cytotoxic T cells with abundant karyorrhexis, or in infections like infectious mononucleosis that have a large number of immunoblasts and possibly necrosis. In these circumstances, flow cytometry remains the test of choice to assist in the differential diagnosis {30156771}.

However, the interpretation of flow cytometry data must invariably be guided by cytomorphological examination and clinical history. This is especially pertinent in cases of reactive lymphoid hyperplasia, for which an approximate 5% false positive rate has been previously described {21228371}. In a large-scale study comparing immunophenotypes obtained from reactive lymph nodes using FNAB and excision biopsies, the FNAB sample immunoprofiles closely resembled those obtained from tissue samples in terms of distribution of B-cell, T-cell, and NK-cell antigens, reinforcing the effectiveness of flow cytometry analysis of FNAB samples in reactive conditions {30194715}.
