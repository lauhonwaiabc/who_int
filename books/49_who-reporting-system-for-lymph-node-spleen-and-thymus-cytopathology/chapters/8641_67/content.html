8.6.4.1 Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma

Definition

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) is a B-cell neoplasm composed of monomorphic small mature B cells that frequently coexpress CD5 and CD23, presenting with nodal (lymphadenopathy > 15 mm), splenic, or other extramedullary involvement as SLL accessible to FNAB cytopathology.

Clinical features and imaging

CLL and SLL are the same B-cell neoplasm, distinguished only by the location where the neoplasm primarily occurs. When the neoplastic cells are located in the bone marrow and blood with a peripheral blood B-cell count of > 5 × 109/L, the disease is referred to as CLL, and because this definition relates to a peripheral blood B-cell count, this has not been included in the FNAB cytopathology definition. The term SLL is used when there is lymphadenopathy > 15 mm, and spleen and other extramedullary sites are usually involved, and the circulating CLL cell count is < 5 × 109/L {32023533; 29540348}. CLL/SLL affects mostly older patients, in the seventh and eighth decades of life, with a mean age at presentation of 71 years, although it can occur in the younger population. It has a higher incidence in White males {27936980}. CLL/SLL can evolve from a clinically indolent, slow-growing lymphoma to an aggressive lymphoma in 8% cases. In the indolent form, the patient is frequently unaware of the disease, and the presence of lymphadenopathy is often the first clinical manifestation. The onset of the so-called B symptoms, such as fever, chills, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, and malaise, is an indication for treatment. Clinical transformation to aggressive lymphoma, usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS) or Richter transformation, is associated with adverse patient outcomes, and patients present with localized or generalized lymphadenopathy, with or without hepatosplenomegaly, mucocutaneous bleeding, or petechiae in conjunction with a rapidly rising LDH level {23413591}.

Transformation to Hodgkin lymphoma, plasmablastic lymphoma, or leukaemia / lymphoblastic lymphoma also occurs, and early diagnosis of the transformation, identification of predictive biomarkers, and specific molecular pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of the transformation allow a personalized therapeutic approach {27414262; 19206112}. Recently published evidence suggests that Richter transformation typically arises from the predominant CLL clone through the acquisition of multiple genetic lesions, such as disruption of TP53, activation of NOTCH1, and disruption of CDKN2A/B {24127483}. In a patient with suspected Richter transformation, a biopsy of any nodal or extranodal lesion with increased PET-CT avidity (standardized uptake value > 5.0) is recommended {23656196; 23915194}.

Histopathology

SLL is characterized by a diffuse proliferation of small lymphoid cells effacing the lymph node architecture, with scattered pale proliferation centres of varying size. Partial involvement with an interfollicular or perifollicular infiltration may be seen. The lymphoid cells have round nuclei, clumped chromatin and a small nucleolus, irregular nuclear contours, and a narrow rim of cytoplasm, and plasmacytoid differentiation can occasionally be seen. The proliferation centres are composed predominantly of the small lymphoid cells, prolymphocytes, and paraimmunoblasts {11021809; 25860247} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Monomorphous population of small round lymphoid cells Small lymphoid cells have scant cytoplasm, round nuclei and clumped chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli. Plasmacytoid forms can be seen. Scattered prolymphocytes (intermediate in size, with clumped chromatin and small nucleoli) and paraimmunoblasts (with eccentric cytoplasm, round to oval nuclei with dispersed chromatin, and single central nucleoli) can be present. Absence of mitotic activity and tingible-body macrophages Absence of true germinal-centre tissue fragments containing follicular dendritic cells

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The diagnosis of SLL is characterized by the presence of a monotonous population of small lymphoid cells with scattered larger prolymphocytes and paraimmunoblasts in the absence of evidence of germinal centres. The differential diagnosis includes a reactive lymph node and other types of low-grade lymphoma. A reactive lymphoid population is usually mixed with tingible-body macrophages, which are absent in SLL. Distinguishing SLL from other types of low-grade lymphoma on cytopathology can be difficult but is based on SLL being characterized by a monomorphous population of small round lymphoid cells with clumped chromatin, while the lymphoid cells in other types of low-grade lymphoma show the presence of nuclear irregularities and less coarse clumped chromatin. Flow cytometry is a very useful tool in making the diagnosis, but if it is not available, then CNB or excision biopsy with immunocytochemistry is recommended.

The other entity within the “preneoplastic and neoplastic small lymphocytic proliferations” category of the fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Ancillary testing

SLL shows monoclonal surface immunoglobulin light chain expression in flow cytometry, although the intensity of the light chain is typically weak, as is that of CD20, although weak expression may be difficult to define in a reproducible fashion {29024461}. There is coexpression of CD5, CD19, CD20, CD23, CD200, and ROR1 {32579248; 19230971}. CD5 expression is seen in both mantle cell lymphoma and SLL; however, mantle cell lymphoma cells usually do not express CD23 and/or CD200 {19846810; 25389338}. Follicular lymphoma cells usually do not express CD5 and usually do express CD10, while SLL cells are negative for CD10 {25743023; 29024461}. Most cases of marginal zone lymphoma do not show expression of CD5 {21123968}. The presence of an increased number of large cells in the form of prolymphocytes is associated with a worse prognosis and might represent an accelerated phase or Richter transformation {22693682}.

In immunocytochemistry, SLL demonstrates the presence of an atypical population expressing CD5, CD20, CD23, and LEF1 {20595513; 25713417}. FISH for the detection of del(11q), del(13q), trisomy 12, and del(17p); CpG-stimulated metaphase karyotype and TP53 sequencing; and molecular genetic analysis for IGHV mutation status can provide useful prognostic information and may guide the selection of therapy {32023533; 16840733; 21156225; 26846718; 29164608; 15820941; 29984867}.
