5.2.2.1 Follicular hyperplasia

Definition

Follicular hyperplasia is a common reactive lymphadenopathy characterized by follicle germinal-centre tissue fragments in a heterogeneous background including predominantly small lymphocytes and a mix of medium and larger lymphoid cells, tingible-body macrophages, and follicular dendritic cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Follicular hyperplasia can affect any lymph node and may manifest with local or generalized lymphadenopathy. It can occur in all ages and can represent a response to local antigen or allergen stimulation of B cells associated with infections, or it can be part of generalized lymphadenopathy associated with systemic diseases such as autoimmune disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis and Sjögren syndrome, or reactions to other agents including medications and pollutants. It is the most common cause of lymphadenopathy in the paediatric population and is probably related to recurrent antigen stimulation {12920745}. A comprehensive clinical history and physical examination may be sufficient in the management of this reactive condition, but in many cases lymph nodes with florid follicular hyperplasia mimic a lymphoproliferative disorder, and imaging assessment is essential, with ultrasound representing the initial step in most cases {32454496}. Ultrasound assessment provides crucial information regarding the lymph node size and structure, but even with the application of colour or power Doppler, the ultrasound image may be indistinguishable from that of a lymphomatous process {28439430}. In such cases, FNAB assessment, ideally with flow cytometry, is helpful in confirming the reactive nature of the lymphadenopathy.

Histopathology

Variably sized hyperplastic follicles are present and include reactive primary lymphoid follicles without a germinal centre and secondary follicles with a germinal centre. Some follicles may become serpiginous in shape and coalesce to form peculiar configurations. Secondary follicles are characterized by polarity, i.e. a hyperplastic germinal centre with a dark zone chiefly composed of centroblasts, which have basophilic cytoplasm, large round nuclei, and distinct nucleoli, and a pale zone consisting mainly of centrocytes, which have scant cytoplasm, irregular or cleaved nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli, along with small reactive T lymphocytes, plasma cells, and follicular dendritic cells [[Jaffe ES, Arber DA, Campo E, et al., editors. Hematopathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2016.]]. Germinal centres may frequently harbour tingible-body macrophages containing phagocytosed apoptotic debris. The follicular mantle zone contains resting and memory B cells, and it is usually a clearly demarcated zone rimming the germinal centres.

The follicular dendritic cell processes are not seen. Polymorphous mix of small lymphocytes and small, intermediate-sized, and large lymphoid cells, in a spectrum of differentiation Centrocytes: medium, non-dividing lymphoid cells with scant cytoplasm and irregular indented or cleaved nuclei, mature clumped chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli Large centrocytes may overlap in size with small centroblasts Centroblasts: large proliferating lymphoid cells three to four times the size of mature lymphocytes, with a narrow rim of basophilic cytoplasm, large round to oval nuclei containing finely dispersed immature chromatin, and two to four peripheral nucleoli. There may be numerous mitoses. Immunoblasts have more prominent central single nucleoli and eccentric cytoplasm. Follicular dendritic cells: present as round to oval nuclei with “beaded” or finely reticular chromatin, seen singly or in “kissing” pairs with indistinct cytoplasm due to the invisible interdigitating processes. When multinucleated they are known as polykaryosomes (Warthin–Finkeldey–like cells). Their single nucleoli are much smaller than those seen in Reed–Sternberg cells. Tingible-body macrophages: large macrophages, loosely scattered in the polymorphous lymphoid background and in the germinal-centre tissue fragments, have abundant pale cytoplasm containing apoptotic debris (“tingible bodies”) from phagocytosed cell debris. Their nuclei are round to oval with granular chromatin and contain a variable number of nucleoli. In some cases, there is a mix of predominantly smaller mature lymphocytes, with a variable number of centroblasts and centrocytes, and a few immunoblasts and plasma cells, with no germinal centres and no tingible-body macrophages. Discussion and differential diagnosis

A cellular FNAB composed of a polymorphous population of lymphoid cells with small lymphocytes predominating and including germinal-centre cells and a variable number of tingible-body macrophages is a reliable indication of reactive follicular hyperplasia. Follicular hyperplasia is frequently present in lymph nodes affected by other specific pathologies, which must be excluded before a diagnosis of nonspecific follicular hyperplasia alone can be rendered.

The main differential diagnoses are low-grade non-Hodgkin lymphomas showing lymphoid cell heterogeneity, especially low-grade follicular lymphoma or marginal zone lymphoma with conspicuous residual reactive germinal centres. Flow cytometry is helpful in complex scenarios {20735454}.

The presence of tingible-body macrophages is a valid marker of follicular hyperplasia. However, these cells can also be present in high-grade lymphomas. Therefore, careful evaluation of the lymphoid cells is essential, and a monotonous population of any cell type should raise the possibility of lymphoma {22351626} [[Field AS, Geddie WR. Lymph node and spleen cytohistology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2014. (Cytohistology of small tissue samples series).]].

Germinal-centre tissue fragments rich in follicular dendritic cells and macrophages can sometimes be confused with granulomas. Other reactive conditions within the spectrum of follicular hyperplasia include hyaline-vascular Castleman disease and Kimura disease.

Hyaline-vascular Castleman disease typically affects adolescents and young adults, presenting as a localized mass, commonly in mediastinal and cervical lymph nodes. It is histopathologically defined by regressively transformed germinal centres {27296355}; the cytopathological features show a polymorphous lymphoid pattern with predominance of small to medium lymphocytes with dense chromatin, vascularized and expanded germinal-centre fragments penetrated by capillaries, concentric layering of follicular dendritic cells, numerous plasma cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and variable amounts of hyalinized material [[Field AS, Geddie WR. Lymph node and spleen cytohistology. Cambridge (UK): Cambridge University Press; 2014. (Cytohistology of small tissue samples series).]].

Kimura disease is a chronic inflammatory process of unknown etiology usually affecting Asian men; it presents as a head and neck mass involving single or multiple lymph nodes, soft tissue, and salivary glands, associated with eosinophilia and increased serum levels of IgE {15087670}. The cytopathological findings of Kimura disease are defined by follicular hyperplasia with increased numbers of polykaryon follicular dendritic cells (Warthin–Finkeldey–type cells) in an eosinophil-rich background {22351626}.

A relative increase in the number of follicular dendritic cells, epithelioid macrophages, and small lymphoid cells representing mantle zone lymphocytes, compared with germinal-centre cells, may indicate progressive transformation of germinal centres in the right clinical setting {14502795}, and excision biopsy should be considered. If nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma is of concern, an excision biopsy is recommended {14508396}.

Ancillary testing

The vast majority of cases showing reactive follicular hyperplasia on FNAB do not require further investigation when interpreted in a clinical setting in which lymphoma is unlikely. In some centres, flow cytometry is routinely performed. Cases with high clinical suspicion of lymphoproliferative disease benefit from flow cytometry and clonality analysis in combination with cytomorphological assessment.

Immunophenotyping by flow cytometry is performed using a variable range of T/NK-cell markers (CD3, CD4, CD8, CD5, CD7, CD56, CD57) and B-cell markers (CD20, CD19, CD38, CD10, CD23), together with light chain expression. In follicular hyperplasia, flow cytometry may demonstrate a population of reactive B cells with positive CD10 and bright CD20. Light chain staining indicates these cells are polyclonal. They are brighter for CD19, CD20, and CD38 than the neoplastic cells in follicular lymphoma {19382196}.

Immunocytochemistry on smears or cell blocks confirms the presence of a reactive lymphoid population comprising germinal-centre B cells (CD20+/CD10+/BCL6+/BCL2−) and follicular dendritic cells (CD21+/CD23+).

The diagnostic accuracy of flow cytometry in lymph node FNAB reaches 88.4%, with a sensitivity of 85.8% and specificity of 92.9% {21228371}. In small series the sensitivity of flow cytometry for reactive follicular hyperplasia approached 100%, with a specificity of 89.1% {18986555}. The false negative cases of approximately 20% were mostly represented by classic and nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphomas, where the diagnosis rests on the identification of sparse Reed–Sternberg, Hodgkin, and L&H cells, and in a smaller number of cases by large B-cell lymphoma and anaplastic large cell lymphoma, in which cell disruption with bare nuclei and loss of cytoplasm appears to be the likely cause. In integrating flow cytometry with cytomorphological assessment of smears in follicular hyperplasia, cytopathologists should also be aware of a low false positive rate of about 5%, which can be related to immunoglobulin-negative B cells present in reactive germinal centres or very small populations of light chain–restricted B cells in follicular hyperplasia or other reactive conditions such as Castleman disease. A study comparing flow cytometry of FNAB samples and excision biopsy samples from benign lymph nodes has shown overlapping patterns, including outliers such as skewed CD4:CD8 ratios, CD7 loss, and expansion of T/NK-antigen–expressing subsets that may be present in a variety of conditions, including smoking, immunodeficiencies, and infections {30194715}. The above findings confirm flow cytometry as a robust ancillary technique in diagnosing follicular hyperplasia when integrated with cytomorphology assessment and clinical picture.
