1.1.1.2 The role of lymph node, spleen, and thymus cytopathology

The role of cytopathology in the diagnosis of lymph node [[Zeppa P, Cozzolino I. Lymph node FNC: cytopathology of lymph nodes and extranodal lymphoproliferative processes. Basel (Switzerland): Karger Publishers; 2018. (Monographs in clinical cytology series).]], spleen, and thymus lesions is to evaluate lymphadenopathy or a mass lesion in the thymus or spleen. FNAB of lymphadenopathies can be performed by palpation, which has been standard practice over a long period with great success. But wherever possible, lymph node FNAB should be performed after ultrasound assessment of the lymph nodes, with ultrasound guidance when superficial or endoscopic ultrasound guidance when deep-seated: for example, with endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) for abdominal/retroperitoneal spaces or with endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) for mediastinal and lung lesions. For splenic and thymic lesions, ultrasound or in some cases CT is required to guide the FNAB.

The advantages of FNAB are the minimally invasive nature of the procedure, patient acceptance, the rapid turnaround time, the low cost, and the rarity of complications. The importance of ROSE cannot be overemphasized. ROSE significantly reduces the number of insufficient specimens and facilitates the cost-effective and efficient triage of the specimen according to the different infectious, reactive, or potentially neoplastic lesions observed; it also ensures provision of material for the most appropriate ancillary studies {29989244}.

The primary differential diagnoses of lymphadenopathy and masses in the thymus or spleen include reactive hyperplasia, infection, metastatic carcinoma, and lymphoma. Other considerations in the thymus include primary thymic carcinoma. Consideration of the clinical and imaging presentation before the FNAB is performed is required to start the diagnostic process, including the patient’s age, possible exposures to infection, the extent of lymphadenopathy, and the lesion size.

The key first role of cytopathology is to confirm the clinical assessment and expectation of a benign process, such as a nonspecific reactive hyperplasia in adults presenting with 10–20 mm enlarged lymph nodes. The lymph node may be larger and persistent in children or young adults. The presence of an abundant and polymorphous population of lymphoid cells, plentiful tingible-body macrophages, and dendritic cells is highly suggestive of reactive hyperplasia, whereas a prominence of immunoblasts in a background of small lymphocytes can suggest a viral reaction. However, caution is recommended in elderly patients with lymph nodes larger than 20 mm, or in patients of any age with multiple and/or deep-seated lymphadenopathies. Depending on local resources, clonality assessment by flow cytometry or immunocytochemistry of cell block material, direct smears, or cytospins is recommended in cases where the cell types are less typical of a reactive pattern to exclude a lymphoma, particularly follicular lymphoma {25611108}.

The second role of cytopathology is to identify granulomatous patterns such as those observed in tuberculosis or sarcoidosis. Integration of clinical data and cytomorphology with special stains and microbiological tests is tremendously helpful to confirm or characterize a diagnosis of a specific bacterial or fungal lymphadenitis, cat-scratch disease, mycobacterial lymphadenitis, Rosai–Dorfman disease, Kikuchi lymphadenitis, and viral-induced lymphadenopathy, as well as dermatopathic lymphadenopathy and lymphadenopathy related to silicone in the appropriate clinical setting.

The third crucial role of cytopathology is to diagnose metastases from carcinomas, melanomas, germ cell tumours, and (rarely) sarcomas. Carcinomas are the most frequent metastatic tumours to lymph nodes, followed by melanomas. Diagnosis of a lymph node metastasis based on cytopathology is usually straightforward, but establishing the specific primary tumour and the site of origin may be challenging. In some cases, cytopathology can be suggestive and concordant with a known primary tumour of the head and neck, breast, or lung, or of the reproductive, gastrointestinal, or genitourinary tracts. In cases of poorly differentiated tumours or where there is no known primary tumour, immunocytochemistry is required and can then provide prognostic indicators. Molecular tests are increasingly used to identify specific targets for therapy. For example, the diagnosis of a primary lung carcinoma as adenocarcinoma by cytopathology and immunocytochemistry on a cell block then requires molecular testing for known genetic targets to enable “precision medicine”. Further examples include the first presentation in a lymph node of metastatic melanoma in any site, or cervical lymph nodes that contain metastases from HPV-associated head and neck squamous cell carcinomas.

The fourth role of cytopathology is the diagnosis of lymphoma. Although the cytopathological diagnosis of a recurrent or transformed lymphoma may be straightforward and accepted by clinicians and oncologists, the utility of FNAB as a primary and sole procedure for the primary diagnosis of lymphoma is still a matter of debate. FNAB is able to suggest and in many cases recognize lymphoma based on cytopathological criteria and categorize such cases as “Suspicious for malignancy” or “Malignant”, but it may not be able to precisely diagnose the type of lymphoma due to cytomorphological overlaps and the lack of architecture. The use of ancillary testing such as flow cytometry and cytogenetics based on FNAB has radically increased the utility of FNAB in this setting.

The development of the WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology (WHO System) for the first time provides an international system for diagnosing and reporting FNAB of lymph node and related organ cytopathology, to match those developed for the cervix, thyroid, salivary gland, urine, breast, and effusions. Cytopathologists should be aware of and should use the current WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]], which correlates with the WHO System, and should also be aware of the diagnostic potential, as well as the limitations and potential pitfalls of FNAB. Cytopathologists reporting FNAB of lymph node, spleen, and thymus should work closely with haematopathologists and clinicians to maximize the synergies between the two complementary techniques of FNAB cytopathology and surgical pathology, along with their related ancillary tests. Ancillary techniques are crucial in both FNAB cytopathology and CNB, as well as in surgical excision biopsies. These include immunocytochemistry on cell blocks; clonality assessment and assessment of specific CD markers by flow cytometry; and PCR, ISH, and other molecular tests to look for specific somatic genetic rearrangements {30156771}. Cytopathology alone cannot diagnose all lymphomas, which can have overlapping features and require specific flow cytometry and genetic diagnostic testing, and this is also a problem for CNB and even excision surgical biopsies, because many lymphomas now require a specific ancillary test result for their diagnosis. This is particularly important in this era of precision medicine {31004534}.

FNAB and other cytopathological samples, such as fluids, can be used for the full range of ancillary testing that can be applied to histopathological material, and they are well suited for routine immunocytochemistry, flow cytometry, and molecular methods. Immunocytochemistry can be applied to cell blocks, direct smears, and liquid-based cytopathology. The rinsings of the needle and separate passes provide excellent material in suspension for flow cytometry to characterize lymphoid cells {32454496}. The collection and triage of cytopathological samples is more operator-dependent than taking a CNB or particularly a surgical excision biopsy, but this operator dependency is compensated for by the markedly decreased degree of invasiveness, cost, and time required for the procedure. Many diagnoses are obvious to a cytopathologist even at ROSE, or within a 24-hour period when all the standard smears and stains have been assessed. The specific diagnosis can be refined in ancillary studies depending on the differential diagnoses, and the ancillary tests can then be reported in one integrated cytopathology report.

In conclusion, cytopathologists interested in FNAB of lymph nodes, spleen, and thymus have a pivotal role in integrating clinical information and cytopathology findings and in selecting appropriate cost-effective and diagnostic additional investigations. A cytopathologist able to perform ROSE and in many cases deliver a preliminary diagnosis optimizes the FNAB diagnostic potential by reducing the rate of insufficient specimens and improving the triage of additional material and selection of ancillary testing. The recommended first-line approach in assessing a superficial lymph node is for an interventional cytopathologist to perform the FNAB, provide ROSE, and triage the material for ancillary testing, and where required facilitate a CNB. This approach provides the most expert FNAB, maximizes the diagnostic potential of the FNAB and the material it provides, avoids delays, and reduces the costs and potential invasiveness of a surgical excision, to the benefit of patients. Thymus, spleen, and deep-seated lymphadenopathies require specialist proceduralists and imaging guidance, ideally with ROSE at the FNAB.
