8.6.8.2 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Definition

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a clonal neoplasm of myeloid dendritic cells that have a Langerhans cell phenotype of CD1a and CD207 positivity and lack high-grade cytopathological features.

Clinical features and imaging

LCH can be differentiated from Langerhans cell sarcoma (LCS), which is the aggressive malignant counterpart with the same Langerhans cell immunophenotype of CD1a and CD207 positivity with high-grade cytopathological features.

LCH is characterized by a spectrum of clinical manifestations related to the wide age range, various affected sites, and unpredictable clinical course. LCH is predominantly a disease of childhood, and it can be localized to one organ system, termed single-system LCH (SS-LCH), or multiple sites, as the disseminated multisystem LCH (MS-LCH).

Solitary lesions in SS-LCH most commonly involve bone and soft tissue, followed by lymph nodes, skin, and lung, and they affect older children and adults. Multifocal SS-LCH presents in younger children, with multiple destructive bone lesions, especially in skull and mandible. MS-LCH affects infants, and it manifests with fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, and skin and bone lesions, with a considerably high mortality rate.

Staging of the disease is essential for management, and imaging including CT, MRI, and PET can reveal the extent of destructive bony lesions and multisystem involvement {14556926; 23109216; 15770639}.

LCH has been described in association with other haematolymphoid malignancies, and a clonal relationship has been suggested between LCH and precursor T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma in cases where LCH cells and T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma cells show identical rearrangement of the T-cell receptor gene {15925822}. LCH neoplasms associated with follicular lymphoma have also been reported, although the pathobiology remains poorly understood {33676171}.

LCS represents the aggressive malignant counterpart of LCH, presenting in the majority of cases as extranodal lesions predominantly involving skin and bone, with multiorgan involvement frequently in high-stage disease [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Histopathology

The essential histopathological feature for the diagnosis is the presence of the LCH cells, with their nuclear grooves and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, in a background of variable numbers of small lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells, and eosinophils. Eosinophils can be prominent and produce microabscesses containing Charcot–Leyden crystals. Osteoclast-type giant cells can also be present and numerous in bone and soft tissue lesions. LCH cells infiltrate lymph nodes with a typical sinus pattern particularly evident in the early phases. As the disease progresses, there is infiltration of the paracortex and complete replacement of the architecture. Mitotic figures are infrequent. In some cases of disseminated disease, haemophagocytosis can be seen in LCH cells.

LCS is characterized by pleomorphism and high proliferation fraction [[Jaffe ES, Arber DA, Campo E, et al., editors. Hematopathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2016.]].

Large clusters of LCH cells together with numerous eosinophils can be seen in lymph nodes involved by lymphomas, sarcomas, and other malignancies; however, in most cases these are polyclonal and better regarded as Langerhans cell hyperplasia {16360413}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate to high cellularity Neoplastic LCH cells in a mixed background LCH cells are large cells (10–15 μm) with low N:C ratios and abundant cytoplasm. LCH cells are characterized by irregular “coffee bean–like” nuclei with indentations, folds, and longitudinal grooves with fine chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli (Pap stain). Background includes abundant eosinophils, which are often the predominant cell type, which explains the former term of “eosinophilic granuloma”, and these tend to form aggregates, with small lymphocytes, neutrophils, histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and fibroblasts in the background. Charcot–Leyden crystals can be present. Occasionally necrosis In late-stage old lesions, only foamy histiocytes and fibroblasts can be seen with sparse LCH cells.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Although a rare disease in lymph nodes, LCH is seen more commonly in FNAB services in paediatric institutions. The presence of an eosinophil-rich background requires the exclusion of other neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions showing similar features, such as Kimura disease, drug-induced reactive lymphadenopathies, nodal involvement by Churg–Strauss syndrome (eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis), T-cell lymphomas, Hodgkin lymphoma, and myeloid sarcoma with maturation {26608102}.

Necrotizing and non-necrotizing granulomatous diseases, such as tuberculosis and sarcoidosis, and necrotizing histiocytic lymphadenitis or Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease should also be excluded {15613235}. In Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, an eosinophilic and neutrophilic background is not prominent, and characteristic “crescentic” histiocytes with irregular crescentic nuclei and eccentric cytoplasm and scattered plasmacytoid dendritic cells are seen and are usually detectable by immunocytochemistry. Other differential diagnoses include non-neoplastic Langerhans dendritic cell proliferations, non-neoplastic histiocytic granulomatous lesions, rare histiocytic dendritic cell neoplasms, and metastatic disease. Dermatopathic lymphadenopathy contains, especially in the late stages, large numbers of skin-derived non-neoplastic Langerhans cells, which tend to be elongated and have nuclear grooves, ridges, and indentations, and dendritic cells, as well as melanin-laden histiocytes forming loose clusters in the so-called “lipomelanotic reticulosis”. The characteristic nuclear grooves, nuclear ridges, and indentations are also observed. Despite LCH and dermatopathic lymphadenopathy being morphologically distinct entities, they can occasionally be indistinguishable in FNAB samples, and ancillary testing is required {9850640}.

Benign and malignant histiocytic neoplasms including Erdheim–Chester disease, Rosai–Dorfman disease, disseminated juvenile xanthogranuloma, histiocytic sarcoma, and follicular dendritic cell sarcoma must be considered in cellular samples with numerous atypical histiocytes and spindle cells in an inflammatory background. Nuclear grooves with “coffee-bean” appearances can be present in metastatic papillary carcinoma of thyroid and metastatic amelanotic melanoma with bland cytopathological features. Clinical details and ancillary testing are essential in the differential diagnosis.

Ancillary testing

Immunocytochemistry using cell blocks or cell-transfer methods is indispensable for a correct cytopathological diagnosis of LCH. LCH cells are positive for S100, CD1a, and CD207 (langerin), which is strongly correlated with the presence of Birbeck granules {14610287}. In addition, CD4, CD68, and HLA-DR are positive; CD45 is often weakly or partially positive; and T-cell and B-cell markers and follicular dendritic cell markers CD21, CD23, and CD35 are usually negative. The plasmacytoid cell markers CD138, CD123, and/or CD303 are useful in the differential diagnosis with Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, where these markers can be present in a large number of cells.

Flow cytometry using CD1a and CD207 has also been described {28847997}, but monoclonality of the LCH cells cannot be demonstrated by flow cytometry, and a multidisciplinary integrated approach is recommended. More recently, mutations of MAPK pathway genes, including BRAF and MAP2K1, have been reported. Immunocytochemistry for the BRAF p.V600E protein can be useful to confirm the diagnosis and facilitate the treatment of BRAF-mutant LCH with BRAF inhibitors {30157397; 28219109}.

Although no longer required for the diagnosis, electron microscopy on FNAB material can demonstrate the “tennis-racket” shaped Birbeck granules, which are a highly characteristic ultrastructural hallmark of LCH.
