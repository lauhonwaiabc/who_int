5.5.2.5 Prominent necrosis

Definition

Prominent necrosis is defined as the presence of necrosis with only scant to moderate numbers of lymphoid and inflammatory cells present.

Clinical features and imaging

The causes of granular necrosis in a lymph node FNAB include infections, particularly mycobacterial and fungal infections; nodal infarction; and autoimmune idiopathic inflammatory diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Kikuchi lymphadenitis, and Kawasaki disease. Necrosis may also occur in metastases to lymph nodes, most commonly metastatic keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma. This section focuses on the non-infectious and non-neoplastic necrosis reaction patterns due to extensive apoptotic cell death with a minor inflammatory cell component.

The autoimmune-associated necrosis lesions are characterized by the occurrence of enlarged lymph nodes accompanied by systemic symptoms of fever, arthralgia, rash, and malaise, as well as involvement of other organs such as kidney, heart, and skin, with or without vasculitis {29208331}. The lymphadenopathy may be generalized, as in SLE, or localized, as in Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD) necrotizing lymphadenitis. Lymphadenopathy occurs in the course of SLE in up to 75% of patients; however, it is only rarely a presenting symptom {19107085}.

KFD is a self-limiting disorder characterized by lymphadenopathy with a paracortical proliferation of immunoblasts, infiltration by histiocytes with characteristic nuclear features, and apoptosis or necrosis with abundant nuclear debris. KFD is considered a prominent T-cell–mediated immune reaction. It most commonly affects the cervical lymph nodes, usually unilaterally, of young women and is usually self-limiting, typically subsiding within 6 months; it is more frequent in Asian populations {30407860}. In the paediatric age group, KFD affects more boys than girls. Up to 21% of patients have recurrences, which may be multinodal and associated with underlying autoimmune diseases, and rare fatalities do occur due to pulmonary haemorrhage, haemophagocytic syndrome, and cardiac failure {19208493; 28255545}.

Kawasaki disease mostly affects children and is characterized by vasculitis, particularly of coronary arteries, with accompanying lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms {34523885}.

Histopathology

The necrotic patterns of reactive benign lymphadenopathy of SLE, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, and Kawasaki disease may show microscopically overlapping features. Depending on the stage of the disease, the extent of the necrosis varies, being especially prominent in more advanced stages {17540509}. Partial architectural effacement is present, with paracortical histiocyte-rich areas containing abundant karyorrhectic nuclear debris. Necrotic areas in Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease typically do not contain neutrophils or numerous plasma cells and tend to be focal {19941366; 21953815; 30407860}. More numerous plasma cells are present in SLE lymphadenopathy at the periphery of the geographical necrotic areas {17540509}. Necrotic areas in Kawasaki disease may contain neutrophils and typically show microthrombi with vasculitic change adjacent to the necrotic areas {23071864; 23020240}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Apoptotic debris of the prominent necrosis pattern of the reactive lymphadenopathy should not be confused with amyloid deposits, which have irregular “fluffy” margins to the dense amorphous material, which is pale grey-green (Pap) or mauve (Giemsa) and associated with lymphoid cells. Coagulative necrosis can be observed in infarcted lymph nodes due to infection, trauma, or vasculitis / vascular disease with thrombosis, with a variable amount of shadowy necrotic cellular lymphoid material. The patient’s history and associated diseases are essential for correct assessment. Necrosis can be associated with CMV, EBV, and herpes simplex viral infections; cat-scratch disease; and most commonly mycobacterial or fungal infection, which must be excluded with appropriate stains, cultures, and PCR in almost every case {22821115}. In patients with compromised immunity, the necrosis in mycobacterial infection can be extensive {22821115}. The most important differential diagnosis is extensively necrotic metastatic carcinoma or melanoma or necrotic and apoptotic large cell lymphoma {22846456}.

The necrotic debris of Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease is accompanied by fewer plasma cells than in SLE lymphadenopathy {17916091; 21953815; 11726124; 10667163; 11599104; 17540509}, while haematoxyphilic SLE bodies can be observed in SLE lymphadenitis. Neutrophils do not occur in Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, while they are present in SLE and Kawasaki disease lymphadenopathy {23071864; 23020240}.

Correlation with the patient’s age and sex, the symptoms, the location of the affected lymph nodes, the laboratory findings, and the history of a chronic disease and medication is crucial for interpretation of the necrotic pattern in lymph node FNAB.

Ancillary testing

Because patients with autoimmune diseases are often treated with immunosuppressive medication, opportunistic infections with agents such as mycobacteria and fungi must be excluded in the lymph node FNAB showing prominent necrosis by special stains, particularly the Ziehl–Neelsen stain or similar acid-fast stain and the Warthin–Starry, PASD, and Grocott methenamine silver stains, as well as by PCR or culture {22821115}. Immunocytochemistry for epithelial, melanocytic, and lymphatic markers may help rule out an extensively necrotic carcinoma or melanoma metastasis, as well as necrotic infiltration by an aggressive lymphoma. Flow cytometry may not be helpful in necrotic samples due to technical problems caused by necrotic debris.
