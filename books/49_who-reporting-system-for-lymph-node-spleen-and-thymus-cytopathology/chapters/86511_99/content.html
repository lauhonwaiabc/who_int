8.6.5.11 Anaplastic large cell lymphoma

Definition

Anaplastic large cell lymphomas (ALCLs) are a group of mature systemic T-cell neoplasms that share a cytomorphology composed of usually large cells with abundant cytoplasm and pleomorphic, often horseshoe-shaped nuclei and uniform strong CD30 expression; the group includes two lymphomas distinguished by the presence or absence of ALK expression.

Clinical features and imaging

ALCL is an uncommon type of T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma that represents about 2% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas in adults and 10–20% in children {29617304; 33152703; 34111312}. ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCLs are now considered two separate entities due to important clinical and genetic differences. Definitive diagnosis of the two lymphomas cannot be established on FNAB cytopathology alone, and ancillary testing is required.

ALK-positive ALCL is a CD30-positive mature T-cell lymphoma with aberrant expression of ALK secondary to rearrangements of the ALK gene. It is more common in the first three decades of life, with a male predominance, and it frequently involves lymph nodes and extranodal sites. The most common extranodal sites are skin, bones, soft tissue, lungs, and liver. Most patients present with advanced-stage disease, including peripheral and/or abdominal lymphadenopathy associated with extranodal involvement. B-type symptoms are common {9808552; 10194450; 25869285; 11090048}.

ALK-negative ALCL is a mature T-cell lymphoma with uniform strong expression of CD30 without ALK expression or ALK rearrangement. It occurs between the ages of 40 and 65 years, with a slight male predominance. ALK-negative ALCL is more common in peripheral and/or abdominal lymph nodes, with extranodal involvement being less common {24128129; 10845893; 14636272}.

Histopathology

The histopathological findings of ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCLs are very similar. ALCL may diffusely replace tissues and in lymph nodes shows a prominent intrasinusoidal growth pattern. These neoplasms often have a cohesive appearance and are characterized by the presence of so-called “hallmark cells”, which have horseshoe or kidney-shaped nuclei, often with an eosinophilic cytoplasmic region near the nucleus known as a hof, and often with eccentric cytoplasm. Pseudonuclear inclusions may be seen, conferring a doughnut-like appearance. Several patterns of ALK-positive ALCL have been described, including the lymphohistiocytic, small cell, and Hodgkin-like patterns. More than one pattern can be seen in the same case, and the lymphohistiocytic and small cell patterns commonly coexist. In the lymphohistiocytic subtype, numerous small lymphocytes and histiocytes form the predominant populations while neoplastic cells are relatively few and usually surround blood vessels, with few hallmark cells. The small cell subtype is composed of a predominant population of small neoplastic cells, and hallmark cells may be absent {9490693; 9736036; 9094977; 16434897}. ALK-negative ALCL cases exhibit the Hodgkin-like patterns, and lymphohistiocytic and small cell subtypes of ALK-negative ALCL are not recognized {24128129; 7655022}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of ALK-positive ALCL includes haematopoietic neoplasms such as ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma, classic Hodgkin lymphoma, ALK-positive histiocytosis, and histiocytic sarcoma {29403168; 26183330; 33686426; 17511113}, as well as non-haematopoietic mesenchymal neoplasms that express ALK, such as inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour and rhabdomyosarcoma.

The cytomorphology of the ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCL cases overlaps substantially. ALK-negative ALCL may be characterized by a greater number of anaplastic cells with severe pleomorphism and anaplasia than ALK-positive ALCL. The ALK-negative ALCL entity must be distinguished from peripheral T-cell lymphoma and Hodgkin lymphoma. The differential diagnosis between ALK-negative ALCL and Hodgkin lymphoma is based on the presence of hallmark cells in a background of smaller atypical lymphoid cells and a mix of inflammatory cells in ALCL, and the presence of Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells in a background of small lymphocytes with a lesser number of eosinophils and plasma cells in Hodgkin lymphoma, but immunocytochemistry is very useful and is required for a specific diagnosis. The differential diagnosis with CD30-positive peripheral T-cell lymphoma, based on cytomorphological criteria and strong and uniform expression of CD30, is more complex. The differential diagnosis also includes germ cell tumours, anaplastic carcinoma, and melanoma. Hallmark cells are helpful to support an ALCL diagnosis {8693905; 32491282; 17511113}.

Ancillary testing

The neoplastic cells in both ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCLs strongly and uniformly express CD30 in a membranous and perinuclear (Golgi-like) pattern. Flow cytometry and immunocytochemistry are useful for diagnosis {11090048; 9490693; 9736036; 8916967; 3876124; 2827494; 9028330; 18385450; 25873174; 19123736}. Most cases are positive for one or more T-cell antigens, but CD3 and CD5 are often negative {11090048; 9490693; 9736036; 8916967; 3876124; 2827494; 9028330; 18385450; 25873174; 19123736}. Most cases (80%) express CD4, with the remainder being CD8-positive. Patients with ALK-positive and CD8-positive ALCL have a higher relapse rate, but CD8 expression does not correlate with overall patient survival or progression-free survival {32032618}.

ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCLs are also positive for EMA, clusterin, and CD25, as well as the cytotoxic markers TIA1, granzyme B, and/or perforin. Expression of CD45 and CD45RO is variable. PAX5 is almost always absent. CD15 is usually absent in ALK-positive ALCL but can be positive in ALK-negative cases. STAT3 is often positive in ALCL. BCL2 is rarely expressed in ALK-positive cases but can be positive in ALK-negative ALCL {11485911}. EBV infection is uncommon in ALCL in developed nations {15116326}.

In ALK-positive ALCL cases, ALK expression is constitutively upregulated, and the pattern of ALK expression shown by immunocytochemistry correlates, in part, with the ALK partner (#29077Table 7.08). t(2;5)(p23;q35), involving ALK at chromosome 2p23 and NPM1, is the most frequent rearrangement found in ALCL, seen in about 80–85% of cases, and ALK is expressed in a nuclear and cytoplasmic pattern. Other translocations (e.g. the TPM3::ALK translocation) show only a diffuse cytoplasmic ALK expression with a peripheral intensification {11781220}. The rare CLTC::ALK and MSN::ALK translocations result in a granular cytoplasmic and a membranous pattern of ALK expression, respectively {12401829; 8547653; 2156548}.

ALK translocations can be detected by FISH using a break-apart probe or using PCR or RT-PCR methods. However, immunocytochemistry for ALK is very convenient and adequate for routine practice, as ALK is not expressed by benign lymphoid cells. Conventional cytogenetic analysis showing translocations involving chromosome 2p23 is presumptive evidence of an ALK translocation. Conventional cytogenetic analysis often shows a complex karyotype with multiple numerical and structural changes {27468968}.

Molecular analysis of both ALK-positive and ALK-negative ALCLs often shows clonal TRB and TRG rearrangements. All cases of ALCL, through different mechanisms, activate the JAK/STAT, NOTCH, and PI3K cell pathways. At the molecular level, ALK-negative ALCL is a heterogeneous entity with at least three subsets: cases with DUSP22 rearrangements, cases with TP63 rearrangement, and cases that lack both. Cases of ALK-negative ALCL with DUSP22::IRF4 tend to lack large pleomorphic cells or have predominantly so-called doughnut cells {26379151}. Patients with ALK-negative ALCL associated with DUSP22 rearrangement have a 5-year overall survival rate of 85%. In contrast, patients with ALK-negative ALCL with TP63 rearrangement have a 5-year overall survival rate of only 19% {30719810}. The absence of cytotoxic markers, such as TIA1, granzyme B, and/or perforin, should raise the possibility of DUSP22 rearrangement in ALK-negative ALCL.
