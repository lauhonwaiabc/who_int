8.6.4.20 Thymic B-cell lymphomas

Definition

Primary thymic B-cell lymphomas involve the anterior mediastinum; the most common is primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL), which is an aggressive large B-cell lymphoma with distinctive clinicopathological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features, thought to arise from thymic medullary B cells.

Clinical features and imaging

The most common thymic B-cell lymphomas, in decreasing order of frequency, are PMBCL, classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL), mediastinal grey zone lymphoma (MGZL), and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT). This section focuses on PMBCL and its differential diagnosis with these lymphomas, as the other diseases and systemic lymphomas involving the thymus, the commonest of which is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS), are discussed elsewhere in this volume.

PMBCL is a mature aggressive B-cell lymphoma of putative thymic B-cell origin, arising in the anterior mediastinum. It represents 2–3% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas {34398178; 34330673; 34496020}. Familial clustering of PMBCL cases has been reported based on shared genetic mutations {23457195}. PMBCL occurs predominantly in young adults in their third decade, with a female predominance of 2:1, but it can also occur in children. Patients commonly present with cough, dyspnoea, and/or dysphagia, and many patients have bulky anterior mediastinal masses that can be associated with the superior vena cava syndrome. B-type symptoms may occur, and serum LDH levels are elevated in most patients {34398178; 34496020}. PMBCLs are localized primarily to the anterior mediastinum but can variably invade the lungs, pleurae, pericardium, or regional lymph nodes (most often the supraclavicular lymph nodes) {10029446; 31567132; 34398178}. Most patients have localized stage I or II disease at the time of initial diagnosis. Involvement of bone marrow and other extranodal sites, including the CNS, liver, kidneys, or adrenal glands, is more common at the time of relapse {34330673; 34496020}. The cytopathological features presented below relate to PMBCL.

MGZL is a B-cell lymphoma with overlapping clinical, morphological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features between PMBCL and CHL, particularly nodular sclerosis CHL. It represents < 1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas {20971819; 27758822; 30540571}. The presenting symptoms and imaging findings of MGZL are similar to those of PMBCL.

Histopathology

PMBCL has a diffuse pattern and is composed of medium to large lymphoma cells, with abundant pale cytoplasm and round to oval nuclei with small nucleoli, arranged in sheets within a variably fibrotic background {10029446; 20971819; 31567132; 34398178}. A subset of anaplastic cells can be present, including cells resembling Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg (HRS) cells, and retraction artefact can impart a lacunar cell–like appearance. The fibrosis can be fine and reticular, surrounding groups of lymphoma cells resembling alveoli, or collagenous and coarse. Necrosis can be prominent. Variable numbers of reactive lymphocytes and/or histiocytes can be present, and remnants of thymic epithelium are observed in occasional cases.

In histopathology, MGZL exhibits a morphological spectrum, from cases that superficially resemble CHL with plentiful Hodgkin and HRS-like cells to cases that more closely resemble PMBCL.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB is a screening method to establish the diagnosis of a large cell haematopoietic neoplasm in the mediastinum, thus excluding non-haematopoietic neoplasms including thymomas {11842377; 15880713}. B-cell lineage can be established using ancillary methods, but it can be highly challenging to distinguish PMBCL from systemic DLBCL-NOS involving the mediastinum on the basis of cytomorphological and immunophenotypic findings alone. Clinical context and staging studies are helpful for diagnosis.

MGZL is a B-cell neoplasm with mixed features of DLBCL-NOS and CHL {27758822; 29296913; 30540571}. It is highly challenging to recognize MGZL in FNAB {32418255}, even with extensive immunocytochemistry. It should be noted that the reproducibility of the diagnosis of MGZL is poor among experts using surgical biopsy specimens {29296913}.

CHL involving the thymus is most often of nodular sclerosis type {27441757}, and it can be associated with prominent cystic change. In CHL there is a variable number of HRS-like cells associated with a heterogeneous, predominantly lymphocytic background that usually includes eosinophils.

Extranodal MALT lymphomas are rare in the thymus {34605949}. These neoplasms are composed of small lymphoid cells that can be small and round or have irregular centrocyte-like nuclei or exhibit monocytoid, abundant pale cytoplasm or plasmacytic differentiation. The diagnosis relies on ancillary testing including flow cytometry.

Ancillary testing

Flow cytometry immunophenotyping is helpful for establishing the diagnosis of PMBCL, which has a well-developed B-cell immunophenotype, uniformly positive for CD19, CD20, CD22, and CD79a, but usually negative for surface or cytoplasmic immunoglobulin, HLA-DR, and T-cell antigens {28732145; 34398178; 34878224}. PMBCL cases are usually positive for CD45/LCA, and 70–80% are positive for CD23, CD30, and CD200. CD10 is positive in 20–30% of cases.

Immunocytochemistry using smears and particularly cell blocks is helpful. CD30 is often heterogeneously or weakly expressed in PMBCL, unlike in CHL. PMBCL is also usually positive for PAX5 (strong), OCT2, PU.1, BOB1, and MUM1/IRF4 (strong). PMBCL cases are often positive for CD23, CD200, MAL, and PDL1/L2; variably positive for BCL2 and BCL6; and usually negative for CD10, CD15, and EBV.

PMBCL carries clonal immunoglobulin gene rearrangements {34398178}. MYC and BCL6 are rarely rearranged, and BCL2 is not rearranged. Gene expression profiling and other molecular methods have shown that PMBCL cases are characterized by activation of the NF-κB and JAK/STAT pathways, and by activation of mechanisms to evade host immunosurveillance, including the PDL1/L2 pathway {26549456; 26608593; 34330673}. A number of molecular changes involved in pathway activation have been described, including CIITA rearrangements, copy-number gains of CD274 (PDL1) and PDCD1LG2 (PDL2), and mutations of SOCS1 and STAT6 {20628145; 24497532; 26549456; 30257882; 31697821}. Immunocytochemistry can be used to infer the presence of pathway activation. PMBCL cases are commonly positive for TRAF1 and nuclear c-REL, indicating NF-κB pathway activation {17197926}. Expression of pSTAT6 and PDL1 and/or PDL2 suggests JAK/STAT and PDL1/L2 pathway activation, respectively {34330673; 34398178}. In general, testing for these pathway abnormalities is not required in routine practice.

For the diagnosis of MGZL, importantly, there is a mismatch between the morphological findings and immunophenotype. Cases that resemble CHL are often positive for CD45/LCA and strongly positive for B-cell markers such as CD20, CD79a, PAX5, and OCT2. These neoplasms often express CD30 and MUM1/IRF4, but they are usually negative for CD15. Cases that morphologically resemble PMBCL are usually positive for CD30 and MUM1/IRF4, are often positive for CD15 (~50%), and are variably positive or negative for B-cell markers and CD45/LCA. EBV is usually negative.

Immunocytochemistry is best performed on a cell block and is helpful in the diagnosis of CHL, as HRS cells are positive for CD15 (~70%), CD30 (strong and diffuse), PAX5 (weak), and MUM1/IRF4. HRS cells are negative for CD45/LCA, CD19, and CD22 {20971819}. EBV is present in 20–30% of cases. HRS cells are usually negative for CD20 and CD79a, but these markers can be variably expressed in about 20% and 5–10% of cases, respectively. OCT2 and BOB1 are often negative in CHL.

Extranodal MALT lymphomas are positive for monocytic surface light chain and pan–B-cell antigens, and negative for CD5 and CD10. The proliferation rate, as assessed by Ki-67, is low {34605949}.
