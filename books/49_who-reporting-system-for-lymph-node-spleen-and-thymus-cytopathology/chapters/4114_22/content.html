4.1.1.4 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

The ROM for this category has been reported in a recent review {39097796} of 10 studies based on the similar category in the Sydney System as between 17% and 67% {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657}, attributable to a high percentage of samples consisting of haemorrhagic or cystic material, or containing exclusively necrotic debris, features often associated with malignancy. The percentage of cases categorized as “Insufficient/Inadequate” varied from 0.6% to 9.8% in these 10 studies, which in part related to the use of ROSE {39097796}. A recent meta-analysis based on 9 of these articles showed a pooled ROM of 34% {38096796}. In the setting of endobronchial ultrasound–guided FNAB (EBUS-FNAB), unsatisfactory or non-diagnostic results are reported in 7–27% of cases, with a ROM of up to 30–42% {31051765; 25186645}. However, these figures are biased and limited to studies with histopathological confirmation, most often in lymph nodes showing suspicious clinical and/or imaging features.

Given the above limitations, if “Inadequate/Insufficient” specimens are defined as being quantitatively or qualitatively unsuitable for reliable interpretation, then the ROM is, by definition, undefined. These specimens should never be reported as “Negative for malignancy”.

The rapid turnaround time of FNAB, particularly with ROSE, allows appropriate triage following an “Insufficient/Inadequate” categorization on a lymph node FNAB. Management then depends on the local setting, the cause of the inadequate sample, the clinical setting, and correlation with the imaging. If the target lymph node is clearly abnormal, further investigation is required, and specific actions based on the integration of clinical and imaging findings and other laboratory investigations should be suggested rather than an immediate reflex recommendation of “Repeat FNAB” or “Advise biopsy”. If the pretest probability of malignancy based on history, imaging characteristics, and other laboratory investigations is low, no further action other than appropriate follow-up may be required. Repeat FNAB should be recommended whenever there is a distinct clinical or imaging suspicion of malignancy, and this may include a different technique, such as image guidance or with ROSE and dedicated sampling for ancillary studies. Dependent on the differential diagnosis, a CNB or excisional biopsy may be suggested.

As part of the quality management programme of the cytopathology laboratory, there should be regular audits of the rates of “Inadequate” lymph node, spleen, and thymus FNAB. Feedback, including suggestions for modification of technique, should be given to practitioners persistently submitting inadequate samples. Acceptable rates for inadequate samples must be decided on an individual laboratory basis.
