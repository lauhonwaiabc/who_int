5.1.1.4 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

Management for this group of benign lymphadenopathies is generally conservative (i.e. non-surgical). Excisional biopsy is recommended for cases where cytopathological features are in disagreement with clinical and imaging findings and laboratory tests, including for patients with high white blood cell count, ESR, and LDH levels.

The specificity and negative predictive values (NPVs) reported in the literature for lymph node FNAB series range from 83% to 99% and 45% to 93%, respectively {20132132; 22431318}. The expected ROM for this group of benign lymphadenopathies is low, and a recent review of 10 retrospective and heterogeneous studies on the similar Sydney System for reporting lymph nodes {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657} showed a ROM ranging from 2% to 16% {39097796}. A recent meta-analysis that pooled the ROM of nine of these studies found a ROM of 5% {38096796}. The use of ancillary techniques, particularly immunoprofiling applied to direct smears, cytospins, liquid-based cytopathology, and cell block sections with different fixatives such as methanol, alcohol, and formalin, can influence the diagnostic yield.

Lymphadenopathies diagnosed as reactive and benign by FNAB that match the clinical context should be followed up clinically by physical examination, imaging, or a combination of both, as well as by laboratory tests to monitor the lesions’ clinical stability. These laboratory tests include, for example, complete blood count, ESR, LDH level, and antinuclear antibody; serology for infections including CMV, syphilis, infectious mononucleosis, and toxoplasmosis; and tuberculosis skin test {32454496}. Patients may receive antibiotic therapy if an infection is suspected. Any changes in clinical or imaging findings, or strong concern for a lymphoproliferative disorder, such as due to accompanying type B symptoms or a persistent and enlarging hard mass > 20 mm, should result in either a repeat FNAB with flow cytometry or a recommendation for CNB or excisional biopsy {24695506}). CT and PET can be helpful, especially in lymphoma follow-up patients. Procedurally, if the first FNAB was performed by palpation, it is advisable to repeat the FNAB with ultrasound guidance to further ensure the targeted lymph node is adequately sampled. In the case of an emergency, such as superior vena cava syndrome or spinal cord compression, patients may receive steroids and undergo possible surgical resection or receive radiation of symptomatic unifocal nodal or extranodal disease based on the FNAB, preferably with ROSE. Specific lymphadenopathies, for example Rosai–Dorfman disease, that continue or are refractory to the aforementioned therapies may warrant more aggressive diagnostic management.
