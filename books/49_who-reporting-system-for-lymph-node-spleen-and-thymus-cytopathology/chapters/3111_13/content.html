3.1.1.1 Introduction: The role of ancillary testing

While microscopy is the cornerstone of assessment of cytopathological samples, there is increasing application of ancillary techniques {30156771}. Increasingly, lymphomas and other malignancies are defined by a molecular pathology feature, rather than (or in correlation with) cytopathological and histopathological features [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]], and recent guidelines on lymphoma diagnosis recommend that ancillary testing should be performed on cytopathology samples {33219376}. This chapter covers the roles of flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, FISH, and molecular testing. It is important to plan ancillary testing at the time of tissue sampling so that sufficient passes are made, as well as to ensure that samples are handled correctly. Samples for flow cytometry must not be fixed but can be placed in cell culture medium or in a variety of stabilizing solutions {32479677}. By contrast, immunocytochemistry may be performed on various types of cytopathological preparations using different fixatives {21771093; 21771092}. For molecular studies, nucleic acids can be extracted from any cytopathological material, including archived glass slides, liquid-based cytopathology slides, various solutions for cell collection/transportation, and cell blocks.

Flow cytometry and immunocytochemistry are complementary methodologies to determine immunophenotype of lymphoid proliferations, and the choice may depend on local expertise. Flow cytometry is useful if there is a need for rapid immunophenotypic information. It is powerful in detecting monoclonal lymphoid populations and is more effective than immunocytochemistry in quantifying signals and detecting coexpression of antigens. By contrast, immunocytochemistry is more effective in assessing a large range of intracellular markers {30156771}, and in the diagnosis and workup as to specific type of metastases.

Cytogenetic abnormalities may be useful in the differential diagnosis of different subtypes of B-cell lymphoma {21560251; 22693682}. To this end, interphase FISH is frequently used to further characterize the subtype of a lymphoid proliferation.

In the current era of precision medicine, the role of cytopathology has rapidly expanded to include molecular diagnostics. With the advent of routinely available powerful sequencing tools, the role of the cytopathologist has become critical for the judicious use of materials. A major challenge is the sometimes scant target material available, which limits the applicability of molecular testing and is associated with a risk of false negative results {31021538; 27333361}.

In general, cytopathological specimens represent a valuable and sometimes underestimated source for ancillary testing, provided that sufficient numbers of cells have been collected for the various techniques. In the case of flow cytometry, lymph node FNAB samples normally provide sufficient material for a comprehensive analysis. By contrast, the limited quantity of material available may restrict the utility of immunocytochemistry when a large panel of antibodies is indicated. FNAB cytopathology provides a far better specimen for FISH than biopsy, due to the ability to rapidly fix freshly procured intact cells. Regarding molecular testing, cytopathological specimens prepared by various methods are often equivalent or even superior to small biopsies and cell blocks {32980613; 29981866}. Numerous studies have shown the usefulness of various types of cytopathological material, including archived slides, for molecular testing {20959611; 26921541}.

Diagnosis of a lymphoproliferative disorder relies on an integrated evaluation of clinical, imaging, cytomorphological, immunophenotypic, cytogenetic, and molecular data. ROSE or immediate assessment of a sample arriving in the laboratory by use of a rapid stain may establish a presumptive diagnosis of a lymphoproliferative disorder {30156771}. ROSE, in combination with clinical history, may suggest a more specific diagnosis and facilitate the most efficient, accurate, and cost-effective selection of ancillary testing. This information should be communicated to the relevant laboratories, allowing priority to be given to appropriate investigations.
