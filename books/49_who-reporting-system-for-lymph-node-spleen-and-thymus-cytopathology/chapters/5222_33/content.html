5.2.2.2 Immunoblastic reactions

Definition

Immunoblastic reactions are reactive lymphadenopathies with numerous immunoblasts in a mixed background of small lymphocytes, plasmacytoid cells, histiocytes, occasional eosinophils, and occasional larger lymphoid cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Infectious and non-infectious disorders in which localized or generalized lymphadenopathy is present, such as infectious mononucleosis, cytomegalovirus lymphadenitis, herpetic lymphadenitis, and drug-induced (e.g. phenytoin) lymphadenopathy, can be associated with a reactive immunoblastic reaction. Infectious mononucleosis typically presents in children and young adults with pharyngitis, fever, fatigue, splenomegaly, and transient cervical lymphadenopathy. The diagnosis is usually confirmed by positive monospot and heterophile antibody tests with no need for FNAB {3043677; 22123662}. In cases with persistent lymphadenopathy, a lymph node FNAB for morphological examination and flow cytometry followed by EBV ISH can confirm the diagnosis. Cytomegalovirus infection can present with symptoms similar to those of infectious mononucleosis in immunocompetent hosts {14689339}, while the presentation and clinical course can be more severe in immunocompromised patients {31153807}. Herpes simplex lymphadenitis is commonly a localized disease but rarely can be generalized in immunosuppressed patients {1850953}. Lymphadenopathy and an immunoblastic reaction have also been described in the setting of anticonvulsant therapy with phenytoin, where an increased incidence of other lymphoproliferative disorders has also been reported {7755154}.

Histopathology

Diffuse immunoblastic proliferations can be difficult to differentiate from high-grade lymphomas. The lymph node architecture is never totally effaced, but in the late stages, the number of large immunoblasts in the paracortex is prominent, and occasional Reed–Sternberg–like cells may be identified. In such cases, ancillary techniques are needed to support the morphological interpretation [[Jaffe ES, Arber DA, Campo E, et al., editors. Hematopathology. 2nd ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2016.]].

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Highly cellular smears Background includes small lymphocytes, plasma cells, tingible-body macrophages, and occasional eosinophils. Dendritic reticulum and tingible-body macrophages from germinal centres of associated follicular hyperplasia are seen, usually without distinct follicular tissue fragments. Lymphovascular tangles may be present. Numerous large immunoblasts are present, with large rounded nuclei, dispersed chromatin, one or two nucleoli, and dense basophilic (Giemsa) cytoplasm that can form a rim or be eccentric – sometimes with a perinuclear clearing (“hof”). In late stages of infectious mononucleosis, the large cells represent the most prominent population, in a background of small lymphocytes and neutrophils. Scattered binucleated or multinucleated Reed–Sternberg–like cells can be present. Occasional large cells with dense round nuclear inclusions (“owl’s eye”) can be seen in cytomegalovirus lymphadenitis (more easily identified histopathologically). Large, less-defined nuclear inclusions are also present in herpetic infections with chromatinic margination.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The reactive immunoblastic pattern is also known as a paracortical immunoblastic reaction or reactive immunoblastic hyperplasia.

The number of immunoblasts seen in viral infections (infectious mononucleosis, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex) or in anticonvulsant (phenytoin)-related lymphadenopathy varies noticeably in the course of the disease. A comprehensive clinical history is essential, particularly in phenytoin-associated lymphadenopathy.

In the early stages, features of follicular hyperplasia are present in association with a small number of large immunoblasts, indicative of a benign diagnosis. Attention should be paid to possible flow cytometry outliers of T-cell markers and light chain ratio, which may occur in a limited number of cases {30194715}.

However, in the late stages, large immunoblasts are the predominant cell type, and the main differential diagnosis is with large B-cell lymphomas and classic Hodgkin lymphoma. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS and other large B-cell lymphomas, the population of large cells tends to be more monomorphic, and nuclear atypia is more pronounced {26414904; 22351626}. Classic Hodgkin lymphoma is characterized by a mixture of mononuclear Hodgkin and binucleated Reed–Sternberg cells showing larger nucleoli and abundant pale cytoplasm, compared with true immunoblasts, where the cytoplasm is scant or eccentric with a hof {11241266; 29131085}. Immunoblastic reaction smears are generally more cellular than those of classic Hodgkin lymphoma, with numerous mitoses and capillaries {22351626; 29131085}. Immunoblastic proliferations can also be observed in post-vaccination (smallpox, measles) lymphadenopathy. Clinical history, blood investigations, and serology are essential in the differential diagnosis {22351626; 29131085}.

Ancillary testing

Flow cytometry and/or immunocytochemistry plays a key role in identifying immunoblasts as non-clonal reactive cells. Flow cytometry crucially shows polyclonal B cells and a normal distribution of T cells, with a predominance of CD8 cytotoxic T lymphocytes compared with CD4 in mononucleosis.

Immunoblasts often express CD30 by immunocytochemistry, as well as B-cell markers (CD20+/BCL6+) and/or T-cell markers {2158227}. In infectious mononucleosis, the large immunoblasts are strongly positive for EBV (LMP1 or EBER ISH) {23725487; 28592786}. Immunocytochemistry on cell blocks for CMV and herpes simplex is frequently diagnostic in cases where nuclear inclusions are identified {15494709}.

In most cases, the correlation of cytomorphological features and flow cytometry with clinical history is sufficient for a correct diagnosis. If flow cytometry is not available and watch-and-wait management is not possible, a repeat FNAB or CNB may be necessary, and an excision biopsy may be considered.
