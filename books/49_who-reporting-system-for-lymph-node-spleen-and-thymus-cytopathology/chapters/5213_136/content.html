5.2.1.3 Granulomatous inflammation

Definition

Granulomatous inflammation is characterized by epithelioid granulomas with or without necrosis and giant cells.

Clinical features and imaging

The etiology of granulomatous inflammation can be infectious and non-infectious, such as reactions to foreign bodies, autoimmune disorders, and malignancy. Clinically, patients present with localized or generalized lymph node enlargement, which can be accompanied by constitutional symptoms including fever, night sweats, and weight loss. The lymph nodes can be matted. Abscess formation can occur. The overlying skin may show evidence of inflammation, sinus formation, or a healed scar. Involvement of pulmonary hilar lymph nodes results in respiratory symptoms such as cough and dyspnoea, while intra-abdominal granulomatous inflammation is associated with abdominal pain. Some granulomatous infections have distinctive imaging features, but many do not. In addition, the imaging findings of lymph nodes with granulomatous inflammation can overlap with malignancy {25208278; 29400999; 22706525; 11054963}.

Histopathology

Lymph nodes show granulomas consisting of epithelioid cells and a variable number of multinucleated giant cells, with or without associated necrosis. The residual lymph node background contains a reactive lymphoid population (see #29544Table 4.01, p. XXX).

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Epithelioid granulomas with or without necrosis Background can show acute suppuration with neutrophils or chronic infection with lymphocytes and plasma cells. Epithelioid histiocytes have abundant, often eccentric cytoplasm; oval to indented and bent nuclei; finely granular chromatin; and inconspicuous nucleoli. They can be cohesive and in some cases syncytial in a well-formed granuloma or more loosely cohesive and seen as single cells. The features of the granulomas may suggest an etiology, such as central necrosis in mycobacterial infection or a solid non-necrotizing granuloma in sarcoidosis. An etiological agent may be seen, such as a fungus or a foreign body, which may be birefringent.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

An excess of a particular inflammatory cell in the presence of granulomas can suggest a particular etiology: abundant neutrophils suggest a suppurative granulomatous bacterial infection, tuberculosis, or Hodgkin lymphoma; abundant histiocytes seen singly suggest non-tubercular mycobacterial infection, leprosy, sarcoidosis, Whipple disease, Rosai–Dorfman disease, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, or phagocytosis of foreign material; abundant plasma cells suggest leishmaniasis, syphilis, a plasma cell neoplasm, Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or Castleman disease; and abundant eosinophils can suggest parasites, fungal infection, Kimura disease, Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or Langerhans cell histiocytosis.

The characteristic features of mycobacterial infection are lymphocytes with epithelioid granulomas that include Langhans giant cells and a variable amount of necrosis. With impaired immunity, the cytomorphology varies, and granulomas become less well formed, the inflammatory infiltrate is mixed, and necrosis becomes more watery. As immunity wanes further, the cytomorphology can feature abundant necrosis with occasional or absent granulomas or suppurative necrotic inflammation or large numbers of histiocytes with no granulomas.

Clues to the etiology of granulomatous inflammation include the presence of negative-staining bacilli in the background of Giemsa-stained smears in non-tubercular mycobacterial infection, yeasts or hyphae in fungal-related granulomatous inflammation, and parasitic organisms within histiocytes (as in leishmaniasis) or free-lying (as in microfilariae). Sarcoid typically shows well-formed non-necrotizing granulomas in a background of chronic inflammation with no necrosis. Malignancies, including Hodgkin lymphoma, metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and metastatic seminoma, can lead to granuloma formation. Smears should be carefully examined to ensure there is no underlying malignancy.

Ancillary testing

Special stains such as the Ziehl–Neelsen stain and other acid-fast stains, PASD, and Grocott methenamine silver stains for fungi are required in all cases of granulomatous inflammation, and they should be correlated with microbiological cultures and molecular techniques such as PCR to confirm the causative organism or to exclude a microbiological etiology in cases of the diagnosis of sarcoidosis by exclusion (#29544Table 4.01). In cases of granulomas associated with malignancy, immunocytochemistry and molecular studies can be performed to confirm the diagnosis {26143421; 30407860} [[Pantanowitz L, Michelow P, Khalbuss WE. Cytopathology of infectious diseases. Springer New York (NY): 2011. (Essentials in cytopathology series).]].

Large histiocytes containing bradyzoites, reactive lymphoid population, epithelioid microgranulomas, plasma cells, bradyzoites in background: crescent-shaped organisms within histiocytes and lymphocytes
