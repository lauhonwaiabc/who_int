8.6.5.12 Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma

Definition

Breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) is a mature, uniformly CD30-positive T-cell lymphoma that arises in relation to a breast implant as an effusion confined by a fibrous capsule, and less commonly forming an invading mass.

Clinical features and imaging

The overall risk of developing BIA-ALCL is very low {28157769; 29302687}. Women have breast implants for a median of 8–9 years (range: 2–32 years) before development of the lymphoma {24323027; 26628470}. Approximately 70% of patients present with effusion around the implant manifested by a large and/or painful breast, while about 30% of patients present with a tumour mass. Local invasion of the chest wall and skin can occur. A subset of patients may present with bilateral disease. About 20% of patients have regional lymphadenopathy, most commonly involving the axilla, but also the infraclavicular or supraclavicular regions {24323027; 29194092}. BIA-ALCL can rarely disseminate to distant sites, including bone marrow {29635424}. By Ann Arbor staging, 90% of patients have localized disease (80% stage I, 10% stage II).

Effusion surrounding breast implants is common in the immediate postoperative period and is not concerning. In contrast, effusions occurring later, usually more than 1 year after implant placement, require workup. Ultrasound is most helpful for detecting an effusion, with a sensitivity of 84% and specificity of 75% in one study {25073777}. CT and PET are more optimal methods for detecting a mass. Mammography has a sensitivity of 73% and specificity of 50% for detecting an abnormality, but distinguishing effusion from a mass using this modality is challenging {25073777}. Following detection of a late effusion, aspiration is usually the first diagnostic procedure.

Effusion fluid is often referred to as a seroma, but this term is inaccurate. Seroma fluid should be a transudate that is protein-poor. In contrast, effusion fluid in patients with BIA-ALCL is rich in protein and often necrotic cells.

Histopathology

Removal of a breast implant and surrounding pseudocapsule en bloc is optimal management for patients with resectable tumours {26628470; 31383966}. The surrounding fibrous capsule and any adherent material must be systematically examined histopathologically.

In patients with effusion, a layer or aggregates of lymphoma cells is adherent to the luminal surface, often with minimal infiltration of the capsule {22613996; 24323027}. Extensive capsular infiltration is often associated with a tumour mass. The neoplastic cells are large, with pleomorphic or anaplastic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Hallmark cells, with a horseshoe-shaped nucleus and perinuclear hof, are present in about 70% of cases. Many necrotic ghosts of neoplastic cells are present, within the effusion fluid or adherent to the surface. Infiltration of the capsule is staged using the TNM system as follows: T1, no infiltration of capsule; T2, minimal infiltration of capsule; T3, extensive infiltration of capsule but neoplastic cells are confined by the capsule; and T4, infiltration through the fibrous capsule {31383966}. In regional lymph nodes, the neoplasm most often involves the sinusoids, but more extensive involvement can also occur {29194092}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The evidence to implicate breast implants in causation of BIA-ALCL is powerful. Almost all cases have occurred in patients with textured-surface implants {28157769}. In contrast, the contents of the implants, either saline or silicone, do not appear to play a role in pathogenesis. The surface of implants degrades slightly over time, resulting in leakage of antigens that elicit a histiocyte-driven host T-cell immune response. Analysis of cytokines in effusion fluid and peri-implant capsules has shown a high number of IL-17–producing T cells {22286447}. The levels of IL-13, which is involved in allergic inflammation, are also high {29689246}. A bacterial biofilm has also been implicated in pathogenesis {23924649; 26890506}. The pathogenesis of BIA-ALCL probably also involves a number of genetic abnormalities, although the molecular pathogenesis is incompletely understood. JAK/STAT pathway alterations and a hypoxia gene signature have been shown in some cases {29921615; 32414854}. Loss of chromosome 20q13.13 occurs in two thirds of cases {33331925}.

The differential diagnosis includes a number of neoplasms reported to involve breasts with implants (#29138Table 7.09). FNAB can diagnose an effusion, or FNAB of a mass can diagnose lymphoma and suggest BIA-ALCL, but final specific diagnosis requires ancillary testing, or excision with immunocytochemistry. Other types of anaplastic large cell lymphoma must be excluded. ALK expression and ALK rearrangements characterize ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma in other body sites, and recurrent chromosomal translocations occur in systemic ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma. The most common B-cell lymphomas that arise in the breast are diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue {18636016}, both of which have been reported rarely in association with breast implants {32315816}. One rare type of B-cell lymphoma that that can closely mimic BIA-ALCL is EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma {34226673}.

Ancillary testing

Flow cytometry analysis of effusion fluid can be helpful, but it may not succeed if the lymphoma cells are poorly viable. The neoplastic cells are uniformly and strongly CD30-positive, and they express one or more T-cell antigens such as CD4 (~80%) and CD43 (~80%) (#29137Table 7.10). About 70% express cytotoxic markers, such as TIA1, perforin, and granzyme B. These neoplasms are often negative for CD3, CD5, CD7, and T-cell receptors. By flow cytometry, the cells often have higher forward and side scatter than normal lymphocytes {32525554}. BIA-LCL is negative for B-cell markers, ALK, and EBV-encoded RNA.

Most cases of BIA-ALCL carry clonal TRG and/or TRB rearrangements. Conventional cytogenetic analysis of a few cases has shown complex karyotypes without recurrent chromosomal translocations.
