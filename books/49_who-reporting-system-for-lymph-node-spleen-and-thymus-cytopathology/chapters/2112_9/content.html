2.1.1.2 Ultrasound guidance of FNAB

Ultrasonography is a widely accessible and affordable imaging method and a powerful tool for evaluation of the lymph nodes, spleen, and thymus. There has been technical progress in recent decades with the development of standard ultrasound devices that can be operated not only by radiologists but also by other medical specialists, including interventional cytopathologists [[Bardales RH. The invasive cytopathologist: ultrasound guided fine-needle aspiration of superficial masses. Berlin (Germany): Springer; 2014. (Essentials in cytopathology series).]] {25677264; 20923312}. Theoretical knowledge of ultrasonography, as well as proficiency in the performance of ultrasound-guided FNAB, may be acquired by non-radiologists in dedicated multidisciplinary courses {31213257; 28699306}.

The ultrasound device for FNAB guidance should be equipped with a linear-array transducer probe, 35–50 mm, with a frequency of 7.0–12.0 MHz. The needle is generally introduced at the short end of the probe, which allows the demonstration longitudinally of the whole length of the needle track, including the needle tip location. Alternatively, depending on the topography and local practice, the entry site of the needle may be centrally at right angles to the long axis of the probe. Different inclinations of the needle, with an angle from 0 to 90 degrees, can be precisely demonstrated by the ultrasound based on the depth of the lymph node. In both approaches, the bevel of the needle should be oriented towards the probe to increase ultrasound reflectivity {29131115}. Special guiding tools may be used, but they increase the cost of the FNAB procedure. The availability of power and colour-flow Doppler allows the evaluation of the vascular pattern within the lymph node and the localization of the adjacent blood vessels.

Ultrasound guidance contributes to the safety of the procedure by helping to avoid inadvertent punctures of blood vessels and also assists in avoiding the vascular hilum of the lymph node. Ultrasound imaging enables the selection of the suitable target node and assures the sampling of the correct lymph node, including the precise control of the intranodal needle tip {28130574}. Ultrasound guidance of the FNAB is primarily recommended for non-palpable lymph nodes and all head and neck lymph nodes, where avoidance of inadvertent puncture of blood vessels or air spaces is of particular importance {20949470}. Palpable lymph nodes in other sites may be targeted without direct ultrasound guidance, but ultrasound assessment of all targets is recommended when available and provides better sampling of non-necrotic, diagnostic intranodal sites {31051754}.

The ultrasound features of the lymph node (#28017Table 2.01) provide valuable information assisting the final diagnosis, both in palpable and non-palpable nodes {23946589; 32454496}. Operators performing the FNAB with ultrasound image guidance obtain high-quality cytopathological samples with the possibility, especially when ROSE is performed, of immediate triage of the sample for additional studies, such as microbiology, flow cytometry, or molecular methods, based on the clinical data, ROSE result, and ultrasound features {21671411}. Application of transcutaneous ultrasound guidance improves the diagnostic performance of the FNAB as compared with sole palpation guidance, with best results achieved by cytopathologists performing the FNAB themselves or in collaboration with other specialists {29697276; 18956459; 31051745; 29599813; 18418854}.

Integration of miniaturized transducers into endoscopic devices allows targeting of deeply located mediastinal, retroperitoneal, and intra-abdominal lymph nodes by endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) {32487873; 26402427}. A convex transducer with frequencies of 20–30 MHz and special endoscopic needles (25, 22, or 19 gauge) are used for these procedures. The application of endoscopic sonography allows precise, minimally invasive diagnosis and staging of malignancy (e.g. in the management of lung cancer), as transoesophageal EUS can access lymph node stations 7, 8, and 9 (and 5 if enlarged), and EBUS can access the mediastinal lymph node stations 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 11, and 12. In a meta-analysis of intra-abdominal lymph nodes, EUS-FNAB showed a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 98% {31881165}. In the pooled analysis of mediastinal and abdominal EUS-FNAB, the sensitivity reached 87% and the specificity 100%, with sensitivity for mediastinal lymph nodes enhanced by ROSE from 85% to 91% {32979158}. The rate of adverse effects in both studies was low (1.5%).
