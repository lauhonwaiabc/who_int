8.6.4.5 Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma

Definition

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) is a neoplasm consisting of small B-lymphoid and plasmacytoid lymphoid cells and plasma cells, usually involving the bone marrow and sometimes lymph nodes and spleen.

Clinical features and imaging

There are two types of LPL in the fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours: IgM LPL / Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (WM), representing > 90% of LPL, and non–WM-type LPL, which includes cases with IgG monoclonal proteins, non-secretory LPL, and IgM LPL with no bone marrow involvement. LPL represents approximately 1% of all lymphomas. WM is defined as LPL in the bone marrow with serum IgM paraprotein of any level. MYD88 p.L265P is a driver mutation in most LPL/WM cases and in 40–50% of non-WM LPL cases. Median age at diagnosis is in the seventh decade, and this neoplasm is more common in men and White people {22239669; 32090327; 34522842}. LPL/WM is often preceded by IgM monoclonal gammopathy of uncertain significance.

About 30% of LPL/WM patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis. Symptoms in LPL/WM patients can be related to (1) elevated serum IgM levels; (2) infiltration of organs by lymphoma; and (3) paraprotein, such as amyloid deposition in tissues. The most common presenting symptoms in LPL/WM are attributable to anaemia and include fatigue and weakness. Anaemia can be caused by LPL/WM infiltration of the bone marrow and/or by increased hepcidin levels, the latter interfering with iron uptake in the gastrointestinal tract. Thrombocytopenia and leukopenia are less common. About 20% of patients have lymphadenopathy and/or hepatosplenomegaly at diagnosis; these findings are more common at relapse. Serum hyperviscosity symptoms occur in 10–20% of patients, including blurred vision, headache, epistaxis, and dyspnoea {11736938}, and they are more likely to occur when IgM levels are > 6 g/dL {28485115}. Bleeding tendency can occur due to acquired von Willebrand disease and/or thrombocytopenia. IgM paraprotein can exhibit autoantibody or cryoglobulin activity resulting in autoimmune phenomena or type II cryoglobulinaemia. As a result, vasculitis, skin lesions, renal complications, and/or neuropathy may develop. IgM-related neuropathy occurs in approximately 20% of patients; this may result from IgM paraprotein reacting with myelin sheath antigens or gangliosides containing disialosyl epitopes. The latter results in CANOMAD syndrome (chronic ataxic neuropathy with ophthalmoplegia, M protein, cold agglutinins, and disialosyl ganglioside antibodies) {28198999}. Amyloid deposition can also cause neuropathy.

Patients with non-WM LPL present in a more heterogeneous manner; they tend to be younger, more often have adverse prognostic factors, and less frequently have hyperviscosity syndrome or neuropathy {31992103; 34959252}. LPL associated with an IgG paraprotein is more common than IgA or non-secretory LPL {34959252}. A serum paraprotein may be absent or present at a low level, and bone marrow involvement is less frequent than in LPL/WM patients {31378966}. Lymph nodes, spleen, liver, and other extranodal sites are involved more frequently in non-WM LPL patients as compared with LPL/WM patients.

Histopathology

Bone marrow smears show a mix of small and plasmacytoid lymphoid cells and plasma cells in variable proportions. Dutcher bodies and/or Russell bodies are common. Reactive mast cells as well as haemosiderin-laden histiocytes are often present. In the trephine biopsy specimen, LPL can be present in a diffuse, interstitial, or paratrabecular pattern {27329639; 25972321; 31980558; 29868855}. A purely paratrabecular or nodular pattern is rare. AL amyloid deposition can be associated with LPL infiltrates. After therapy, lymphocytes may be absent and only plasma cells may persist.

LPL in lymph nodes usually has a diffuse pattern and tends to spare sinuses and lymphoid follicles, with a variable mix of small and plasmacytoid lymphoid cells and plasma cells. Dutcher bodies and/or Russell bodies and mast cells and haemosiderin-laden histiocytes are common. Atypia is usually minimal, and the mitotic rate is low. Rarely, amyloid deposition or a histiocytic infiltrate with cytoplasmic immunoglobulin crystals (crystal-storing histiocytosis) may be present.

LPL may involve the splenic white or red pulp with a similar mix of cells {12883242}, and rarely other sites are involved (usually at the time of relapse), including the lungs, soft tissues, kidneys, bones, and skin, as well as the CNS (so-called Bing–Neel syndrome). The infiltrates in these organs closely resemble those in the lymph nodes.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses are other small-celled lymphomas, and flow cytometry is a useful tool in excluding other lymphomas and confirming the diagnosis. Extranodal location and prominent splenomegaly suggest extranodal and splenic marginal zone lymphomas, respectively, and a serum paraprotein is uncommon. Marginal zone lymphomas are composed of a heterogeneous cell population with numerous monocytoid B cells with pale cytoplasm and/or evidence of residual reactive lymphoid follicles. By contrast, LPL is more monotonous, with plasmacytoid lymphoid cells and plasma cells, and mast cells are commonly present in LPL.

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) presents with lymphocytosis, which is uncommon in LPL patients. CLL / small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) shows a monotonous small lymphoid cell population with coarse or block-like chromatin, and may show scattered prolymphocytes and paraimmunoblasts.

Mantle cell lymphoma presents with lymphadenopathy and/or leukaemic involvement, and classic mantle cell lymphoma shows a monotonous population of small and monotonous “centrocyte-like” lymphoid cells with variably irregular nuclei, and few if any plasma cells.

Ancillary testing

Flow cytometry shows two cell populations in variable proportions in LPL: B-lymphoid cells that are positive for monotypic surface immunoglobulin light chain, CD19, CD20, CD22, and CD79a {12720134; 21454202} and variably positive for CD38 and CD45 and negative for CD3, CD10, CD11c, CD103, CD123, and CD200, and plasma cells, which are positive for cytoplasmic light chain, CD19, CD38, CD45, and CD138 and negative for CD56 and CD117. CD5 and CD23 are usually negative but can be weakly positive in a small subset of cases {12883242; 21454202}. In LPL, the small lymphoid cells are surface-positive and the plasma cells show cytoplasmic positivity for IgM. IgG or IgA is often positive in LPL cases of non-WM type. Cases can be biclonal, with subsets of cells either IgM or IgG. IgD is usually negative. It is important to remember that flow cytometry typically underestimates the number of plasma cells in an FNAB sample {31980558}.

In the differential diagnosis (see #33280Table 2.04, in section Flow cytometry), CLL/SLL cells are positive for CD5, CD19, CD20 (dim), CD23, and surface light chain (dim), unlike LPL/WM, and the MYD88 p.L265P mutation is uncommon. Mantle cell lymphoma cells are positive for CD5, CD19, CD20, cyclin D1, and often SOX11, and the MYD88 p.L265P mutation is uncommon. The MYD88 p.L265P mutation, although not exclusive, supports LPL over marginal zone lymphoma.

Immunocytochemistry shows that the B-lymphoid cells are usually positive for PAX5, OCT2, and BOB1, and negative for DBA.44. The plasma cells are positive for CD138 and MUM1/IRF4, and negative for CD20. Ki-67 is typically low. After therapy, plasma cells can predominate, mimicking myeloma.

Many cases of LPL have a normal diploid karyotype, but clonal 6q deletion (del 6q21-25) is a common cytogenetic abnormality, found in 40–50% of LPL/WM patients, and MYD88 p.L265P mutations can be identified in 93–97% of LPL cases using sensitive molecular methods. Non-p.L265P MYD88 mutations have also been identified in 1–2% of LPL patients. MYD88 p.L265P mutation can also be detected in suspected cases of non-WM LPL, with a frequency of about 50% {32083995}. Testing for MYD88 p.L265P by targeted NGS may lead to false negatives in up to a third of patients {34291197}. Mutations involving the C-terminal domain of CXCR4 (nonsense and frameshift mutations) are present in up to 40% of LPL patients, mostly concurrent with MYD88 mutations {27301862; 30401751}. CXCR4 nonsense mutations are associated with high serum IgM, symptomatic hyperviscosity, earlier time to treatment, von Willebrand deficiency, and shorter progression-free survival when patients are treated with ibrutinib.
