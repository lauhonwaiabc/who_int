3.1.1.3 Immunocytochemistry

Cytomorphological assessment of FNAB of lymph node, spleen, and thymic lesions can be challenging, and the advent of immunocytochemical methods has without doubt enabled the success and acceptance of FNAB as a legitimate diagnostic modality. Immunocytochemistry is an important ancillary method that assists the cytopathologist in making an accurate diagnosis of reactive hyperplasias versus malignant processes including lymphoproliferative lesions and metastases on FNAB {21771093; 29131004; 34310059}. Immunocytochemistry can be performed on a range of cytopathological preparations, including direct smears, cell block, centrifuged preparations, and liquid-based slides, using different types of fixatives {24836219; 3348061; 21771092}.

Fixation and controls

In general, fixation times are considerably shorter in cytopathology than on thicker paraffin-embedded tissue sections, and sample preparation essentially consists of fixing the target cells to the slide. Several protocols with different fixatives for cytopathology samples have been reported in an attempt to improve the yield and accuracy of immunocytochemistry results {10820525; 10961385; 8872443}. A wide range of fixatives are commonly used, including acetone, methanol, ethanol, paraformaldehyde, and formalin. The appropriate fixation method should be chosen according to the laboratory equipment, types of technical support and samples, and diagnostic orientation. Good results on cytospin immunocytochemistry have been reported {10820525; 10961385; 8872443}. Equally satisfactory results are obtained on direct smears. With regard to liquid-based cytopathology, unfortunately, the fixative solution is not optimal for evaluation of all types of neoplasms, such as lymphoid neoplasms, because methanol fixation can limit the quality of immunocytochemical staining. However, formalin fixation can overcome many preanalytical problems, and it uses protocols similar to those used for histopathological samples, especially as regards predictive markers.

Air-fixed additional smears and cytospins can be stored for many years at −70 °C without loss of antigenicity and can be used as a bank of control slides for routine immunocytochemistry runs.

Management and technical requirements of types of cytopathological specimens

Unstained direct smears of lymph node FNAB material are better than destained alcohol-fixed smears for immunocytochemistry, especially if they have been prepared for this purpose {21771092}. However, destained alcohol-fixed smears have the advantage that they can be evaluated to establish their quality and the presence of diagnostic cells prior to the immunocytochemistry procedure, particularly when only a few diagnostic cells are present on the smears, such as in Hodgkin lymphoma or for the search for lymph node metastases. Direct smears are more suitable for immunolabelling of nuclear antigens (e.g. Ki-67 and TTF1) than for membrane or cytoplasmic antigens, because of the high background staining due to the presence of blood and cell damage caused by smearing. Furthermore, although the cost of preparing direct smears is low, the amount of antibody needed to cover the entire slide is relatively high, and the number of slides may be limited, preventing the use of large antibody panels.

In contrast, using centrifuged preparations or cytospins is ideal to set up multiple mirror-image cytopathology preparations by expelling successive FNAB into a preserving medium such as RPMI medium or PBS, until an adequate number of cells are obtained. Cellular adequacy may be confirmed by a Coulter cell counter or haematocytometer, or, with experience, by visual assessment based on the haziness of the solution. Most lymph node FNAB will easily yield > 1.0 million cells/mL of RPMI solution, depending on the number of FNAB needle passes. Performing the FNAB without suction is the preferred method to obtain highly cellular specimens without contamination by blood.

Cells collected in RPMI medium may be enriched for mononuclear cells by using a gradient separation centrifugation step over a Ficoll-Hypaque medium. The resultant buffy coat is highly enriched in mononuclear cells, without contaminating neutrophils and red blood cells, which have been sedimented to the bottom of the tube {7395814}. The buffy coat is diluted with RPMI medium until an ideal concentration of approximately 100 000 cells/mL is obtained. Each cytospin requires 100 μL (1 mL) of buffy coat, and if necessary 10–15 cytospins can be prepared, with the remaining cells in RPMI medium being triaged for flow cytometry, molecular studies such as NGS, or FISH, while the residual pellet may be frozen and banked. An air-dried cytospin can be immediately Giemsa-stained, and an alcohol-fixed cytospin can be stained with Pap stain. Both preparations may be used for ROSE for adequacy and the presence of diagnostic cells. For immunocytochemistry, multiple cytospins (dependent on how many are required for the proposed panel of biomarkers) can be immediately spray-fixed with 100% ethanol and stored overnight at room temperature for next-day testing, or at 35 °C. The major advantages of performing immunocytochemistry on cytospins prepared by gradient separation are the excellent preservation of the cytomorphology of the atypical cells and the ability to clearly match the marker to the cytomorphology of the cell, with neither the contamination of red blood cells in the background nor a smearing artefact caused by nuclear and membranous damage, which is especially noted in high-grade lymphomas {2111624; 30089946}. Also, importantly, cytospins conserve the amounts of antibody and other reagents that are required in the immunocytochemistry process {2111624; 30089946}.

Alternatively, additional slides may be prepared from material stored in liquid-based cytopathology solution {23020285; 18702358; 25545197} for the application of different specific antibodies. Good results have been reported with regard to the application of different panels, for example for breast, lung, thyroid, and melanoma metastatic to lymph nodes. The number of slides that can be prepared may be low, although generally there are more slides than with direct smears.

Finally, cell blocks are one of the most effective and reliable sources of tissue preparation using FNAB material, especially a cell block technique performed with formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue fragments or cells. This method guarantees an antigenicity comparable to those of conventional histopathological sections and allows the use of most of the antibodies validated on FFPE tissue. Other advantages of immunocytochemistry on cell block are the relatively large number of available sections, the possibility of long-term preservation, and the availability of tissue archiving for future research.

Diagnostic applications

Immunocytochemistry on lymph node FNAB may be used to distinguish reactive lymphadenopathies from atypical or neoplastic lymphoproliferative processes. The application of well-designed immunocytochemistry panels can help to diagnose different subtypes of lymphoma or confirm a benign cytomorphological diagnosis. In cases of a reactive process with follicular expansion, immunocytochemistry can clarify the differential diagnosis between reactive germinal centres versus a follicular lymphoma based largely on the quantification of cell phenotypes, such as CD3, CD5, CD10, CD19, CD20, CD23, FMC7, and light chain. These assessments on lymph node FNAB have been mainly performed by flow cytometry {21438908; 21219488; 11353059; 20132132; 27554776}, but immunocytochemistry {27456410; 10455210; 11038430; 8178762; 10820525; 18702358; 25545197} may also be used. Immunocytochemistry on non-Hodgkin lymphoma samples aims to identify the phenotype of the cell population and the possible expression of aberrant antigens, to assess the light chain type, and to evaluate the proliferative index by Ki-67 {30452126}. Suggested immunocytochemistry panels for establishing immunoprofiles of lymphomas are shown in #40675Table 2.06 . Immunocytochemistry is more commonly used on cytospins than other types of cytopathological preparations, such as cell block, especially when dealing with small or medium non-Hodgkin lymphoma cells {21438908; 21219488; 11353059; 20132132; 27554776; 27456410; 10455210; 11038430; 8178762; 10820525}.

A few studies have reported on large series using immunocytochemistry on the cell block of lymph node FNAB material, where high sensitivity and specificity for lymphoma and reactive processes diagnosis has been claimed {25550808; 29143491; 26414904; 20391989}. Immunocytochemistry on cell block may be effective mainly in high-grade non-Hodgkin lymphoma, in which nuclear atypia is diagnostic of malignancy without a strict need for clonality assessment, and immunocytochemistry can identify the specific phenotype even on samples with a limited amount of material {26414904; 10911803}. In some reports, cell block immunocytochemistry is reported to be more useful than flow cytometry for T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and Hodgkin lymphoma, as well as anaplastic large cell lymphoma and other large cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas {12589644; 17941004}. The use of cell block has been reported in a large series of lymph nodes and extranodal FNAB encompassing both small cell and large cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and Hodgkin lymphoma {25550808}. These authors reported a high accuracy (86.6%; 84/87 cases) in the diagnosis and classification of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. More recently, cell block has been successfully used in a large series of lymph node FNAB of mantle cell lymphoma for SOX11 evaluation, which demonstrated strong positivity in all cases and good correlation with histopathological controls, but the precise diagnosis of small cell lymphomas on cell blocks alone remains challenging {25056830}.

FNAB showing metastatic tumours may require immunocytochemistry to determine the origin of the tumour and assess for prognostic markers and therapeutic targets, which ideally can be performed on slides processed from FFPE cell blocks.

Markers of differentiation in metastatic lymph nodes are shown in #40676Table 2.07 . For tumour staging, direct smears usually meet diagnostic needs, and for targeted therapies that require specific antibody or molecular tests, immunocytochemistry on “FFPE cells” perfectly matches with the recommended standardized protocols for immunocytochemistry {30500997}. Lymph node FNAB of most non-lymphomatous tumours can yield tissue fragments, which are more useful than isolated cells. Similarly, in nodal T follicular helper cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic type, fragments of lymphoid tissue and endothelial vessels may yield excellent cellularity, on par with CNB. Double labelling of cells (e.g. CD3 and CD5) can assist in some cases. Cell block is usually favoured for the workup of lymph node metastases.
