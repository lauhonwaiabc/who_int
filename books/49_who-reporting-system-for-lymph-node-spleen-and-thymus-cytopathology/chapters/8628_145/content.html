8.6.2.8 Mastocytosis

Definition

Mastocytosis is a group of closely related conditions caused by a clonal proliferation of neoplastic mast cells, typically with KIT mutation, that accumulate in a variety of organs.

Clinical features and imaging

The application of FNAB cytopathology to the diagnosis usually relates to systemic mastocytosis, which is a myeloid neoplasm characterized by clonal proliferation of mast cells, typically harbouring mutant KIT, involving at least one extracutaneous organ system, with or without evidence of skin lesions, which are rarely sampled by FNAB. FNAB may be applied to mast cell sarcoma, which is a rare and clinically aggressive form of mastocytosis characterized by a locally destructive solid tumour comprising highly atypical mast cells.

The presentation of mastocytosis is heterogeneous, varying from only cutaneous involvement to widespread systemic disease associated with symptoms that may result from mast cell secretion of various substances and/or mass effects. Mastocytosis can be subdivided into cutaneous mastocytosis without systemic involvement (CM), which develops preferentially in childhood and has a favourable prognosis, presenting most commonly with urticaria pigmentosa with brown or red-brown skin papules, and systemic mastocytosis (SM), which virtually always involves the bone marrow and can involve haematopoietic, gastrointestinal, and hepatobiliary organs, with or without cutaneous lesions {11377679}. SM patients may have localized symptoms related to mast cell proliferation in different organs, such as the CNS, gastrointestinal tract, or skeleton, as well as systemic symptoms related to the release of specific mediators {23325841}. For example, SM patients may simultaneously experience bleeding due to thrombocytopenia and recurrent infections due to neutropenia {23325841}. According to the fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, SM is further categorized as bone marrow mastocytosis, indolent SM, smouldering mastocytosis, aggressive SM, SM with an associated haematological neoplasm, and mast cell leukaemia [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Mast cell sarcoma is a separate subtype. Each of these may present with “well-differentiated SM”. Therefore, the clinical presentation is extremely variable and depends on the organ system involved and the severity of the process, ranging from skin-limited disease to advanced SM that may have multiple organ dysfunction and a fatal outcome {22410759}.

The prognosis of SM is variable, ranging from clinically indolent with normal life expectancy to rapidly deteriorating courses {17537151}. Cutaneous involvement is generally less frequent in aggressive and high-grade SM proliferations {12930381; 18684881; 11377679}. Clinical and laboratory parameters, including B-findings (related to mast cell burden) and C-findings (related to mast cell organ damage), with an evident diagnostic and/or prognostic impact, have been identified and used to develop specific criteria for subtypes [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {23325841; 27355533}. Bone marrow is involved in virtually all patients. SM can also be associated with other clonal haematological neoplasms, most often chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia, as well as acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, and rarely B-cell lymphomas. CM in children may undergo spontaneous remission during or shortly after puberty {19787646}.

The diagnosis of SM is based on the identification of major and minor criteria. Major diagnostic criteria are multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in the bone marrow and/or other extracutaneous organs. Minor criteria are represented by abnormal mast cell morphology in the bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs (> 25%); the presence of the KIT p.D816V mutation in bone marrow, blood, or extracutaneous organs; CD25 expression on mast cells, with or without CD2; and serum levels of tryptase > 20 ng/mL without associated myeloid neoplasm. When at least one major and one minor criteria or three minor criteria are present, the diagnosis of SM is established. Therefore, in cases with a clinical suspicion of SM, a clinical and imaging staging is needed to detect possible progressive organ enlargement such as hepatosplenomegaly and lymphadenopathy. Moreover, in these patients, organ involvement should be confirmed by a biopsy to demonstrate mast cell infiltration as the cause of impaired organ dysfunction {11377686}. Lymph node enlargement in SM may be caused by various conditions, ranging from benign reactive hyperplasia to lymphadenopathic mastocytosis with eosinophilia, mast cell leukaemia and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, or even metastases.

Histopathology

Mastocytosis can show two histopathological patterns. The more common pattern is the presence of multiple cohesive and compact aggregates or a diffuse infiltration. The second pattern shows a diffuse interstitial infiltration in the absence of compact aggregates. The cells of mastocytosis are of medium size and can be round or spindle, often with pale cytoplasm. Mast cell aggregates are often associated with eosinophils and fibrosis. Small lymphocytes and plasma cells can also be associated with mast cell infiltration. In tissue sections, mast cells are stained by chloroacetate esterase, toluidine blue, and Giemsa.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Atypical medium-sized mast cells Round, polygonal, or spindle mast cells Round to oval, sometimes lobulated nuclei Abundant pale eccentric cytoplasm with densely packed numerous metachromatic granules (Giemsa) Atypical mast cells with hypogranularity may be present. Mitotic figures are rare. Numerous eosinophils, lymphocytes, histiocytes, and plasma cells may be present in the background. Occurrence of metachromatic blasts is typical of mast cell leukaemia. In aggressive mast cell proliferations, the mast cells often have bilobated or multilobated nuclei.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB features of SM have been rarely described, and cytopathological diagnostic criteria have not been extensively reported [[Cozzolino I, Picardi M, Lucci R, et al. Lymph node fine needle aspiration cytology in systemic mastocytosis: cytological features with ancillary tests and literature review. Cytopathology. 2015 Feb;26(1):31–7. doi:10.1111/cyt.12136.]]. Lymph node surgical or CNB is not always straightforward in SM patients because of the risk of complications, mainly in cases of deeply located lymph nodes or elderly patients with SM systemic symptoms or other diseases {22409821; 22120999}. Therefore, lymph node FNAB may play an important role in the diagnosis of lymph node involvement by SM. Lymph node FNAB combined with ancillary techniques may be conveniently used, because all the suggested diagnostic criteria may be applied on corresponding samples [[Cozzolino I, Picardi M, Lucci R, et al. Lymph node fine needle aspiration cytology in systemic mastocytosis: cytological features with ancillary tests and literature review. Cytopathology. 2015 Feb;26(1):31–7. doi:10.1111/cyt.12136.]] {8610583; 9582576; 17910354; 20497208; 19787646; 29131006}. Nonetheless, cytopathological diagnosis may be extremely difficult in cases for which there is still no diagnosis of CM or SM at the time of the FNAB {17910354}. Bone marrow aspirate smears may also be evaluated in SM to determine significant infiltration where > 25% of all mast cells are immature or atypical. On cytopathological samples of SM, a differential diagnosis with dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and classic Hodgkin lymphoma should be considered.

Ancillary testing

The most specific assays for identifying immature and atypical mast cells are the evaluation of mast cell tryptase and CD117 by immunocytochemistry, and of CD25/CD2 coexpression by flow cytometry. In particular, CD117 distinguishes mast cells from basophils, which may morphologically mimic mast cells, as they are both positive in toluidine blue stain {17910354}. CD25/CD2 coexpression is generally an aberrant expression limited to neoplastic mast cells in adult mastocytosis {11377681}. CD25 and CD2 can also be identified by immunocytochemistry. In a subset of cases, mast cells also express CD30 {21261503; 21186345}. The European Competence Network on Mastocytosis (ECNM) recommends that the diagnosis of mast cell neoplasms include routine assays to detect KIT mutations and during follow-up to quantify KIT p.D816V allele burden. The KIT p.D816V mutation can be detected on cytopathological samples {19787646}.

SM is more common in adults and is a clonal disorder. The clonal disorder hypothesis is based on the finding of gain-of-function mutations of KIT that undergo ligand-independent dimerization and autophosphorylation, leading to increased cell survival and proliferation of mast cells {23593539}. Substitution mutations of KIT at codon 816 (KIT p.D816V) in the cytoplasmic kinase domain characterize SM and occur in > 90% of cases {23720340}. However, they do not seem to have a significant prognostic or predictive value. On the other hand, other mutations can affect a series of genes such as ASXL1, CBL, IDH1/2, JAK2, KRAS, MLL (KMT2A) partial tandem duplication, NPM1, NRAS, TP53, SRSF2, SF3B1, SETBP1, and U2AF1 and predict the biological behaviour of SM {23958953}. Additional molecular aberrations are identified in 89% of aggressive SM cases and 25% of indolent SM cases {23958953}. These data suggest that the prognosis and predictive behaviour of SM cases are related to the pattern of mutated genes acquired along the evolution of SM.

Burden of disease findings

 

·   BM biopsy: > 30% infiltration of cellularity by MCs; serum total tryptase > 200 ng/mL

·   Signs of dysplasia or myeloproliferation (a definitive diagnosis of AHN is not possible) in non-MC lineages, with normal or only slightly abnormal blood counts

·   Hepatomegaly without impairment of liver function; palpable splenomegaly without hypersplenism; lymphadenopathy on imaging or palpation

Cytoreduction-requiring findings

 

·   BM dysfunction: cytopenia (ANC < 1.0 × 109/L; haemoglobin level < 10 g/dL; platelet count < 100 × 109/L)

·   Palpable hepatomegaly with impairment of liver function, ascites, and/or portal hypertension

·   Osteolytic lesion with or without pathological fractures without other causes

·   Palpable splenomegaly with hypersplenism

·   Malabsorption with weight loss due to gastrointestinal MC infiltrates
