8.6.4.11 Large B-cell lymphomas

Introduction

Large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) is the most common type of aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma. It is a biologically heterogeneous group of diseases that in the fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours includes diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS) and some 17 other distinct entities (see #35852Table 7.04, p. XXX) characterized by clinical presentation, cell of origin, molecular features, and (most recently) frequently recurring mutations {35732829}. However, at a cytomorphological level most of these neoplasms are indistinguishable, and so they are presented here in one section. Specific classification requires correlation with clinical presentation and the use of ancillary studies, including immunocytochemistry, flow cytometry, and/or molecular studies. The precise classification is necessary as they represent entities with differing outcomes but similar cytomorphology: medium to large B-lymphoid cells whose nuclei are the same size as (or larger than) those of normal macrophages, or more than twice the size of normal lymphocytes, frequently with one or multiple prominent nucleoli and vesicular nuclei, i.e. perinucleolar chromatin clearing (Pap stain) {22730604}. The cytomorphological spectrum of large B-cell lymphomas is summarized in #35853Table 7.05 .

The fifth-edition WHO haematolymphoid classification terminology should be applied to all lymphomas. Not all the LBCLs mentioned in this classification are presented in separate sections below. In this newly modified WHO tumour classification, there are three newly named entities that are discussed in the Discussion and differential diagnosis subsection of DLBCL-NOS below, rather than in distinct sections. The same applies to DLBCL / high-grade B-cell lymphoma (HGBCL) with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements, LBCL with IRF4 rearrangement, and mediastinal grey zone lymphoma. Because it presents with a different cytopathological pattern, T-cell/histiocyte–rich LBCL is discussed along with other lymphomas presenting with a “single very large atypical cell pattern”, as is lymphomatoid granulomatosis.

Types of large B-cell lymphomas
1. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS

Definition

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified (DLBCL-NOS) is a large cell lymphoma without any of the characteristic cytomorphological, phenotypical, molecular, or clinical findings of other types of LBCLs, and it is divided according to the cell of origin into the germinal-centre B-cell (GCB) subtype and the activated B-cell (ABC) subtype.

Clinical features and imaging

DLBCL-NOS is the most common type of lymphoma in adults, accounting for 35% of all adult lymphoma. DLBCL-NOS can be nodal or extranodal and can involve any organ, such as the gastrointestinal tract (particularly the stomach and ileocaecal region), bone, skin, liver, salivary gland, thyroid, Waldeyer ring, CNS, and testis {9166827; 9739436}. The most common clinical presentation is lymphadenopathy or symptoms related to an extranodal tumour mass in an organ {9166827; 9739436; 9704731; 22730604}.

The prognosis is variable and can be assessed on multiple clinical parameters included in the International Prognostic Index (IPI), the most used clinical tool for predicting outcomes in patients with DLBCL-NOS. Important clinical predictors include age, stage, serum LDH, performance status, and the extent of extranodal site involvement {8141877}. Staging is a powerful indicator of outcome, and it depends on the detection and extent of disease in multiple organs. Although bone marrow staging biopsy was in the past the standard-of-care staging tool, the use of sensitive imaging and nuclear medicine techniques has increased {25113771; 25113753}. Bone marrow involvement ranges from 25% to 35% at the time of diagnosis and can often be identified with concurrent PET and CT studies {16327427; 26679865; 16529599; 25998512; 21383296}. Ancillary studies such as flow cytometry, molecular studies, and immunocytochemistry increase the sensitivity of bone marrow biopsies, thus decreasing the frequency of low-stage diseases (stage I and II).

Histopathology

DLBCL-NOS typically replaces, partially or totally, the normal architecture of the lymph node or extranodal tissue in a diffuse pattern. An interfollicular or sinusoidal pattern may be observed when the lymph node involvement is partial. Infiltration of perinodal soft tissue by neoplastic cells is often observed.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

DLBCL-NOS is defined by its large cell cytomorphology, mature B-cell phenotype, and lack of diagnostic criteria that define other specific LBCLs {35732829}, and it is a heterogeneous group of lymphomas. There are two main subtypes recognized: the GCB subtype and the ACB subtype, which differ in their gene expression profiles, immunocytochemical findings, and mutations, but clinically these cell-of-origin subtypes have limited significance, although various immunocytochemistry algorithms are used to distinguish them {35732829}. NGS studies have demonstrated that DLBCL-NOS has heterogeneous genetic drivers, but at present there is no grouping of these drivers to assist clinical management, and no molecular classification has been clarified {35732829}.

The recent fifth-edition WHO classification has 17 specific entities as well as DLBCL-NOS in the “large B-cell lymphoma” (LBCL) grouping, and some of these have new names in this classification; for example, the previous “high-grade B-cell lymphoma” has been renamed as “diffuse large B-cell lymphoma / high-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 rearrangements” (DLBCL/HGBCL-MYC/BCL2) and includes lymphomas with large, intermediate, or blastoid cells and is defined by the dual genetic rearrangements, which are a distinctive germinal-centre gene expression profile {35732829}. Lymphomas with MYC and BCL6 rearrangements are now classified as a type of DLBCL-NOS or HGBCL-NOS {35732829}.

The diagnosis of smears of large cell lymphomas as malignant is usually straightforward, with dispersed large atypical lymphoid cells in a relatively monomorphic population with large nuclei and often prominent nucleoli and “vesicular” chromatin clearing (Pap stain). The differential diagnosis can usually be established by evaluating the clinical presentation, cytomorphological features, and results of ancillary testing to make a diagnosis of LBCL. Within DLBCL-NOS there is a cytomorphological spectrum of subtypes.

Benign processes that can mimic DLBCL-NOS include some cases of florid follicular hyperplasia where the sample may be dominated by centroblasts, viral lymphadenopathy with a prominent immunoblastic reaction, and Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease in the proliferative phase when numerous atypical mononuclear cells may be present. Samples from nodes involved by chronic lymphocytic leukaemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma may contain large numbers of prolymphocytes or paraimmunoblasts, and in some marginal zone lymphomas numerous large cells may be present causing suspicion of transformation. The blastoid and pleomorphic subtypes of mantle cell lymphoma closely mimic DLBCL-NOS, while follicular LBCL (previously known as follicular lymphoma 3B) and DLBCL/HGBCL-MYC/BCL2 may be cytomorphologically and immunophenotypically indistinguishable from DLBCL-NOS in FNAB. Other lymphomas of large B cells, such as primary mediastinal LBCL (PMBCL), may overlap cytomorphologically with DLBCL-NOS but are usually distinguished by clinical or immunophenotypic criteria.

Some cytomorphological subtypes of DLBCL-NOS may favour a non-lymphoid neoplasm. These include cases containing signet-ring cells or spindle cells or showing pseudorosettes or pseudoaggregation and rare instances of DLBCL dominated by myxoid stroma. Myeloid sarcoma can be hard to differentiate in FNAB material, particularly when the patient is aleukaemic or in mixed-lineage acute leukaemias. Cytoplasmic granularity and blastic chromatin pattern are useful cytomorphological features that may be difficult to assess in liquid-based cytopathology slides, and flow cytometry panels should include myeloid markers. Non-lymphoid lesions that enter the differential diagnosis include small cell carcinoma presenting as a mediastinal mass, melanoma and adenocarcinomas showing a diffuse single-cell pattern, seminoma, and round cell sarcomas.

In the fifth-edition WHO classification, three new LBCL entities have been added [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Firstly, LBCL of immune-privileged sites includes primary CNS, vitreoretinal, and testicular LBCLs in immunocompetent patients.

Secondly, fluid overload–associated LBCL is an HHV8-negative lymphoma arising in usually older patients, with chronic mainly pleural effusions related to renal, cardiac, or liver failure {35732829}. The cells are mature B cells, non-plasmablastic, and KSHV/HHV8-negative; they are EBV-positive in less than one third of cases. These features distinguish this lymphoma from primary effusion lymphoma, which is HHV8-positive, and one of the newly grouped KSHV/HHV8-associated B-cell proliferations and lymphomas, along with KSHV/HHV8-positive DLBCL and KSHV/HHV8-positive germinotropic lymphoproliferative disorder.

Thirdly, fibrin-associated LBCL shows large atypical immunoblastic cells embedded in fibrin in anatomical or acquired sites where fibrin has accumulated and acts as an apparent barrier to immunological monitoring. The cells are positive for pan–B-cell markers and EBV. Possible sites include periprosthetic breast, cysts, pseudocysts, chronic haematomas and cardiac prosthetic valves, and myxomas. There is no mass formation or infiltration at these sites, distinguishing this lymphoma from EBV-positive DLBCL (EBV+ DLBCL) and DLBCL associated with chronic inflammation (CI-DLBCL).

The cytopathology of these lesions awaits further clarification and development, and these lesions have not been given a specific section below.

LBCL with IRF4 rearrangement is a type of LBCL with strong expression for IRF4/MUM1, occurring predominantly in children and young adults, with equal sex distribution, and mainly affecting the head and neck lymph nodes and the Waldeyer ring. Extranodal gastrointestinal involvement has been also reported. The median age at presentation is 12 years {23108024; 22855608; 21487109; 22700993}. Histopathologically, it can be entirely diffuse, mixed follicular and diffuse, or completely follicular. There are no cytopathological reports for this entity. The characteristic immunophenotype is strong expression of IRF4/MUM1 in addition to other mature B-cell markers such as CD20, CD79a, and PAX5. Expression of CD10 and BCL2 is seen in two thirds of the cases {21487109}. In cases where CD10, BCL6, and IRF4/MUM1 are all expressed, IRF4 gene rearrangement screening is recommended.

The new classification has also renamed “B-cell lymphoma, unclassifiable, with features intermediate between DLBCL and classic Hodgkin lymphoma” as “mediastinal grey zone lymphoma”, an LBCL specific to the mediastinum with a spectrum of cytomorphological and immunophenotypic features resembling classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL), particularly nodular sclerosis Hodgkin lymphoma, through to PMBCL {35732829}.

T-cell/histiocyte–rich LBCL and lymphomatoid granulomatosis are discussed in the section on “Single very large atypical cell pattern” lymphomas.

Ancillary testing

Ancillary testing is important in the classification of cytopathology samples of DLBCL-NOS, provided resources for testing are available and sufficient sample has been obtained. If sample size is insufficient and/or ancillary testing is not available, CNB or excision biopsy is required.

Immunocytochemical studies for surface and intracellular markers play a key role in classifying DLBCL-NOS into GCB and ABC phenotypes, although some cases are unclassifiable. Cases with the GCB phenotype have a better prognosis, are more likely to respond to standard therapy, and according to the Hans algorithm are either CD10+ or CD10−/BCL6+/MUM1 (IRF4)−, whereas cases with the ABC phenotype are CD10− and either BCL6− or BCL6+/MUM1+ {14504078}. The Hans algorithm is widely used, although there are other algorithms with additional markers {29167021}.

MYC and BCL2 should also be assessed by immunocytochemistry, as overexpression of both in DLBCL/HGBCL-MYC/BCL2 is associated with a poorer prognosis. Most cases are ABC phenotype {22851565}. Several other markers can be assessed by immunocytochemistry. High Ki-67 expression is associated with a poorer prognosis {24597851}. CD5 is positive in about 5–10% of DLBCL-NOS, and such cases are associated with ABC type and poorer survival {25760242}. CD30 is positive in about 10–15% of DLBCL-NOS, and such cases may have a better prognosis {14504078}.

Translocations involving MYC, BCL2, BCL6, and IGH should be assessed by cytogenetics or FISH if possible. MYC rearrangement occurs in 5–15% of DLBCL-NOS and is associated with a worse prognosis, similar to those of DLBCL/HGBCL-MYC/BCL2 and LBCL with MYC and BCL6 rearrangements, which together make up about 10% of LBCL {21119107; 26980727}. Paradoxically, these high-grade lymphomas are usually GCB type {29167021}.

Immunophenotyping by flow cytometry may be less important in DLBCL-NOS than in small B-cell lymphomas, because assessment of monoclonality by light chain restriction is less critical in DLBCL where the FNAB is clearly malignant {30549186}. But immunocytochemistry is the preferred methodology to assess intracellular markers such as MYC, BCL2, BCL6, and MUM1. Nevertheless, flow cytometry can quickly assign lineage to a suspicious population. The pan–B-cell markers CD19 and CD20 are positive in the vast majority of DLBCL-NOS cases, the cells of which usually have higher forward and side scatter and dimmer CD45 than normal lymphocytes {30549186}. Some cases of DLBCL-NOS lack surface light chains, and some cases are not detected by flow cytometry due to the fragility of the cells and a lack of intact cells for flow cytometry to assess {26352428; 37632200}.

2. ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive large B-cell lymphoma (ALK+ LBCL) is a diffuse monomorphic neoplasm of large B cells with a plasmablastic immunophenotype and ALK expression due to ALK rearrangement.

Clinical features and imaging

This is a very rare type of LBCL, accounting for < 1% of LBCL. It affects males more than females, with an M:F ratio of 5:1, and is more common in young males. The age range is wide (9–85 years), with a median age of 43 years. Approximately one third of cases occur in children, and there is no relationship with history of immunosuppression {19636007; 17277765}. The main presentation is lymphadenopathy with occasional mediastinal mass. Involvements of extranodal sites can occur, including stomach, tongue, spleen, skin, nasopharynx, liver, and soft tissue {14576483; 9057627; 12763927; 16086416; 17277765; 17509395; 12750159; 12816858; 31735345}. In 60% of the cases, patients present with advanced stage (stage III and IV), and bone marrow infiltration is seen in about 25% of cases {19636007; 31735345}.

Although this subtype of LBCL is quite aggressive, it is stage-dependent. The median overall survival ranges from 11 to 156 months, and it is shorter for stage III/IV and longer for earlier stages. Because these neoplasms are CD20-negative, anti-CD20 therapy such as rituximab is not helpful {19636007; 17277765}.

Histopathology

Lymph node involvement is characterized by diffuse infiltration of the node, predominantly expanding the sinusoids, by large atypical monomorphic plasmacytic immunoblast-like cells with large pale nuclei; large central single nucleoli; and abundant, often eccentric, pale cytoplasm. Large neoplastic multinucleated cells can also be found.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Variably cellular with a predominant single dispersed cell pattern Focal pseudocohesion with aggregates, especially in highly cellular specimens Monomorphic cell population of large round cells with round to more pleomorphic nuclei Single central large nucleolus Abundant, pale, often eccentric cytoplasm Occasional large multinucleated neoplastic cells

Reference(s): {23457005; 24228132}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Based solely on the cytomorphological features, the differential diagnosis is wide and includes other subtypes of LBCL, multiple myeloma, and metastatic carcinoma. Immunocytochemistry and flow cytometry are required for an initial specific diagnosis.

Ancillary testing

The key oncogenic factor of this tumour is ALK overexpression due to the fusion protein generated by the translocation of the ALK locus on chromosome 2, t(2;17)(p23;q23).

The immunocytochemical findings can be variable; however, the characteristic features are strong expression of ALK protein, EMA, IRF4/MUM1, and plasma cell markers such as CD138. The neoplastic cells are usually negative or only focally positive for B-cell markers such as CD20, CD79a, and PAX5, as well as CD45 and CD30. Rarely, the neoplastic cells can express cytokeratin, which may lead to misdiagnosis as carcinoma, especially with EMA positivity and the sinusoidal infiltration pattern. Occasional cytoplasmic expression of immunoglobulin can be found, especially IgA and less commonly IgG. EBV and HHV8 are usually negative. Although there are exceptions, the T-cell markers are usually negative {19636007; 17509395; 31735345; 9057627}.

3. High-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration

Definition

High-grade B-cell lymphoma with 11q aberration (HGBCL-11q) is an aggressive mature B-cell lymphoma with a morphology similar to that of Burkitt lymphoma (BL) or an intermediate to blastoid appearance and a characteristic chromosome 11q gain/loss pattern. Cases with concomitant MYC rearrangements are excluded.

Clinical features and pathological features

HGBCL-11q was previously known as “Burkitt-like lymphoma with 11q aberration”, and it is an aggressive lymphoma resembling BL or a blastoid subtype, negative for the MYC rearrangement, with a chromosome 11q alteration, lacking the 1q gain seen frequently in BL, and showing a more complex karyotype than BL {26104998; 20661666; 24398325; 30504293; 33685720}. Lymphomas resembling BL cytomorphologically but lacking MYC rearrangements should be tested for the 11q gain/loss pattern.

The clinical course is similar to that of classic BL, and due to the small numbers of reported cases, further work is needed to allow its diagnosis using cytopathology and appropriate ancillary tests. Very similar cases have been reported in patients with a history of transplantation {24398325}. The immunophenotypic features are also similar to those of BL, although there are subtle distinguishing features including greater cytopathological pleomorphism, an occasional follicular pattern, and more frequent nodal presentation.

4. EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Epstein–Barr virus–positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (EBV+ DLBCL) is an LBCL in which the majority of the neoplastic cells harbour EBV, occurring in patients without a history of lymphoma or underlying immunodeficiency/dysregulation other than immunosenescence. The neoplasm should not fulfil criteria for other EBV+ lymphoproliferative disorders or lymphomas.

Clinical features and imaging

EBV+ DLBCL accounts for 5–15% of DLBCL in Asian and Latin American populations but less than 5% of cases in patients of European decent. Most cases are in patients over the age of 50, with a peak in the eighth decade of life, but the disorder can also present in younger individuals [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Nodal involvement is predominant in younger patients, whereas extranodal disease is more common in older patients and is especially found in the lungs and gastrointestinal tract.

Histopathology

Cases are divided into polymorphic and monomorphic types. In polymorphic cases, 2–40% of cells are large atypical lymphoid cells resembling immunoblasts or Hodgkin cells in an inflammatory background of T cells and histiocytes. The monomorphic cases are more similar to DLBCL-NOS, with a diffuse proliferation of medium to large lymphoid cells. Necrosis may occur in both types, more frequently in the monomorphic type {34427583}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

The cytopathological features of EBV+ DLBCL are the same as those of other types of LBCL and cannot be distinguished from them.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

EBV+ DLBCL is a diagnosis of exclusion, in which the neoplasm does not satisfy the diagnostic criteria of other types of EBV-positive disorders. EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) positivity is essential for the diagnosis, although cut-offs for the percentage of positive cells are inconsistently applied {32072672}. Demonstration of EBV positivity is required to distinguish the monomorphic type from EBV-negative DLBCL. The polymorphic type may resemble T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma. Distinction from EBV-positive disorders such as EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer and lymphomatoid granulomatosis can be difficult. EBV+ DLBCL is cytomorphologically and immunophenotypically very similar to EBV-positive disorders in patients with immune deficiency, and the distinction must be made on clinical grounds [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Ancillary testing

The majority of the large atypical B-lineage cells are positive for EBER by FISH. The neoplastic cells are positive for CD20 and other pan–B-cell antigens, and they are positive for CD30 and PDL1 in the majority of cases {31958882; 34427583; 32072672}.

5. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma associated with chronic inflammation

Definition

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma associated with chronic inflammation (CI-DLBCL) is an EBV-associated neoplasm occurring in the setting of longstanding chronic inflammation involving confined natural body spaces or acquired tissue spaces. Pyothorax-associated lymphoma (PAL) is the prototypical form developing in the pleural cavity of patients with longstanding pyothorax.

Clinical features and imaging

In PAL, patients may present with chest or back pain, fever, or respiratory symptoms, and imaging commonly reveals a mass in the pleura. Other sites include bone, joints and periarticular soft tissue associated with chronic osteomyelitis, metallic implant insertion, surgical mesh insertion, and chronic skin venous ulcer. Involved bone typically shows lytic lesions [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Histopathology

Most cases show centroblastic or immunoblastic large cells in sheets, with infiltrating variably sclerotic and inflammatory tissue. Massive necrosis and angiocentric growth may be present [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

The cytopathological features of CI-DLBCL are the same as those of DLBCL-NOS and cannot be distinguished from those of other forms of LBCL, while the site and presentation provide the diagnosis.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Cases with plasmablastic differentiation are distinguished from plasmablastic lymphoma by the clinical setting. The presence of a mass lesion is characteristic of CI-DLBCL and distinguishes it from fibrin-associated LBCL. PAL differs from primary effusion lymphoma in that the latter is KSHV/HHV8-positive and generally lacks a tumour mass [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Ancillary testing

The tumour cells are positive for EBER by FISH {33564306}. Immunoglobulin gene clonal rearrangement is usually detectable {32072672}. Most cases express pan–B-cell markers, but some cases show plasmablastic differentiation with expression of IRF4/MUM1 and CD138 {12023576; 32072672}.

6. Plasmablastic lymphoma

Definition

Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) is an aggressive lymphoid neoplasm composed of large atypical lymphoid B-cells with plasmablastic and/or immunoblastic cytomorphology and a terminal B-cell differentiation phenotype, predominantly arising at extranodal sites.

Clinical features and imaging

PBL usually arises in the setting of immunodeficiency but can present as transformation of pre-existing follicular lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, or conventional myeloma. It usually occurs extranodally, with the nasal cavity and oropharynx being the most common sites (involved in 50% of cases), followed by the gastrointestinal tract, bone, and soft tissue sites including skin. Rarely, nodal involvement is found without evidence of demonstrable extranodal disease. PBL is frequently, but not invariably, associated with EBV, especially in the setting of HIV/AIDS.

Histopathology

PBL is characterized by sheets of large atypical lymphoid cells, which show plasmablastic and/or immunoblastic morphology and infiltrate extensively. Mitotic activity is high, and individual cell necrosis and a starry-sky pattern with tingible-body macrophages can be seen.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Highly cellular smears Single dispersed large lymphoid cells with plasmablastic or immunoblastic morphology in variable proportions, often against a background showing marked basophilia Plasmablasts are characterized by large nuclei with immature reticular (Giemsa stain) or clumped (Pap stain) chromatin, one or more prominent nucleoli, and limited to moderate deeply basophilic eccentric cytoplasm, where a well-defined perinuclear clearing or hof is usually not seen. Immunoblastic cells have large, round, central nuclei, with immature fine (Giemsa stain) or vesicular (Pap stain) chromatin, single large nucleoli, and pale (Pap stain) cytoplasm. Multinucleated cells and polylobated nuclei may be seen.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

PBL, although rare, should be included in the differential diagnosis when a high-grade lymphoma is encountered in the setting of constitutional or acquired immunodeficiency. Cytomorphologically and immunophenotypically, the principal differential diagnosis is plasmablastic (anaplastic) myeloma, which may arise de novo or through transformation of conventional myeloma.

Ancillary testing

Cells of PBL express CD138, CD38, cytoplasmic immunoglobulin, XBP1, and PRDM1/Blimp1, and they are negative for CD20 and show reduced or absent CD45 and PAX5. CD79a is positive in about 40% of cases. CD56 is positive in about 30% of cases. HHV8 is negative. EBER is positive in 60% of cases, usually those associated with HIV. FISH can be used to demonstrate MYC translocations in about 60% of cases.

The immunophenotype of plasmablastic myeloma and PBL may be the same except for EBER positivity, which favours PBL over myeloma. The cytomorphology and immunophenotype of DLBCL-NOS can also overlap with those of PBL, especially when EBV is positive, but expression of CD20 and strong complete expression of PAX5 favour DLBCL-NOS. CD45-negative and usually CD20-negative solid-type primary effusion lymphoma is distinguished from PBL by expression of HHV8.

7. Primary cutaneous diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type

Definition

Primary cutaneous diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, leg type (PCLBCL-LT) is a lymphoma composed exclusively of centroblasts and immunoblasts, most commonly arising in the leg.

Clinical features and imaging

PCLBCL-LT has a poor prognosis. It is most often diagnosed on the legs (80%) of the elderly. It frequently relapses and has a tendency towards extracutaneous dissemination in lymph nodes, as well as CNS, lung, and other visceral organs. Patients usually present with one or multiple rapidly growing, red or bluish-red skin tumours that can ulcerate {30635287; 17875875}.

Histopathology

PCLBCL-LT is characterized by dermal and/or subcutaneous infiltration by sheets of immunoblastic and centroblastic cells in a diffuse pattern, sparing the epidermis.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Low to moderate cellularity Monotonous population of large atypical lymphoid cells with round to more pleomorphic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and perinucleolar chromatin clearing (“vesicular” nuclei) in the Pap stain

Reference(s): {28631652; 18306596}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

PCLBCL-LT is composed of large cells making up > 80% of the infiltrate, which has no extracutaneous involvement demonstrated at staging investigations. FNAB material is usually derived from lymph nodes, where the diagnosis of an LBCL can be made, but specific typing is dependent on the clinical presentation and site of the skin lesion and ancillary tests. The differential diagnosis includes patterns of primary cutaneous follicular lymphoma and secondary involvement of the skin by systemic LBCLs, which is rarely seen in FNAB material.

Ancillary testing

PCLBCL-LT may have an ABC immunophenotype. It expresses pan–B-cell antigens as well as other markers commonly expressed: BCL2 (> 90%), BCL6 (60%), MUM1/IRF4 (85%), MYC (80%), and FOXP1 (90%) {17875875; 29581544}. CD10 is uncommonly positive (15%), CD5 and CD30 are rarely expressed, and EBV-encoded small RNA 1 (EBER1) is negative {17875875; 30113335; 29581544}. Aberrant expression of p63 is observed in 41–70%, and PDL1 and PDL2 can be expressed, often on a subset of cells {26332336; 29112015}. MYC rearrangements are seen in 33% of cases {30113335}, while BCL6 and BCL2 rearrangements are rare. The immunoglobulin genes are clonally rearranged, with a high frequency of somatic hypermutation of the immunoglobulin gene variable regions {30635287; 35183824}.

8. Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL) is an aggressive extranodal LBCL characterized by the proliferation of large neoplastic B cells virtually exclusively within the lumina of blood vessels.

Clinical features and imaging

IVLBCL represents 0.05% of all cases of LBCL. It affects patients in different age groups. The clinical IVLBCL diagnosis may be difficult, and it often is made at autopsy [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. Patients may present with a complexity of symptoms reflecting possible organ dysfunction secondary to vascular obstructions, or with nonspecific symptoms such as fever. Multiorgan failure, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, and haemophagocytic syndrome or symptoms related to involvement of the CNS and skin may be present {17488659; 11706077; 16985183; 19363707}. Three clinical subtypes of IVLBCL are recognized: classic, haemophagocytic syndrome–associated, and cutaneous.

Despite a relatively high proportion of false negatives, staging workup is recommended with contrasted whole-body CT, contrasted whole-brain MRI, peripheral blood smear, cerebrospinal fluid cytopathology, and biochemical examination and bone marrow biopsy {17577023}. PET may also be useful for the early diagnosis of IVLBCL and can guide biopsies of affected organs {20675506}.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of IVLBCL is made by demonstrating the presence of large atypical lymphoid cells within small to medium blood vessels. The lymphoma is usually widely disseminated in extranodal sites, including bone marrow, spleen, liver, lungs, skin, nervous system, and (rarely) blood. Skin biopsies, even without any obvious abnormality, may be diagnostic {18053461}, and biopsy of other sites of suspected involvement may be undertaken in the appropriate clinical setting.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Medium to large atypical lymphoid cells Round to oval large nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and “vesicular” perinucleolar chromatin clearing (Pap stain) Scant to moderate cytoplasm

Reference(s): {26027996; 19687591; 21053541}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Cytopathological diagnosis of IVLBCL has been reported in very few cases. IVLBCL is a rare systemic disease characterized by the occlusion of arterioles, capillaries, and venules throughout the body by malignant lymphomatous cells. Infiltration of the lungs, kidneys, adrenal glands, and prostate is common, but only 5–9% of patients show peripheral blood involvement {8156502}. Delays in diagnosis due to the subtlety and focal nature of the intravascular infiltrates often lead to a terminal disease, with death before chemotherapy is initiated. R-CHOP plus CNS prophylaxis is the treatment of choice in patients without initial nervous system involvement {18699850}. CNS involvement and recurrence are still challenging despite the improvement in clinical outcomes induced by rituximab plus chemotherapy. CNS prophylaxis is strongly recommended, because the rate of CNS recurrence at 3 years is still as high as 25% {21448935}.

Ancillary testing

Lymphoma cells are positive for B markers including CD19, CD20, CD79a, and HLA-DR, and they can be positive for BCL2, MYC, MUM1/IRF4, BCL6, and CD10 in 90%, 70%, 75%, 25–60%, and 10% of the cases, respectively. They can also express CD5 in about 30–50% of cases {10439806; 9796727}. Ki-67 ranges from 60% to 100%. IVLBCLs often do not express adhesion and homing receptors, which may explain their intravascular location {2450463}. Most cases have a complex karyotype. Involvement of chromosome 1 is most common, reported in 72% of cases, followed by chromosomes 3, 6q, 14, and 18 {29767679}. Gene mutation analysis has shown mutations in PIM1 (67%), MYD88 (44–59%), CD79B (26–67%), PRDM1 (44%), BTG2 (22%), and EZH2 (4%) {29514783}. Immunoglobulin genes are clonally rearranged.

9. Primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma

Definition

Primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) is a mature aggressive B-cell lymphoma of putative thymic B-cell origin, arising in the anterior mediastinum, with distinctive clinical, immunophenotypic, and molecular features.

Clinical features and imaging

PMBCL occurs predominantly in young adults in their third decade, with a female predominance (F:M ratio: 2:1). It can also occur in children. PMBCL may occur before, simultaneously with, or at the time of relapse of nodular sclerosis CHL {29296913; 16224207}. Familial clustering has been described based on shared gene mutations identified in affected families {23457195}.

The initial symptoms include cough and/or dyspnoea or superior vena cava syndrome. PMBCL is primarily localized in the anterior-superior mediastinum, with variable extension to lung, pleura, or pericardium. Most patients present with stage I or II disease. LBCLs with features of PMBCL not arising in the mediastinum should be classified as LBCL with distinct phenotypic and genetic features {33684939}. Supraclavicular lymph node involvement is common. Involvement of distant sites is uncommon at initial presentation, but bone marrow involvement and extranodal involvement can be seen at relapse {34496020}.

Histopathology

PMBCL is characterized by a diffuse infiltration of medium to large atypical lymphoid cells, with abundant pale cytoplasm and round or ovoid nuclei with small to sometimes large nucleoli distributed in strands or sheets in a fibrotic background. The amount of fibrosis ranges from finely reticular with alveolar compartmentalization of malignant cells to coarsely collagenous {3491784; 10029446; 3923821}. A variable number of admixed reactive lymphocytes, histiocytes, and more rarely granulocytes may be seen. Remnants of thymic epithelium (Hassall’s corpuscles) and foci of necrosis can occasionally be noted.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderately cellular to hypercellular specimens Intermediate to large atypical lymphoid cells with high N:C ratio and variable eccentric cytoplasm Round to oval to more pleomorphic and lobulated nuclei, granular chromatin (Giemsa) that can be “vesicular” with perinucleolar clearing (Pap) 2–4 nucleoli that can be close to the nuclear membrane, or in some cells single and large Cell block may show lymphoid cells associated with a dense fibrous background.

Reference(s): {9500649}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The cytopathological features can diagnose the lesion as LBCL, but distinguishing DLBCL (DLBCL-NOS) involving the mediastinum from PMBCL can be difficult, even on the basis of histopathological and routine immunophenotypic studies. Knowledge of the clinical setting is critical, given that DLBCL-NOS shows involvement of mediastinal lymph nodes rather than the thymic region, as well as systemic extramediastinal disease. The presence of remnants of thymic epithelium admixed with the large lymphoid cells supports the diagnosis of PMBCL, but the atypical lymphoid cells resemble DLBCL-NOS.

Ancillary testing

PMBCLs express CD45 and B-cell lineage markers such as CD19, CD20, and CD79a. They also express B-lineage transcription regulator markers such as BOB1, PU.1, OCT2, and PAX5 {7887454; 12507907; 14694522}. They are usually positive for CD30 with heterogeneous and weak intensity, in contrast with CHL, which shows strong staining {10439805; 12507907}. Other markers frequently associated with PMBCL include CD23, MAL (myelin and lymphocyte protein), CD200, PDL1, and PDL2 {15569053; 10552968; 12507907; 22899296; 27879516; 25025450}. Strong expression of MUM1 is also present, with variable expression of BCL6 and BCL2, while CD10 expression is uncommon {15569053; 11688462; 12507907; 27419920}. Expression of c-REL (65–77%), TRAF1 (62–86%), TNFAIP2 (87%), and pSTAT6 (73%) is frequently observed as a result of NF-κB, TNF, and JAK/STAT signalling pathway activation {17197926; 22899296; 21921781; 15044251; 15870177}. Detection of CIITA rearrangements/mutations, copy-number gains of CD274 (PDL1) and PDCD1LG2 (PDL2), or gene point mutations in the SOCS1 and STAT6 genes also supports the diagnosis {26549456; 20628145; 24497532; 31697821; 30257882}. In a subset of PMBCLs, these mutations arise in a setting of aberrant somatic hypermutation, likely mediated by activation-induced cytidine deaminase {17638864; 31697821}. The mature B-cell phenotype can often be incomplete (e.g. immunoglobulin expression is frequently absent) {11675349}. PMBCL often lacks HLA class I and/or class II molecule expression {3103712; 3491784}. The presence of EBV infection {8669537} and CD15 expression are rare {27419920}.

PMBCL gene expression overlaps with that of nodular sclerosis CHL and is markedly distinct from that of other aggressive B-cell lymphomas, in particular DLBCL-NOS (ABC or GCB phenotype) {12975453; 12933571; 30257882}. Absence of CD45 and B-cell antigens like CD79a, CD19, and transcription factors, as well as strong expression of CD30 on all tumour cells favours CHL over PMBCL {16224207; 20971819}. EBV positivity favours the diagnosis of CHL, although it can also be observed in very rare cases of PMBCL.

Another differential diagnosis is mediastinal grey zone lymphomas, which show CD30 expression with partial or complete loss of the B-cell markers and/or strong CD15 expression {30540571; 29296913} and usually require an excisional biopsy for diagnosis.

10. High-grade B-cell lymphoma NOS

Definition

High-grade B-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified (HGBCL-NOS) represents a heterogeneous type of aggressive mature B-cell lymphoma composed of medium-sized or blastoid cells that does not fulfil the diagnostic criteria for other defined lymphoma entities.

Clinical features and imaging

HGBCL-NOS affects elderly patients, without sex predilection. Patients generally have a poor outcome.

Histopathology

HGBCL-NOS generally shows a diffuse proliferation of medium to large cells without stromal reaction or fibrosis. A starry-sky pattern of tingible-body macrophages distributed among the atypical lymphoid cells, apoptosis, and many mitoses may be present. This subtype is morphologically heterogeneous, with some monomorphic cases resembling BL and other cases exhibiting more variation in nuclear size and nucleolar features.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Highly cellular monomorphic smears Dispersed large atypical lymphoid cells with stripped nuclei and lymphoid cell cytoplasmic fragments (“lymphoglandular bodies”) in the background Large nuclei with slightly irregular contours Small nucleoli and fine but not very dense chromatin Slightly basophilic cytoplasm Tingible-body macrophages imparting a starry-sky pattern may be present in some cases.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

HGBCL-NOS is a heterogeneous group in NGS analyses. It shares some cytomorphological, immunophenotypic, and genetic features with other types of DLBCL and BL, and it is a diagnosis by exclusion of these more common entities. Some cases may show a blastoid cytomorphology.

Ancillary testing

The lymphoma cells are CD20-positive. Most cases show expression of BCL6, while CD10 expression is variable. MUM1 is absent. The Ki-67 proliferation index shows a heterogenous positivity. By definition, the presence of a BCL2 and/or a BCL6 rearrangement in combination with a MYC rearrangement is not present, but some 20–35% of cases have a MYC rearrangement, with or without increased copy numbers or amplification of 18q21 involving BCL2. Cases with BCL2 rearrangement and increased copy number or high-level amplification of MYC have also been identified {25103070; 23600716}.
