6.1.1.4 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

In the hierarchical system of the WHO System, the “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low to intermediate ROM and maintain a high negative predictive value (NPV) for a “Benign” diagnosis, while the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to have a higher ROM and provide for a high positive predictive value (PPV) approaching 100% for a “Malignant” categorization. The standardized categories stratify the ROM and allow for the development of diagnostic management recommendations in an algorithm based on the ROM.

A review of 10 recent studies {37378919; 37231736; 36917967; 35148033; 34957622; 34817104; 34661975; 34289486; 34441249; 33830657} based on categorization using the Sydney System, which has a structure and definitions very similar to those of the WHO System, showed that the “Atypical” category made up a highly variable number of cases and percentages in each study, varying from 5 of 1500 (0.33%) to 52 of 349 (14.9%) {39097796}.

The ROM also varied greatly, from 29% to 77% when two outliers were removed {39097796}. These studies represent different population cohorts and a marked variation in the use of ultrasound to guide the biopsy, the percentage of FNAB procedures performed by cytopathologists, the use of ROSE, and most importantly the use of ancillary tests.

A recent meta-analysis that pooled nine of these studies found a ROM of 65% {38096796}. Both the range and the pooled ROM are at best estimates of the ROM for the “Atypical” category of the WHO System, and future prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to further elucidate a more accurate ROM range.

The use of ancillary techniques, particularly immunoprofiling applied to direct smears, cytospins, liquid-based cytopathology, and cell block sections with different fixatives such as methanol, alcohol, and formalin, can influence the diagnostic yield.

It is recommended that clinical and imaging findings are reviewed and correlated before classifying a specimen as “Atypical”, and assessment of the presenting history and clinical signs and wherever possible imaging is an absolute prerequisite before the FNAB procedure or the issuing of an “Atypical” report.

The management of an “Atypical” categorization is to review the clinical and imaging findings, review the cytopathology features with colleagues and more experienced cytopathologists, and discuss the findings in a multidisciplinary setting. If there is material available for ancillary testing this should be performed, but care should be taken in performing and interpreting ancillary tests on scant material in cell blocks {29575423; 28639854; 30917368}.

In many cases a repeat FNAB with or without a complementary CNB will be recommended. Wherever possible, the triage of the case with ROSE will maximize the use of ancillary testing. The selection of further management will be dependent on the clinical expectation as to the most likely diagnosis as infection, benign lesion, metastasis, or lymphoma; the status of the patient; the location of the lymph node as superficial or deep or location in spleen or thymus; and the local expertise and infrastructure.
