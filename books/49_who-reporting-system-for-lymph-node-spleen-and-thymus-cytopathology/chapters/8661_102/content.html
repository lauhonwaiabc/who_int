8.6.6.1 Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma

Definition

Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (NLPHL) is a germinal-centre–derived B-cell neoplasm composed of scattered large neoplastic B cells with multilobated nuclei (lymphocyte-predominant [LP] cells) within nodules dominated by mantle zone B cells and follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), although in some cases nodules may be infrequent.

Clinical features and imaging

The majority of patients with NLPHL present with localized peripheral lymphadenopathy, with most cases identified at early stages and having indolent courses. More advanced disease with B symptoms and organ involvement is uncommon. NLPHL affects people at all ages, with a male predominance. NLPHL is uncommon and accounts for only 5–10% of all cases of Hodgkin lymphoma {16009944; 29222274; 34208705; 31785822; 33274673; 31344253}. Progression to large B-cell lymphoma has been reported in up to 5% of patients with NLPHL.

Histopathology

NLPHL extensively or completely effaces the lymph node, with six patterns described, and may vary from the typical nodular pattern to diffuse or mixed nodular and diffuse {14508396; 20923296}. In most cases of NLPHL, large LP cells or “popcorn cells” with multilobated or folded nuclei with multiple nucleoli are present amid a mix of plentiful lymphocytes, an expanded network of FDCs, plasma cells, epithelioid histiocytes, and in some instances eosinophils. Sclerosis is uncommon in primary presentations.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

High cellularity Large LP “popcorn cells” with high N:C ratio, nuclear multilobation and folds and one or more nucleoli, and often limited pale cytoplasm (Giemsa) Nucleoli are often smaller than in classic Reed–Sternberg cells and are eosinophilic (Pap) or pale (Giemsa stain). Background population of predominantly small lymphocytes with few to no eosinophils and plasma cells Epithelioid histiocytes often present

Discussion and differential diagnosis

NLPHL is more correctly called “nodular lymphocyte-predominant B-cell lymphoma” [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]], but the historical grouping is maintained to support current clinical trials.

The initial diagnosis of NLPHL may be difficult due to the mixed background with small lymphocytes predominating and a variable number of LP cells, and misdiagnosis as reactive lymph nodes can occur. Ancillary testing is frequently required along with excision biopsy. Recurrences of known NLPHL are more readily confirmed by the combination of cytopathology and ancillary testing {32343479}. Vague nodularity may be identifiable in touch preparations and cell blocks, as well as in well-made FNAB smears.

The differential diagnosis includes T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma, which overlaps with NLPHL, classic Hodgkin lymphoma (particularly the lymphocyte-rich subtype), and follicular hyperplasia. Megakaryocytes with multilobated nuclei and multiple small nucleoli can mimic LP cells in smears from lymph nodes and lung when extramedullary haematopoiesis is present at these and other body sites.

Ancillary testing

Gene expression studies have demonstrated that LP cells are similar to the large lesional cells of T-cell/histiocyte–rich large B-cell lymphoma {18794340; 33567641}. Unlike classic Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells, LP cells exhibit a phenotype of germinal-centre–like B cells and are generally positive for CD45, CD20, CD75, CD79a, PAX5, BCL6, BOB1, and OCT2 and negative for CD30 and CD15. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is typically negative in LP cells. The LP cells may be surrounded by a collar of helper T cells, which are positive for CD4 and PD1. CD21 immunocytochemistry can highlight an expanded network of FDCs. Mutations of PAX5, MYC, and other genes have been reported in NLPHL {16614247}.
