1.1.1.7 Lymphoid proliferations and lymphomas associated with immune deficiency and dysregulation

Definition

Lymphoid proliferations and lymphomas associated with immune deficiency and dysregulation (IDD) include the following:

1. Hyperplasias that are heterogeneous, non-clonal lymphoid and/or plasmacytic proliferations, with preservation of underlying tissue architecture, usually but not exclusively driven by EBV or KSHV/HHV8.

2. Polymorphic lymphoproliferative disorders (polymorphic LPDs) that are heterogeneous lymphoid cell infiltrates, with variable numbers of B cells exhibiting a full spectrum of B-cell differentiation, that efface the architecture of involved tissue and can be monoclonal or oligoclonal, usually EBV-driven, with no or few genetic alterations.

3. Lymphomas arising in IDD cover a spectrum of lymphoma types frequently but not exclusively associated with EBV and/or KSHV/HHV8.

4. Inborn error of immunity–associated lymphoproliferative disorders (IEI-LPDs) are mass-forming or infiltrative proliferations of B cells or (more rarely) T cells that arise as a result of an underlying inborn error of immunity.

Clinical features and imaging

IDD-associated hyperplasias may occur as follicular hyperplasia (FH), infectious mononucleosis–like hyperplasia (IMH), plasmacytic hyperplasia (PCH), and KSHV/HHV8-positive multicentric Castleman disease [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Polymorphic LPDs in IDD are described in any immunodeficiency setting but have been more commonly associated with transplantation and account for 20–80% of posttransplant LPDs [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {9587133; 17469100; 21385849; 29112013; 28395107}.

IDD-associated lymphomas present clinically with features similar to those of their counterparts in immunocompetent patients, as well as findings that are more specific for the type of IDD. The cytomorphological features of the individual lymphomas, such as large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), classic Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL), and Burkitt lymphoma (BL), as well as the time of development, are related to the severity of immunodeficiency and/or dysregulation of the immune cell population involved [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. IDD-associated lymphomas occur mainly in the setting of HIV infection–related immunodeficiency, posttransplantation immunosuppression, inborn error of immunity, and other (iatrogenic) immunodeficiencies. A large number are associated with EBV and/or HHV8 [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] {9042803; 8392401; 1328576; 10950375; 7700311; 28506687}.

In the clinical setting of HIV infection, LPDs can be noted in nodal, extranodal, and unusual sites {23442063; 24164103; 29021921; 34514344}. Patients usually present with single lymph node enlargement, or bulky disease and high tumour burden in addition to atypical features, such as a leukaemic presentation in BL. Lymphoma may be the presenting illness, and an HIV test should be considered for patients who present with aggressive B-cell lymphomas. Clinical and imaging findings depend on tumour site; for example, tumours of the CNS may be multifocal, which is apparent on brain imaging {34125369}. #28893Box 1.01 lists the types of LPDs most commonly associated with HIV infection. In individuals who have been HIV-infected for many years, primary CNS lymphomas and LBCLs tend to occur; in HIV-infected individuals with better immunity (as evidenced by higher CD4 counts), BL and CHL can be seen. The paradoxical increased incidence of CHL in the highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) era with relative preservation of CD4+ T cells is a complex phenomenon possibly related to a direct role of HIV other than just immunosuppression {26773045; 21368291}. HIV-related factors and EBV infection play a central role in the pathogenesis of LBCL and BL.

EBV infection seems to be relevant for the genesis of lymphoma, particularly in primary CNS lymphoma, where LMP1 is always expressed {28506687}. Mutations of TNFAIP3 with subsequent loss of the A20 protein, NF-κB pathway activation, and other genetic alterations (involving MYC, BCL6, and TP53) have been described {21406721; 12714522}. IGH::MYC t(8;14) is present in HIV-related BL, and point mutations in TP53 and MYC, as well as EBV latency type I, are also commonly seen {23942293; 23451747}. The interaction between HIV/immunosuppression and EBV infection in CHL is confirmed by the consistent positivity for LMP1 and EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) {19339934}.

Posttransplant IDD lymphomas show strong association with EBV and possible regression, in a minority of patients, of corresponding entities through reduction of immunosuppressive therapy and restoration of the host immunocompetence {8620407; 11100284}. The prevalence of posttransplant lymphomas in IDD ranges from 1% to 20% among transplant patients, depending on various factors, including the rate of immunosuppression and the transplanted organ: bone marrow and kidney are associated with the lowest risk, and intestine with the highest risk {27189056}. Young age, serum negativity for EBV at the time of transplant, and specific therapies are additional risk factors {29414277}.

Lymphomas in the inborn error of immunity setting tend to present in the first and third decades of life, with high stage, aggressive disease, and increased risk of EBV+ LPD-associated haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

Histopathology

The main morphological features of the most common types of IDD-associated lymphoproliferative lesions are essentially similar to those of cases occurring in immunocompetent hosts, which are described elsewhere in this volume. Some pertinent differences include the following:

1. IDD-associated reactive lymphadenopathies show histopathological features similar to those of the corresponding non–IDD-related, suppurative, granulomatous, or follicular patterns.

2. IDD-associated hyperplasias are characterized by the preservation of the lymph node histopathological architecture, whereas the different compartments may be expanded or regressed. FH shows expansion of the cortical area with enlarged lymphoid follicles of different sizes. Follicles have enlarged dark zones and thin or absent mantle zones, with variable reduction of the interfollicular areas, paracortex, and medulla [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]]. In immunoblastic hyperplasia (IBH), there is a marked expansion of paracortex with a significant number of immunoblasts; in the background, together with small lymphocytes, plasma cells, and histiocytes, large Hodgkin-like cells may be present, mimicking an LBCL. PCH shows hyperplastic follicles and numerous plasmacytoid cells or mature plasma cells and immunoblasts in the interfollicular areas. KSHV/HHV8-positive multicentric Castleman disease shows hyperplastic features with a similar follicular pattern {17134734; 10849316; 11358009; 31116708; 21385849}.

3. In polymorphic LPD, the lymph node architecture is partially or totally effaced by a heterogeneous infiltrate including small lymphoid cells, plasmacytoid cells, mature plasma cells, plasmablasts, histiocytes, and immunoblasts. The immunoblasts may include pleomorphic and/or Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg (HRS)-like cells. EBV is present in variable proportions of both small and large cells. In some cases, T cells and histiocytes can represent the predominant population.

4. There is conspicuous plasmacytic differentiation in both nodal and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (MZL), and in nodal MZL in IDD settings this is generally associated with class-switched antibody production from IgM to IgG or IgE.

5. HRS-like cells are present in LBCL.

6. BL shows greater variation in cell size and shape, as well as plasmacytoid differentiation with abundant eccentric cytoplasm containing immunoglobulins and a single central nucleolus {1868025; 19339934}.

7. There is a preponderance of mixed cellularity and nodular sclerosis subtypes in CHL with stronger and more uniform expression of CD20 and often expression of CD79a and CD19 {28395108; 25999451}.

8. T-cell lymphomas (TCLs), including NK/T-cell lymphoma, anaplastic large cell lymphoma, hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (HSTL), mycosis fungoides, and nasal-type NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTCL), have been rarely reported in the posttransplant IDD setting {28395105; 31257346; 23402267; 11300939; 7844598}, and their extremely rare incidence does not support a possible relationship with the posttransplant IDD. Further, with the exception of ENKTCL and systemic TCL, the majority of TCLs are EBV-negative {28395105; 31257346}.

9. The CD4:CD8 ratio in the microenvironment is inverted in proportion to the numbers of CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells predominate {27750386; 23307058; 21368291}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features
IDD hyperplasias: The cytopathological features are similar to those of the corresponding suppurative, granulomatous, or follicular patterns. FH may show a large number of germinal-centre cells and require ancillary techniques to exclude non-Hodgkin lymphoma. IMH (IDD): The cytopathological features are similar to those seen in infectious mononucleosis or other related viral lymphadenopathies. Mature lymphocytes intermingle with follicle centre cells and a variable number of immunoblasts with large nuclei, one or two large nucleoli, and eccentric cytoplasm, as well as large lymphoid cells with one or two nucleoli and a rim of dense blue cytoplasm (“blue cells”). The proportional rate of B cells and T cells and the balanced light chains by flow cytometry and/or by molecular tests assessing the IGH integrity support the benign hyperplasia. EBV is detected in cytopathological preparations by ISH assays for EBER. Polymorphic LPD: Smears can be cellular and comprise a mixed population of cells including small lymphoid cells, medium-sized plasmacytoid cells, mature plasma cells, histiocytes, and a variable number of immunoblasts – some of which may show Reed–Sternberg–like features. IDD-related lymphomas may not always show conventional cytomorphological, molecular, and cytogenetic features, complicating specific classification. LBCL: The cytopathological features are similar to those seen in immunocompetent patients. High cellularity with rather monomorphic large atypical lymphoid cells in a dissociated dispersed single-cell pattern is usually seen. Neoplastic cells often show plasmacytoid or immunoblastic differentiation with eccentric cytoplasm and a single prominent nucleolus. Sparse reactive small lymphocytes can be seen in the background. Necrosis and crushed nuclei because of fibrosis or nuclear fragility may occur. Light chain restriction, sometimes limited to part of the lymphoid cell population, can be detected. A high proliferative rate of the neoplastic cells is present. BL: High cellularity with monotonous medium-sized lymphoid cells and numerous tingible-body macrophages is seen. However, as documented in the literature, especially in the HIV setting, BL cells can be larger and pleomorphic and can show plasmacytoid differentiation with a single, centrally located nucleolus. CHL: The heterogeneous, mixed pattern is less frequently seen than in cases in immunocompetent hosts, and depending on the severity of immunosuppression, reduced numbers of small lymphocytes and large numbers of multinucleated and bizarre elements and mononuclear blasts with prominent nucleoli can be seen. Plasmablastic lymphoma: The cytopathological features vary depending on the pattern. Generally, these are highly cellular preparations. Some cases show dissociated large immunoblasts similar to those seen in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS (DLBCL-NOS). In plasmablastic lymphoma with plasmacytic differentiation, the cells show more pleomorphism, the nuclei are round to oval with vesicular chromatin (Pap stain), and there is abundant eccentric cytoplasm, while other cases demonstrate pseudoaggregation. Nucleoli can be small and multiple or single, prominent, and centrally located. The cytopathological appearance is indistinguishable from that of plasmablastic myeloma. Tingible-body macrophages can be numerous. Cytopathological features of peripheral IDD TCL or NK-cell lymphomas have not been described so far.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The recently standardized nomenclature proposed to define these heterogeneous IDD-associated lymphoproliferative disorder (IDD-LPD) entities encompasses the name of the histopathological lesion, the presence or absence of one or more oncogenic viruses, and the clinical setting and immunodeficiency background [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]].

In previous editions of the WHO Classification of Tumours, IDD-LPDs were classified according to the immunodeficiency background in which they arise: primary immunodeficiencies, HIV infection, posttransplantation status, or other (iatrogenic) immunodeficiencies. The fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Haematolymphoid tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 11). https://publications.iarc.who.int/637.]] recognizes common histopathologies, specific causal etiologies, and involvement of EBV and KSHV/HHV8, as well as specific clinical presentations, management approaches, and prognostic outcomes.

The diagnosis, classification, and management of IDD-related hyperplasias, polymorphic LPD, and lymphomas are complex and strictly related to the clinical, serological, and imaging presentation {29414277}. FNAB alone is insufficient for an accurate cytopathological diagnosis in most cases, and ancillary techniques are necessary for this purpose. Lymph node excision is generally performed for histopathological and molecular evaluation of lymphomas and clinical- or ultrasound-equivocal hyperplasias. Nonetheless, lymph node surgical excision in the IDD setting is generally hampered by the specific clinical context; therefore, FNAB and CNB may be required as first-line diagnostic procedures. In addition, FNAB offers the ability to rapidly and in a minimally invasive technique exclude specific infections and metastatic carcinomas, and it provides the initial diagnosis to assess possible recurrences and response to treatment.

Therefore, cytopathologists are often involved in the diagnosis of nodal or extranodal IDD-LPD {29414277; 11917591}. The accuracy and the ROM of lymph node FNAB in IDD can be calculated according to those of the corresponding non–IDD-related entity {32454496}. However, no large published series are available, and the survival rate is influenced mostly by the specific clinical context.

BL cases may present with more variation in cell size and shape compared with those in the immunocompetent population. The differential diagnosis includes lymphoblastic lymphoma and DLBCL-NOS. Large numbers of multinucleated and bizarre cells and mononuclear blasts with prominent nucleoli seen in IDD-associated CHL may be confused with anaplastic large cell lymphoma and non-haematopoietic malignancies such as metastatic nasopharyngeal and other poorly differentiated carcinomas and germ cell tumours, especially seminoma/dysgerminoma. Immunocytochemistry may be required in this situation. EBV latency type II is seen in almost all CHL but only in up to 20% of nasopharyngeal carcinoma. EBV is not associated with anaplastic large cell lymphoma or germ cell tumour. Plasmablastic lymphoma can, histopathologically, present with sheet-like proliferations. This is reflected cytopathologically with more cohesive tissue fragments of cells, and the differential diagnosis includes carcinoma; immunocytochemistry can assist.

The sole presence of EBV-positive cells in a lymphoid or plasmacytic proliferation is not sufficient for a posttransplant IDD classification, and EBV positivity is not strictly necessary to diagnose posttransplant IDD, but the EBER test is recommended to make the diagnosis {29414277}.
