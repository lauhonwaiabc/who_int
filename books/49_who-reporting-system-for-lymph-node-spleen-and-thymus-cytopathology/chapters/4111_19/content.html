4.1.1.1 Introduction

An “Inadequate” or “Insufficient” or “Non-diagnostic” FNAB lymph node specimen is one that for qualitative and/or quantitative reasons does not provide sufficient material for diagnostic purposes. The reason(s) a specimen is deemed “Inadequate” must be documented in the report. Before a sample is deemed “Inadequate”, all the material obtained at the FNAB must be examined. In some cases, for instance, a cell block or flow cytometry from a case that has inadequate material on the direct smears may provide a diagnosis. Similarly, if a liquid-based cytopathology case has insufficient material on the preparations, further specimens can be prepared from residual fluid for cytopathology or a cell block.

There is only scant reference to this category in the literature {25186645; 26091519; 32454496; 31051765; 33830657}, with no universally accepted definition as to what constitutes an adequate cytopathological preparation in lymph node FNAB. In lymph node cytopathology, the clinical and imaging findings, and frequently ancillary studies such as flow cytometry, are essential for interpreting the material and attaining a reliable diagnosis. For these reasons, a descriptive definition is preferred over a definition based on a specified number of lymphoid cells.

Any specimen is regarded as adequate if it demonstrates a pathological process consistent with the clinical setting and imaging findings. Conversely, if there is considerable lymphoid material providing a nonspecific benign finding in the setting of an imaging-suspicious mass or lesion, a malignant process cannot be fully excluded, and repeat FNAB or CNB of the lesion (or other investigations) will be required. Some pathologists prefer to describe the benign lymphoid material present in these cases and categorize these lesions as “Benign”, with a mandatory caveat in the report that “the material sampled may not correlate with the clinical and imaging findings”. Other cytopathologists use the term “Non-diagnostic” for these cases as well as for cases where there is insufficient material on the smears. It is recommended that each pathology department choose a preferred approach and term for consistency to allow for consistent reporting and communication with clinicians.

If there is non-lymphoid material such as mucin, amyloid, or necrotic debris present, a pathological process is implied and the lesion may be categorized according to these features in conjunction with the clinical findings. For example, if mycobacterial or other organisms are identified on acid-fast bacilli or other stains of a necrotic smear, the findings are consistent with a benign infectious process. If any atypical cells or features are identified, the specimen should be categorized as Atypical; for example, if mucin is present in the setting of a known mucinous colorectal carcinoma, the features are atypical, even if a definitive diagnosis is not possible.

The use of any of these terms may suggest poor FNAB and smearing technique; however, there may be other, unavoidable reasons for an inadequate preparation, and it is important to convey this information to the clinical team. General reasons for an inadequate lymph node sample reflect the inherent qualities of the lesion, the expertise of the FNAB operator, and the qualities of the smear. These may include the following:

Artefact – including material obscured by blood and artefactual changes related to preparation or fixation, for example slow air-drying in Giemsa-stained direct smears, slow immersion into the alcohol in Pap-stained alcohol-fixed smears, lysis of cells due to contamination with local anaesthetic, formalin vapour effect, and ultrasound gel; Low cellularity – including technical difficulties in attaining material, blood only, and acellular smears; The interpretation as to whether a lymph node FNAB is adequate also depends on the clinical reason for the procedure; Lymph nodes that appear normal on imaging and are sampled for staging, for example under endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) guidance, generally require lymphoid cells to confirm that the lymph node has been sampled. Carbon-laden macrophages alone may be regarded as sufficient evidence of sampling of an anthracotic node. In the setting of a haematological malignancy, sufficient material for ancillary studies may be essential; Lymph nodes that are abnormal clinically or on imaging require diagnostic material that correlates with the clinical and imaging findings. In some settings, if there is insufficient material for ancillary studies, such as cell block and flow cytometry, the preparation may be inadequate for diagnosis.
