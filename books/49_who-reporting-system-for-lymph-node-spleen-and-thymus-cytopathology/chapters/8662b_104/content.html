8.6.6.2B Metastases from carcinomas

Definition

Metastatic carcinoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm arising from an extranodal organ that involves one or more lymph nodes.

Clinical features and imaging

Most patients with nodal metastases have a clinical history of a malignancy, but patients may present with lymphadenopathy as the first evidence of a malignancy. If the enlarged lymph nodes are deep-seated, they often remain asymptomatic until they press on nearby structures or organs, causing pressure symptoms.

Imaging assessment of nodal status is critical in treatment planning for oncological patients. Structural imaging methods, including ultrasound, CT, and MRI, depict the location, size, shape, and texture of lymph nodes, but they do not reliably distinguish benign lymph nodes from malignant ones. Functional imaging modalities, such as PET-CT or MRI, show the differences in metabolism between benign and malignant cells with relatively high accuracies but relatively low spatial resolution {7980769}.

Histopathology

The histopathology of metastatic carcinomas depends on the histopathology, degrees of differentiation, and primary sites of the primary tumour. Nodal involvement can be partial or complete, and partial involvement can be further classified as a micrometastasis or macrometastasis. Necrosis and cystic degeneration are frequently present.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Generally speaking, lymph nodes are the most common site of metastatic carcinomas, either by direct extension or via lymphatic or haematological spread. Not infrequently, lymphadenopathy is the initial clinical presentation of the disease. FNAB of lymph nodes aims to identify the presence of a malignant epithelial tumour and determine possible primary sites.

FNAB of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma in lymph nodes often produces creamy, mucoid material mimicking pus from an abscess.

There are several carcinomas in which nodal metastases may closely mimic malignant lymphomas. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma often manifests initially with cervical lymphadenopathy, and there may be no apparent mass in the nasopharynx. Although the neoplastic cells of nasopharyngeal carcinoma are typically arranged as cohesive epithelial tissue fragments, a small percentage of nasopharyngeal carcinomas present with a dispersed single-cell pattern in a background of a mixed lymphoid population {15754369}, which may be mistaken for Hodgkin disease. Cytopathological features that favour classic Hodgkin disease include the presence of fewer neoplastic cells {11393058}, and the large Reed–Sternberg cells show more abundant cytoplasm, whereas nasopharyngeal carcinoma is favoured when there are more frequent stripped naked nuclei and the nuclei are less pleomorphic, with smoother contours, and often less polylobulated and multinucleated.

Metastatic breast carcinoma, particularly lobular carcinoma, may show predominantly single dispersed cells and be confused with non-Hodgkin lymphomas. Cytopathological features that favour metastatic lobular carcinoma include the finding of small cohesive tissue fragments, as well as dispersed single cells, the presence of eccentric intracytoplasmic vacuoles, more pleomorphic nuclei, and a lack of lymphoid cell cytoplasmic fragments (“lymphoglandular bodies”) – especially in the pleomorphic pattern of lobular carcinoma.

Metastatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma from pulmonary or extrapulmonary sites, including Merkel cell carcinoma, can show a predominantly discohesive cell pattern with inconspicuous small tissue fragments, and the distinction from lymphoma is particularly difficult when the samples are poorly preserved: pseudoaggregation of lymphoma cells may simulate nuclear moulding in small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Lymphoid cell cytoplasmic fragments may rarely be seen in small cell carcinoma {8388161}.

Ancillary testing

Knowledge of the patient’s sex and age, location of the involved lymph node, and history can be extremely helpful in narrowing down potential primary sites (#42303Table 7.13), as can the cytopathology features (#42304Table 7.14, and #42305Table 7.15), and this information can be helpful in the judicious selection of appropriate immunocytochemistry antibody panels. Immunocytochemistry is particularly helpful to distinguish carcinomas and lymphoma. A panel of antibodies should be used, preferably including antibodies to pancytokeratins and CD45 (LCA). Immunocytochemical findings must be interpreted in light of the cytopathological and clinical findings in order to avoid potential pitfalls such as the observation of cytokeratin-positive dendritic cells in lymph nodes and the LCA expression in rare cases of undifferentiated carcinoma {9872653}. Ultimately, about 3–4% of carcinomas presenting with nodal metastases are from a primary of unknown origin. CK7 and CK20 are often used in combination to suggest the possible site of origin of metastatic carcinoma {29621151} (#42306Table 7.16). The comparative expression of CK7 and CK20 gives an overall indication, but “aberrant“ patterns of CK7/CK20 expression are not infrequent. For example, gastric carcinomas have been reported to exhibit a highly variable profile: CK7+/CK20+ in 32% of cases, CK7−/CK20+ in 35% of cases, and CK7−/CK20− in 14% of cases {12010363}. About 90% of breast carcinomas demonstrate a CK7+/CK20− profile, but other patterns (CK7+/CK20+, CK7−/CK20+, and CK7−/CK20−) have also been observed {12010363}. The cytopathology findings should be correlated with clinical and imaging findings and ultimately may not correlate with the immunocytochemistry.

Several relevant organ-specific antibodies can be used to make a precise identification of the most likely primary origin of the carcinoma. Historically, organ-specific antibodies were predominantly cytoplasmic and/or membranous markers, which often demonstrate reduced expression in poorly differentiated tumours (#42307Table 7.17). More recently, new immunocytochemical markers including lineage-restricted transcription factors have increasingly become available and tend to be nuclear markers that often maintain strong expression in poorly differentiated tumours. #42308Table 7.18 lists some of the commonly used lineage-restricted transcription factors and their associated diagnostic applications.

It is important to recognize that there is no “perfect” immunocytochemical marker, even with lineage-restricted transcription factors; for example, TTF1 is expressed by lung, thyroid, and mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma of the uterine cervix {30148742}, and GATA3 is positive in breast and urothelial carcinomas {20173733; 17460449}, chromophobe renal cell carcinoma {24145643}, and paraganglioma {23599157}, as well as in most skin squamous cell carcinomas and a small number of lung squamous cell carcinomas {26595821; 36702357}. These observations highlight the usefulness of GATA3 in unique diagnostic contexts and immunocytochemistry panels.

Molecular profiling to identify site-specific gene expression profiles is used to determine the most likely primary site of metastatic carcinoma {23032625; 30653423}, using microarrays and RT-PCR to quantify mRNA or microRNA. To date, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved the use of a 2000-gene microarray-based gene expression assay to predict the origin of cancer with an overall agreement rate of 88.5% with established diagnoses {18414470; 21227394}.

Another approach is epigenetics testing {27575023}. Epigenetics refers to heritable phenotype changes without an alteration in the DNA sequence {21734376}. DNA methylation of the carbon-5 of cytosine moieties is the most frequently used epigenetic marker in the human genome {27575023}. Cancer-specific markers have been identified by DNA-methylation profiling of the hypermethylated CpG island, which enables the differentiation of various tumour types {27575023}.
