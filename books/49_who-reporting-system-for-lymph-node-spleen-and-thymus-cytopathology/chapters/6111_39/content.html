6.1.1.1 Introduction

The “Atypical” category includes cases where there is scant, partially or largely obscured, or poorly smeared and/or stained material, which are the usual causes of an indeterminate categorization. This category also includes cases with considerable lymphoid material that shows mainly benign cytopathological features, but where there are some features that may raise the possibility of malignancy, for example highly cellular lymphoid smears showing a mix of lymphoid cell types with a relative lack of tingible-body macrophages and small lymphocytes, which raise the differential diagnosis of follicular hyperplasia and follicular lymphoma. These cases are expected to make up the bulk of the “Atypical” category. Because of this expectation, it was decided by the expert editorial board not to have separate atypical categories for “Atypical lymphoid cells of uncertain significance" and for those rare cases of probable benign inclusions such as the finding of naevoid cells in an axillary lymph node FNAB. If any atypical features are seen in a case showing otherwise insufficient material, the case is regarded as “Atypical” and not “Insufficient”.

The causes of an “Atypical” categorization are predominantly a poor-quality FNAB procedure, poor-quality preparation and smearing and fixation of slides, a small amount of material, and a lack of experience in the cytopathology reporting team, as well as, most importantly, abundant lymphoid material with features favouring a benign reactive process but overlapping with those of low-grade lymphomas such as classic follicular lymphoma, small lymphocytic lymphoma, marginal zone lymphoma, and lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma. These are the cases where flow cytometry can be diagnostic. In other cases, high-grade lymphomas may yield mainly apoptotic debris and stripped nuclei, and although such cases are usually categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy”, flow cytometry due to cell damage may not be diagnostic.

The specific diagnosis of highly cellular lymphoid material from an FNAB of a lymph node is an area of debate and current research. The ability of individual cytopathologists and their teams to confidently diagnose on cytopathology alone various inflammatory reactive patterns as benign or to place them in the “Atypical” category, encompassing the concept of “Atypical lymphoid cells of uncertain significance”, varies with local practice and expertise, as well as the expectations and support of their clinical colleagues. FNAB of lymph nodes in any setting is a powerful, minimally invasive, and inexpensive tool and can diagnose a broad range of the many pathological processes that involve lymph nodes. Many of these will be infections and metastatic malignancies, leaving a proportion of cases that are lymphoid, which will vary in number depending on the nature of practice and the country of the cytopathologist. The crucial areas of debate are how many of these lymphoid cases can be specifically diagnosed by FNAB alone; how many need to be categorized as “Atypical” or “Suspicious for malignancy”; how many can be diagnosed on cytopathology supported by ancillary testing; and how many require excision biopsy with surgical histopathology supported by flow cytometry, immunocytochemistry, and ISH. Research is required on these issues.
