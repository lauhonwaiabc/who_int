4.1.1.3 Discussion and background

In individual studies the reported rates of insufficient or inadequate or non-diagnostic FNAB of lymph nodes vary widely {26091519; 10800402; 31041851}, being influenced by the criteria used to define adequacy, the type of procedure, imaging and clinical findings, and the skill of the practitioner in ensuring that the target has been adequately sampled. Achieving a final diagnosis based on minimally invasive tissue samples of lymph nodes is generally a multidisciplinary effort, combining clinical information with the imaging findings and microscopic analysis of the tissue samples, enriched by the results of the relevant ancillary techniques [[Field AS, Zarka MA, editors. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017. (Pattern recognition series).]] {32454496; 26091519}. Suboptimal input at any of these levels may result in an “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” cytopathological categorization.

Achieving an optimal-quality diagnostic FNAB sample is often more complex and operator-dependent than performing a CNB, particularly in the context of lymph nodes, spleen, and thymus. Obtaining a CNB with direct transfer into fixative does not require correct smearing technique, but the triage of material for ancillary studies is usually required and is technically more difficult, relying on rolling a delicate core across a slide as a touch preparation {10800402; 20735454; 29989244}. A lack of awareness on the part of some FNAB operators, in particular non-cytopathologists, of the amount of cellular material needed, or a lack of knowledge of the ancillary techniques available, may result in suboptimal handling of the sample and render it “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic”. Close cooperation with FNAB operators through interdisciplinary educational sessions, direct feedback, and ROSE (including triage of the samples) will enhance the performance of the procedure. Cytopathologists performing ultrasound guidance for FNAB (interventional cytopathologists) have been reported to deliver the lowest rates of “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” specimens [[Bardales RH. The invasive cytopathologist: ultrasound guided fine-needle aspiration of superficial masses. Berlin (Germany): Springer; 2014. (Essentials in cytopathology series).]] {18418854}. Direct communication between cytopathologists and other medical specialists during assisted FNAB and ROSE also facilitates achievement of maximal rates of adequate preparations and specific diagnoses. In endoscopic ultrasound–guided FNAB (EUS-FNAB), the use of ROSE can increase the sensitivity of the procedure by at least 6 percentage points, from 85% to 91% in one meta-analysis {32979158}.

Defining a minimal number of lymphocytes for a diagnosis of a sufficient, representative, and diagnostic lymph node FNAB sample is not practical in encompassing all scenarios, and is context-dependent. Attempts have been made to define a specific number of lymphocytes for endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB) of mediastinal lymph nodes in lung cancer staging {31051765; 25186645}, but such criteria are not universally applicable {33830657}. In one prospective controlled trial comparing EBUS-TBFNAB and mediastinoscopy for lymph node staging, about 29% of EBUS-TBFNAB samples (122 of 426) obtained by a highly experienced endoscopist, using an average of three passes, were deemed inadequate due to low lymphoid cellularity, but most of these samples were from PET-negative lymph nodes measuring < 5 mm in short axis, and none showed metastases on final pathology, suggesting that the assessment as “Inadequate” was of no true value {21963329}.

If the FNAB smears demonstrate any atypical, suspicious, or malignant cells, even if poorly preserved or scant in number, independent of the number of lymphocytes present, the sample should be classified in one of those respective categories rather than as “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic”.
