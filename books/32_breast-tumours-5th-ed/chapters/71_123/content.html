7.1 Haematolymphoid tumours of the breast: Introduction

Primary lymphoma of the breast

Definition

Primary lymphoma of the breast is often defined as lymphoma confined to one or both breasts and/or regional lymph nodes, in the absence of a prior history of lymphoma {4555557; 3922871; 8333556; 17932394}. However, many studies use criteria less stringent than those for other extranodal lymphomas: presentation with the dominant mass or symptom in the breast in a patient without known lymphoma, even if distant involvement is found on staging {3545107; 2249200}.

Epidemiology

Primary lymphoma of the breast is uncommon, accounting for < 0.5% of all malignant neoplasms of the breast {3315180; 4555557; 12051628; 2249200} and approximately 2% of all extranodal lymphomas {5007387; 10717152}. According to SEER Program data, the incidence of primary breast lymphoma has increased over recent decades, from 0.66 cases per 1 million woman-years in 1975–1977 to 2.96 cases per 1 million woman-years in 2011–2013 {28376147}.

Clinical features

Most patients are middle-aged or elderly women, although younger women can also be affected, including pregnant or lactating women presenting with massive bilateral breast masses {28376147; 19199367; 11967901}. Rarely, men are affected {2249200; 22323922; 17932394}. Patients usually present with a painless unilateral breast lump, but in some cases the tumour is detected on radiological examination {17541937}. As many as 11% of cases show bilateral breast involvement {28376147; 20799767; 17541937; 11967901; 17932394}.

Spectrum of primary breast lymphomas

Although any type of lymphoma can occur as primary breast lymphoma {8116797; 19570964}, three histological types account for almost all cases: diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (40–73% of cases), extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT lymphoma; 9–25%), and follicular lymphoma (13–19%) {28376147; 17541937} [[Martinelli G, Ryan G. Primary breast lymphoma. In: Cavalli F, Stein H, Zucca E, editors. Extranodal lymphomas: pathology and management. London (UK): Informa; 2008. pp. 168–73.]]. Some series report a much higher proportion of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (84%) or MALT lymphoma (64%) {19199367; 12051628}. A site-specific type of lymphoma is breast implant–associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma, which accounts for 2% of all breast lymphomas {28376147}. The breast is also a distinct (though rare) site for the occurrence of Burkitt lymphoma {20799767}. Treatment and prognosis depend on the specific type of lymphoma {11967901; 17541937} [[Martinelli G, Ryan G. Primary breast lymphoma. In: Cavalli F, Stein H, Zucca E, editors. Extranodal lymphomas: pathology and management. London (UK): Informa; 2008. pp. 168–73.]].

Other haematolymphoid neoplasms of the breast

Plasmacytoma of the breast is uncommon, accounting for 1.5% of all plasmacytomas and 0.2% of malignancies of the breast {20429767}. It can occur as solitary extraosseous plasmacytoma or (more commonly {20429767}) as a local manifestation of an underlying plasma cell myeloma {11473462; 3918437; 6378141; 2649190}. Occasional cases can show bilateral breast involvement {24673829; 28805290}.

Histiocytic sarcoma, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, and interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma have rarely been reported to occur as primary tumours of the breast {25031040; 12189511; 10196389; 15806378}. Of particular diagnostic interest is a reported case of follicular dendritic cell sarcoma accompanied by abundant myxoid stroma, mimicking mucinous carcinoma {10196389}.

Myeloid sarcoma can occur in the breast either as a solitary lesion not accompanied by leukaemia or as a local manifestation of acute myeloid leukaemia {25890617; 29684340; 15628906}. Histologically, it can potentially be mistaken for malignant lymphoma or invasive lobular carcinoma, in particular the latter due to a single-file non-cohesive growth pattern and lack of immunoreactivity for E-cadherin [[Martinelli G, Ryan G. Primary breast lymphoma. In: Cavalli F, Stein H, Zucca E, editors. Extranodal lymphomas: pathology and management. London (UK): Informa; 2008. pp. 168–73.]].
