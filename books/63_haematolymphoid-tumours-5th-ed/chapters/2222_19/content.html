2.2.2.2 Cutaneous mastocytosis

Definition

Cutaneous mastocytosis (CM) is a form of mastocytosis primarily affecting the skin.

ICD-O coding

9740/1 Cutaneous mastocytosis

ICD-11 coding

2A21.1 Cutaneous mastocytosis

2A21.1Y Other specified cutaneous mastocytosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: urticaria pigmentosa; telangiectasia macularis eruptiva perstans.

Subtype(s)

Maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (MPCM); diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis (DCM); mastocytoma

Localization

Maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (MPCM) favours the trunk and proximal extremities, but other areas may be involved. The palms, soles, and central face are usually spared. Diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis (DCM) involves the whole skin surface. Mastocytomas may appear anywhere, more frequently on the trunk and scalp {26476479; 26152315}.

Clinical features

The classification of CM is based on the number of lesions and morphological features (see #32077Table 2.06). Swelling, reddening, pruritus, or blistering can occur when mast cells are activated, usually upon stroking (Darier sign) or temperature changes. Systemic symptoms originating from the release of mast cell mediators can occur in some cases and include flushing, headache, diarrhoea, abdominal pain or cramping, and even anaphylaxis in the most severe cases. These are more frequently seen in children with extensive skin involvement and elevated tryptase levels {22458675}. However, elevated tryptase is not a reliable marker for systemic mastocytosis in children, because most children with elevated tryptase levels have disease limited to the skin {26044856}.

Epidemiology

CM is a rare disease, most commonly affecting children {21668033; 11919426; 17537151; 11377676}. The true incidence and prevalence of CM are unknown.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Somatic activating mutations in KIT cause mastocytosis {9990072}. The KIT p.D816V mutation in exon 17 is present in > 80% of adult cases and in 35–40% of paediatric cases {16741248; 12598308; 25662299}. Of the paediatric cases, KIT mutations in exons 8–11 are observed in as many as 40%, whereas 15–20% are KIT-wildtype {19865100}. Germline KIT mutations have been reported in familial forms of mastocytosis {16183119; 28978170}.

KIT mutations cause constitutional, ligand-independent hyperactivation of the receptor tyrosine kinase KIT {26158763}. Downstream mediators of oncogenic KIT signalling include PI3K, STAT5, NF-κB, mTORC2, and PKCδ. They induce the proliferation and increased growth and survival of neoplastic mast cells, leading to their pathological accumulation in organs and tissues {26158763}.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Skin biopsies show a highly variable increase in dermal mast cells when stained for Giemsa, toluidine blue, mast cell tryptase, or CD117 {10224380; 3170991; 11377676; 26476479}. In MPCM, mast cells are often loosely scattered, with a tendency to aggregate around blood vessels and adnexa or form small, or even band-like, compact infiltrates in the upper dermis {11377676; 26476479}. Mast cells are usually markedly increased in DCM and form nodular infiltrates in mastocytomas {11377676; 26476479}. Mast cells appear as unremarkable histiocyte-like cells {11377676; 30252695}. With Giemsa, mast cells can be spindle-shaped or round, with hypergranulated cytoplasm (well-differentiated morphology) {26476479; 11377676; 26100086}. They always coexpress tryptase and CD117. Expression of CD2, CD25, and/or CD30 supports their neoplastic nature {18162781; 23807778; 21186345; 25402852; 29794740}. Mast cells with a well-differentiated phenotype typically lack CD25 and CD2, but they express CD30 in most cases {26100086; 27741105; 22370312}. Increased numbers of mast cells showing striking nuclear pleomorphism with bilobed and multilobed nuclei (promastocytes) can be observed in CM with well-differentiated features. There is no evidence that promastocytes represent a malignant phenotype {30252695; 26100086}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The detection of KIT mutations supports the diagnosis of mastocytosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cutaneous lesions with clinical features typical of mastocytosis; absence of clinical signs of systemic mastocytosis; bone marrow evaluation (when indicated) not meeting the criteria for systemic mastocytosis; skin biopsy showing increased numbers of mast cells; mast cells immunoreactive for KIT (CD117) and/or tryptase.

Desirable: aberrant dermal mast cell immunophenotype (CD2+, CD25+, and/or CD30+); detection of KIT mutation in lesional skin.

Staging

In children with mastocytosis involving the skin and with normal serum tryptase levels, the diagnosis of CM is assumed without the need for further investigations. A complete bone marrow study is recommended in all adults regardless of tryptase levels {34901755}.

Prognosis and prediction

CM in children tends to resolve around puberty. DCM may be potentially fatal, but most cases involute in childhood, leaving wrinkled and thickened skin. In adult-onset CM, skin lesions usually persist for life, with variable degrees of mast cell activation symptoms, but the disease does not have an impact on life expectancy {26476479}. In cases with aberrant/clonal bone marrow mast cells that do not meet enough criteria for a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, close follow-up is recommended in order to detect eventual progression to systemic mastocytosis.

 

Subtype

Clinical features

Maculopapular cutaneous mastocytosis (MPCM) {26476479; 26152315}

 

Polymorphic MPCM

·   Usually seen in children

·   Brown, yellow, or pale red papules, plaques, and/or nodules

·   Heterogeneity in number, shape, size, margination, and degree of confluence of the lesions

 

Monomorphic MPCM

·   The most common subtype in adults; may be present from infancy or childhood

·   Small, round, pigmented macules and papules, ranging from a few scattered lesions to generalized skin involvement with confluent lesions

Diffuse cutaneous mastocytosis

{25662299; 22092511}

·   An extremely rare form of cutaneous mastocytosis occurring in infants

·   Generalized erythroderma, thickened skin, exaggerated folds, and episodic blistering often accompanied by severe systemic symptoms

·   Absence of individual skin lesions

Mastocytoma

{26476479; 26525106}

·   The most frequent type of mastocytosis in infants

·   Mastocytomas are usually solitary lesions, but as many as three lesions are accepted

·   Yellow to brown papules, nodules, or plaques, often with a leathery texture; less frequently, small and pigmented macules

·   Rubbing may cause wheals, blisters, and occasionally flushing and hypotension
