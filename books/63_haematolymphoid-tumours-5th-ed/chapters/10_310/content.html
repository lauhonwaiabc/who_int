1.0 General introduction to haematolymphoid tumours

Evidence-based classification of disease is fundamental for the treatment of individual patients; for monitoring of global disease incidence; and for investigating all aspects of disease causation, prevention, and therapy. The World Health Organization (WHO) classification of haematolymphoid tumours has provided a global reference since its third edition was published in 2001. The definitions laid down in the successive WHO classifications have not only been adopted for use by pathologists, clinicians, and basic and translational research scientists, but they have also been incorporated into the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and have thus served as a global reference for epidemiological monitoring across national and international health policy organizations.

The fifth edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours (WHO-HAEM5) is the first major update to the classification since the publication of the fourth edition in 2008, which was followed by a revision in 2017. While this classification builds on prior editions, it incorporates several fundamental structural and content updates that reflect scientific developments and clinical advances that occurred in the years following the publication of the fourth edition. The current edition has enhanced the hierarchical organization of the classification, improved the clarity and practicability of disease categories, and incorporated for the first time a clinical advisory component within the fabric of the team that wrote and edited this volume. At a fundamental level, the classification aims to strike a balance between incorporating scientific advances and retaining clinical pertinence and worldwide applicability.

The production of this volume was overseen by an editorial board composed of standing members, nominated by the major pathology societies around the world, and expert members, selected for contemporary multidisciplinary expertise and recognition in the field. The editorial board, in turn, carefully identified authors through an informed bibliometric process, with an eye on relevant expertise and a balance across geography and other equity factors. A total of approximately 420 individuals contributed to this volume as editors or authors. By design, multidisciplinary author/editor groups shared overlapping coverage of disease categories to ensure conceptual continuity and content harmonization. This approach reflects the ways in which the classification is meant to be implemented, with multidisciplinary input that emphasizes a holistic approach to patient care from diagnosis through to disease monitoring.

Applicability and diagnostic integration

The definition and diagnosis of distinct disease (tumour) types in this classification continues to be based on multiple clinicopathological parameters, but with refinements of diagnostic criteria and defining genetic features, as well as an emphasis on therapeutically and/or prognostically actionable biomarkers. Indeed, the application of this classification is predicated on integrating morphological (cytological and histological), immunophenotypic, molecular, and cytogenetic data. Therefore, using the classification to its fullest extent requires one or more specialized diagnostic techniques, which may include immunophenotyping, karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and/or molecular diagnostics. This is in line with previous editions, with expanded numbers of disease types and subtypes that are molecularly defined. This point necessitates, in turn, an integrated diagnostic approach that incorporates multiple layers of information, sometimes from different sources, often coming in at different times. Various diagnostic modalities may also be needed to exclude differential diagnostic considerations, even for entities whose diagnosis might not per se require a particular type of diagnostic evaluation.

To enhance the practicability of the classification and the timeliness of its utilization in clinical care settings, the structure was redesigned in a way that facilitates the placement of a disease in a higher tier of the hierarchy when necessary. This permits reverting to category-level or family (class)-level definitions when detailed molecular genetic analyses may not be available at a given timepoint, or even feasible at all.

The subsection termed “essential and desirable diagnostic criteria”, introduced for the first time in the fifth edition of the series, is intended to facilitate the applicability of the classification by distilling the key diagnostic components needed to classify a particular disease type. Essential diagnostic criteria are considered must-have features to establish a diagnosis, whereas desirable diagnostic criteria are good-to-have features (i.e. they support the diagnosis but this information is not mandatory). Obviously, it should be realized that it is neither intended nor feasible to cover within a concise list of essential and desirable diagnostic criteria all possible exceptions and variations that may be encountered in rare cases. In such instances, a final decision remains based on a balanced integration of clinical, morphological, immunophenotypic, and genetic data.

The listing of essential diagnostic criteria that have the broadest possible applicability is also intended to aid in applying the classification with the lowest burden of resources where feasible. Notwithstanding, it is hoped that the broader genetic underpinnings of WHO-HAEM5 will prompt and encourage the mobilization of healthcare resources, public or otherwise, to ensure that necessary clinical testing platforms are available so that the classification can be applied to its fullest potential for the broadest number of people possible worldwide. The importance of such resources being available locally stems from the fact that sending out tests to distant reference laboratories tends to limit the availability of accurate local public health data in some regions or even in some countries.

Classification principles and structure

The broadest organization of the WHO-HAEM5 taxonomy is along haematopoietic lineages, each in its own chapter, plus a distinct chapter on stroma-derived neoplasms of lymphoid tissues and another on genetic tumour syndromes. This lineage-based framework then flows broadly from benign to malignant, and precursor cell neoplasms are followed by mature cell malignancies. The classification then branches down hierarchically from category to family, followed by type (entity), and then subtype. That is, it organizes diseases in order of increasing levels of specification: category (e.g. mature B-cell neoplasms), family/class (e.g. large B-cell lymphomas), entity/type (e.g. diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS), and subtype (e.g. diffuse large B cell lymphoma NOS, germinal centre B-cell–like).

Where possible, a triad of attributes was systematically applied and included: lineage + dominant clinical/anatomical attribute + dominant biological attribute. Lineage attribution rests on immunophenotyping using flow cytometry and/or immunohistochemistry. Dominant clinical attributes are general features of the untreated disease and include descriptors such as acute or chronic, association with cytopenias (myelodysplasia) or cytoses (myeloproliferation), or anatomical location (splenic, cutaneous, etc.). Most biological attributes include gene fusions, rearrangements, and mutations. Fusions are part of the nomenclature of types/subtypes when the identities of both implicated genes are required or often desirable criteria for diagnosis (e.g. PML::RARA). Rearrangements, a broad term that encompasses a range of structural genomic alterations leading to gene fusions, are part of the nomenclature of types/subtypes when there are multiple possible fusion partner genes of a biologically dominant gene (e.g. KMT2A). Of note, the use of the term “rearrangements” is maintained in the classification due to its wide usage across prior editions, although we recognize that this term is more appropriate for genomic modifications in genes consisting of various segments (e.g. immunoglobulin genes and T-cell receptor genes). A deliberate attempt has been made to prioritize classifying tumour types based on defining genetic abnormalities where possible. Taken together, this approach was the basis for organizing several categories within the myeloid chapter, as well as precursor B-cell neoplasms.

The first portion of the classification is dedicated to myeloid proliferations and neoplasms, the biology of which has now been relatively well elucidated. Key changes to this portion of the classification include the introduction of clonal haematopoiesis; the reorganization of myelodysplastic neoplasms and revision of their nomenclature; and the reorganization of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), secondary myeloid neoplasms, myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase fusion genes, and acute leukaemias of mixed or ambiguous lineage.

Reflecting their unique lineage, histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms are classified together in a separate following chapter in a manner that naturally highlights the distinctive features shared by many of these diseases despite their varied clinical manifestations.

WHO-HAEM5 introduces preneoplastic diseases and tumour-like lesions that mimic B-cell and T-cell neoplasms. Lymphoblastic leukaemias/lymphomas are grouped by lineage. The chapter on B-cell lymphoid proliferations and lymphomas covers a large group of diseases that together constitute the most common lymphoid neoplasms worldwide. An attempt to separate the taxonomy of T-cell and NK-cell neoplasms was considered by the editorial board but was ultimately set aside for this edition, because it was felt that such a separation remains too difficult to apply in current routine practice.

Following a structure similar to that applied for myeloid and lymphoid proliferations and neoplasms, the classification of neoplasms derived from lymphoid tissue stroma was improved by creating a framework that permits their classification on the basis of their putative cells of origin.

For the first time, the initial draft of this edition of the haematolymphoid classification was presented online for public input. The editorial board reviewed and considered public comments before finalizing the classification framework. Extensive input on the content of the beta version of this volume was also received from subscribers to the WHO Classification of Tumours Online. Such feedback was immensely constructive and permitted an unprecedented level of community engagement and curated contributions.

Gene and protein nomenclature

The fifth-edition WHO Classification of Tumours follows the Human Genome Organisation (HUGO) Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) system for gene symbols and gene names (https://www.genenames.org/) {32747822}, the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) recommendations for sequence variants (https://varnomen.hgvs.org/) {26931183}, and the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN) 2020 reporting guidelines for chromosomal alterations [[McGowan-Jordan J, Hastings RJ, Moore S, editors. ISCN 2020: an international system for human cytogenomic nomenclature (2020). Basel (Switzerland): Karger Publishers; 2020.]]. Gene symbols are presented in italics, but the names of proteins and gene groups (e.g. the IDH gene family) are not italicized. The new designation of gene fusions using double colons (::) has been adopted in this volume {34615987}.

A sequence alteration relative to a transcript reference sequence is reported using the prefix “c.” for the coding DNA sequence, followed by the nucleotide number and the nucleotide change. The predicted protein sequence change then follows the prefix “p.”, specifying the reference amino acid, the amino acid number, and the variant amino acid resulting from the mutation. For example, the most common BRAF variant is BRAF c.1799T>A p.Val600Glu (or BRAF c.1799T>A p.V600E if single-letter amino acid codes are preferred, as have been used throughout this volume). Notably, however, this example assumes that a particular BRAF transcript reference sequence accession and version have previously been defined (e.g. NM_004333.5).

Newly recognized entities

A number of newly recognized entities have been added into this edition of the WHO classification. The majority reflect entities that became increasingly recognized thanks to the wider availability of sequencing techniques permitting the detection of novel fusions or mutations.

Emerging entities are listed as disease subtypes under a novel rubric of “other defined genetic alterations” or are discussed in the text under the “Histology” subheading where appropriate. This is envisioned as a landing spot in the WHO classification to incorporate new/rare entities whose recognition is increasing as high-throughput molecular diagnostic tools become more available. Provisional entities were not created in WHO-HAEM5, because these, by definition, lack sufficient evidence. It is recognized that the diagnosis of such subtypes might not be feasible in all practice settings. A set of decision-support guidelines was adopted to aid in determining what subtypes would qualify in this context; the criteria include: (1) having distinct molecular or cytogenetic features driven by established oncogenic mechanisms; (2) not meeting subtype criteria under other tumour types with defining genetic abnormalities; (3) having distinct pathological and clinical features, including but not limited to response to therapeutic interventions; and (4) having been described in at least two quality peer-reviewed publications by distinct investigator groups.

Terminology and revised nomenclature

The disease names and other terms refined or introduced in this edition reflect a consensus approach by experts in the field. Decisions were based on evidence where such evidence exists, and expert consensus was sought where data were limited. The broad principles that guided nomenclature revisions included the following: reflecting/improving scientific accuracy, harmonizing across related categories, and minimizing the use of confusing terminology that could pose clinical risks.

The classification retains the use of the term “not otherwise specified” (NOS) to designate disease types for which no terminal defining attribute is currently known. In other words, NOS is used to denote a disease type where specific defining genetic abnormalities or other defining features have been excluded. It replaces the previous term “unclassifiable” in some instances. Of note, the term NOS should not be used to classify a case as a result of technical limitations (e.g. insufficient material) or resource limitations (e.g. lack of availability of certain diagnostic modalities). Such limitations, particularly the latter, could be mitigated by going up the classification hierarchy to the family level or category level, or by using the term “not further classified” (NFC).

Concluding remarks

The WHO Classification of Tumours is a remarkable global achievement. It is a body of work that has been in the making for more than 60 years. It transcends borders and provides a common thread for the study of cancer – and the fight against it – around the world. WHO-HAEM5 is another building block in that effort, rooted in scientific evidence, dedicated expertise, and a shared vision to achieve a more perfect classification scheme for the benefit of humanity. The result is an enhanced, contemporary, evidence-based classification based on molecular biology and systematized within an organizational structure that will ensure future scalability as new discoveries inexorably continue to inform future editions.
