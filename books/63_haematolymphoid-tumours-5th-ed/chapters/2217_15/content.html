2.2.1.7 Primary myelofibrosis

Definition

Primary myelofibrosis (PMF) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by abnormal proliferation of the megakaryocytic and granulocytic lineages associated with progressive marrow fibrosis, osteosclerosis, and extramedullary haematopoiesis.

ICD-O coding

9961/3 Primary myelofibrosis

9961/3 Primary myelofibrosis, prefibrotic

9961/3 Primary myelofibrosis, fibrotic

 

ICD-11 coding

2A20.2 Primary myelofibrosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: chronic idiopathic myelofibrosis; myelofibrosis with myeloid metaplasia; agnogenic myeloid metaplasia; idiopathic myelofibrosis.

Subtype(s)

Primary myelofibrosis, prefibrotic; primary myelofibrosis, fibrotic

Localization

The blood and bone marrow are always involved. In the later stages of the disease, extramedullary haematopoiesis becomes prominent, particularly in the spleen. This is because the spleens of PMF patients appear to be capable of sequestering malignant haematopoietic stem cells {23023702}. Extramedullary disease may be detected in nearly any organ.

Clinical features

One third of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis, and many of these cases are discovered after unrelated blood tests show modest abnormalities, such as anaemia and thrombocytopenia. Splenomegaly and hepatomegaly are common and may be massive {28351937}. Extramedullary lesions may be detected in a variety of organs, including lymph nodes, CNS, skin, pericardium, peritoneum, pleura, ovaries, kidneys, adrenals, gastrointestinal tract, and lungs.

Many patients experience a hypermetabolic state manifesting with fever, anorexia, weight loss, and night sweats, sometimes early in the disease. Other symptoms include fatigue, bone pain, and splenomegaly-related symptoms such as pain and early satiety. The presence of specific symptoms, such as weight loss, night sweats, and fever, is prognostically significant. It is generally recommended that symptoms should be assessed using a specific score such as the MPN symptom assessment form (MPN-SAF) or MPN-10 {23071245; 33483290}.

Thrombosis and haemorrhage are prevalent in patients with PMF and prefibrotic PMF (pre-PMF) {33641861}. Anaemia is frequent, especially in more advanced stages, and often coincides with thrombocytopenia {28419426}. Leukaemic transformation occurs in 20–25% of patients and is characterized by the persistent presence of 20% blasts in blood or bone marrow {23071245}.

Epidemiology

The estimated incidence of fibrotic PMF is 0.44–1.5 cases per 100 000 person-years {23768070; 24372927; 36680511; 34689695}. Data on the incidence of pre-PMF are limited, but some reports suggest that it accounts for 30–50% of all PMF cases. The prevalence of PMF is probably increasing because of earlier diagnosis (at the stage of pre-PMF) and prolonged survival {22826273}. PMF affects men and women nearly equally, and although the disease may be diagnosed in young adults, it occurs most commonly in the sixth to seventh decades of life {38030891; 30157297; 22212965; 29296803; 29164670}.

 

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

A central pathogenic feature of PMF is activation of the JAK/STAT signalling pathway as a result of mutations in JAK2, MPL, or CALR {31217200; 25572172}. Of note, constitutive activation of JAK/STAT signalling is a signature that is also present in cases that lack these driver mutations {24740812}.

Pathogenic mechanisms underlying PMF remain incompletely understood and have been inferred largely through mouse studies. Driver mutations and other alterations that initiate PMF result in increased generation of megakaryocytes by haematopoietic progenitor cells coupled with impaired apoptosis resulting from overexpression of antiapoptotic proteins such as BCL-XL {17473062}. These primary events result in secretion of TGF-β and MMP9, which induce stromal fibrosis {12437648}. Increased production of TGF-β results from hyperactivation of the thrombopoietin/MPL/JAK2 axis by downregulating GATA1 expression in megakaryocytes {28240607; 28622305}. TGF-β1 is a dominant signal emanating from megakaryocytes for maintaining haematopoietic stem cell quiescence. Notably, aberrant megakaryocytes harbouring activating JAK2 mutations result in increased proliferation and number of haematopoietic stem cells through secretion of CXCL4 {25326798; 25326802}.

Epigenetic and spliceosome dysregulation are present in the majority of PMF patients {29296803}. The most common of these mutations involve ASXL1, TET2, SRSF2, and U2AF1. The precise mechanism by which these alterations contribute to the pathogenesis of PMF remains incompletely understood.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Extramedullary haematopoiesis is neoplastic in PMF {16118625; 17707884}. The most common site of extramedullary haematopoiesis is the spleen, followed by the liver. Patients of PMF rarely undergo splenectomy, and available reports are few in number {16118626; 22388763}.

Histopathology

The morphological features of PMF are quite variable, and they differ significantly between pre-PMF and PMF. Because the key distinction between these subtypes rests on the severity of myelofibrosis (MF), grading using reproducible and standard criteria is necessary {16421727}. This edition adopts the semiquantitative WHO grading system for MF as detailed in #30704Table 2.03 {26402166; 16079113}.

MF grading is required at baseline and during follow up evaluation to document amelioration in the severity of MF in response to certain treatments, particularly JAK2 inhibitors and allogeneic stem cell transplantation {24056820; 29544547; 27211272; 26357842; 17976523}. Prompt reduction in bone marrow fibrosis after allogeneic stem cell transplant is a favourable marker of overall survival {24589549}.

Pre-PMF

It is estimated that 30–50% of PMF cases are first detected in the prefibrotic/early stage {28351937; 25189724}, with no significant increase in reticulin and/or collagen fibres (MF-0 and MF-1). Pre-PMF is a subtype of PMF characterized by a hypercellular bone marrow, minimal to absent reticulin fibrosis (grade 0 or 1), and a more latent disease course.

The peripheral blood smear usually shows thrombocytosis and/or leukocytosis. Bone marrow evaluation shows a hypercellular marrow with myeloid predominance showing granulocytic predominance. Erythroid islands may not be well formed, but erythroid precursors do not show any dysplastic features. The megakaryocytes are increased in number and arranged in loose clusters. They are frequently present in perisinusoidal and paratrabecular locations. Megakaryocytes show marked variation in size and nuclear lobulation; larger forms are more characteristic. Megakaryocytic nuclei are frequently hyperchromatic and bulbous, and they show cloud-like chromatin. Both hyperlobated and hypolobated forms are noted {19844986; 16210032; 20425436}. Megakaryocyte numbers and their morphological features may be further highlighted using immunohistochemistry for CD42b or CD61. There is no increase in blasts or overt dysplasia.

A recent study suggested that the presence of megakaryocytic activation, a morphological triad comprising megakaryocytic emperipolesis, megakaryocytic clustering (in groups of three or more), and perimegakaryocytic fibrosis, was specific as a predictor of fibrotic evolution {33543865}.

Fibrotic PMF

The peripheral blood smear shows anisopoikilocytosis of red blood cells, dacrocytes (teardrop forms), and leukoerythroblastosis.

The key morphological feature is atypical megakaryocytic proliferation. The megakaryocytes are increased in number and often seen in tight clusters, and they show abnormal paratrabecular, perisinusoidal, and intrasinusoidal localization. The megakaryocytes show defects in maturation, leading to abnormal N:C ratios, dense chromatin clumping, nuclear hyperchromasia, bulbous (cloud-like) nuclear lobes, and naked nuclei. Erythropoiesis is relatively decreased, as compared with granulopoiesis. There is no significant dysplasia in erythroid or granulocytic precursors at the time of initial diagnosis.

Moderate (MF-2) or severe (MF-3) MF is by definition present. Cellularity may be normal or decreased for age. The bone marrow eventually gets replaced by reticulin and/or collagen fibres, connective tissue, and adipose tissue interspersed with scattered foci of residual haematopoiesis. Additional features include prominent vasculature with increased numbers of vessels and dilated sinuses showing intrasinusoidal haematopoiesis. The degree of osteosclerosis can vary, ranging from focal bud-like appositional new bone formation to broad interconnecting bridges extensively replacing the marrow {16202684}.

CD34 and/or KIT (CD117) may be used to help enumerate blasts and immature myeloid cells. CD61 or CD42b can further help highlight the number and topography of megakaryocytes. Megakaryocytes would be positive for CAL2 by immunohistochemistry in cases with mutant CALR {27686170}.

Extramedullary disease

Extramedullary disease in the spleen can have a diffuse, nodular, or mixed pattern. All three haematopoietic lineages are represented in these patterns. In the diffuse pattern, the abnormal infiltrate is mostly restricted to the red pulp. Erythroid precursors are noted predominantly within the splenic sinusoids, the granulocytic precursors are noted mostly within the splenic cords and megakaryocytes at both sites. Megakaryocytes are frequently pleomorphic, showing the entire range of morphological features similar to those seen in the bone marrow. The nodular pattern forms an expansile tumour. A subset of cases can demonstrate megakaryocyte-rich splenic nodules, which are composed of haematopoietic cells with numerous abnormal megakaryocytes with a peripheral meshwork of reticulin fibres {33934231}. A proportion of cases can show > 20% blasts (usually CD34-positive) qualifying the case as blast-phase and requiring correlation with bone marrow findings.

Differential diagnosis

The distinction of pre-PMF from other MPNs and reactive proliferations is important because most cases of pre-PMF eventually evolve into fibrotic PMF. No single morphological feature can assist in this distinction; a constellation of morphological features and clinical presentation is essential to evaluate cases that are diagnostically challenging. The bone marrow in pre-PMF is hypercellular (unlike most cases of essential thrombocythaemia [ET]) and most often shows a significant increase in myeloid-to-erythroid ratio (unlike both ET and polycythaemia vera [PV]). Prominent clustering of megakaryocytes and a prominence of megakaryocytes with hyperchromatic nuclei and marked atypical features such as the presence of smaller cells favour a diagnosis of pre-PMF.

Fibrotic PMF should be distinguished from post-PV MF and post-ET MF, chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia with fibrosis {29192651}, desmoplasia associated with metastatic epithelial tumours, metastatic mesenchymal tumours such as splenic angiosarcomas {23800601}, and autoimmune fibrosis {25282037}. The bone marrow in autoimmune conditions typically shows erythroid-predominant hyperplasia with lymphoid aggregates and polytypic plasmacytosis without megakaryocytic clustering or atypia. Autoimmune fibrosis lacks peripheral blood changes of leukoerythroblastosis and teardrop poikilocytosis {25282037}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular diagnostic tests are typically aimed at detecting mutations in JAK2 (p.V617F or exon 12 mutations), CALR, or MPL. The JAK2 p.V617F mutation is detected in nearly 60% of patients {29226763}. Mutations in the calreticulin gene, CALR, are found in 25–35% of patients; type 1 CALR mutations are much more common than type 2 mutations in PMF, and in this context they carry a more favourable prognosis than other mutations. Activating point mutations in the thrombopoietin receptor gene, MPL, are found in 5–10% of patients. The advent of next-generation sequencing has allowed the detection of such mutations in the context of broader mutation panels.

Mutations in other cancer driver genes are found in more than half of all patients. They commonly include mutations in TET2, ASXL1, and DNMT3A. Less prevalent are mutations in splicing regulators (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, and ZRSR2) and other regulators of chromatin structure, epigenetic functions, and cellular signalling (e.g. EZH2, IDH1, IDH2, CBL, KRAS, NRAS, STAG2, and TP53). These additional mutations are more common in PMF and blast phases than in pre-PMF, PV, and ET. Some of these mutations are known to be associated with a worse prognosis (e.g. EZH2, IDH1, IDH2, SRSF2, and ASXL1). Mutation of TP53 is associated with leukaemic transformation and identifies a disease category with a particularly unfavourable outlook.

Cytogenetic abnormalities are detected in up to 45% of PMF patients {29472717; 25037629} and in about 18% of patients with pre-PMF {28351937; 29987745}. By definition, the Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome and BCR::ABL1 should be absent. Rare cases with concurrent BCR::ABL1 and mutations in JAK2 are often a result of the two distinct myeloproliferative neoplasms {29327708}. The most common recurrent abnormalities are del(20q), del(13q), trisomy 8, and trisomy 9 {29472717}. Complex karyotype, inv(3), monosomy 5 or del(5q), monosomy 7 or del(7q), del(12p), 11q23 rearrangement, and i(17q) are considered unfavourable and can be detected in 4–8% of pre-PMF patients {28351937; 29987745} and up to 40% of PMF patients {19901264; 21149668; 30824279}.

Prognosis and prediction

Pre-PMF

See Pre-PMF under the Histopathology subheading, above.

Fibrotic PMF

The detection of 10–19% blasts in the peripheral blood and/or bone marrow, especially with CD34 immunohistochemistry showing clusters of blasts, indicates progression to the accelerated phase. Detection of ≥ 20% blasts indicates transformation to the blast phase. Other evidence of progression includes new-onset monocytosis and/or the development of dysplastic changes.

The prognosis for patients with fibrotic PMF has been studied extensively, whereas data on the prognosis for those with pre-PMF are quite limited but suggest that PMF prognostic scoring tools are probably not applicable. Additionally, the prognosis for patients with post-ET or post-PV MF is distinct (based on multiple features, especially the fact that cytopenias are not as common or as severe in these disease states; see #30708Table 2.05, and the MF Secondary to PV and ET Prognostic Model [MYSEC PM] score) {27354022; 18988864; 21149668; 20008785; 27885272; 28561069}. Survival time in pre-PMF (median: 14.7 years) appears to be shorter in general than in ET (median: 31 years; P < 0.0001). Furthermore, being diagnosed with fibrotic PMF was reported to correspond to a hazard ratio for reduced survival of 2.3 (P < 0.0001), as compared with being diagnosed with pre-PMF. Leukaemia-free survival was shorter in fibrotic PMF than pre-PMF; the cumulative incidence rates of leukaemia in patients with pre-PMF and with fibrotic PMF were, respectively, 7% and 11% at 5 years, and 12% and 23% at 10 years – much higher than those in patients with ET (0% and 1% at 5 years and at 10 years, respectively) {28351937}.

A few key observations have been made regarding the prognosis of PMF. First, survival times can vary dramatically, from < 3 years on the higher-risk side to decades on the lower-risk side. Second, the disease features that reproducibly impact prognosis include multiple disease features from patient features, peripheral blood counts, bone marrow features, and genetic studies. Third, the clinical state, availability of data for the required variables, and usage need to be considered when applying. Fourth, the prognosis does not necessarily equate to the clinical disease burden, because certain high-risk patients may be less symptomatic, and certain lower-risk patients may be more symptomatic {32198525}. The main clinical utility of PMF prognostic scores is in separating lower-risk patients from everyone else, and these scores have more of an impact on guiding decisions on stem cell transplantation than on the selection of medical therapy. The prognostic scores and required data elements are included in #30708Table 2.05 . The International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) is unique in being able to be used solely at the time of diagnosis, whereas the others can be used throughout the disease course {18988864}.

The Dynamic IPSS (DIPSS) is probably the most universally used tool, whereas the DIPSS Plus provides greater clarity when the karyotype is available {20008785; 21149668}.

The Mutation-Enhanced International Prognostic Scoring System for Transplant-Age Patients (MIPSS70) and MIPSS70 2.0 are applicable for patients aged < 70 years (with a view to guiding stem cell transplantation), but they do require molecular phenotype. Although other scores have been published, this portfolio serves most clinical needs, with additional scores perhaps refining the prognosis for the stem cell transplant decision {29226763; 29708808; 29472717}.
