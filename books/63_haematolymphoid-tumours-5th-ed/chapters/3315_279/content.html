3.3.1.5 ALK-positive histiocytosis

Definition

ALK-positive histiocytosis is a histiocytic neoplasm lacking high-grade cytological atypia and characterized by ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) immunoreactivity, usually due to ALK gene rearrangement.

ICD-O coding

9750/3 ALK-positive histiocytosis

ICD-11 coding

EK92 Histiocytoses of uncertain malignant potential

Related terminology

Acceptable: ALK-related histiocytosis, ALK-rearranged histiocytosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Multisystem with systemic haematopoietic involvement: Systemic involvement of the liver, spleen, and/or marrow, but additional sites can be involved, such as the skin and CNS {18660380; 30573850; 29224419; 34727172}.

Multisystem (others): Tumorous involvement of two or more organ systems. Commonly involved sites include the central and peripheral nervous system, bone, lung, and skin {32826530; 34727172}.

Single-system: Tumorous involvement of one single organ system, with solitary or multiple lesions. Commonly involved sites include the central and peripheral nervous system, skin, breast, and soft tissue {31119374; 30573850; 32826530; 34482333; 34727172}.

Clinical features

Multisystem systemic disease occurs in infants, who present with hepatomegaly and/or splenomegaly, severe anaemia, and thrombocytopenia, in the absence of fever, and sometimes accompanied by coagulopathy {18660380; 34727172}. Patients with multisystem (others) or single-system disease present with symptoms referable to involved sites, such as a mass lesion. Neurological involvement is common, manifesting with symptoms such as seizures, headaches, vomiting, ataxia, hypotonicity, paroxysmal neurological symptoms, trigeminal neuralgia, and diplopia {34727172}.

Epidemiology

ALK-positive histiocytosis is rare. The multisystem systemic form occurs in infancy, whereas the multisystem (others) and single-system forms can occur in any age group, including children and adults {30573850; 34482333; 31119374; 32826530; 31371408; 32721959; 29224419; 34727172}. It has a female predilection {34727172}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The cell of origin of ALK-positive histiocytosis is unknown; however, the disease phenotype closely resembles that of the juvenile xanthogranuloma family of lesions, which is believed to be derived from a long-lived haematopoietic stem cell, as has been studied in Erdheim–Chester disease and Langerhans cell histiocytosis {28512190; 28566492}. Like other histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms, a small proportion of cases show a lineage relationship with lymphoid neoplasms, such as the presence of clonal IG gene rearrangement {34482333; 33011863}.

ALK-positive histiocytosis is characterized by ALK gene translocation, most commonly with exon 24 of KIF5B fused to exon 20 of ALK {30573850; 34727172}. Uncommon partner genes include CLTC, TPM3, TFG, EML4, DCTN1, COL1A2, and TRIM33 {34727172; 30573850; 33243034}. ALK rearrangement can lead to the activation of many different signalling pathways, including the MAPK pathway, as evidenced by frequent positive staining for phosphorylated ERK {34727172}.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Tumorous lesions of ALK-positive histiocytosis are circumscribed or poorly defined, with whitish-yellow cut surfaces {34482333; 30573850}.

Histopathology

The histiocytes can assume different appearances including large oval (epithelioid) cells, foamy cells, and spindle cells, which are present in highly variable proportions. They do not exhibit significant nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic activity is low. The large oval histiocytes show irregular nuclear contours, in the form of indentation, irregular folding, deep clefts, or lobulation, as well as fine chromatin and small nucleoli; occasional cells can be multinucleated. They have abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm that can show fine vacuolation, haemosiderin deposition, and emperipolesis. Foamy histiocytes have abundant multivacuolated cytoplasm, and the nuclei often show irregular folds. There can be admixed Touton giant cells. Spindle histiocytes are particularly common in tumours occurring in the breast {32826530; 34482333} and may assume a fascicular or storiform growth pattern. These cells have elongated nuclei with smooth or indented nuclear membranes and eosinophilic cytoplasm.

In the liver, the histiocytes infiltrate the sinusoids and portal tracts, and may coalesce into small nodules. The histiocytes, with their large size and eosinophilic cytoplasm, can be difficult to distinguish from the hepatocytes. Bone marrow infiltration, if present, ranges from subtle to patchy and, in exceptional cases, to extensive involvement {18660380; 30573850}.

Involved skin shows a non-circumscribed, non-epidermotropic infiltrate comprising sheets of histiocytes, which may be accompanied by variable degrees of fibrosis. Some examples are morphologically indistinguishable from juvenile xanthogranuloma {30573850; 34727172}. Other involved tissues show a circumscribed or infiltrative mass-forming lesion comprising aggregates and sheets of atypical histiocytes.

Immunohistochemistry

The histiocytes (including foamy histiocytes) are ALK-positive (by definition), in a cytoplasmic pattern or (rarely) in a membranous or Golgi dot pattern, but practically never in a nuclear pattern {30573850; 32826530; 34727172}. ALK immunostaining can occasionally be weak and focal {34727172}. There is variable positivity for histiocytic markers, such as CD68, CD163, CD14, CD4, and lysozyme. Some cases show staining for S100, cyclin D1, or OCT2 {34727172}. Fascin and factor XIIIa are commonly positive, whereas CD30, CD1a, and CD207 (langerin) are negative. The Ki-67 proliferation fraction is low.

Differential diagnosis

See #32035Table 3.02 in the section on Rosai–Dorfman disease.

 

Cytology

It is difficult to make an unequivocal diagnosis of this condition from fine core needle biopsies.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of ALK translocation is helpful but may be difficult in some cases, particularly with limited material.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: tissue infiltration by aggregates and sheets of histiocytes lacking high-grade atypia; positive immunostaining for two or more histiocytic markers (CD163, CD68, CD14, CD4, lysozyme); positive immunostaining for ALK (albeit focal and weak sometimes).

Desirable: histiocytes showing irregular nuclear folds; ALK translocation (except in limited specimens).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Although infants with multisystem systemic disease often experience spontaneous regression or remission after chemotherapy {18660380; 30573850; 34727172}, supportive care is imperative. Disease progression can cause death in rare cases {34727172}.

Multisystem (others) disease may benefit from systemic chemotherapies, but resistance often occurs, and targeted therapy with ALK inhibitors may better achieve a durable response {30573850; 32826530; 34727172}. The outcome is typically favourable.

Single-system involvement (e.g. disease limited to the skin, soft tissue, breast, or CNS) can be cured by surgical ablation, although incompletely resected or unresectable tumours may require systemic or targeted therapy {30573850; 31119374; 34482333; 34727172}.
