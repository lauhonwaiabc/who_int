2.2.1.5 Polycythaemia vera

Definition

Polycythaemia vera (PV) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by erythrocytosis, frequently accompanied by leukocytosis and/or thrombocytosis, and typically associated with activating JAK2 mutations.

ICD-O coding

9950/3 Polycythaemia vera

ICD-11 coding

2A20.4 Polycythaemia vera

Related terminology

Not recommended: polycythaemia rubra vera; primary polycythaemia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Blood and bone marrow

Clinical features

The presentation of PV can be quite variable and can include asymptomatic erythrocytosis (or leukocytosis or thrombocytosis) discovered at the time of routine laboratory testing (e.g. annual health examination or preoperative testing), at the time of evaluation of PV-related symptoms, or at the time of evaluation of an unexpected vascular event (e.g. thrombosis or bleeding). PV is classically characterized by an increase in erythrocytes not explainable by a secondary physiological driver such as chronic hypoxia from cardiorespiratory diseases. Laboratory studies at presentation of PV usually demonstrate clear erythrocytosis, but at times erythrocytosis may be masked by an underlying iron deficiency if present. Leukocytosis (usually neutrophilia) and/or thrombocytosis are variably present at baseline in PV.

In nearly 20% of cases of PV, presentation is at the time of an unexpected venous event (e.g. deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolus, or sagittal vein thrombosis) or arterial event (e.g. myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular event). Moreover, patients who experience vascular events in the portal circulation, especially mesenteric, portal, and splenic vein thrombosis, are likely to be diagnosed with either PV or a related MPN driven by JAK2 p.V617F. Patients with such presentations of PV can have normal blood counts, with more overt erythrocytosis and other haematological findings sometimes occurring subsequently.

Patients with PV are usually symptomatic, with the most common symptoms including fatigue, pruritus (frequently aquagenic and worsened by bathing), and night sweats, as well as a range of symptoms driven by high red blood cell mass, such as headaches (including migraines), difficulties with concentration, and less commonly bone pain or splenomegaly-related symptoms.

Physical manifestations of PV are not universal but can include plethora (especially pre-phlebotomy), occasionally splenomegaly, and rarely hepatomegaly.

There has been active discussion regarding the optimal numerical values to be used for the formal diagnosis of PV and which assessment of erythrocytosis (elevated haemoglobin, haematocrit, or red cell mass) should be used to define the diagnostic hallmark of PV. Additionally, a “masked PV” label has been proposed for patients with JAK2 mutation and suspicion of PV but who lack sufficient erythrocytosis. However, we prefer to avoid this term by assigning a diagnosis of MPN-NOS and reviewing the patient closely. Clinical studies that facilitate the diagnosis of PV include the detection of JAK2 p.V617F or functionally similar mutation (e.g. JAK2 exon 12) and subnormal serum erythropoietin levels.

Epidemiology

According to data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program, the overall incidence rate is 1.57 cases (95% CI: 1.55–1.60) per 100 000 person-years {34689695}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Acquired somatic mutations in JAK2, at chromosome band 9p24.1, play a role in the pathogenesis of PV. The most common mutation, JAK2 p.V617F, results in a ligand-independent constitutively active cytoplasmic JAK-2 protein, which activates the STAT, MAPK, and PI3K signalling pathways to promote the transformation and proliferation of haematopoietic progenitors. Valine 617 is located in the JH2 domain of JAK-2, which acts to supress its kinase activity {35271300}. Other mutations in exon 12 of JAK2, usually in-frame insertions or deletions affecting the pseudokinase domain, have been reported in almost all cases lacking JAK2 p.V617F; these patients have a more isolated erythrocytosis {17267906}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Peripheral blood and bone marrow findings

At higher haematocrit levels, peripheral blood smear evaluation may show close packing and cell distortion. Red blood cells are usually normochromic and normocytic, but iron deficiency may result in hypochromic and microcytic erythrocytes. Neutrophilia with some left shift and rarely basophilia may be present. There is moderate thrombocytosis. Postpolycythaemic myelofibrosis is usually associated with leukoerythroblastic findings, including poikilocytosis and teardrop-shaped red blood cells (dacrocytes).

Bone marrow smears show a prominent erythroid proliferation with either a normal or a reduced myeloid-to-erythroid ratio. Erythroid precursors mostly show a normoblastic maturation pattern. Granulocytic cells show a normal pattern of maturation, and blasts are not increased. Megakaryocytes are increased in number and show atypical morphology, with enlarged and hypersegmented nuclei.

Histological sections usually show a hypercellular bone marrow with proliferation in all lineages (panmyelosis), often with erythroid predominance in untreated patients. Erythroid islands are usually large and prominent. Granulocytic maturation is normal. Megakaryocytic abnormalities are typical and include increased numbers, aberrant distribution, and atypical morphology. Megakaryocytes often form loose clusters and lie in proximity to the endosteal surface of bone trabeculae. Megakaryocytes vary in size and morphology and usually include forms with hypersegmented (staghorn-like) hyperchromatic nuclei. {23900239}. Blast-phase PV is characterized by overt myelodysplasia and moderate to severe myelofibrosis {29285580}.

Reticulin and Masson trichrome stains usually show absent (MF-0) or mild (MF-1) myelofibrosis (European Consensus System) {22246040; 23900239; 16079113}. Reactive nodular lymphoid aggregates can be seen in some cases. Most cases show reduced iron stores {16781481}.

Postpolycythaemic myelofibrosis

About 20% of PV patients develop postpolycythaemic myelofibrosis (post-PV MF) over time {25037629}. Bone marrow cellularity is highly variable and varies from hypercellular to hypocellular. Sinuses are dilated, and intrasinusoidal haematopoietic cells are present. Megakaryocytes tend to be a prominent component, exhibiting atypical morphology and present in tight clusters. Myelofibrosis graded as MF-2 or MF-3 is present, by definition, often in conjunction with osteosclerosis {23787440; 24897074}. Bone marrow aspirate smears are usually of limited diagnostic value.

Differential diagnosis

PV must be distinguished from reactive or secondary polycythaemias on the one hand and from other MPNs (essential thrombocythaemia [ET] and prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis [pre-PMF]) on the other. Some cases in patients with PV may initially be diagnosed as MPN-NOS, often in the context of splanchnic vein thrombosis. Similarly, post-PV MF must be distinguished from primary myelofibrosis (PMF) and myelodysplastic neoplasm with fibrosis. Furthermore, accelerated- and blast-phase PV needs to be distinguished from therapy-related myeloid neoplasms.

 

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular diagnostic tests are typically aimed at detecting JAK2 p.V617F or mutations in exon 12 of JAK2. The advent of next-generation sequencing has allowed the detection of such mutations in the context of broader mutation panels. Other genes commonly mutated in in PV include TET2, ASXL1, IDH2, and SRSF2 {29296692; 31945802}.

Up to 20% of patients have an abnormal karyotype at diagnosis, including isolated del(20q), trisomy 8, and trisomy 9 {28473622}. Disease progression and blast phase transformation are associated with additional abnormalities and complex karyotypes.

Prognosis and prediction

PV is a relatively indolent disease, but it does impact negatively on survival, as compared with an age- and sex-matched population (see #33228Box 2.06 and #33229Box 2.07) {30824279; 12551821}. Leukaemic transformation occurs in 2.3–14.4% of patients with PV within 10 years and in 5.5–18.7% within 15 years {23739289; 26565403}. Risk factors for leukaemic transformation include age and leukocytosis {23739289; 26565403; 15710945; 33035330; 23151052}. Cytogenetic abnormalities associated with transformation include del(20q), +9, +8, double abnormalities, and complex karyotype {28473622; 23739289; 26565403; 28695995; 29285580}. In addition, mutations involving SRSF2, IDH2, RUNX1, TP53, ASXL1, IDH1, and IDH2 have been correlated with leukaemic transformation {31945802; 29285580; 33035330; 26565403; 29181548; 29296692}. Transformation has also been associated with the acquisition of one or more additional mutations during the chronic phase {33035330}. Some have reported an association between certain cytoreductive agents and leukaemic transformation {23739289}.

Major criteria:

1.   Elevated haemoglobin concentration (> 16.5 g/dL in men, > 16.0 g/dL in women) or elevated haematocrit (> 49%a in men, > 48% in women)

2.   Bone marrow biopsy showing age-adjusted hypercellularity with trilineage growth (panmyelosis), including prominent erythroid, granulocytic, and megakaryocytic proliferation with pleomorphic, mature megakaryocytes (differences in size)b

3.   Presence of JAK2 p.V617F or JAK2 exon 12 mutation

Minor criterion:

·    Subnormal serum erythropoietin level

The diagnosis of polycythaemia vera requires either all three major criteria or the first two major criteria plus the minor criterion

aHaematocrit for diagnosis in the presence of a JAK2 mutation. In the absence of a JAK2 mutation, a higher haematocrit target (e.g. 52%) could be considered in men before further investigation is required. bMajor criterion 2 (bone marrow biopsy) may not be required in patients with sustained absolute erythrocytosis (haemoglobin concentrations of > 18.5 g/dL in men or > 16.5 g/dL in women, or haematocrit values of > 55.5% in men or > 49.5% in women) if major criterion 3 and the minor criterion are present.

Note: Determination of the red cell mass with 51Cr-labelled red cells allows the differentiation between true polyglobulia and pseudopolyglobulia. This is not a method for routine clinical use.
