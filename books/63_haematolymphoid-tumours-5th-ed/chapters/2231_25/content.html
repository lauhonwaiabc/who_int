2.2.3.1 Myelodysplastic neoplasms: Introduction

This edition introduces the term “myelodysplastic neoplasm” (MDS) to replace “myelodysplastic syndrome”, underscoring the neoplastic nature of these diseases and harmonizing the terminology with that of myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN). The abbreviation “MDS” was favoured over “MDN” in view of the phonetic and syntactical similarities between “MDN” and “MPN”. Fundamentally, the WHO classification recognizes MDS as a category of clonal haematopoietic stem cell neoplasms, defined by cytopenias and morphological dysplasia, and clinically characterized by progressively ineffective haematopoiesis and an increased risk of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML).

According to data from the SEER database, the age-adjusted incidence rate of MDS for 2014–2018 was 4.2 per 100 000 person-years. Epidemiological studies in Europe and Australia have shown similar incidence rates {30314642; 23572136; 24388662; 23305556}. Patients with MDS have a median age of 77 years at diagnosis, and < 10% are younger than 50 years of age. The incidence of MDS is very low in people aged < 40 years (0.1 cases per 100 000 person-years) but increases progressively between the ages of 60 and 90 years (from 2.2 to 56.8 cases per 100 000 person-years) {37260869; 30314642}. A younger age at disease onset is more common among Asian patient populations {9652732; 12592327}. There is a slight male predominance, except in MDS with low blasts and 5q deletion (MDS-5q), which has a higher incidence in women {21708407; 22735748}. Evaluation of the SEER data for race/ethnicity indicates a higher rate of MDS among White individuals than among non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Asian / Pacific Islander individuals (4.6, 3.5, 3, and 2.8 per 100 000 person-years, respectively). Data from the SEER database and from the two large international populations of patients with MDS that were utilized to generate the International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) and the Revised IPSS (IPSS-R) indicated that 34–39% of patients present with higher-risk disease {9058730; 22740453}.

A unified framework for defining cytopenias is now adopted across clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance, MDS, and myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms in view of their biological and clinical overlap. These definitions include haemoglobin < 13 g/dL in male patients and < 12 g/dL in female patients for anaemia, absolute neutrophil count < 1.8 × 109/L for leukopenia, and platelets < 150 × 109/L for thrombocytopenia {27535995}. Cytopenia in at least one haematopoietic lineage is required for a diagnosis of MDS. A diagnosis of MDS may still be made in patients with milder degrees of anaemia if definitive morphological and cytogenetic findings are present {27535995}. In determining whether a patient is cytopenic, it is important to be cognizant of each laboratory’s lower reference range and to consider conditional variants of these values, including ethnicity and sex. Persistent neutrophilia, monocytosis, erythrocytosis, or thrombocytosis in a patient with cytopenias and dysplastic morphology generally warrants classification as a myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm or myeloproliferative neoplasm. However, thrombocytosis (platelet count ≥ 450 × 109/L) is allowed in MDS-5q.

The utilization of a variety of diagnostic techniques in the evaluation of MDS improves diagnostic accuracy, because no single technique captures the broad diagnostic complexity of this disease. Diagnostic evaluation should always include microscopic examination of adequate bone marrow and peripheral blood smears, coupled with one or more of the following: chromosome analysis (e.g. conventional karyotyping, FISH, etc.), mutation analysis (e.g. next-generation sequencing mutation profiling), and immunophenotypic evaluation by multiparameter flow cytometry. Although mutation profiling has improved the diagnostic accuracy of MDS with normal karyotype by conventional cytogenetics, the role of karyotyping remains paramount for diagnostic and prognostic purposes. Flow cytometry may be a helpful adjunct for diagnosing MDS, but its role as a primary diagnostic tool in this context remains to be determined.

Dysplasia in one or more myeloid lineages is the morphological hallmark of MDS. The threshold for dysplasia is set at 10% for all lineages and across all MDS types, as well as for myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms. Dysplasia may be accompanied by an increase in myeloid blasts in the peripheral blood and/or bone marrow, but the blast percentage is always < 20%. The myeloid lineages affected by cytopenias are not necessarily those that manifest dysplasia {22421409; 23122806}. To determine blast percentage in the bone marrow and blood, a 500-cell differential count of all nucleated cells in a smear or trephine biopsy imprint is recommended for the bone marrow, and a 200-leukocyte differential count for the peripheral blood. The blast count is expressed as a percentage of all nucleated cells in the bone marrow (always including nucleated erythroid cells) and as a percentage of the leukocytes (excluding nucleated erythroid cells) in the peripheral blood.

Dyserythropoiesis manifests principally as nuclear alterations, including budding, internuclear bridging, karyorrhexis, and multinuclearity. Megaloblastic (preferred over the term “megaloblastoid”) changes are common but by themselves are insufficient to establish dyserythropoiesis {29917221}. Cytoplasmic features include the formation of ring sideroblasts, vacuolization, and aberrant PAS positivity (either diffuse or granular). Dysgranulopoiesis is characterized primarily by nuclear hyposegmentation (pseudo–Pelger–Huët anomaly) or hypersegmentation, cytoplasmic hypogranularity, pseudo–Chédiak–Higashi granules, and small size {24439566}. Megakaryocyte dysplasia is characterized by micromegakaryocytes, non-lobated nuclei in megakaryocytes of all sizes, and multiple widely separated nuclei {27107657}. Megakaryocytic dysplasia is readily apparent in bone marrow sections, and both biopsy (or clot) and aspirate specimens should be evaluated. The morphological manifestations of dysplasia in each lineage are summarized in #36674Table 2.10 .

As a general precaution, no patient should be diagnosed with MDS if the clinical and drug history is unknown, and no case of MDS should be reclassified while the patient is on growth factor therapy, including erythropoietin. Certain drugs, infections, metabolic deficiencies, and immune disorders can cause both cytopenias and morphological dysplasia; these possible secondary etiologies must be carefully considered before rendering a diagnosis of MDS.

In this edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, MDS entities are grouped into two families: (1) those with defining genetic abnormalities and (2) those that are morphologically defined (see #36675Table 2.11). The intention of this reorganization is to enhance classification rigour by emphasizing genetically defined disease types and ceding the prior emphasis on risk-based grouping (based on blast percentage, ring sideroblasts, and the number of lineages with dysplasia) in favour of more comprehensive risk-stratification schemes that incorporate other relevant variables such as age, severity of cytopenias, and cytogenetic findings. An additional modification is a clarified terminology to distinguish between MDS with low blasts and MDS with increased blasts, while retaining longstanding cut-off values.

MDSs with defining genetic abnormalities are now grouped together and comprise MDS-5q, MDS with low blasts and SF3B1 mutation (MDS-SF3B1), and MDS with biallelic TP53 inactivation. This last diagnosis supersedes MDS-5q and MDS-SF3B1. The qualifier “low blasts” has been added to MDS-5q and MDS-SF3B1 for clarity.

The diagnostic criteria for MDS-5q have not changed. The presence of SF3B1 or a TP53 mutation (not multi-hit), although recognized as a factor that may potentially alter the biology and/or prognosis of the disease, does not per se override the diagnosis of MDS-5q.

Recent studies have identified MDS-SF3B1 as a distinct disease type that includes > 90% of cases of MDS with ≥ 5% ring sideroblasts. Nevertheless, the term “MDS with low blasts and ring sideroblasts” has been retained as an acceptable alternative to be used for cases with wildtype SF3B1 and/or ≥ 15% ring sideroblasts, to help broaden applicability in low-resource settings and provide an option to include rare MDS cases harbouring driver mutations in other RNA splicing components.

The introduction of MDS with biallelic TP53 inactivation recognizes the unique biological and clinical features of such cases, which often have very high risk features and complex cytogenetics. This remains an area of active research, which will undoubtedly result in future refinements. How such cases are optimally detected in various practice settings and whether biallelic TP53 inactivation in myeloid neoplasms is per se an AML-defining molecular alteration are some of the questions for consideration in future editions of the WHO classification.

Changes under morphologically defined MDSs include the recognition of hypoplastic MDS as a distinct disease type. Long recognized as having distinctive features, hypoplastic MDS is associated with a T cell–mediated immune attack on haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, along with oligoclonal expansion of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells overproducing interferon and/or TNF-α. Several features overlap across the triad of hypoplastic MDS, paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria, and aplastic anaemia, including an association with clonal haematopoiesis. Because the number of dysplastic lineages is usually dynamic and often represents clinical and phenotypic manifestations of clonal evolution within an MDS type, rather than being a distinctive separate MDS type per se, the distinction between single-lineage and multilineage dysplasia is now considered optional. The updated MDS classification scheme and the incorporation of clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance in the present WHO classification obviate the need for the attributes “NOS” or “unclassifiable”. Specifically, MDS, unclassifiable, which was present in the prior edition, has been removed.

Reassessment of the bone marrow blast percentage defining the boundary between MDS with increased blasts-2 (MDS-IB2) and AML has been advocated for several cogent reasons and in view of novel therapeutic approaches that show efficacy in cases currently classified as MDS or AML with 10–30% myeloid blasts. Salient practical challenges underpinning arguments for such a reassessment include the following: (1) any blast-based cut-off point is arbitrary and cannot reflect the biological continuity naturally inherent in myeloid pathogenic mechanisms; (2) blast enumeration is subject to sampling variations/error and subjective evaluation; and (3) no gold standard for blast enumeration exists, and orthogonal testing platforms can and often do produce discordant results. The pros and cons of merging MDS-IB2 with AML and adopting a 10% cut-off point for what would be called MDS/AML were explored in multidisciplinary expert discussions and at editorial board meetings in the course of producing this volume. Lowering the blast cut-off point to define AML was felt to suffer from the same challenges listed above and would merely replace one cut-off point with another. Furthermore, an arbitrary cut-off point of 10% blasts to define AML (even if qualified as MDS/AML or AML/MDS) carries a risk of overtreatment. Accordingly, a balanced approach was adopted by eliminating blast cut-off points for most AML types with defining genetic alterations but retaining a 20% blast cut-off point to delineate MDS from AML. Nevertheless, there was broad agreement that MDS-IB2 may be regarded as AML-equivalent for therapeutic considerations and from a clinical trial design perspective when appropriate.

Childhood MDS is a clonal haematopoietic stem cell neoplasm arising in children and adolescents (by definition ≤ 18 years of age) leading to ineffective haematopoiesis, cytopenia, and risk of progression to AML. The terminology of childhood MDS types has been updated. The qualifying term “childhood” emphasizes that this category of myeloid neoplasms is biologically distinct from that seen in adults, underscoring the need to further elucidate its pathogenesis, which remains incompletely understood. The term “childhood MDS with low blasts” replaces the former term “refractory cytopenia of childhood”. It includes two subtypes: childhood MDS with low blasts, hypocellular; and childhood MDS with low blasts NOS. Childhood MDS with increased blasts is defined as having ≥ 5% blasts in the bone marrow or ≥ 2% blasts in the peripheral blood.
