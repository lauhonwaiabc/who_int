2.2.3.8 Myelodysplastic neoplasm with biallelic TP53 inactivation

Definition

Myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) with biallelic TP53 inactivation (MDS-biTP53) is a myeloid neoplasm with cytopenia, dysplasia, < 20% blasts, and < 30% proerythroblasts, characterized by two or more TP53 mutations or one TP53 mutation and evidence of concurrent TP53 copy loss or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (LOH).

ICD-O coding

9985/3 Myelodysplastic neoplasm with biallelic TP53 inactivation

ICD-11 coding

2A4Y Other specified myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative neoplasms

Related terminology

Acceptable: myelodysplastic neoplasm with muti-hit TP53 inactivation.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Blood and bone marrow

Clinical features

The clinical symptoms are related to cytopenias, usually including anaemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia.

Epidemiology

TP53 alterations of any type – sequence variations, gross deletions, and copy-neutral LOH – have been detected in 7–11% of all MDS cases {32747829; 30635634; 32747829}. TP53 alterations are biallelic in about two thirds of MDS cases with TP53 alteration {21708407; 32747829}. Based on these data, the estimated incidence of MDS-biTP53 is about 0.03 cases per 100 000 person-years.

Etiology

MDS-biTP53 can occur spontaneously, after exposure to certain DNA-damaging agents (e.g. chemotherapeutic drugs, radiation therapy), or in the context of an inherited predisposition (e.g. Li–Fraumeni syndrome or inherited bone marrow failure syndromes), mostly via expansion of age-related TP53-mutated haematopoietic cell clones rather than via direct induction of TP53 damage in a haematopoietic stem or progenitor cell {31089247; 31395785; 25487151; 31990679}.

Pathogenesis

TP53 is the most commonly altered tumour suppressor gene in human cancers. The protein p53 has multiple cellular functions, including maintenance of genome stability, regulation of the cell cycle, cell death, and response to oxidative stress. As p53 is required to prevent aneuploid cells from moving through the G2 checkpoint, biallelic TP53 alterations are associated, in myeloid neoplasms as in other cancers, with extensive aneuploidy, genomic instability, and other complex chromosomal abnormalities.

Monoallelic and biallelic TP53 alterations may drive myeloid neoplasms through divergent mechanisms. Compared with MDS with single, monoallelic TP53 alterations, MDS-biTP53 cases have significantly fewer additional driver mutations and higher numbers of copy-number abnormalities and cytogenetic lesions {32747829}. Complex cytogenetic abnormalities commonly include 5q deletion; 17p LOH with and without copy-number alterations; monosomy 7 or 7q deletion; monosomy 18; abnormalities of chromosome 13, 3q, 9, and 21, frequently resulting in chromosomal monosomies {32747829; 28223278; 30635634}. The majority of TP53 alterations are missense mutations with hotspots across different cancer types and dominant-negative functional effects.

Mutation of TP53 can be detected in the context of clonal haematopoiesis in otherwise healthy individuals, conferring a significantly increased risk of mortality from a myeloid neoplasm {25426838; 25426837}. In such a setting, concurrent 17p LOH, suggestive of biallelic TP53 alterations, is associated with an increased mortality rate (16%) at 10 years compared with clonal haematopoiesis without 17p LOH {34239136; 33020477}. Indeed, inactivation of the second TP53 allele in a patient with monoallelic alteration can occur after years or even decades, leading to rapid progression and expansion of a genetically unstable clone that often also acquires complex karyotypic changes {31010849; 21519010; 32414002}.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

This type of MDS is associated with high-risk morphological findings, such as a higher blast count and fibrosis in the bone marrow. Dysplastic changes overlap with those of other MDS types.

Immunophenotype

Detection of a mutant p53 expression pattern by immunohistochemistry can be a helpful tool to screen for TP53 mutation {27081179; 26123119; 24682512; 32414002} and shows a significant association with variant allele frequency (VAF) {27081179}. Several studies have shown that strong nuclear staining correlates with TP53 mutations {35390143; 24682512; 26123119}. Mutant p53 expression often manifests as aberrant protein accumulation (overexpression) but may occasionally result in complete loss of staining (null). CD34 immunohistochemistry may be helpful for estimating the bone marrow blast count, particularly in cases with fibrosis {19103730}. E-cadherin may also be helpful for estimating the number of proerythroblasts.

Differential diagnosis

MDS-biTP53 supersedes other MDS types and should be distinguished primarily from defined types of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Importantly, cases where proerythroblasts comprise ≥ 30% of marrow cellularity should be designated as acute erythroid leukaemia (formerly, pure erythroid leukaemia). Although some clinicopathological features may overlap with those seen in MDS with low blasts with or without defined genetic alterations, biallelic TP53 alterations are highly enriched in MDS with increased blasts {32747829}.

 

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Biallelic TP53 alterations may consist of multiple mutations or mutations with corresponding allelic deletion. This multi-hit mutation status results in a neoplastic clone that lacks any wildtype p53 protein. Detection of biallelic TP53 alterations requires sequencing analysis (covering at least exons 4–11) {24220272; 24030381}, coupled with a technique to detect copy-number status, usually FISH with a probe set specific for the TP53 locus on 17p13.1. Loss of genetic material at the TP53 locus may also be detected using other copy-number assessment techniques, such as array comparative genomic hybridization, or inferred by next-generation sequencing {32747829}. A suitable copy-number assessment should be performed in addition to chromosome banding analysis to demonstrate loss of the TP53 locus in cases with 17p13.1 deletion, because the mere detection of 17p13.1 deletion is not usually sufficient to establish TP53 copy-number loss.

A TP53 VAF of > 49% may be regarded as presumptive (not definitive) of copy loss on the trans allele or copy-neutral LOH when a constitutional TP53 variant can be ruled out. When two or more TP53 mutations are detected, they usually affect both alleles and can be considered multi-hit events {32747829}.

More than 90% of patients have complex – mostly very complex (more than three) – chromosome abnormalities {32747829; 30635634; 32747829}, which constitutes the highest cytogenetic risk category in the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-R) {22740453}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cytopenia involving one or more lineages; dysplasia involving one or more lineages; blasts constitute < 20% of cells in the peripheral blood and bone marrow; detection of one or more TP53 mutations; in the presence of one TP53 mutation: direct or indirect evidence of TP53 copy loss or copy-neutral LOH.

Desirable: complex karyotype (at least three abnormalities).

 

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with MDS with monoallelic TP53 alteration have outcomes similar to those without mutant TP53, whereas patients with MDS-biTP53 have increased blast counts, a higher risk of leukaemic transformation, and a higher risk of death, independent of IPSS-R risk categorization and independent of treatment (including lenalidomide, azacitidine, and allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation) {26580448; 28223278; 32747829}. If comprehensive multi-hit TP53 analysis is not available, it should be noted that patients with MDS with mutant TP53 and a VAF of ≥ 40% and/or complex cytogenetics may have a similarly poor prognosis {26514544; 32027746; 30635634}.
