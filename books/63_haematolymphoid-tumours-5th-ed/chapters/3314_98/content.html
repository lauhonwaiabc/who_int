3.3.1.4 Rosai-Dorfman disease

Definition

Rosai–Dorfman disease (RDD) is a histiocytosis characterized by nodal or extranodal accumulation of large, S100-positive histiocytes/macrophages that commonly exhibit emperipolesis.

ICD-O coding

9749/3 Rosai–Dorfman disease

ICD-11 coding

EK92 Histiocytoses of uncertain malignant potential

Related terminology

Acceptable: Rosai–Dorfman–Destombes disease.

Not recommended: sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

RDD most frequently involves lymph nodes, particularly in the cervical region. Extranodal disease occurs in 43% of cases, most often involving the head and neck, skin, and CNS {2180012; 8491488; 17241598; 17325475; 26966089} (see #28822Table 3.01).

Clinical features

The typical presentation is that of painless, slow-growing lymphadenopathy, with accompanying B symptoms in one third of patients, or symptoms related to involved extranodal sites {2180012; 29720485}. Involved lymph nodes can be huge and matted together.

Epidemiology

The global incidence of RDD is unknown; the annual prevalence in the USA is 1:200 000 {29720485}. RDD affects all ethnic groups but is more common in patients of African descent. Men are 3.2 times as frequently affected as women, except with cutaneous RDD, which is more prevalent in Asian women {17241598; 17325475}. Nodal RDD usually arises in the second and third decades of life, whereas extranodal RDD arises in the fifth decade of life {2180012}.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown. Rare inherited disorders predispose to familial RDD (see #28822Table 3.01), including disorders associated with germline mutations in the nucleoside transporter gene SLC29A3 (H syndrome / Faisalabad histiocytosis), and FAS deficiency with heterozygous germline mutations in FAS (TNFRSF6) (autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome) {20140240; 24172204; 15958855}. These mutations are not known to be associated with sporadic RDD.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is incompletely understood. Approximately 50% of cases carry gain-of-function mutations in MAPK/ERK pathway genes, including KRAS, NRAS, MAP2K1, ARAF, CSF1R, and (rarely) BRAF p.V600E, suggesting that at least a proportion of cases of RDD represent a neoplastic disorder {26566875; 26922062; 28597077; 28664935; 29236635; 32601132}.

Macroscopic appearance

Lymph nodes are enlarged and matted, and they often form multinodular masses.

Histopathology

Lymph nodes show marked sinus expansion by distinctive large histiocytes that possess round nuclei and prominent nucleoli, surrounded by abundant pale cytoplasm with poorly defined borders. Emperipolesis – the presence of intact haematopoietic cells (typically lymphocytes, but also plasma cells, neutrophils, and erythrocytes) within the histiocyte cytoplasm – is characteristic of RDD. Cytological atypia, mitoses, and multinucleation are infrequent. The microenvironment contains small B and T cells and numerous polytypic plasma cells within medullary cords. Neutrophils and neutrophilic microabscesses may be present, and eosinophils are inconspicuous. Capsular and stromal fibrosis may be prominent and can extend into perinodal soft tissue. Advanced cases may show nodal effacement {2180012; 34781270; 29720485; 26966089}.

For extranodal RDD, histiocytic clusters and the lymphoplasmacytic background impart an alternating light and dark pattern at low magnification, resembling the architectural features of nodal RDD despite an absence of sinuses. Inconspicuous emperipolesis, admixed foamy histiocytes, and stromal fibrosis may be present, making the diagnosis challenging. Perivascular plasma cells are a helpful feature in the diagnosis of extranodal RDD {2180012}.

Immunohistochemistry

RDD histiocytes are immunoreactive for S100, and the emperipolesis phenomenon can be remarkably highlighted. They are positive for OCT2 {2180012; 33177341}, frequently positive for phosphorylated ERK and cyclin D1 {28664935; 31172509}, and variably positive for histiocytic markers such as CD68 and CD163. They lack CD1a and CD207 (langerin) {28664935; 26966089; 29720485}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes infectious diseases, IgG4-related disease, and other histiocytoses {26637774; 30573850; 35789115; 30565719} (see #32035Table 3.02). IgG4-positive plasma cells may be numerous, but other features of IgG4-related disease are usually absent {23955459}. RDD can also coexist with Langerhans cell histiocytosis or lymphoma {30323237}.

 

Cytology

FNAC shows large histiocytes with emperipolesis associated with plasma cells and lymphocytes. S100 positivity is confirmatory {22438629; 31942098; 27673407}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

For suspected familial RDD, the workup should include SLC29A3 and FAS (TNFRSF6) germline mutation analysis {24172204; 20140240; 15958855}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large histiocytes with round nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and abundant pale cytoplasm, often with emperipolesis; abundant plasma cells in background; positive immunostaining for S100, which also highlights the emperipolesis.

Desirable: in diagnostically challenging cases, expression of OCT2 and cyclin D1 in the absence of CD1a, CD207 (langerin), and ALK.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The disease often resolves spontaneously. Surgical intervention is warranted if the upper airway, spinal cord, or end organs are compromised. Persistent disease or recurrence may require further treatment. In such patients, MAPK/ERK pathway–targeting MEK inhibitors may be helpful {28664935}. Immune dysregulation and involvement of select extranodal sites (kidney, tracheobronchial tract / lung, and liver) contribute to an adverse prognosis; disease-specific death is reported in 5–11% of patients {2180012}.
