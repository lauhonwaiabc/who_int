2.5.2.1 Acute leukaemias of mixed or ambiguous lineage: Introduction

Acute leukaemias of ambiguous lineage (ALALs) are leukaemias composed of ≥ 20% abnormal progenitors that do not show differentiation along a single lineage.

Included are acute undifferentiated leukaemia lacking clear lineage commitment and mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia (MPAL) showing differentiation along more than one lineage. Leukaemia demonstrating a consistent pattern of antigen expression associated with a single lineage but with expression of one or more antigens associated with another lineage should not be considered MPAL unless it meets the lineage definitions outlined below. Leukaemias that can be assigned to another well-defined entity should not be considered MPAL despite meeting the lineage criteria below. These include acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion; AML with CBFB::MYH11 fusion; AML, myelodysplasia-related; myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase fusions; and chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) in blast crisis. Such cases should be classified as defined, with a note that they exhibit a mixed-lineage immunophenotype.

Epidemiology

Ambiguous-lineage leukaemia is rare, accounting for < 4% of all acute leukaemia cases. However, the true incidence of these leukaemias is likely to be lower because of the prior inclusion of otherwise typical acute lymphoid or myeloid leukaemia with aberrant cross-lineage antigen expression that does not meet current lineage definitions, as well as cases with differentiation along more unusual lineages. These leukaemias occur both in children and in adults {10457405; 16437134}.

Terminology

MPAL may consist of a single population of abnormal progenitors (morphological blasts) expressing antigens from two or more lineages, termed biphenotypic, or two populations of abnormal progenitors each of a different lineage, termed bilineal or bilineage cells {7687860; 9450804; 9107085; 1694392}. Although simple conceptually, these terms may be difficult to apply in practice, because of the complexity of patterns of antigen expression; fortunately, these terms are not associated with known differences in clinical outcome.

Diagnosis and lineage assignment

The diagnosis of ALAL requires the presence of ≥ 20% blasts or abnormal progenitors by morphology or immunophenotyping, respectively. In bilineal MPAL cases, each abnormal progenitor population must meet the immunophenotypic criteria for that lineage, but the numerical blast criterion of ≥ 20% need only be met in aggregate. Importantly, demonstration of immunophenotypic abnormality on each progenitor population is necessary to avoid mistaking expansions of normal progenitors for part of the neoplasm. In bilineal MPAL cases, each lineage often arises from a common precursor, and demonstration of this pattern greatly improves specificity for the diagnosis of MPAL.

Lineage assignment is best performed by flow cytometric immunophenotyping in order to correlate the patterns of antigen expression on one or more cell populations. Immunohistochemistry may be used to demonstrate the presence of two distinct leukaemic populations or show expression of lineage-associated antigens such as myeloperoxidase, CD3, and PAX5, but discrimination of normal from leukaemic populations may be difficult and lead to misattribution of expression. Cytochemical stains for myeloperoxidase may also be used to demonstrate myeloid lineage in conjunction with flow cytometric immunophenotyping.

The assignment of lineage by immunophenotyping is dependent on the strength of association between each antigen and the lineage being assessed. As a general principle, the closer the expression of an antigen is to the intensity and/or pattern of expression seen on the most similar normal population, the more likely it is to reflect commitment to that lineage. For instance, variable myeloperoxidase expression with an intensity and pattern similar to that seen in early myeloid maturation is more strongly associated with myeloid lineage than uniform dim myeloperoxidase expression. In addition, demonstration of a coordinated pattern of expression of multiple antigens from the same lineage further improves the specificity of those antigens for lineage assignment; for example, the combined expression of CD19, CD22, and CD10 is more strongly associated with B lineage than the expression of each antigen individually. Given these principles, the immunophenotypic criteria to be used for lineage assignment in cases where a single lineage is not evident are as in #36864Table 2.25 and are discussed below.

The use of thresholds or cut-off points for antigen expression in order to define lineage is attractive because of their perceived simplicity and objectivity. However, percentage cut-off points are unable to take into account intensity of expression relative to normal counterparts or describe the variability in distribution seen with maturation, important features that provide specificity for lineage assignment. Relatively few modern data relevant to MPAL classification using thresholds have been published, so it is unclear whether a simple threshold will be suitable for lineage assignment in ALAL or MPAL.

T lineage

The hallmark of T lineage is the expression of CD3, either in the cytoplasm or, less frequently, on the cell surface. A higher level of expression is more strongly associated with T lineage, so a level of expression exceeding 50% of that seen on normal mature T cells in the same sample should be seen on at least a portion of the leukaemic cells. This is best performed by flow cytometry using a bright fluorochrome (e.g. PE, APC, or BV dyes) to provide an increased dynamic range that aids in this determination. T lineage can also be demonstrated by immunohistochemistry for CD3, with the caveat that the polyclonal antibodies used may react with the CD3 ζ chain also present in normal NK cells and so may not be entirely T lineage–specific.

B lineage

No single antigen is entirely specific for B lineage, so it is the expression of multiple antigens in combination that provides specificity. Expression of CD19 approaching the level seen on normal B progenitors (exceeding 50% of the level seen on normal B progenitors on at least a portion of the leukaemia) has considerable specificity for B lineage, but it still should occur with expression of one or more other B-lineage antigens (CD10, CD22, or CD79a) to define B lineage. Lower CD19 expression is less specific for B lineage, and demonstration of at least two of the other B antigens listed above is required. When T lineage is a consideration, the specificity of CD79a for B lineage is limited because of its frequent expression in T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma; therefore, CD79a should not be included in the assessment of B lineage in such cases. PAX5 by immunohistochemistry is considered to have a high specificity for B lineage, but there is relatively little literature describing its lineage specificity in ALAL or MPAL; therefore, its use for this purpose should be considered tentative. B lineage in the absence of CD19 expression is rare, but it can be established when PAX5, CD79a, and CD22 are present.

Myeloid lineage

The most specific feature of myeloid lineage is the expression of myeloperoxidase in the cytoplasm of the leukaemic blasts, particularly when its level exceeds 50% of that seen on mature neutrophils and/or it shows a variable pattern reminiscent of that seen on normal CD34-positive myeloid progenitors. The significance of dim expression of myeloperoxidase is controversial; it can be seen in otherwise typical B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma, often relatively uniformly or on a discrete subset, so it is not likely to be lineage-specific in that context. However, the specificity of dim, variable myeloperoxidase is improved by the demonstration of a leukaemic subset with increased light-scatter properties or coordinated expression of other myeloid-associated antigens (e.g. CD117 or bright CD13/CD33) that differ from the remaining leukaemic population, which has more lymphoid characteristics. In cases where myeloperoxidase is dim or negative, demonstration of monocytic differentiation by the abnormal expression of more than one monocyte-associated antigen, including CD11c, CD14, CD36, CD64, or lysozyme, or diffuse positivity for nonspecific esterase by cytochemistry, is also considered sufficient.

Various groups have proposed flow cytometry thresholds for myeloperoxidase expression to be used for lineage assignment in acute leukaemia; these range from 3% to 28% {23325578; 23432206; 33161592}. The 3% myeloperoxidase cut-off point, historically used for cytochemistry, was determined to have high sensitivity but poor specificity for general lineage assignment in acute leukaemia by flow cytometry {23432206; 33161592}. A threshold of > 10% for myeloperoxidase positivity seems to improve specificity {23325578}, but no consensus cut-off point has been established.

Genetics

An increasing variety of molecular and genetic abnormalities have been described in ALAL, often associated with particular lineages or immunophenotypes (see #36865Table 2.26, and the following sections). However, immunophenotypic features in acute leukaemia may change over time or with therapy. In the most extreme form, this is seen with single-lineage acute leukaemias (e.g. acute lymphoblastic leukaemia or AML) that appear to switch from one lineage to another {8618457; 8111722}, a phenomenon that has been particularly associated with the presence of KMT2Arearrangements {35482927}, ZNF384 fusions, DUX4 rearrangement, and PAX5 p.P80R {32646889}. This phenomenon suggests that these leukaemias contain progenitors with lineage plasticity and may be biologically related to MPAL; in fact, these entities have been observed to present de novo as MPAL at times.
