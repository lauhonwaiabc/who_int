2.2.1.8 Juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia

Definition

Juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia (JMML) is a RAS pathway activation–driven myeloproliferative neoplasm of early childhood leading to peripheral granulocytosis and monocytosis, with frequent organ infiltration.

ICD-O coding

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Not recommended: juvenile chronic myeloid leukaemia; infantile monosomy 7 syndrome.

Subtype(s)

PTPN11-mutated JMML; NRAS-mutated JMML; KRAS-mutated JMML; JMML in neurofibromatosis type 1; JMML in children with CBL syndrome; JMML-like disorders in children with Noonan syndrome

Localization

The bone marrow and peripheral blood are always involved, and leukaemic infiltrates are almost always seen in the spleen and frequently seen in the liver. Lymph nodes, skin, lung, and gastrointestinal tract may also show leukaemic infiltrates {9885207; 9160658}. JMML rarely involves the CNS {10647883; 14608204; 17984696; 33404720}.

Clinical features

Initial symptoms are usually pallor and fever (in > 50% of cases) and less frequently upper respiratory infection, dry cough, pleomorphic skin lesions, bleeding, abdominal distention, and gastrointestinal symptoms {9160658; 28179213}.

Physical examination usually shows splenomegaly (in ~90% of cases), hepatomegaly (in ~80%), lymphadenopathy (in ~50%), and maculopapular skin rash (in ~30%) {9160658; 9226148; 9885207; 28179213}. JMML may also cause enlarged tonsils, myeloid sarcoma of the CNS, facial palsy, and diabetes insipidus {9160658}.

Approximately 25% of JMML cases develop in the context of a germline genetic syndrome that may be suggested by associated features. Patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1; MIM number: 162200) can present with xanthomas and café-au-lait spots (≥ 6 lesions of > 5 mm in size). Patients with Noonan syndrome (MIM number: 163950) show typical facial dysmorphia, congenital heart defects, and lymphatic effusions, among other developmental defects, and patients with germline CBL syndrome also typically show autoimmunity, particularly vasculitis (Noonan syndrome–like disorder with or without JMML; MIM number: 613563) {23875798; 25952305; 33375775}.

The complete blood count generally shows leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, and often anaemia {9885207; 9160658}. The median reported white blood cell count varies from 25 to 30 × 109/L; occasionally the total white blood cell count is within the normal range. In addition to immature granulocytes, occasional blasts may be present {6589029; 9160658}. There is usually a striking monocytosis, with a monocyte count > 1 × 109/L {9414312; 21239819; 25564399}.

Many patients with JMML display markedly increased synthesis of haemoglobin F {9160658}.

Occasional features include elevated immunoglobulin levels and the presence of autoantibodies, which rarely cause clinical symptoms {6589029; 9160658}.

Epidemiology

JMML is a rare disorder (annual incidence: 1.2 cases per 1 million children aged 0–14 years) with a male predominance (M:F ratio: > 2:1) {8025513; 7703482}. The median age at diagnosis is 2 years (range: 0.1–11.4 years) {9885207; 9160658}. About 9% of patients are diagnosed before 4 months of age, and only 5% of patients are older than 4 years of age {9160658; 29279013; 8954903; 15087069}.

Some inherited syndromes, such as NF1, Noonan syndrome, and CBL syndrome, which belong to the RASopathies (developmental disorders associated with constitutional hyperactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway; see section RASopathies), predispose to JMML or JMML-like disorder. Approximately 25% of JMML cases develop in a context of germline mutation.

Etiology

JMML is associated with the presence of a RAS-activating genetic lesion in the haematopoietic stem cell {31776464; 33416891}.

This lesion can occur either during the perinatal period (including prenatally) {34183765} or be inherited in the context of a developmental disorder associated with RAS pathway constitutional activation (RASopathies) such as NF1 or CBL syndrome.

In patients with an inherited CBL- or NF1-associated syndrome, JMML is usually associated with the somatic alteration of the second allele in the haematopoietic compartment {8302341; 20694012}.

 

Pathogenesis

JMML is considered a unique example of RAS-driven oncogenesis because it is associated in > 90% of cases with mutations in RAS genes (NRAS in 15–25%; KRAS in 15–20%; rarely RRAS, RRAS2, or RIT1) or in regulators of the RAS pathway (PTPN11 in 35–40%; NF1 in 5–10%; or CBL in 12–15%) leading to constitutive activation of the RAS signalling pathway and increasing the sensitivity of myeloid progenitors to granulocyte–macrophage colony–stimulating factor.

RAS-activating mutations are generally mutually exclusive and define subgroups with distinct clinical and haematological presentations. In 40% of somatically acquired mutations, the initial event can be traced back to birth, before disease development, implying that the initial genetic event occurs in utero {34183765}.

Secondary somatic mutations can be seen in select patients and can be associated with tumour progression and poor outcomes {26457648}. They include second hits in one of the other RAS pathway genes, as well as mutations in various other targets {23832011; 26457647; 26457648}.

Altered DNA methylation is a common feature {21406719}, and DNA hypermethylation may be associated with a more aggressive clinical course and secondary mutations {29259247; 29259179; 29437595; 33139265}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not applicable

Histopathology

Peripheral blood smears typically show leukocytosis > 10 × 109/L, including monocytosis > 1 × 109/L. Monocytes often have atypical morphology. Neutrophilia is usually present and sometimes shows abnormal nuclear segmentation and pseudo–Pelger–Huët forms. Granulocytic precursors (e.g. promyelocytes, myelocytes, and metamyelocytes) are seen, and basophilia and eosinophilia may be occasionally present. Red blood cells are usually normocytic, but macrocytosis is observed in some patients, particularly those with monosomy 7. Microcytosis was reported in children with acquired thalassaemia or iron deficiency {28749240}. Nucleated red blood cells, large polychromatophilic red cells, and dacrocytes are frequent. Blasts and promonocytes usually constitute about 2% of white blood cells {9160658; 25564399}. Platelet count is often decreased.

Bone marrow morphology alone is not diagnostic but can be suggestive in the appropriate clinical setting. Myeloid cells are hyperplastic with maturation and show no or minimal dysplastic features, such as hypogranular or pseudo–Pelger–Huët neutrophils. Auer rods are typically absent. Erythroid precursors may show megaloblastic features.

On bone marrow sections, cellularity is normal or increased, with a predominant granulocytic proliferation, occasional erythroid hyperplasia {9160658}, and limited monocyte infiltration (5–10% of cells), as compared with the prominent peripheral blood monocytosis (monocyte recognition can be improved by immunohistochemical stains for CD14 or CD68, including CD68R). Megakaryocytes are usually reduced in number, without significant dysplastic features {1915702}. Blasts, including blast-equivalent promonocytes, can be moderately elevated but lower than 20%.

Flow cytometry studies on the bone marrow show an aberrant blast population expressing CD7 and decreased levels of CD13 and CD33. Abnormal maturation pattern of granulocytes can also be detected particularly using CD13 and CD16. HLA-DR expression is decreased on monocytes {34387930}.

Leukaemic infiltrates are commonly identified in numerous tissues and organs. In the liver, leukaemic cells (monocytes/histiocytes, myeloid precursors, and mature granulocytes) infiltrate portal tracts and sinusoids. In the spleen, these cells infiltrate the red pulp predominantly; in the skin, the perivascular and periadnexal areas; in lymph nodes, the interfollicular and sinusoidal areas; and in the lung they spread from the vascular bed into alveolar septa and alveoli.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The presence of one of the five canonical RAS pathway mutations (KRAS, NRAS, PTPN11, NF1, CBL) facilitates the diagnosis. These mutations can be somatically acquired (55–65% of cases) or inherited in the context of RASopathies (20–30% of cases) {20694012; 33375775; 8302341}. It is therefore strongly recommended that genetic testing also be performed on non-haematopoietic tissue {25420281}.

Occasional cases of fusion-driven JMML causing the activation of genes upstream of the RAS pathway, such as ALK, PDGFRB, ROS1, FLT3, and PDGFRA, have been reported in a group of older patients with JMML {29437595; 15087372; 29437595; 17301809}.

Secondary events are most commonly detected in SETBP1, ASXL1, EZH2, JAK3, or SH2B3, or in additional RAS pathway genes. These secondary events are exceedingly rare in CBL-associated JMML.

In some children with Noonan syndrome (MIM number: 163950) related to germline mutation in PTPN11 {9202609; 9149755; 25097206}, or rarely in NRAS {19775298}, KRAS {16474405}, or RIT1 {26757980; 26518681}, a transient myeloproliferative disorder that can be hard to distinguish from JMML may be encountered, because most germline variants impart a weaker gain-of-function effect than do the somatic PTPN11 variants observed in children with JMML {15928039; 25097206}. However, some cases may run an aggressive course, requiring therapy, and some patients may have persistent monocytosis or splenomegaly for an extended period of time {25097206; 20829714}.

JMML with somatically mutated NRAS or KRAS shares features with a disorder named RAS-associated lymphoproliferative disease, a rare intrinsic lymphocyte apoptosis defect associated with somatic gain-of-function mutations in KRAS or NRAS that induces a leukopenia with frequent lymphadenopathy and immunological manifestations but also shares many clinical and laboratory findings with JMML. JMML features associated with KRAS or NRAS mutations, in patients who show spontaneous regression of myeloproliferative manifestations despite the persistence of RAS-mutated clones and autoimmune manifestations, are compatible with RAS-associated lymphoproliferative disease {21063026}. The two entities may represent different phenotypes or a spectrum of the same disease {25691160; 33011939}.

Cytogenetic studies show a normal karyotype in 65% of cases, sole monosomy 7 in about 25% of cases {25564399}, and other aberrations in 10% of cases {9160658}. The likelihood of an abnormal karyotype varies among genetic subtypes, with monosomy 7 most often noted in KRAS-mutated diseases {33139265}.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Poor prognostic factors include older age (> 2 years), low platelet counts (< 33 × 109/L), elevated haemoglobin F levels and/or overexpression of LIN28B (a regulator of haemoglobin F expression) {9160658; 26712910}. Somatic variants in PTPN11-related or NF1-related JMML {19047918; 26457648} and acquisition of secondary somatic mutations of SETBP1, ASXL1, EZH2, and other genes {25564399; 26457648; 26457647; 24578498} have also been associated with adverse outcomes. A DNA-hypermethylated profile {29437595; 29259247; 29259179} has been shown to be predictive of outcome in JMML.

In contrast, some patients with germline CBL-associated JMML and many patients with Noonan syndrome–associated myeloproliferative disorder show spontaneous remission.

The NRAS-mutated subgroup is the most heterogeneous.

Blast crisis occurs in approximately 30% of patients, with no identified predictive factors.

Finally, Noonan syndrome and CBL syndrome with or without loss of heterozygosity in haematopoietic cells are associated with a wide spectrum of haematological manifestations, ranging from transient myeloproliferation with thrombocytopenia or serious immune deregulation to life-threatening neonatal myeloproliferative neoplasms meeting the definition of JMML {15928039; 25097206; 23823657}. Despite a certain degree of genotype–phenotype correlation in Noonan syndrome, predicting the course and behaviour of JMML-like myeloproliferative disorder remains challenging. A watch-and-wait approach may be considered in these situations.

 

Clinical, haematological, and laboratory criteria (all five criteria are required):

·      Peripheral blood monocyte count ≥ 1 × 109/L

·      Blast and promonocyte percentage of < 20% in peripheral blood and bone marrow

·      Clinical evidence of organ infiltration, most commonly splenomegaly

·      No Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome or BCR::ABL1 fusion

·      No KMT2A (MLL1) gene rearrangement

Genetic criteria (one criterion is required):

·      Mutation in a component or a regulator of the canonical RAS pathway:

o   Clonal somatic mutation in PTPN11, KRAS, or NRASa, or

o   Clonal somatic or germline NF1 mutation and loss of heterozygosity or compound heterozygosity of NF1, or

o   Clonal somatic or germline CBL mutation and loss of heterozygosity of CBLb

·      Non-canonical clonal RAS pathway pathogenic variantc or fusions causing activation of genes upstream of the RAS pathway, such as ALK, PDGFRB, and ROS1

Other criteria:

Cases that do not meet any of the genetic criteria listed above (or for which genetic testing is not available) must meet at least two of the following criteria in addition to the aforementioned clinical, haematological, and laboratory criteria:

·      Increased haemoglobin F for age

·      Myeloid (promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes) and erythroid precursors on peripheral blood smear

·      Thrombocytopenia with hypercellular marrow often showing a decreased number of megakaryocytes; dysplastic features may or may not be evident

·      Hypersensitivity of myeloid progenitors to GM-CSF as tested in clonogenic assays in methylcellulose or by measuring STAT5 phosphorylation in the absence of or with a low dose of exogenous GM-CSF

GM-CSF, granulocyte–macrophage colony–stimulating factor.

·         Patient manifesting with JMML compatible clinical and haematological picture

·         Absent BCR::ABL1 fusion gene and KMT2A gene rearrangement

·         Detection of a molecular pathogenic lesion in leukaemic cells in one of the five canonical RAS pathway driver genes in leukaemic cells (> 90% of JMML cases are defined by these molecular alterations):

o   Biallelic pathogenic alterations of NF1

o   Pathogenic PTPN11 variant

o   Pathogenic NRAS variant

o   Pathogenic KRAS variant

o   Biallelic pathogenic (in some cases heterozygous only) variants involving the CBL gene

OR detection of a non-canonical genetic abnormality:

o   Fusions leading to upstream RAS pathway activation (ALK, PDGFRB, and others)

o   Pathogenic variant in a non-canonical RAS pathway (RRAS or RRAS2) variant

·         In the absence of any of these alterations (or if molecular analysis is not possible):

o   Performing additional laboratory studies as outlined in <<#32433>>Box 2.12

o   Exclusion of conditions mimicking JMML, such as viral infection {11990701; 21571368}, infantile malignant osteopetrosis, and Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome

o   If monosomy 7 is detected, elimination of syndromes commonly associated with this cytogenetic abnormality, such as GATA2-associated, SAMD9-associated, or SAMD9L-associated disorders
