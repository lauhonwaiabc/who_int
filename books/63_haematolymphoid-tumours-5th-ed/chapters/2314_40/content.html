2.3.1.4 Acute myeloid leukaemia with CBFB::MYH11 fusion

Definition

Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with CBFB::MYH11 fusion is characterized by fusion of the core-binding factor beta subunit gene (CBFB) and the myosin heavy chain 11 gene (MYH11).

ICD-O coding

9871/3 Acute myeloid leukaemia with CBFB::MYH11 fusion

ICD-11 coding

2A60.0 Acute myeloid leukaemia with recurrent genetic abnormalities

Related terminology

Acceptable: acute myeloid leukaemia with CBFB rearrangement; acute myeloid leukaemia with t(16;16)(p13.1;q22); acute myeloid leukaemia with inv(16)(p13.1q22).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The blood and bone marrow are nearly always involved. Some patients may present with myeloid sarcoma.

Clinical features

Presenting symptoms and signs are typically secondary to bone marrow failure and include fatigue, bruising or bleeding, and infection. Patients often present with an elevated white blood cell count. Extramedullary disease may constitute the only evidence of relapse in some patients.

Epidemiology

AML with CBFB::MYH11 fusion constitutes 5–8% of all AML cases in younger patients, and the incidence decreases in older patients. CBFB::MYH11 is the predominant core-binding factor (CBF) rearrangement in White AML patients (60–70% of CBF-AML in these patients). The incidence is lower in African-American patients or in patients of eastern Asian ancestry {14976037; 32669171}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The CBF is a transcription factor complex comprising a DNA-binding CBFα subunit (RUNX1, RUNX2, or RUNX3) and a non–DNA-binding heterodimerization partner CBFβ subunit (encoded by CBFB). MYH11, which codes for smooth muscle myosin heavy chain, combines with CBFB. The resulting fusion protein interferes with the function of RUNX1/CBFb transcription factor complex, causing dysregulation of myeloid cell growth and differentiation {27798625; 32929473}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The bone marrow is hypercellular, with a myeloid predominance and increased immature cells. Bone marrow smears show increased blasts, usually with morphological features of myelomonocytic differentiation. Marrow eosinophilia and the presence of abnormal eosinophils is a feature of AML with CBFB::MYH11 and is seen in the majority of cases, with few exceptions. Eosinophils have immature granules resembling those normally seen in the late promyelocytic and myelocyte stages of eosinophil maturation. However, these granules are often abnormally large and are distinctly dark purple-violet on Romanowsky-type stains. They may be few or abundant in some cells. Peripheral blood eosinophilia may be present, but abnormal circulating eosinophils are usually rare.

Immunophenotype

Two aberrant blast populations are usually identified. One, a CD45-dim immature blast population, expresses CD34 and myeloid markers such as CD13, KIT (CD117), and myeloperoxidase. The second, a more mature monocytic CD45-bright population, expresses monocytic antigens such as CD14, CD64, and lysozyme, in addition to myeloid markers, but lacks CD34 expression.

Cytochemistry

Blasts are positive for myeloperoxidase. Monoblasts and promonocytes are usually weakly positive for nonspecific esterase reactivity. The naphthol AS-D CAE reaction, which is normally negative in eosinophils, is characteristically faintly positive in the abnormal eosinophils.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of CBFB::MYH11 and/or inv(16)(p13.1q22) or t(16;16)(p13.1;q22) can be achieved using chromosomal banding analysis, FISH, and various molecular techniques. Although detection of the fusion and/or fusion transcript is optimal, the diagnosis is permissible on the basis of CBFB rearrangement using a break-apart FISH probe in the appropriate morphological context. The most common cytogenetic alteration, inv(16)(p13.1q22), detected in about 95% of patients, may be difficult to identify on conventional karyotyping in some cases. Cases with suspicious morphological features that lack inv(16)(p13.1q22) or t(16;16)(p13.1;q22) should be assessed thoroughly with the aid of FISH or molecular diagnostics.

Secondary chromosomal aberrations are present in about 45% of AML cases with CBFB::MYH11 and include trisomy 8 (10–20%), trisomy 21 (~5%), and trisomy 22 (15–25%), as well as del(7q) (~5%) {15289486; 23115274; 34003250}.

Different fusion transcripts of CBFB::MYH11 are recognized, with 85–88% of cases having transcript type A. Transcripts D and E are each detected in about 5% of cases. At least eight other fusion transcript types have been reported, but these are rare {17287858; 34547772}.

Somatic mutations are detected in > 90% of cases and most commonly involve NRAS (30–50%) and KRAS (10–15%), followed by KIT, ASXL2, ASXL1, NF1, TET2, EZH2, FLT3 tyrosine kinase domain (TKD), and FLT3 internal tandem duplication (ITD) {23115274; 26980726; 27276561; 27798625; 30610028; 31896782}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid neoplasm with increased peripheral blood and/or bone marrow blasts (may be < 20%); detection of CBFB::MYH11; not fulfilling diagnostic criteria for myeloid neoplasm post cytotoxic therapy.

Desirable: detection of inv(16)(p13.1q22) or t(16;16)(p13.1;q22).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of AML with CBFB::MYH11 is favourable, with long-term survival rates of about 50% in adults {26660431; 27276561; 27895058; 29991495}. Treatment with high-dose cytarabine-containing regimens and the addition of gemtuzumab ozogamicin to induction therapy has improved survival {18559876; 25008258}.

The impact of additional chromosomal abnormalities on outcome is still unclear. Trisomy 8 and trisomy 22 have been shown to be associated with better outcomes in some studies but not all {15289486; 16110030; 23115274; 34003250}. A lack of molecular remission after induction therapy represents an independent prognostic factor for relapse and poor survival {20625124; 22875911; 29884905}. CBFB::MYH11 fusion types are associated with distinct clinical and genetic features {23160462} but do not seem to have a prognostic impact {17287858}. The presence of trisomy 8 is associated with better overall survival {34003250}. The presence of KIT mutations (exon 17) or multiple kinase mutations (e.g. KIT, RAS, FLT3) seems to correlate with a higher relapse rate in some studies {16921041; 23321257} but not others {20056794; 29884905; 34135311}.
