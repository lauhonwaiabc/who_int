2.3.1.2 Acute promyelocytic leukaemia with PML::RARA fusion

Definition

Acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL) with PML::RARA is a type of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), characterized by a predominance of abnormal promyelocytes and fusion of the promyelocytic leukaemia gene (PML) with the retinoic acid receptor alpha gene (RARA).

ICD-O coding

9866/3 Acute promyelocytic leukaemia with PML::RARA fusion

9866/3 Acute promyelocytic leukaemia with a variant RARA translocation

ICD-11 coding

2A60.0 & XH1A50 Acute myeloid leukaemia with recurrent genetic abnormalities & Acute promyelocytic leukaemia

Related terminology

Acceptable: acute promyelocytic leukaemia with t(15;17)(q24.1;q21.2); acute promyelocytic leukaemia.

Subtype(s)

Acute promyelocytic leukaemia with a variant RARA translocation

Localization

The peripheral blood and bone marrow are the principal sites of involvement. However, rare cases have extramedullary involvement.

Clinical features

Two thirds of patients present with leukopenia. APL is frequently associated with coagulopathy and a risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation resulting from a complex interplay of disease-related thrombocytopenia and hyperfibrinolysis, often exacerbated on initiation of treatment {29703489}. Coagulopathy is significantly associated with early death in patients with APL and requires careful monitoring and adequate blood product support {30803991}. In microgranular APL (see below), patients present with a very high white blood cell (WBC) count with a short doubling time.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence rate in the USA is now 0.08 cases per 100 000 population {22707337}. A higher incidence is reported in US patients originating from Latin American counties {12935956}. APL accounts for 5–8% of all AML cases in younger patients, with a lower relative frequency in elderly patients. The disease can occur at any age, but most patients are aged 20–59 years {12935956}. There is no sex predilection.

Etiology

The etiology is unknown in most cases. Some cases arise in individuals with prior exposure to cytotoxic therapy {25845577; 25975189; 34462244}.

Pathogenesis

The genetic hallmark of APL is a balanced chromosomal translocation, t(15;17)(q24;q21), leading to fusion of PML and RARA. The fusion gene results in a chimeric oncoprotein, PML::RARA, which plays a causative role in APL pathogenesis, impairing myeloid differentiation. The resulting PML::RARA protein silences genes that are critical for cell differentiation, resulting in both a differentiation block and an expansion of myeloid progenitors {2170850; 24907021}. Secondary cooperating genetic events, including chromosomal and genetic alterations, also contribute to the leukaemic phenotype.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The bone marrow is usually hypercellular, but aspirate smears may be aparticulate. Abnormal promyelocytes in APL are considered equivalent to blasts in terms of blast enumeration. Hypergranular (classic) and microgranular (hypogranular) cytomorphological variants are recognized on Romanowsky-type stains, and the two may coexist in some cases. Abnormal promyelocytes are also observed in the peripheral blood but may be few.

Hypergranular APL is characterized by abnormal promyelocytes whose cytoplasm contains densely packed (or even coalescent) large granules, staining bright pink, red, or purple. Characteristic cells containing bundles of Auer rods randomly distributed within the cytoplasm (faggot cells) are present in most cases. Myeloblasts with single Auer rods may also be observed. The Auer rods are usually larger than those in other types of AML. Nuclear size and shape in the abnormal promyelocytes can be highly variable, often including kidney-shaped or bilobed nuclei.

Microgranular APL is characterized by blasts with an apparent paucity or absence of granules and many bilobed nuclei. The hypogranular appearance of the cytoplasm is due to the submicroscopic size of the granules. The morphological features may overlap with those of acute myelomonocytic or acute monocytic leukaemia on Romanowsky-stained preparations; however, a small number of abnormal promyelocytes with clearly visible granules and/or bundles of Auer rods can be identified in many cases.

Immunophenotype

Hypergranular promyelocytes are characterized by negative expression of CD34 and HLA-DR, with characteristically high side scatter and forward scatter {11279655; 12935957; 15108165; 21173127; 21812014; 25239423; 29113330}. They are positive for CD13, CD33, and KIT (CD117). CD64 is frequently positive, but CD15, CD65, CD66b, and CD66c are often negative {10329918; 21812014; 25239423; 29113330}. CD56 is expressed in approximately 10% of APL cases, frequently with CD34 and CD2 {10715300; 21148082; 26920716; 30322324}. Leukocyte integrins such as CD11a, CD11b, and CD18 are typically negative {25239423; 15108165}. Expression of monocytic markers (CD14 and CD36) and other lymphoid markers (e.g. CD4, CD5, CD7, CD19, c/sCD22, and cCD79a) is rare {12935957; 15108165}. Rare cases may have weak cCD3 or TdT expression {16857994}.

APL and a subset of AML with NPM1 mutation may have overlapping immunophenotypic features, but CD2, CD13, CD33, and CD110 are usually differentially expressed and could help with this distinction {21812014}.

Microgranular blasts are frequently located in the traditional blast gate on plots of CD45 versus side scatter, and they have immunophenotypic features that are largely similar to those of cells in the hypergranular variant {22535601}. Exceptions include CD2 and CD34, which are more frequently expressed in the microgranular variant and correlate with the expression of breakpoint cluster region 3 (see below) {11279655; 15108165; 9734655}. CD2 expression in APL has been associated with FLT3 internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutation {12935957}.

Cytochemistry

Myeloperoxidase is uniformly strongly positive in leukaemic cells, in both hypergranular and microgranular APL. Butyrate esterase is negative or (rarely) weakly positive.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The cytogenetic diagnosis of APL is confirmed by establishing fusion of the RARA gene on chromosome 17q21.2 with a nuclear regulatory factor gene, PML, on 15q24.1 {2170850; 2218500}. PML::RARA is present in 90–95% of morphologically typical APLs. Depending on the PML breakpoint location on chromosome 15, three PML::RARA transcript isoforms can be detected: (1) long (located in intron 6, breakpoint cluster region 1), (2) variant (located in exon 6, breakpoint cluster region 2), and (3) short (located in intron 3, breakpoint cluster region 3) {10942371; 32182684}. Atypical isoforms of PML::RARA transcripts involving intron 4, exon 6, and exon 7 can also be detected in sporadic cases {32182684}. Rare APL cases have submicroscopic insertion of RARA into PML, known as cryptic or masked t(15;17)(q24.1;q21.2), or complex rearrangements involving an additional chromosome {10233871; 27063598}. These may not be detected by conventional cytogenetics, but they can be detected by other molecular methods.

Approximately 40% of patients with APL harbour additional chromosomal alterations identified by conventional cytogenetics. Deletion 7q and trisomy 8 are the most frequent alterations {32182684}.

Somatic mutations detected at diagnosis in APL include those in FLT3 (~40%), WT1 (14%), NRAS (7–10%), USP9X (9%), ARID1A (4%), EED (4%), and KRAS (3–4%). Mutations commonly seen in other AMLs, such as DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and IDH1/IDH2, are rare {27063598; 30575821; 31635329}. FLT3 mutations include FLT3 ITD and FLT3p.D835; they are generally associated with an elevated WBC count {30803991; 32182684}.

Variant RARA translocations in APL

A subset (~5%) of cases with morphological, cytochemical, and immunophenotypic features of APL show variant translocations involving RARA at 17q21.2. These variant fusion partners include ZBTB16 at 11q23, NUMA1 at 11q13, NPM1 at 5q35, STAT5B at 17q21, PRKAR1A at 17q24, FIP1L1 at 4q12, BCOR at Xp11.4, NABP1 (OBFC2A) at 2q32, TBL1XR1 (TBLR1) at 3q26, GTF2I at 7q11.23, IRF2BP2 at 1q42, and FNDC3B at 3q26. Cases with RARA variant translocations involving fusion partners ZBTB16 and STAT5B respond poorly to all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide (ATO) therapies {30803991}. A subgroup of cases with ZBTB16::RARA shows a predominance of cells with regular nuclei, many granules, usually an absence of Auer rods, neutrophils with pseudo–Pelger–Huët changes, and strong myeloperoxidase reactivity {10942370}. Cases with these variant translocations should be diagnosed as APL with a variant RARA translocation.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid neoplasm with increased peripheral blood and/or bone marrow atypical promyelocytes showing characteristic abnormal hypergranular promyelocytes or microgranular blasts (may be < 20%); detection of PML::RARA; no history of exposure to cytotoxic therapy.

Desirable: detection of t(15;17)(q24;q21).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

At diagnosis, a WBC count of ≥ 10 × 109/L places patients into a high-risk subset, whereas those with lower WBC counts are further stratified into low- and intermediate-risk categories on the basis of platelet count cut-off values of > 40 × 109/L and ≤ 40 × 109/L, respectively {29743722; 34193815}.

Combination therapy with all-trans retinoic acid and arsenic trioxide (ATRA+ATO) without conventional chemotherapy is highly effective and largely curative for patients with low/intermediate-risk APL and is currently the standard of care for these patients {10190895; 23841729; 27400939; 29743722; 30803991; 34047178}. In patients with high-risk APL, either an additional anthracycline along with ATRA and ATO regimens or the use of ATRA plus conventional chemotherapy regimens is recommended {30803991}.

Previously reported adverse prognostic factors, including hyperleukocytosis, CD56 expression, and FLT3 ITD, are not relevant with current treatment regimens {30803991; 34193815}. Indeed, long-term cure rates for patients with APL exceed 90% for low/intermediate-risk and high-risk groups.
