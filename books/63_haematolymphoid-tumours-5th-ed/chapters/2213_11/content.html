2.2.1.3 Chronic neutrophilic leukaemia

Definition

Chronic neutrophilic leukaemia (CNL) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by sustained isolated peripheral blood neutrophilia and the presence of activating CSF3R mutations in most cases, without morphological dysplasia or detectable BCR::ABL1.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The peripheral blood and bone marrow are always involved, and the spleen and liver usually show leukaemic infiltrates.

Clinical features

Patients have sustained, mature peripheral blood neutrophilia, which may be identified incidentally. Monocytosis, eosinophilia or basophilia are notably absent. Most patients have mild anaemia at presentation {25850813}. Presenting symptoms may include fatigue, severe infection, easy bruising or bleeding from mucosal surfaces, gout, or pruritis {35494007; 25850813}. Hepatomegaly and/or splenomegaly, occasionally symptomatic, may be present {31769070}.

 

Epidemiology

The disease is rare. A review of public datasets showed an overall incidence of 0.1 cases per 1 million person-years in the USA {32541648}. CNL generally affects older adults, but cases have been reported in adolescents and young adults {8826884; 289288; 8148416}. In one study, the median age at presentation was 66 years and the M:F ratio was 1.6:1 {26218186}.

Etiology

Some cases have arisen after cytotoxic chemotherapy {25850813}, and there have been reports of familial/germline predisposition {10233414; 27581539; 30348809; 31697825}. The majority of cases harbour genetic features consistent with clonal haematopoiesis.

Pathogenesis

In the majority of cases, the primary pathogenic event is an activating mutation involving CSF3R, either CSF3R p.T618I or other activating CSF3R mutations {23656643}. However, most cases have at least four pathogenic point mutations, suggesting that this disease arises from the sequential acquisition of mutational events within a bone marrow haematopoietic stem cell. This probably begins with a state of clonal haematopoiesis and progresses to a state of extreme skewing of haematopoietic differentiation towards the neutrophilic lineage. In a minority of cases of CNL or an apparently similar condition, the neutrophilia was associated with an underlying neoplasm, most often multiple myeloma or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance. The majority of such cases constitute a neutrophilic leukaemoid reaction resulting from the synthesis of G-CSF by neoplastic plasma cells; however, in rare reported cases, patients appear to have had both a plasma cell neoplasm and true CNL {24033109; 23713456; 26389776}.

Histopathology

The peripheral blood shows neutrophilia. The neutrophils are usually segmented, but there may also be an increase in band forms. Neutrophil precursors (promyelocytes, myelocytes, and metamyelocytes) usually account for < 5% of the white blood cells (WBCs), although they can account for up to 10%. Myeloblasts are almost never observed in the blood. There are no diagnostic cytomorphological features. The neutrophils often show toxic granulation and Döhle bodies, but they may also appear normal. It should be noted that toxic granulation and Döhle bodies are more often present in plasma cell–associated leukaemoid reactions than in CNL {26218186}. Neutrophil dysplasia should be absent. Red blood cell and platelet morphology is usually normal.

The bone marrow is uniformly hypercellular, with notable neutrophilic proliferation and an increased myeloid-to-erythroid ratio that may exceed 20:1. Myelocytes and mature neutrophils are increased in number and percentage, but precursor forms are not increased in percentage. Megakaryocytes may be cytologically normal or there may be increased smaller forms. Significant dysplasia is not present in any of the cell lineages. If sufficient dysplastic changes are present, another diagnosis, such as myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with neutrophilia (MDS/MPN-N), should be considered. Reticulin fibrosis is uncommon.

Splenomegaly and hepatomegaly result from tissue infiltration by neutrophils. In the spleen, the infiltrate is mainly ­confined to the red pulp; in the liver, infiltration may affect the sinusoids, portal areas, or both {289288; 8148416}.

No significant immunophenotypic abnormalities are detected by immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry.

Differential diagnosis

Neutrophilic leukocytosis can be observed in a variety of clinical conditions. The diagnosis of CNL requires careful exclusion of underlying causes of reactive neutrophilia and other neoplastic myeloid disorders such as myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) and MDS/MPN, particularly MDS/MPN-N {31769070}.

Given the frequency of neutrophilic leukaemoid reaction in association with plasma cell disorders, the bone marrow should be examined for evidence of a plasma cell neoplasm. If plasma cell abnormalities are present, clonality of the neutrophil lineage should be demonstrated by cytogenetic or molecular techniques before a diagnosis of CNL is made.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The identification of CSF3R mutation as a disease-associated genomic abnormality has aided the assessment of these cases {31769070}. A median of four variants of known significance are observed {31366621; 31880950}. Mutations associated with clonal haematopoiesis are common. Nearly all cases exhibit mutation in ASXL1 (70–80%), TET2 (10–20%), and/or DNMT3A (5–20%). Other genes frequently mutated impact proliferative signalling (e.g. CBL, JAK2, NRAS, PTPN11), splicing machinery (e.g. SRSF2, U2AF1, U2AF2, SF3B1, ZRSR2), transcription factors (CUX1, GATA2, RUNX1), enzymes (ETNK1), epigenetic regulators (SETBP1, EZH2), and chromosomal separation regulators (STAG2) {23896413; 25316523; 26700908; 24445868; 23656643; 23604229; 24212483; 31366621; 31880950}.

The overall mutation profile of CNL is similar to that of chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia, MDS/MPN-N, and MDS/MPN-NOS, with the exception of CSF3R mutations, which are more common in CNL (> 60% in CNL vs < 20% in MDS/MPN-N). Hence, although the above mutations may be helpful for confirming a clonal neoplasm, morphological criteria are important for distinguishing these diagnoses. Complete cytogenetic remission with imatinib was reported in a patient with CNL and t(15;19)(q13;p13.3), suggesting the possibility of unidentified fusion genes in some cases {15551277}, which may rarely involve CSF3R {29025591}.

Cytogenetic studies are normal in nearly 90% of cases. In the remaining cases, reported clonal karyotypic abnormalities are variable and show no specific recurrent alterations {15549147}. Clonal cytogenetic abnormalities may appear during the course of the disease.

Prognosis and prediction

CNL has a variable prognosis; the disease can range from latent to aggressive, with variable survival times (from 6 months to > 20 years). Neutrophilia is usually progressive; anaemia and thrombocytopenia may follow, and when they occur, survival is likely to worsen correspondingly. The development of myelodysplastic features may signal progression and risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia. ASXL1 mutation is associated with a worse prognosis in CNL {25850813}.
