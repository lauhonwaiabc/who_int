2.2.2.3 Systemic mastocytosis

Definition

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a myeloid neoplasm characterized by clonal proliferation of mast cells, typically harbouring mutant KIT, involving at least one extracutaneous organ system, with or without evidence of skin lesions.

Related terminology

Acceptable: systemic mast cell disease; systemic mastocytosis with an associated clonal haematological non–mast cell lineage disease.

Subtype(s)

Indolent systemic mastocytosis (ISM); bone marrow mastocytosis (BMM); smouldering systemic mastocytosis (SSM); aggressive systemic mastocytosis (ASM); systemic mastocytosis with an associated haematological neoplasm (SM-AHN); mast cell leukaemia (MCL)

Localization

The bone marrow is nearly always involved in SM. It is therefore necessary to undertake morphological and molecular analyses of a bone marrow biopsy/aspirate specimen in adults to confirm a diagnosis of SM {24836395; 11277679; 11919419; 114472}. Rarely, circulating mast cells are detected in the peripheral blood {27118407; 34496018}. Other organs (spleen, lymph nodes, liver, gastrointestinal tract) may be involved in SM {264356; 1452127; 1617595; 11377679; 114472; 26154789; 11377686}. Skin lesions are frequent in ISM and SSM, as compared with advanced SM (AdvSM), which includes ASM, SM-AHN, and MCL {2582403; 11377686; 33581142}. In BMM, skin lesions are absent.

Clinical features

SM presents with heterogeneous manifestations ranging from indolent presentations without impairment of life expectancy to highly aggressive neoplasms associated with multiorgan failure and poor survival. The signs and symptoms of SM are summarized in #32135Box 2.16 .

 

Epidemiology

SM is most common in adults and is generally diagnosed after the second decade of life, with an M:F ratio of 1:1–1.5 {16799601; 2582403}. The true incidence of SM is unknown. One study showed an incidence rate of 0.89 cases per 100 000 person-years for all SM subtypes {24761987}. Well-differentiated SM (WDSM; see below) has a female predominance and usually arises in childhood, and familial aggregation is common {26100086}.

Etiology

The etiology of sporadic cases is unknown. Rare cases of familial SM have been reported, mostly among patients with WDSM and childhood onset of disease {33754335; 26100086}.

Pathogenesis

SM is driven by gain-of-function KIT mutations in exon 17 (primarily KIT p.D816V in > 90% of adult cases), regardless of SM subtype {23181448; 25650093; 30007460; 23593539}. KIT p.D816V mutations and consequent aberrant (constitutive) activation of KIT drive the differentiation, proliferation, and survival of mast cells {19787646; 30007460; 23593539}. KIT-mediated signalling activates multiple downstream pathways, including PI3K, JAK/STAT, and MAPK {30007460}.

In almost all cases, KIT mutation is somatic and acquired over the course of life. Familial aggregation with first-degree family members has been described, especially in paediatric patients and more rarely in adults {33754335; 26100086}.

Histopathology

The diagnosis and subtyping of SM are usually based on the histopathological investigation of a bone marrow trephine biopsy specimen. The diagnostic hallmark of SM is the presence of compact mast cell infiltrates consisting of ≥ 15 mast cells in the bone marrow (and/or other extracutaneous organs) {11919426; 11377679; 11377686}. The diagnostic criteria for SM and its subtypes are listed in #32136Box 2.17 and #32137Table 2.07 (for B findings and C findings, see #32138Box 2.18). Of note, in > 50% of BMM cases, in as many as 30% of ISM cases, and in SM-AHN, compact mast cell infiltrates are missing {23807778; 14983941; 23921094}. Four major infiltration patterns have been defined (see #32139Table 2.08) {16146815}.

In smear preparations, normal mast cells are round, medium-sized cells with abundant metachromatic granules in Pappenheim/Wright–Giemsa or toluidine blue stains. In enzyme cytochemistry, mast cells strongly react with naphthol AS-D CAE but do not express myeloperoxidase {11377679}. Usually, mast cells account for far less than 1% of all nucleated bone marrow cells. Enumeration of mast cells must be performed outside crushed bone marrow particles. Neoplastic mast cells in mastocytosis show various degrees of cellular atypia, including spindle shape, hypogranulation, or multinucleation (see #32140Table 2.09) {11377677; 11377686; 24675021}. In WDSM, mast cells show an apparently normal or nearly normal morphology, with frequent small clusters of two or three mast cells outside bone marrow particles {26100086}.

In H&E-stained tissue sections, normal or reactive mast cells may be difficult to differentiate from histiocytic cells or fibroblasts. Mast cells have round to oval nuclei with clumped chromatin and abundant cytoplasm. Nucleoli are usually absent. Compact mast cell infiltrates often appear granulomatoid. Appropriate special stains (e.g. basic dyes like Giemsa and toluidine blue) for metachromatic granules allow the identification of mast cells (see #32140Table 2.09) {114472; 2582403; 11377686}. However, metachromatic granules may be reduced or even absent in neoplastic mast cells. Therefore, immunohistochemistry using antibodies against tryptase and KIT (CD117) is the most specific and reliable method for mast cells and determining the predominant mast cell infiltration pattern, and it should be applied in every case of suspected SM. A (reactive) mast cell hyperplasia can be observed in a variety of myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms, in association with marrow aplasia/hypoplasia or fibrosis, and secondary to stem cell factor (SCF) administration.

Immunohistochemistry

Virtually all reactive and neoplastic mast cells express tryptase and KIT (CD117) {11919419; 28978170; 35884535}. Aberrant expression of CD25, CD2, and/or CD30 in neoplastic mast cells, detected by immunohistochemistry or flow cytometry, underscores the neoplastic nature of the mast cells {24111625; 21186345; 9531582}. Although CD25 can be detected in nearly all SM cases, expression of CD2 and CD30 is less consistent {15371947; 24111625; 21186345}.

Well-differentiated systemic mastocytosis

This morphological pattern with mature-appearing mast cells can occur in any SM subtype (present in about 5% of patients with SM, overall). The percentage of mast cells in the bone marrow is usually high, and the cells are round, with a centrally placed nucleus and well-granulated cytoplasm. In WDSM, mast cells have a CD25−/low, CD2−/low, CD30+ immunophenotype {26100086; 24111625; 34901755}; therefore, CD30 is a very useful diagnostic marker in WDSM. Notably, KIT p.D816V mutation is rare, unlike in SM subtypes {26100086}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Activating mutations in the tyrosine kinase 2 (TK2) domain (exon 17) of the KIT gene, especially KIT p.D816V, are the molecular hallmark of SM and are detected in about 90% of cases, depending on the SM subtype (see #32137Table 2.07) {16741248; 30007460; 25650093}. In a small number of adult patients, especially those with MCL and WDSM, other KIT mutations may be detected in regions outside of the TK2 domain of KIT {28978170; 16741248; 26100086}. Unlike KIT p.D816V, these mutations do not cause imatinib resistance {28978170}. KIT p.D816V mutation is uncommon in WDSM {26100086}.

With highly sensitive methods, KIT p.D816V can be detected in the peripheral blood of most patients with SM and can therefore serve as a tool for screening and disease monitoring {22469616; 24281161; 24750133; 31018976}. Detection of KIT p.D816V outside the mast cell lineage (known as multilineage involvement) has adverse prognostic importance {19541349}. In SM-AHN, KIT p.D816V and other mutations have been shown in both disease compartments, thus demonstrating a clonal relationship and common haematological progenitor {11040941; 12363464; 18165278; 20112369}. Detection of non-KIT mutations usually indicates an associated (myeloid) haematological neoplasm, and some (e.g. SRSF2, ASXL1, and/or RUNX1) have shown adverse prognostic significance {22905207; 23958953; 26464169; 27416984; 30373888; 32106312; 23958953; 31509472; 33508247}.

Prognosis and prediction

The vast majority of patients with ISM have a life expectancy similar to that of the general population. However, a small subset of cases can progress to AdvSM, usually after long-lasting ISM {19541349}. The risk of progression strongly relies on the extent of bone marrow involvement as determined by a high KIT p.D816V variant allele frequency (VAF) at diagnosis {31151985}. The clinical course of SSM is often stable for many years, but progression to AdvSM can occur. The KIT p.D816V mutation burden in bone marrow and peripheral blood correlates with subtypes of mastocytosis and is overall predictive of survival in patients with SM {24750133; 31018976}.

ASM, SM-AHN, and MCL have a poor prognosis {19890907; 31676322}, and the presence of at least one mutation in genes besides KIT, including SRSF2, ASXL1, or RUNX1, has a negative prognostic impact in AdvSM {31509472; 33508247}. Rare patients with AdvSM may have a relatively good outcome. Favourable prognostic markers for AdvSM include absence of significant cytopenias, a low KIT p.D816V VAF, and absence of additional mutations {33806359; 33508247; 24750133}. Moreover, SM patients with atypical KIT mutations involving exons 8–10 (usually patients with the WDSM pattern); SM-AHN patients with PDGFR rearrangements, which are highly sensitive to first-generation KIT or PDGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors {28978170}; and those with SM-AHN presenting with ISM associated with a low-risk associated haematological neoplasm entity (e.g. essential thrombocythaemia), whenever this associated haematological neoplasm is adequately controlled, have a relatively favourable prognosis {31450655}.

KIT p.D816V–selective inhibitors show significant clinical activity, with a high rate of clinical, morphological, and molecular responses in AdvSM, likely impacting on prognosis and the impact of prognostic factors in the near future {35114819; 34873347; 34873345}.
