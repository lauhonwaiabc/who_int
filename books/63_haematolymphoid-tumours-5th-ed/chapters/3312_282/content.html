3.3.1.2 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Definition

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a clonal expansion of non–Langerhans cell histiocytes that share a dermal macrophage phenotype.

ICD-O coding

9749/1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

ICD-11 coding

2B31.0 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Related terminology

Acceptable: disseminated juvenile xanthogranuloma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

JXG is generally confined to the skin, with a predilection for the head and neck, upper trunk, and proximal extremities {12717244; 15613853; 32468628; 26744443; 26189188; 8609257; 29488239}. Ocular involvement can rarely occur and is usually solitary {26189188}. Visceral, spinal, or intracranial (in the skull base, meninges, or parenchyma) JXGs have also been described {12717244}.

Clinical features

Infants with JXG present with one or several cutaneous papulonodular lesions {7577591; 12717244; 29488239}. JXG lesions are generally asymptomatic; they begin as raised, pink to dark-brown lesions and progressively flatten, and they can disappear within months or years with scarring or anetoderma {12717244; 15613853; 32468628}. About 5% of patients show multiple cutaneous lesions {12717244}. Benign cephalic histiocytosis is a clinical subtype of JXG, presenting as an asymptomatic papular eruption with self-healing properties and arising predominantly in the head and neck of young children {15613853; 23551579}. In adults, lesions are often large, solitary, and persistent, and it is important to exclude Erdheim–Chester disease {16319479; 32187362}.

Fewer than 5% of JXGs are systemic {12717244; 15613853; 32468628}. Patients with numerous skin lesions, systemic symptoms, abnormal laboratory tests (blood count, liver enzymes, metabolic tests), or alterations at ophthalmological examination should be evaluated for systemic involvement. Cytopenia is associated with bone marrow involvement. CNS JXG can cause seizures, hydrocephalus, or diabetes insipidus {8765620; 12717244; 15613853}.

Epidemiology

JXG is rare. According to the Kiel tumour registry, JXG represents 0.5% of all paediatric tumours, with a frequency of about 1 case per 1 million children {12717244; 32468628}. More than 70% of JXGs arise in the first year of life (20–35% are congenital), with a male predilection {15613853; 34620874; 16416419}. JXG is very rare in adults.

Etiology

The etiology of JXG is largely unknown. JXG may occur in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 {15078345}. Whether there is a higher prevalence of juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia in neurofibromatosis type 1 patients affected by JXG is debated {7632061; 28189268}. JXG is also reported in Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome {23157619}. The occurrence of JXG in monozygotic twins may suggest a genetic transmission {21091658; 31768065}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenetic mechanisms in JXG are poorly understood. Fascin and factor XIIIa expression supports a histogenetic link with dermal macrophages {34620874}. MAPK/ERK pathway alterations play a pathogenetic role in JXG. Activating mutations of NRAS, KRAS, ARAF, MAP2K1, and CSF1R, as well as NTRK1 and BRAF gene fusions, have been reported {28752840; 31768065; 37115038}. PIK3CD mutations, identified in a few cases, suggest a pathogenetic role of the PI3K pathway in some JXGs {25202140; 26637772}. Reported systemic JXGs with CNS involvement and BRAF p.V600E mutations probably represent Erdheim–Chester disease instead {31685033}.

Macroscopic appearance

Cutaneous JXGs appear as pale yellow-tan, dome-shaped lesions. Early lesions appear as pink macules. Visceral lesions are generally nodular and of variable size and appearance.

Histopathology

JXGs are unencapsulated, circumscribed lesions composed of histiocytes with a dermal macrophage phenotype {17786242; 34620874}. In addition to the classic cell types (large xanthomatous histiocytes, foamy histiocytes, and Touton giant cells [[Ceppi F, Abla O. Juvenile xanthogranuloma and related non-LCH disorders. In: Abla O, Janka G, editors. Histiocytic disorders. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2018.]]), lesions may also be composed of other cell types, exclusively or admixed, including epithelioid cells, spindle cells with bland ovoid indented nuclei, and oncocytic histiocytes with dense eosinophilic cytoplasm and a round nucleus with a nucleolus {8902096; 34620874}. The histiocytes should not show significant nuclear pleomorphism. Mitoses may be seen {12717244; 24582118}. Variable numbers of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, neutrophils, and mast cells are often intermixed. The histological features may vary according to the site and temporal evolution.

Immunohistochemistry

The histiocytes are positive for CD68, CD163, CD4, CD14, factor XIIIa, and fascin {33901419; 34620874}. CD1a and CD207 (langerin) are negative, but a minority may display light nuclear and cytoplasmic staining for S100 {29797336; 9550310}. ALK is negative.

Differential diagnosis

JXG with paucity of xanthomatous cells can be distinguished from Langerhans cell histiocytosis by the lack of consistently deeply grooved nuclei and negative immunostaining for CD1a and CD207 (langerin). Negative staining for ALK differentiates JXG from ALK-positive histiocytosis and epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma. An absence of follicular-sebaceous induction, peripheral entrapment of collagen, and haemosiderin deposits excludes a lipidized fibrous histiocytoma. If CD163-positive macrophages stain for mutation-specific BRAF VE1, adult patients should be clinically evaluated for Erdheim–Chester disease; in children with a known diagnosis of BRAF p.V600E–mutant Langerhans cell histiocytosis, this may indicate a lineage switch related to the primary disease {28194436; 29624648}.

 

Cytology

Smears show mononuclear or multinucleated histiocytes with reniform/oval nuclei intermixed with variable numbers of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils. Touton giant cells or foreign body giant cells may be present. In older lesions, fibroblasts are also seen {21491610}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a circumscribed lesion comprising histiocytes (commonly foamy) lacking significant nuclear pleomorphism; dermal macrophage immunophenotype (CD68, CD163, and factor XIIIa); negativity for CD1a, CD207 (langerin), and ALK.

Desirable: Touton giant cells; clinical exclusion of Erdheim–Chester disease.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

JXG has an excellent prognosis, with a favourable outcome other than in uncommon cases with systemic dissemination. Cutaneous lesions often resolve spontaneously. Surgical excision is indicated for lesions causing functional limitation {33901419}. Ocular JXG may be complicated by glaucoma, uveitis, and blindness [[Ceppi F, Abla O. Juvenile xanthogranuloma and related non-LCH disorders. In: Abla O, Janka G, editors. Histiocytic disorders. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2018.]]. CNS and systemic JXG may behave aggressively {12717244; 26637774; 32383820}. Treatment with clofarabine, cladribine, or cytarabine combined with cladribine has been used in patients with systemic JXG, with variable response {24106153; 34620874}. Treatment with targeted kinase (BRAF) inhibition is also a newer option for cases that fail conventional treatment {32609843; 34620874; 31685033}.
