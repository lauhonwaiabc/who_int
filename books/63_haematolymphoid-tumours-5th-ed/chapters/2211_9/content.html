2.2.1.1 Myeloproliferative neoplasms: Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are a category of myeloid neoplasms characterized by excessive production of mature white blood cells, erythrocytes, and/or platelets. This category is distinct from myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms by virtue of the absence of myelodysplasia at presentation.

The main disease entities/types remain largely unchanged from the prior edition. Initial diagnostic evaluation of MPN continues to depend on close correlation between clinical features, molecular diagnostics, and usually morphological evaluation of a trephine bone marrow biopsy. Most MPN patients are diagnosed in chronic phase (CP), which may progress into a blast phase (BP) associated with the accumulation of secondary cytogenetic and/or molecular aberrations.

Three major changes are introduced in this category. The first includes the omission of “accelerated phase” in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) and consolidation of the disease into chronic and blast phases, with emphasis on risk features in chronic phase. The second change includes updated diagnostic criteria for chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (CEL) and omission of “NOS” from its name. The third major change is the categorization of juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia (JMML) as an MPN. The classification retains an emphasis on distinguishing between polycythaemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythaemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF) using diagnostic criteria established in previous editions, with only minor refinements.

As in prior editions, MPN not otherwise specified (MPN-NOS) is a designation that should be reserved for cases with clinical, laboratory, morphological, and molecular features of MPN but lacking diagnostic criteria of any specific MPN type or with features that overlap across distinct MPN types.

Disease phases in MPN

In the era of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, CML is practically a biphasic disease. The concept of an accelerated phase (AP) was developed when haematological response was the major goal of therapy. The current edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours emphasizes the notion of “high-risk features” as an alternative to the traditional notion of AP. It is important to note that risk-associated features continue to incorporate those previously used to define AP. A major impetus underlying this paradigm shift is the need to develop more refined risk-stratification elements for patients with CML, at presentation and during treatment, to inform future editions. High-risk features identified at presentation include various clinical and genetic findings that should be considered for deciding on a treatment plan. Additionally, awareness of factors associated with resistance during tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy is increasingly needed for selecting the most appropriate next line of treatment. Therefore, risk features identified at presentation should be distinguished from those that arise during the course of treatment; this remains a gap in many studies published on the topic.

The notion of AP in the context of PV, ET, and PMF remains relatively crude and based primarily on increased blasts. In this edition, reference to AP in these diseases is based on the presence of ≥ 10% blasts in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow at any point during the course of the disease.

Myeloid blast phase in CML and other MPN types is defined on the basis of ≥ 20% blasts in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow. Evidence to establish a cut-off value for defining lymphoid blast phase in CML remains limited, and until such scientific evidence is available a diagnosis of lymphoid blast phase should be based on clinical judgement.

Somatic genomic features of MPN

As a group, the majority of MPN types harbour somatic mutations involving genes that encode tyrosine kinases leading to constitutive activation of cell signalling pathways that promote cell proliferation and survival. The BCR::ABL1 fusion is the sine qua non feature of CML, and activating RAS mutations are highly prevalent in JMML, while other types of MPNs are often driven by mutations in JAK2, MPL, and/or CALR.

The JAK2 p.V617F mutation is detectable in > 95% of patients with PV and 50–60% of patients with ET or PMF. Valine 617 is located in the JH2 domain of JAK-2, which acts to supress its kinase activity. Many mutations in exon 12 of JAK2 (usually small in-frame insertions or deletions affecting the pseudokinase domain) have been reported in almost all patients with JAK2 p.V617F–negative PV; these patients tend to have isolated erythrocytosis, and exon 12 mutations are also not found in ET. Activating point mutations in the thrombopoietin receptor gene, MPL, were identified in 2006 in 5–10% of patients with ET and PMF, but not in patients with PV. MPL-mutant disease is characterized by thrombocytosis and/or marrow fibrosis without erythrocytosis, reflecting the activation of pathways limited to the thrombopoietin receptor, unlike in JAK2-mutant disease, in which these pathways are not specifically activated. Mutations in CALR were identified in 2013 and found in 25–35% of patients with ET and PMF, but not in patients with PV. CALR frameshift mutations are all predicted to result in a novel C-terminal protein sequence, the commonest being 52 bp deletion (type 1) or 5 bp insertion (type 2). These are found at similar frequencies in ET; type 1 mutations are much more common than type 2 mutations in PMF. Mutant calreticulin interacts with the thrombopoietin receptor (MPL) within the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to altered trafficking of MPL to the cell surface and activation of downstream signalling pathways. It can also act as a rogue cytokine, activating MPL, which explains the phenotypic similarities with MPL-mutant disease.

Additional mutations in other myeloid driver genes are found in more than half of all patients with MPN; these often include TET2, ASXL1, and DNMT3A {30304655; 35058638}. Mutations affecting splicing regulators (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2) and other regulators of chromatin structure, epigenetic functions, and cellular signalling (e.g. EZH2, IDH1, IDH2, CBL, KRAS, NRAS, STAG2, TP53) are less common. The chronological order of such mutations relative to the main driver mutations seems to influence clinical features, response to therapy, and clonal evolution {25671252}. These additional mutations are more common in the late stage and blast phase than in typical PV and ET, and some are known to carry a poorer prognostic risk (e.g. EZH2, IDH1, IDH2, SRSF2, U2AF1, and ASXL1 mutations in PMF). TP53 mutations are associated with leukaemic transformation and identify a disease category with a particularly unfavourable outlook.

Germline predisposition to MPN

Although JAK2 p.V617F or exon 12, CALR, and MPL mutations are acquired, several germline factors have been found to alter the risk of developing an MPN. A number of common variants that are present in control populations have been found to weakly predispose to the development of MPNs. The JAK2 46/1 haplotype, present in a quarter of the population, is enriched in patients with MPNs, and JAK2 mutations occur preferentially on the 46/1 haplotype allele. Interestingly, 46/1 is associated with JAK2 exon 12–mutant MPNs and also MPL-mutant disease in some studies. SNPs in other genes (e.g. TERT, TET2, SH2B3, MECOM, CHEK2, ATM, LINC-PINT [PINT], GFI1B) have also been associated with MPNs {32532454; 27061303; 27365426}.

Furthermore, first-degree relatives of patients with MPN have a 5- to 7-fold risk of developing an MPN, with familial clusters of cases accounting for < 5% of cases, typically with incomplete penetrance and variable phenotypes, and often with acquired JAK2 p.V617F, CALR, MPL, and additional mutations found in sporadic cases. In most families the predisposing allele has not been identified, but a small number of rare germline variants have been shown to contribute to such familial clusters, including structural variants at the TCL1A/ATG2B/GSKIP locus and RBBP6 variants {34172895}.
