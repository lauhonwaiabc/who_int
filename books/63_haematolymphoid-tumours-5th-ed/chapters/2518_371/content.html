2.5.1.8 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with other tyrosine kinase gene fusions

Definition

Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with other tyrosine kinase gene fusions comprise myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms, typically associated with eosinophilia, resulting from the formation of a tyrosine kinase fusion gene other than those associated with specific myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms.

ICD-O coding

9968/3 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with other tyrosine kinase fusion genes

ICD-11 coding

2A22 Other and unspecified myeloproliferative neoplasms

Related terminology

Not recommended: myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with tyrosine kinase fusion genes (NOS).

Subtype(s)

Defined by the specific tyrosine kinase involved in the fusion gene

Localization

Peripheral blood and bone marrow are nearly always involved. Extramedullary involvement of lymph nodes is also common {33049052}. Myeloid sarcoma involving bone has also been reported {22513837}.

Clinical features

Patients may present with constitutional symptoms, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and/or peripheral blood abnormalities (including leukocytosis, anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and/or monocytosis) {22513837; 24307515}. Eosinophilia is common {17928887; 27058227; 33049052}. Cases with ≥ 20% blasts at initial presentation or during the course of the disease are best classified as the blast phase (either myeloid or lymphoid lineage) of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with a tyrosine kinase fusion gene.

Epidemiology

A male predominance is suggested from the few cases reported in the literature, and the age range is wide (1–82 years) {17928887; 21226763; 22513837; 27058227; 33049052}. Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with ALK fusion genes are more commonly reported in children {20428197}, but they have been reported in adults {24307515}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The formation of a tyrosine kinase fusion gene results in the neoplastic transformation of a pluripotent haematopoietic stem cell that can give rise to other haematopoietic cell lineages. In patients presenting with increased blasts in the range of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) or advanced-stage myeloproliferative neoplasms, the possibility of the tyrosine kinase fusion being a secondary genetic event, rather than transformation of a previously undiagnosed myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with a tyrosine kinase fusion gene, should be considered.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The bone marrow is typically hypercellular, with morphological features overlapping with those of myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative neoplasms, often with notable eosinophilia, with or without monocytosis {24307515; 22513837}. Blasts may or may not be increased {20428197; 33049052}, and phenotypically distinct (myeloid/lymphoid) populations may be present in the same site or different sites. T-cell differentiation is more common in the lymphoid component and may manifest as T-lymphoblastic lymphoma or (rarely) as peripheral T-cell lymphoma {27058227}. Mast cell proliferations with abnormal morphology (spindled) and/or immunophenotype (CD25+, CD123+, etc.) {33049052} and/or bone marrow fibrosis may be present {21226763}.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A suspected diagnosis of a myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with or without eosinophilia should prompt assessment for the more common tyrosine kinase fusion genes. In the absence of the more common tyrosine kinase fusion genes, RNA sequencing is suggested, to evaluate for the presence of additional rare tyrosine kinase fusion genes with potentially novel partners. To date, various other tyrosine kinase fusion genes associated with myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms have been identified. These include but are not limited to ETV6::FGFR2 {33049052}, ETV6::LYN, ETV6::NTRK3 {27058227}, RANBP2::ALK {20428197; 24307515}, BCR::RET, and FGFR1OP::RET {22513837}. The karyotype is typically abnormal and may provide a clue to the specific genes involved and aid in the selection of appropriate break-apart FISH probes (i.e. ETV6 in cases with 12p13 abnormalities, ALK in cases with 2p23 abnormalities, RET in cases with 10q11.2 abnormalities, etc.). Complex chromosomal rearrangements with multiple breaks resulting in the fusion gene may be present, and cryptic rearrangements may also occur {21226763; 27058227}. It is likely that additional fusions involving other tyrosine kinases will be discovered by RNA sequencing in the coming years and will fall within this diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid and/or lymphoid neoplasm; detection of a tyrosine kinase fusion gene other than those specifically defined in distinct entities (PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, FLT3, ETV6::ABL1).

Desirable: eosinophilia; cytogenetic identification of a translocation, suggesting the involvement of a tyrosine kinase gene and prompting the selection of appropriate break-apart FISH probes or other molecular investigation.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is not well defined, because of the limited number of cases reported in the literature, but an aggressive clinical course and treatment-refractory disease with rapid progression to the blast phase has been reported in some patients. Patients with myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with other tyrosine kinase fusion genes may be eligible for experimental targeted therapies with appropriate tyrosine kinase inhibitors, depending on the specific fusion product {22513837; 33049052}.
