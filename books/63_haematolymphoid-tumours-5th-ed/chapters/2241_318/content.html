2.2.4.1 Acute myeloid leukaemia with NPM1 mutation

Definition

Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with NPM1 mutation is a myeloid neoplasm characterized by the presence of somatic mutations involving the nucleophosmin 1 gene (NPM1).

ICD-O coding

9877/3 Acute myeloid leukaemia with NPM1 mutation

ICD-11 coding

2A60.0 & XH74W8 Acute myeloid leukaemia with recurrent genetic abnormalities & Acute myeloid leukaemia with mutated NPM1

Related terminology

Not recommended: myelodysplastic neoplasm with NPM1 mutation; myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with NPM1 mutation.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The blood and bone marrow are nearly always involved. Some patients may present initially with myeloid sarcoma involving the skin, gingiva, lymph nodes, or other extramedullary sites {16051734; 17443224}.

Clinical features

Anaemia and thrombocytopenia are common, typically associated with a high white blood cell count. The platelet count may be higher than that typically seen in other AML types {16051734; 16455956}.

Epidemiology

Nearly one third of adult patients with AML have NPM1 mutation. The peak incidence is in middle age, and there is a female predominance. Most patients have a normal karyotype {15659725; 16051734; 16455956; 17008539}. The incidence decreases with advanced age, but AML with NPM1 mutation still accounts for about 20% of AML cases in patients aged > 70 years {29090343}. Approximately 6–8% of paediatric patients with AML have NPM1 mutation {15870172; 17440048; 17597811; 20979630}.

Etiology

Although NPM1 mutations are not typically detected in individuals with clonal haematopoiesis, emerging data suggest that AML with NPM1 mutation develops from a background of clonal haematopoiesis in most cases {24522528; 30568172; 30692594}.

Pathogenesis

NPM1 encodes a multifunctional nucleolar protein with chaperone and shuttling features. The mutation is typically characterized by a 4 bp insertion in exon 12 of the gene {23436734}. Wildtype NPM1 resides predominantly in the nucleolus, with domains allowing its cytoplasmic, nucleoplasmic, and nucleolar localization. It is involved in several key cellular functions including ribosome biogenesis, maintenance of genomic stability, stress response, growth suppression, and response to apoptotic stimuli {28111462}. The protein may be exported to the cytoplasm to perform select functions under specific biological conditions, mediated by binding of CRM1 to nuclear export signal (NES) motifs.

NPM1 mutations result in the creation of a new NES motif and loss of the tryptophan residue at codon 288 or 290, leading to aberrant localization of mutant NPM1 to the cytoplasm resulting from loss of the nucleolar localization signal and addition of a nuclear export signal {16041368; 16455950; 16720834}. Cytoplasmic localization of mutant NPM1 is thought to be critical for its role in leukaemogenesis. However, the exact mechanisms by which mutant NPM1 drives leukaemogenesis have yet to be determined {16041368; 28111462}. Pathogenetic mechanisms include interaction with chromatin-bound XPO1 at HOX loci to facilitate their expression {30205049}, and binding and exporting several nuclear proteins (including tumour suppressors and transcription factors) to the cytoplasm, probably inhibiting their normal functions {17616680}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Most cases are characterized by markedly hypercellular bone marrow. Myelomonocytic or monocytic differentiation is common {15659725; 17008539}; however, morphological features vary widely, and in some cases blasts may show less maturation. Multilineage dysplasia is observed in 20–25% of cases {19429869; 20203266; 21183700}.

Blasts with cup-like nuclear morphology are strongly associated with the presence of NPM1 and FLT3 ITD mutations {19672946}. Identification of this finding in > 10% of blasts is highly specific for AML with NPM1 mutation {20855646}.

Cytochemistry stains demonstrate the presence of myeloperoxidase in blasts in the majority of cases. In cases with monocytic differentiation, butyrate esterase will be reactive in the monocytic component.

Immunophenotype

The detection of cytoplasmic NPM1 by immunohistochemistry is a surrogate marker of NPM1 mutation {33171486}, including rare mutations occurring outside exon 12 {20378574; 34343258}. Mutant NPM1 may be present in various lineages, including myeloid, monocytic, erythroid, and megakaryocytic {16926285}.

The immunophenotype is variable, but about 80% of cases have an absence of CD34 expression {20542566; 33618432}. CD33, KIT (CD117), and CD123 expression is common, whereas CD13 is usually low. Immunophenotypic categories include cases with a predominance of immature myeloid blasts (CD34+, KIT [CD117]+; or CD34−, KIT [CD117]+, HLA-DR+), cases with acute promyelocytic leukaemia-like features (CD34−, HLA-DR−, KIT [CD117]+, myeloperoxidase+), and cases with a predominance of myelomonocytic or monocytic differentiation (CD14+, CD36+, CD64+) {23601747; 33618432; 20542566; 29274134}. A small population with the immunophenotype of leukaemic stem cells (CD34+, CD38−, CD123+) is detectable by flow cytometry in most cases {20634376}.

Immunophenotypically abnormal CD34+ myeloid progenitors may persist after molecular remission of the NPM1-mutant clone, and they appear to be associated with persistent clonal haematopoiesis. The persistence of these cells does not correlate with relapse-free survival and should not be interpreted as measurable residual AML by flow cytometry immunophenotyping {33618432}.

Differential diagnosis

The broad morphological spectrum of AML with NPM1 mutation may overlap with a variety of myeloid neoplasms. The presence of dysplasia results in morphological overlap with myelodysplasia-related AML and with several AML types with defining genetic abnormalities.

Recent data suggest that the presence of NPM1 mutation is an AML-defining event on biological and clinical grounds; therefore, rare cases with features of myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDSs) or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms, particularly chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia, should be regarded as AML with NPM1 mutation when such mutation is detected at presentation {16678898; 28707414; 30902805; 31085507; 33255988}. Although the presence of an NPM1 variant is considered an AML-defining event irrespective of bone marrow blast count, data on the clinical significance and outcome of rare cases of myeloid neoplasms harbouring NPM1 variants with a very low variant allele frequency (VAF; < 10%) and no increase in bone marrow or peripheral blood blasts are lacking, and for the time being, these cases should be interpreted with caution, because definitive classification as AML may not be possible in such cases.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

NPM1 mutations are invariably heterozygous and primarily restricted to exon 12 (< 1% involving other exons) {32609823}. Leukaemic cells have a normal karyotype in about 85% of cases {15659725; 19429869}. When present, chromosomal abnormalities often include gains of chromosomes 8 and 4 and del(9q) {16455956; 19429869}.

The most frequent concurrent mutations involve FLT3 and RAS pathway signalling (KRAS, NRAS, PTPN11), epigenetic regulators (DNMT3A, TET2, IDH1/IDH2), and the cohesin complex (STAG2) {15659725; 23634996; 33618432}. TET2 and SRSF2 mutations are more commonly co-mutated in elderly patients {33711909}. Although NPM1 mutation is a class-defining lesion, it is frequently a later event in leukaemogenesis, commonly secondary to mutations in epigenetic modifiers {23704090; 24550281; 24522528; 33087716; 33116311}. Therefore, the latter mutations may persist after remission of the NPM1-mutant leukaemic clone {34376804}.

Monitoring of measurable residual disease by sensitive molecular techniques for the detection of mutant NPM1 provides helpful information to guide therapeutic decisions {21555683; 34376804; 34724563}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid neoplasm with increased peripheral blood and/or bone marrow blasts (may be < 20%); detection of NPM1 mutation; no history of exposure to cytotoxic therapy.

Desirable: detection of cytoplasmic NPM1 by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

According to the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) prognostication scheme, determining the mutation status of NPM1 (together with FLT3) is mandatory for accurate risk stratification of AML. AML with mutated NPM1 typically shows a good response to induction therapy {27895058; 15659725}. Hypomethylating agents combined with venetoclax have shown better results than traditional standard-of-care regimens in older patients with NPM1-mutated AML {32251497}. Higher NPM1 mutant transcript levels after induction and consolidation therapy are associated with an increased risk of relapse and death {21555683; 29601269; 26789727}.

Although rare, the detection of NPM1 mutation in a patient with MDS with low blasts might warrant close follow-up to exclude the possibility of a subclonal variant. Furthermore, in patients with MDS with NPM1 mutation, relapses without mutant NPM1 have been described after chemotherapy, showing the subclonal nature of the NPM1 mutation {27509126; 28690314}.

The prognosis of NPM1-mutated AML is influenced by other co-occurring mutations. Cases with a normal karyotype, in the absence of FLT3 internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutation, have a characteristically favourable prognosis {16455956; 16109776}. NPM1-mutated AML without FLT3 ITD or with FLT3 ITD-low (mutant allele ratio: < 0.5) are classified as favourable risk, whereas NPM1-mutated AML with FLT3 ITD-high (mutant allele ratio: > 0.5) is regarded as intermediate risk, according to the ELN risk-stratification scheme {27895058}. Morphological dysplasia in the setting of NPM1 mutation does not appear to affect prognosis {19429869; 20203266; 21183700}. Myelodysplasia-related AML–defining mutations involving chromatin modifiers and the splicing complex are rare in AML with NPM1 mutation and may have a negative prognostic impact {27276561}.

Younger patients with a normal karyotype and no FLT3 ITD have a prognosis comparable to that of patients with core-binding factor (CBF) AML. At least in younger adult patients, the coexistence of FLT3 ITD mutations, particularly in cases with a high VAF, is associated with a poorer prognosis, but these patients still appear to have a better prognosis than those with AML with FLT3 ITD and wildtype NPM1, especially when the VAF of FLT3 ITD is low {17957027; 23377436; 25270908; 28644114; 31826241; 33171486}. The negative prognostic impact of a concomitant FLT3 ITD mutation in older patients with AML with mutated NPM1 is less clear {20026798}. Co-occurrence of NPM1, FLT3 ITD, and DNMT3A mutations has been associated with a particularly poor outcome {25281355; 31977039; 34376804}. Patients with NPM1 and NRAS mutation {27276561} or NPM1 and FLT3 tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) double-mutated AML {29296796} have a relatively good prognosis. The presence of concurrent WT1 mutations appears to be associated with a poor outcome {32461631}. Adverse-risk cytogenetic abnormalities confer a poor prognosis in the setting of NPM1-mutated/FLT3-wildtype (or FLT3 ITD-low) genotype {31430225}.
