3.1.1.1 Histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms: Introduction

Histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms are tumours originating from or showing differentiation towards cells of the mononuclear phagocyte system, which comprise monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The term “histiocyte” has been variably defined, either being equated with the macrophage or encompassing both the macrophage and the dendritic cell. In disease conditions, the term “histiocytosis” is often used as a diagnostic term, without regard to the cell type it corresponds to in the mononuclear phagocyte system.

The mononuclear phagocyte system

Mononuclear phagocytes are specialized cells of the innate immune system, which is essential for homeostasis and immunity. After infection, mononuclear phagocytes are among the first to encounter pathogens. They detect and phagocytose pathogens, recruit other immune cells, and initiate adaptive immunity by presenting antigens to T cells [[Vegh P, Fletcher J, Dixon D, et al. Mononuclear phagocyte system; first published: 2017 May 15 In: Encyclopedia of Life Sciences (eLS) [Internet]. Chichester (UK): John Wiley & Sons, Ltd. Available from: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9780470015902.a0001217.pub2.]]. Although macrophages are traditionally considered to be phagocytes and dendritic cells antigen-presenting cells, these cell types in fact show extensive overlap in their properties and functions in the immune response.

Monocytes are circulating immune cells that during inflammation can release cytokines and can enter tissue to differentiate into effector cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells. They originate from haematopoietic stems cells in the bone marrow, via common monocyte progenitors.

Macrophages are found in all tissues of the body, playing a key role in organ development, homeostasis, immunity, and repair {33363732}. They show a CD45+, CD4+, CD68+, CD163+, and lysozyme+ immunophenotype. In contrast to the previous belief that macrophages are derived from monocytes, it is now known that resident macrophages predominately originate from embryonic precursors, before the establishment of definitive haematopoiesis, and that they are capable of self-renewal {33363732; 30579704} [[Vegh P, Fletcher J, Dixon D, et al. Mononuclear phagocyte system; first published: 2017 May 15 In: Encyclopedia of Life Sciences (eLS) [Internet]. Chichester (UK): John Wiley & Sons, Ltd. Available from: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9780470015902.a0001217.pub2.]]. Monocytes can contribute to the formation of macrophages in the inflammatory process.

Conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) are characterized by the presence of projecting cytoplasmic dendrites, and their major function is to process antigens for presentation to T cells, thus establishing a link between the innate and adaptive immune responses {28198365; 30719026}. They originate predominately from haematopoietic stem cells, via common dendritic cell progenitors, although circulating monocytes can also enter tissues to differentiate into dendritic cells. Two functional subtypes of cDCs are recognized – cDC1 and cDC2 – but it is unclear how these relate to the known dendritic cell populations, including Langerhans cells (S100+, CD1a+, CD207+, CD4+), indeterminate dendritic cells (S100+, CD1a+, CD207−, CD4+), interdigitating dendritic cells (S100+, CD1a−, CD207−, CD4+), and interstitial dendritic cells (fascin+, factor XIIIa+, CD4+/−). Of note, it has recently been suggested that Langerhans cells may not be dendritic cells but rather are macrophages “in dendritic cell clothing” {28720426}.

Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (formerly known as plasmacytoid monocytes) occur in the blood and various tissues, and they lack cytoplasmic dendrites. They usually express a CD123 (IL-3RA)+, CD303 (BDCA2)+, CD304 (BDCA4)+, TCF4+ immunophenotype. pDCs are highly efficient in sensing intracellular viral or self DNA and RNA, mainly via toll-like receptors, and in response produce large quantities of interferons, thus playing a major role in antiviral immunity and systemic autoimmunity {31191558}. They can be generated from common myeloid progenitors (via common dendritic cell progenitors) as well as common lymphoid progenitors {31191558}.

Follicular dendritic cells and fibroblastic reticular cells, which are of mesenchymal origin {10861584; 12163300; 24863064}, are not part of the mononuclear phagocyte system.

The neoplastic counterparts of the mononuclear phagocyte system

The neoplastic counterpart of the monocyte is the leukaemic cell of acute monocytic leukaemia. The neoplastic counterparts of the macrophage include the cells of many entities, such as histiocytic sarcoma, Rosai–Dorfman disease, ALK-positive histiocytosis, Erdheim–Chester disease, and juvenile xanthogranuloma, although this last entity has also been suggested to originate from the dermal/interstitial dendritic cell. The neoplastic counterparts of cDCs are the cells of Langerhans cell histiocytosis, Langerhans cell sarcoma, interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma, and indeterminate dendritic cell tumour. The neoplastic cells of mature pDC proliferation associated with myeloid neoplasm and blastic pDC neoplasm represent the neoplastic counterparts of the pDC. In general, the neoplastic cells exhibit an immunophenotype corresponding to their postulated normal counterparts, although some may show aberrant marker expression or loss. The differential diagnoses of the various histiocytic proliferations that lack overt anaplastic cytological features are listed in #32035Table 3.02 , in the section on Rosai–Dorfman disease.

In most circumstances, the classification of a macrophage or dendritic cell neoplasm as Langerhans cell histiocytosis/sarcoma, indeterminate dendritic cell tumour, interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma, or histiocytic sarcoma is straightforward, because of the presence of a distinctive immunophenotype. Nonetheless, there are rare cases that show overlapping or hybrid features, defying precise classification {18272816; 26966089}.

De novo and secondary histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms

Most histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms arise de novo, but a subset of cases occur in association with or follow a lymphoma/leukaemia, most commonly follicular lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, and T- or B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma {18272816; 19642834; 26966089; 21666687; 32929178}. These secondary histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms are classified in the same way as their de novo counterparts, as Langerhans cell histiocytosis/sarcoma, histiocytic sarcoma, interdigitating dendritic cell sarcoma, or indeterminate dendritic cell tumour. Because the histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms usually express the same clonal markers as the associated or preceding lymphoma/leukaemia, a transdifferentiation mechanism has been proposed to explain their synchronous/metachronous presentation in single patients {18272816; 21666687; 32929178}. However, the histiocytic neoplasm and associated lymphoma/leukaemia often show other genetic alterations exclusive to each component, suggesting that divergent differentiation or transdifferentiation occurs from a common lymphoid progenitor clone {24850291; 31807922; 32929178}.

Changes in this volume

In this edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, changes from the previous edition in the category of “histiocytic/dendritic cell neoplasms” are as follows:

Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma and fibroblastic reticular cell tumour have been moved out of this category and into a separate category, “stromal-derived neoplasms of lymphoid tissues” Blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm, which was previously covered in a standalone chapter, has been moved to this category Newly included entities include mature plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferation associated with myeloid neoplasm, Rosai–Dorfman disease, and ALK-positive histiocytosis

Mature pDC proliferation associated with myeloid neoplasm is often shown to be a clonal process, and Rosai–Dorfman disease often shows gain-of-function mutations in the MAPK pathway. The neoplastic nature of these entities provides a rationale for their inclusion in the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours {29236635; 30867592; 31768065; 32601132}. ALK-positive histiocytosis shows a broad clinicopathological spectrum but is unified by the presence of ALK gene translocation (most commonly KIF5B::ALK) and a remarkable response to ALK-inhibitor therapy {30573850; 34727172}; this entity is included for the first time in the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours.
