2.2.1.6 Essential thrombocythaemia

Definition

Essential thrombocythaemia (ET) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by sustained thrombocytosis and increased numbers of large, mature megakaryocytes in a normocellular bone marrow.

ICD-O coding

9962/3 Essential thrombocythaemia

ICD-11 coding

3B63.1Z & XH4ZM5 Acquired thrombocytosis, unspecified & Essential thrombocythaemia

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Blood and bone marrow

Clinical features

ET is usually diagnosed in the chronic phase. In a small number of cases, ET progresses to the accelerated phase and blast phase, diagnosed when bone marrow / peripheral blood blasts are 10–19% and ≥ 20%, respectively {33882481}.

The presentation of ET can be quite variable clinically, ranging from asymptomatic thrombocytosis observed at the time of routine laboratory testing, to ET-related symptoms (e.g. headache, erythromelalgia) or a thrombotic or haemorrhagic event. Thrombocytosis is the defining feature, especially when sustained and without obvious secondary cause (e.g. trauma, infection, reactive state, iron deficiency, or injury). Indeed, often patients have had thrombocytosis for years before it is evaluated, and a sizable minority of patients may also have leukocytosis, whereas any amount of erythrocytosis or iron deficiency (especially in the setting of the JAK2 p.V617F mutation) should raise the question of whether the diagnosis is more accurately polycythaemia vera (PV).

Presentations can be asymptomatic, and patients with ET have a lower level of MPN symptoms overall than patients with PV or primary myelofibrosis (PMF). About 50–60% of ET patients present with ET-related symptoms, including disease-related fatigue, and many have a range of microvascular symptoms, including erythromelalgia and thrombocytosis-related symptoms such as headaches and difficulties with concentration. Less commonly, there are features that are more typical of PV, such as night sweats and pruritus, or of PMF, such as mild splenomegaly and splenomegaly-related symptoms (pain, early satiety). ET-associated vascular events include both venous thrombosis (deep vein thrombosis, sagittal vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolus) and arterial thrombosis (myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, etc.). Moreover, patients who experience visceral thrombotic events (portal vein thrombosis, mesenteric vein thrombosis, splenic vein thrombosis) have a high rate (up to 40%) of being diagnosed with an underlying MPN. Such patients may be diagnosed with ET, PV, or MPN-NOS, dependent on their variable presenting phenotypes meeting the diagnostic criteria of different MPNs. Bleeding events can also complicate ET, sometimes associated with acquired von Willebrand syndrome, and can manifest as bleeding from mucosal surfaces or gastrointestinal bleeding.

Although many patients may remain asymptomatic, progression to post-ET MF and/or acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) may occur. ET is usually diagnosed in the chronic phase. In a small number of cases, ET progresses to a post-ET MF, or, depending on blast numbers in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow, to the accelerated phase (10–19% blasts) or the blast phase (≥ 20% blasts) {33882481}.

Epidemiology

According to SEER data, the overall incidence rate is 1.55 cases (95% CI: 1.52–1.57) per 100 000 person-years {34689695}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Acquired somatic mutations in JAK2, at chromosome band 9p24.1, play a role in the pathogenesis of ET. The most common mutation, JAK2 p.V617F, results in a ligand-independent constitutively active cytoplasmic JAK-2, which activates the STAT, MAPK, and PI3K signalling pathways to promote the transformation and proliferation of haematopoietic progenitors. Valine 617 is located in the JH2 domain of JAK-2, which acts to supress its kinase activity {35271300}.

Other pathogenic mechanisms include mutations in MPL and CALR. Thereby, the MPL p.W515L/K–mutant thrombopoietin receptors phosphorylate wildtype JAK-2 in the absence of thrombopoietin, resulting in the activation of signalling pathways downstream of JAK-2. Mutant calreticulin acquires an increased affinity to MPL, and their interaction results in constitutive JAK/STAT activation.

Macroscopic appearance

Histopathology

Peripheral blood smear and bone marrow findings

Peripheral blood smear evaluation shows varying degrees of thrombocytosis. The platelets vary in size and morphology, and atypical large or giant platelets, agranular platelets, and other unusual forms may be seen. Most often, the red and white cells do not show any changes. Iron deficiency related to recurrent haemorrhage can cause iron deficiency and resultant microcytic and hypochromic red blood cells {21747083; 16810619; 25037629; 21220604; 21447832}.

Bone marrow cellularity is typically normal for age. Hypocellularity or hypercellularity without a clear cause should prompt careful consideration of other differential diagnostic possibilities. The myeloid-to-erythroid ratio is usually in the normal range, but concurrent anaemia may result in expansion of precursors. The most striking abnormality is a marked proliferation of megakaryocytes, with a predominance of large to giant forms displaying abundant mature cytoplasm and deeply lobed and hypersegmented (staghorn-like) nuclei. Clustering of megakaryocytes is not a prominent feature, and atypical megakaryocytes with hyperchromatic nuclei are not prominent. Some megakaryocytes may show emperipolesis of bone marrow elements, a finding of uncertain significance. Dysplastic changes should not be present in any lineage, and blasts are not increased.

Myelofibrosis is usually absent (MF-0) or mild (MF-1) {22246040; 23900239; 16079113; 21747083; 15802893; 12210809}. Reticulin fibrosis may increase over time, and in a small subset of patients, post-ET MF may ensue.

CD61 or CD42b immunostains can be used to highlight the increased numbers of megakaryocytes and the cytological changes noted within the megakaryocyte population. CD34-positive cells are usually few.

Differential diagnosis

Distinction from reactive megakaryocytic hyperplasias and other MPNs and myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms is essential. In immune thrombocytopenic purpura and other autoimmune or reactive states, bone marrow biopsy histology may show increased numbers of megakaryocytes, but these would be normal in size or even smaller than normal and lack the characteristic atypia. Patients with immune thrombocytopenia who are receiving treatment with thrombopoietic receptor agonists show megakaryocytic hyperplasia with morphological features that may resemble those seen in ET or other MPNs, as well as increased bone marrow fibrosis {31012979}.

Marked bone marrow hypercellularity, significant megakaryocytic atypia, tight clustering of the megakaryocytes, dysplastic features in any of the haematopoietic lineages, or significant myelofibrosis (MF-2 or MF-3) should prompt alternative diagnoses such as PV, prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis (pre-PMF), PMF, or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm. In cases that lack mutant JAK2, CALR, and MPL, a diagnosis of ET might remain challenging.

 

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The JAK2 p.V617F mutation is detectable in 50–60% of patients. Mutations in CALR are found in 25–35% of patients {24325356}. CALR frameshift mutations are all predicted to result in a novel C-terminal protein sequence, the commonest being 52 bp deletion (type 1) or 5 bp insertion (type 2); these types are found with similar frequency in ET. Activating point mutations in MPL are identified in 5–10% of patients {16868251}.

Prognosis and prediction

ET is an indolent disorder characterized by long symptom-free intervals interrupted by occasional life-threatening thromboembolic or haemorrhagic events {21490340; 15667529; 21926959; 16210036; 16810619; 14560781; 9025160; 18790799; 11735160}. After many years, 10–15% of patients develop post-ET MF {26710883; 24897074}. In large series, the relative incidence rates of post-ET MF were found to be approximately half those of post-PV MF {21747083; 25037629}. Risk factors associated with the development of post-ET MF and blast phase transformation include age (≥ 60 years), anaemia, leukocytosis, and therapy resistance to hydroxycarbamide {28795425; 30304655; 21083657}. Other factors associated with post-ET MF include absence of JAK2 p.V617F and mutations in epigenetic regulators and ASXL1 {28795425; 30304655}. Other factors associated with blast phase transformation include platelet count > 1000 × 109/L and mutations of TP53 or RUNX1 {28795425; 30304655}.

Median survival times may approach 20 years {25037629}. Because ET is usually diagnosed late in middle age, life expectancy is near normal for many patients {21747083; 20130601; 25037629}. A substantial difference in prognosis has been reported when pre-PMF is carefully excluded {16810612}.

Treatment indication is based on risk assessment for thrombotic or haemorrhagic complications {2307991; 26890983; 26617062}, and although scores predicting survival are available {22740446; 31945802}, they generally do not guide treatment decisions.

 

Required criteria:

·   Documentation of a previous diagnosis of WHO-defined essential thrombocythaemia

·   Bone marrow fibrosis of grade 2–3 on a scale of 0–3

Additional criteria:

·   Anaemia (below the reference range, given age, sex, and altitude considerations) and a > 2 g/dL decrease from baseline haemoglobin concentration

·   Leukoerythroblastosis

·   Increasing splenomegaly, defined as either an increase in palpable splenomegaly of > 50 mm from baseline (distance from the left costal margin, or on imaging) or the development of newly palpable splenomegaly

·   Elevated LDH level (above the reference range)

·   Development of any two (or all three) of the following constitutional symptoms: > 10% weight loss in 6 months, night sweats, unexplained fever (> 37.5 °C)

The diagnosis of post-ET MF requires either both of the required criteria or at least two additional criteria.

Major criteria:

·   Platelet count ≥ 450 × 109/L

·   Bone marrow biopsy showing proliferation mainly of the megakaryocytic lineage, with increased numbers of enlarged, mature megakaryocytes with hyperlobulated nuclei; no significant increase or left shift in neutrophil granulopoiesis or erythropoiesis; very rarely a minor (grade 1) increase in reticulin fibres

·   WHO criteria for BCR::ABL1-positive chronic myeloid leukaemia, polycythaemia vera, primary myelofibrosis, and other myeloid neoplasms are not met

·   JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutation

Minor criteria:

·   Presence of a clonal marker

OR

·   Exclusion of reactive thrombocytosis

The diagnosis of essential thrombocythaemia requires either all the major criteria or the first three major criteria plus a minor criterion to be met.
