2.3.2.6 Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia

Definition

Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia is an acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with evidence of granulocytic and monocytic maturation that lacks defining genetic abnormalities.

ICD-O coding

9867/3 Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia

ICD-11 coding

2A60.33 Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia

Related terminology

Not recommended: acute myeloid leukaemia, M4.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

This leukaemia predominantly involves the peripheral blood and bone marrow.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with symptoms related to cytopenias, including fatigue. Some may present with peripheral blood leukocytosis with circulating blasts and promonocytes.

Epidemiology

Acute myelomonocytic leukaemia occurs in all age groups, although it is more common in adults. There are limited data using the current definition criteria.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Bone marrow biopsy sections usually show hypercellularity with infiltration of immature cells, sometimes associated with a starry-sky pattern. Smears show myeloblasts and cells with various degrees of myelomonocytic differentiation. Blasts are medium-sized or large, with abundant basophilic cytoplasm that is often vacuolated. Nuclei have delicate, lace-like chromatin and one or more prominent nucleoli. Promonocytes are considered blast equivalents (see also section Chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia).

Peripheral blood smears typically show an increase in monocytes, which are often more mature than those in the bone marrow. The monocytic component may be more evident in the peripheral blood than in the bone marrow.

Immunophenotype

By flow cytometry, blasts commonly express myeloid-associated antigens such as CD13, CD15, CD33, CD65, and/or CD117. Monocytic markers are also typically present, including CD4, CD11b, CD11c, CD14, CD36, CD64, and CD163. CD7 and CD56 expression is common.

Cytochemistry

Blasts are positive (≥ 3%) for myeloperoxidase. The monoblasts, promonocytes, and monocytes are typically positive for nonspecific esterase and may also have myeloperoxidase-positive granules.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of AML with monocytic differentiation should primarily include microgranular acute promyelocytic leukaemia and AML with NPM1 mutation.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Approximately two thirds of cases have a normal karyotype. The remaining cases show either isolated trisomy 8 or rare structural abnormalities {27285584}. Frequently mutated genes include TET2, RUNX1, and ASXL1; less commonly mutated genes include DNMT3A, STAG2, NRAS, IDH2, SRSF2, U2AF1, SF3B1, and ZRSR2. FLT3 internal tandem duplication (ITD) and tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) mutations are present in 25% and 10% of cases, respectively {27285584}. It should be noted that some of the aforementioned mutations now qualify for myelodysplasia-related AML (see section Acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia-related).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: ≥ 20% blasts and blast equivalents (promonocytes) in bone marrow and/or blood; positive for myeloid-associated markers (e.g. myeloperoxidase, CD13, CD33, CD117); maturing granulocytes constitute ≥ 20% of bone marrow cells; monocyte-lineage cells constitute ≥ 20% of bone marrow cells; not fulfilling criteria for AML types with defined genetic alterations; not fulfilling diagnostic criteria for myeloid neoplasm post cytotoxic therapy.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

There are limited data specifically on prognosis and prediction in patients classified as having acute myelomonocytic leukaemia using the current definition criteria.
