2.2.6.1 Chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia

Definition

Chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia (CMML) is a myeloid neoplasm with myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative features, characterized by sustained peripheral blood monocytosis and various combinations of somatic mutations involving epigenetic regulation, spliceosome, and signal transduction genes.

Subtype(s)

Myelodysplastic chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia (MD-CMML); myeloproliferative chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia (MP-CMML)

Localization

The blood and bone marrow are always involved. Common sites of extramedullary disease include the spleen {12804634; 30105755}, skin {22895265}, and lymph nodes {29582697}, but other sites {28572287} may be involved.

Clinical features

The diagnosis is predicated by the detection of persistent absolute and relative peripheral blood monocytosis after excluding other diseases that may be associated with monocytosis. The requirement of ≥ 10% monocytes adds specificity by excluding patients who may meet the absolute monocytosis criterion because of a high white blood cell count even if the monocytosis proportion is low {30723295}. Although the majority of patients with CMML have absolute monocytosis with monocyte counts of ≥ 1 × 109/L, multiple groups have reported that patients with ≥ 10% monocytes and absolute monocytosis with monocyte counts of 0.5 to < 1 × 109/L have clinicopathological features similar to those of patients with CMML {28548124; 33108455; 33156969}. On the basis of these findings, patients who otherwise meet the criteria for CMML but who have relative monocytosis > 10% and absolute monocytosis with monocyte counts of ≥ 0.5 × 109/L may be diagnosed with CMML if characteristic somatic mutations are identified. The diagnostic criteria for CMML are summarized in #30748Box 2.19 .

The median patient age at diagnosis is 71–76 years, and patients are predominantly male (M:F ratio: 1.6–2.6:1) {32501529; 23372164; 24695057; 30054307}. Most patients present with anaemia. Although thrombocytopenia is common, some patients may present with elevated platelet counts {30054307; 23372164}. Patients vary in terms of their white blood cell count at presentation. The clinical and molecular distinction between MD-CMML and MP-CMML based on a white blood cell count cut-off point of 13 × 109/L {7986717}, has been substantiated in multiple studies {24595958; 20371679; 30054307; 23372164; 32501529}. In larger series, the proportion of MP-CMML ranged from 39% to 63% of all CMML cases {30054307; 23372164; 32501529}. Immature myeloid cells tend to be present in the peripheral blood in patients with MP-CMML. Leukocytosis and monocytosis may be associated with end-organ damage {34144312}.

Clinical manifestations are varied and depend in part on the disease subtype. Patients with MD-CMML usually present with signs and symptoms related to peripheral blood cytopenias, including fatigue, easy bruising, and recurrent infections. Patients who present with or subsequently develop MP-CMML tend to have a higher incidence of constitutional symptoms (e.g. weight loss, fever, night sweats, and diffuse bone pain) and splenomegaly {28572287}. Hyperleukocytosis may be detected at presentation or develop during the disease course secondary to clonal evolution or associated events (e.g. systemic infection).

Detection of polyclonal hypergammaglobulinaemia is common, and about 20% of patients may have autoimmune diseases and/or systemic inflammatory syndromes diagnosed shortly before or after their CMML diagnosis {27739921; 27337291; 25413011; 26350487}.

Epidemiology

The age-standardized incidence rate and prevalence of CMML in the Düsseldorf registry are 0.41 cases per 100 000 person-years and 1.05–19.4 cases per 100 000 population, respectively {21708407}. In a recent report from the Swedish registry, the incidence of CMML was reported to be 0.51 cases per 100 000 person-years {32501529}. The age-standardized incidence rate of CMML in the Kingdom of the Netherlands was reported as 0.37 cases per 100 000 person-years {25533930}; in that study, the incidence was highest in patients aged > 80 years (3.8 cases per 100 000 person-years) and higher in males (0.5 cases per 100 000 person-years; 95% CI: 0.4–0.6) than in females (0.2 cases per 100 000 person-years; 95% CI: 0.2–0.3). In the USA, the overall incidence of CMML is 0.35 cases per 100 000 person-years, with a lower disease incidence in the Black and Asian/Pacific Islander populations than among White people {27881041}.

Etiology

Stochastic acquisition of age-related clonal haematopoiesis is a likely cause of CMML {26648538}. Some cases arise in patients with familial germline predisposition {22138511; 26280900; 30963592}.

Pathogenesis

The disease is driven by the accumulation, in the haematopoietic stem cell compartment, of somatic variants that affect DNA methylation (e.g. TET2), histone modifications (e.g. ASXL1), pre-mRNA splicing (e.g. SRSF2, U2AF1), and cell signalling (e.g. NRAS, KRAS, CBL, or JAK2). The molecular signature of diseased cells, analysed by whole-genome sequencing, is characteristic of an ageing-associated disease {26908133}. Clonal architecture analysed at the single-cell level indicates a mostly linear accumulation of somatic variants in CD34+/CD38−/CD90+ cells, usually starting with epigenetic, then splicing, then signalling genes, with limited residual wildtype cells {23319568}. Myeloid cell proliferation and the risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) increase with mutations that activate the RAS/MAPK signalling pathway, NRAS being the most frequent {32344757; 34006870}. When mutant immature cells undergo myeloid differentiation, which is amplified by a frequent hypersensitivity of myeloid progenitor cells to granulocyte–macrophage colony–stimulating factor {23632888}, the leukaemic clone gains a growth advantage; i.e. all the variants are usually expressed in mature myeloid cells {23319568} that include monocytes, granulocytes {19864642}, and, in some patients, plasmacytoid dendritic cells {30894665} or mast cells {23440662}. Recent evidence shows that proliferative CMML can demonstrate early dominant RAS mutations and inherent hypersensitivity to granulocyte–macrophage colony–stimulating factor {34006870}. These variants induce an epigenetic reprogramming of myeloid cells {21537084; 25822018} that accounts for the abnormal partition of peripheral blood monocyte subsets {30575719}. As a consequence, a normal partition of these subsets is restored in patients who respond to hypomethylating agents, even though this treatment has a limited impact on the size of the mutated clone {26908133}. Abnormal synthesis and secretion of cytokines and chemokines by mature cells of the leukaemic clone {33690800}, together with cells of the bone marrow niche {30123289}, contribute to the creation of an inflammatory state, whereas deficient secretion of IL-10 contributes to myelomonocytic expansion {8879210; 30026572}.

Histopathology

CMML is a prototype myeloid neoplasm with both myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative features, with monocytosis representing an overarching disease characteristic. Myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative attributes are reflected to varying degrees in individual patients and at various time points over the course of the disease, as a function of the underlying molecular landscape and clonal evolution.

The morphological spectrum of monocytes and their precursors is divided into four maturation stages: mature monocytes, immature monocytes, promonocytes, and monoblasts {19535346}. Mature and immature monocytes have condensed chromatin and absent or inconspicuous nucleoli; they differ in the degree of nuclear lobulation, cytoplasmic basophilia, and cell size, but such differences tend to be modest. Promonocytes and monoblasts have a delicate, open chromatin pattern and a prominent nucleolus. Promonocytes retain nuclear convolutions and indentations, whereas monoblasts have a round or oval nucleus. Promonocytes are considered equivalent to blasts in terms of blast enumeration in peripheral blood or bone marrow {32297416}.

Previously, cases with < 1% blasts in blood and < 5% blasts in the bone marrow were categorized as the subgroup CMML-0, but this has been shown to have little to no correlation with outcome measures {33932168; 33513373; 30054307; 29775844}.

Two CMML subgroups are now recognized, defined according to the percentage of blasts (myeloblasts and monoblasts) and blast-equivalent cells (promonocytes) in peripheral blood and bone marrow, as follows:

CMML-1: < 5% in the blood and < 10% in the bone marrow CMML-2: ≥ 5% and < 20% in the blood and/or ≥ 10% and < 20% in the bone marrow
Peripheral blood

Peripheral blood smear findings reflect the haematological spectrum of the disease. The salient feature is relative and absolute monocytosis, often with immature monocytes. Patients with MP-CMML may have peripheral blood smear findings that are indistinguishable morphologically from those with chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML). Circulating immature myeloid cells (promyelocytes, myelocytes, and metamyelocytes) are significantly increased in MP-CMML {30054307}. Dysgranulopoiesis, large platelets, and nucleated red blood cells may be seen.

Bone marrow

The majority of patients with CMML have a hypercellular bone marrow with a myelomonocytic predominance and a relative decrease in erythroid precursors {18371110; 17041567}. The bone marrow core biopsy often demonstrates abnormal localization of immature precursors.

Dysgranulopoiesis is present to varying degrees in CMML and is usually more common in MD-CMML. It is often more prominent in the bone marrow than in the peripheral blood. The most common features include hyposegmented or abnormally segmented nuclei and/or abnormal cytoplasmic granulation. Dysplastic changes may be mild in MP-CMML. Detection of Auer rods is rare, and such a finding should first prompt a thorough molecular genetic evaluation to exclude AML. In the absence of evidence to support an alternative diagnosis, the longstanding recommendation to subgroup such cases as CMML-2 remains unchanged.

Monocytes in the bone marrow are usually increased and show left-shifted maturation. Such an increase is helpful in cases where the absolute monocytosis count in the peripheral blood is ≥ 0.5 × 109/L but < 1.0 × 109/L. In some cases, it may be difficult to distinguish morphologically the monocytic population from the dysplastic neutrophilic granulocytic population with hypogranular cytoplasm {7986717; 2070054; 19864642}.

Dyserythropoiesis commonly manifests as megaloblastic changes. Ring sideroblasts can been seen in a subset of cases. These changes may be absent in MP-CMML.

The number and morphological characteristics of megakaryocytes in CMML are variable. An increased number of megakaryocytes may be seen in some cases, but megakaryocyte clustering is not a prominent feature {22421409; 18371110}. Dysmegakaryopoiesis, characterized by small megakaryocytes and/or megakaryocytes with hypolobated or abnormally lobated nuclei, is common {17041567}.

Although some increase in reticulin fibres may be seen {29262560}, a minority (∼3%) of cases can have moderate or severe reticulin fibrosis at initial diagnosis {29596070}. The latter tend to have MP-CMML, with more pronounced absolute monocytosis, splenomegaly, and a higher frequency of JAK2 p.V617F mutation. Rare cases with fibrosis and JAK2 mutations might be difficult to distinguish from primary myelofibrosis or other myeloproliferative neoplasms with monocytosis, and in such cases the morphology of megakaryocytes and the JAK2 variant allele frequency (VAF) may be of discriminatory value {29192651; 30447300}.

Immunophenotype

Peripheral blood monocytes can be partitioned into three subsets according to their expression of CD14 and CD16: CD14+/CD16− classic monocytes, CD14+/CD16+ intermediate monocytes, and CD14-low/CD16+ non-classic monocytes. Patients with CMML demonstrate a characteristic increase in the fraction of CD14+/CD16− classic monocytes. Relative expansion of the classic monocytes subset (> 94%) has been reported to be sensitive and specific in distinguishing CMML from reactive monocytosis and other haematological neoplasms {25852055}. It has also been shown to have good sensitivity and specificity in discriminating between CMML and myelodysplastic neoplasms {28159734}, as well as other myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms {29982327}. Monocyte subsets can be altered in patients with autoimmune disorders and other inflammatory conditions as a result of expansion of the intermediate monocyte subset, thereby limiting the utility of monocyte partitioning in patients who have concomitant autoimmune diseases and/or systemic inflammatory conditions {31420531}. In such patients, quantification of the number of monocytes expressing slan (6-sulfo LacNAc), a carbohydrate modification of P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1, has been proposed, with a reduced slan+ non-classic monocyte subset (< 1.7% of total monocytes) being a feature of CMML {31413091}.

Commitment of normal CD34+ myeloid precursors in the bone marrow is associated with a progressive increase in CD64 expression and a concomitant reduction in CD34 and KIT (CD117) expression, while high-level HLA-DR expression is retained {26282340}. The CD34+ myeloid blast population in CMML exhibits frequent antigenic aberrancies, which include increased intensity of expression of CD13, CD34, KIT (CD117), and CD123, and decreased intensity of CD38 expression, as well as aberrant expression of the lineage fidelity markers CD2, CD5, CD7, CD19, and CD56 and asynchronous expression of mature myelomonocytic markers such as CD15 and CD64 {25354960}.

Immunophenotypic aberrations of mature monocytes usually include overexpression of CD56 and decreased expression of CD14, CD15, CD16, CD36, and CD64 {27413139; 26282340; 25852055}. Other abnormalities may include altered patterns of expression of the granulocytic maturation markers CD11b, CD13, and CD16 {26282340} and decreased neutrophil side scatter.

Immunohistochemistry can demonstrate increased numbers of monocytic cells in tissue sections, but its value is limited in comparison with flow cytometry. CD14, CD68R (as recognized by PGM1), and CD163 may be helpful, with CD14 having the highest reliability for identifying CMML {19242719; 17041567; 18371110}. CD34 and KIT (CD117) can be helpful for highlighting blasts and immature myeloid precursors.

Cytochemistry

Cytochemical staining of bone marrow aspirate smears or touch preparations using α-naphthyl acetate esterase or α-naphthyl butyrate esterase, alone or in combination with naphthol AS-D CAE, can be useful for highlighting monocytic cells and may aid in distinguishing such cells from dysplastic granulocytes and granulocytic precursors.

Mature plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferations in CMML

Nodules of mature plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) proliferations can be detected in the bone marrow and extramedullary sites, particularly the skin, in some patients with CMML. These proliferations typically consist of cells with moderate amounts of cytoplasm, variably irregular nuclear contours, and inconspicuous nucleoli {15105645}. Mature pDC proliferations in the bone marrow are more common in CMML harbouring activating RAS pathway mutations in which stem and progenitor cells are skewed towards pDC differentiation in a FLT3-independent manner {30894665}. Detection of mature pDC proliferations seems to correlate with an increased risk of transformation to AML {30894665}. Mature pDC proliferations have a characteristic immunophenotype, positive for CD123, TCF4, TCL1, CD4, CD43, and CD303; they are usually negative for CD56 {15105645; 18218851; 31261288}.

CMML in extramedullary sites

The histological features of extramedullary disease in patients with CMML are variable. Splenic infiltrates, which cause enlargement due to red pulp involvement, usually include trilineage elements with a predominance of myelomonocytic cells representing CMML components {30105755}. Skin infiltrates in patients with CMML present in different forms, as myelomonocytic proliferations, as mature pDC proliferations, as blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm, or as indeterminate dendritic cell tumour {22895265; 28885734; 32568837; 33416192}. In rare cases, cutaneous infiltrates may exhibit prominent granulomatous features {28442268}.

Differential diagnosis and special considerations

CML cases with the p190 BCR::ABL1 fusion gene can mimic CMML haematologically and morphologically. In view of the importance of distinguishing CML from CMML, RT-PCR and/or FISH analysis for the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene must be performed alongside conventional karyotyping, because of the possibility of rare fusion variants with cryptic cytogenetics that lack t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) on conventional G-band karyotyping.

A subset of patients develop CMML after exposure to cytotoxic therapy (chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy) for other malignancies. Such patients should be classified according to the criteria for myeloid neoplasms post cytotoxic therapy (see appropriate section).

NPM1 mutations have been reported in 2–5% of patients with CMML. In this edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours, such cases fulfil the diagnostic criteria for AML with NPM1 mutation (see appropriate section). In the context of CMML, this approach is supported by reports showing that CMML cases with NPM1 mutation, particularly those with a high mutation burden, tend to progress rapidly to AML {28707414; 25809997}.

Cases showing CMML features but fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for a myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase rearrangement should be classified according to those criteria (see appropriate section). Similarly, in patients with CMML who are found to have systemic mastocytosis, the case should be classified according to the criteria for systemic mastocytosis (see appropriate section). The development of systemic mastocytosis in this context is likely to be a consequence of clonal evolution and acquisition of an activating KIT mutation.

Patients who are diagnosed with a myeloproliferative neoplasm at presentation and whose disease shows evolution to a CMML-like phenotype should be regarded as having disease progression, and their disease should not be reclassified as CMML {23018876}. Patients who present with a myelodysplastic neoplasm and during the course of the disease subsequently develop the diagnostic criteria for CMML may be reclassified as having CMML {28611023}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Cytogenetics

The majority (~70%) of patients with CMML have a normal karyotype on conventional G-band karyotyping. The incidence of clonal cytogenetic abnormalities ranges from 23% to 34%, but no disease-specific alterations are described {25195656; 21109693; 24782398}. The most com­mon abnormalities include trisomy 8, chromosome 7 alterations (−7 or del(7q)), loss of the Y chromosome, and trisomy 21, whereas complex karyotype is less common {25679292; 24782398}. No disease-specific cytogenetic alterations are described. Younger patients (aged ≤ 65 years) have a lower frequency of trisomy 8 and Y chromosome loss {25679292}. Such and colleagues proposed three cytogenetic risk categories: low risk (normal karyotype or loss of the Y chromosome as a single anomaly), high risk (+8, chromosome 7 alterations, or complex karyotype defined as three or more abnormalities), and intermediate risk (all other abnormalities) {21109693}. Another study showed that patients with isolated +8 have outcomes similar to those in the intermediate-risk group {24782398}. Several CMML-specific prognostic systems currently in use include cytogenetic risk-stratification criteria {23372164; 27385790}.

Molecular pathology

The genomic landscape of CMML is characterized by mutations affecting the epigenetic regulation of transcription through DNA methylation (TET2, DNMT3A) and histone modification (ASXL1, EZH2), RNA splicing (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2), and signal transduction (NRAS, KRAS, CBL, PTPN11, JAK2), as well as transcription factors and nucleosome assembly (SETBP1, RUNX1) {26908133; 23335386; 23115106; 23690417; 23443343}. Mutations impacting one or more of these cellular processes have been reported in as many as 91.8% of patients with CMML and appear to persist in cases that respond to therapy with hypomethylating agents {26908133}. A recommended minimal gene set for CMML mutation profiling has been proposed (see #30750Table 2.13) {31723789}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for patients with CMML is generally poor, with a median overall survival time of 2–3 years and a 15–30% risk of transformation to AML {11806985; 27385790}. Although several validated CMML risk-stratification models exist and encompass differing variables and cut-off points for prognostic scoring, they rely on variables that reflect overlapping facets of the disease. Such variables include: cytopenias (anaemia, thrombocytopenia), proliferation (leukocytosis, monocytosis), left-shifted myelomonocytic differentiation (blasts in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow, circulating immature myeloid cells), cytogenetics (high-risk), and somatic mutations (ASXL1, RUNX1, NRAS, SETBP1) {33755092; 27385790; 26230957; 23690417; 24695057; 25195656}. The impact of somatic mutations may be context-dependent, as exemplified by ASXL1 mutations having different prognostic impacts depending on TET2 mutation status {31836856}. Additional parameters, including increased serum LDH level, splenomegaly, extramedullary disease, high B2M, elevated thymidine kinase, and lymphocytosis, as well as the number of detected somatic mutations, have been reported as important factors for predicting the course of the disease. Monitoring serum tryptase levels may be useful for monitoring clonal evolution towards systemic mastocytosis.

Allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is the only potentially curative treatment option for patients with CMML who are clinically eligible. In addition to bone marrow blasts and comorbidities, ASXL1 and/or NRAS mutations seem to be factors that may impact allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation outcomes in CMML {33755092}. Parameters that predict response to treatment with hypomethylating agents remain sparse. In particular, data on the predictive value of somatic mutations for response to hypomethylating agents are contradictory.
