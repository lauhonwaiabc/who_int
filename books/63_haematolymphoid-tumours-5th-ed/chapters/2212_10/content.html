2.2.1.2 Chronic myeloid leukaemia

Definition

Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm defined by the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene and characterized by neutrophilic granulocytosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In the chronic phase (CP), the neoplastic cells are mostly confined to the blood, bone marrow, spleen, and liver. In the blast phase (BP), the blasts can infiltrate any extra­medullary site, with a predilection for the spleen, liver, lymph nodes, skin, and soft tissues.

Clinical features

Most patients (> 95%) with CML are diagnosed in CP, which usually has an insidious onset {25783795; 9012696}. Some newly diagnosed cases can be asymptomatic and discovered when a blood count is performed as part of a routine medical examination. Common findings at presentation include splenomegaly, fatigue, malaise, weight loss, night sweats, and anaemia {25783795}.

Untreated, most cases of CML progress from CP to an advanced phase within a few years after diagnosis. Progression is characterized clinically by declining performance status; constitutional signs such as fever and weight loss; and symptoms related to severe anaemia, thrombocytopenia, increased white blood cell count, and splenic enlargement {22653972; 24729196}.

The natural history of untreated CML before the introduction of the targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) was biphasic or triphasic: an initial indolent CP followed by an accelerated phase (AP) and/or BP. The designation “AP” is less useful in the TKI era, as discussed below, and it is now called “high-risk chronic phase”, which may be identified at presentation or during the course of the disease while the patient is on TKI therapy.

Epidemiology

Worldwide, CML has an annual incidence­ of 1–2 cases per 100 000 population, with a slight male predominance. The annual incidence increases with age, from < 0.1 cases per 100 000 children to ≥ 2.5 cases per 100 000 elderly individuals {25783795; 25814090}. No significant ethnic or geographical variations have been reported, but a lower median age at onset has been observed in low- and middle-income countries {31808889}. Owing to the success of TKI therapy in reducing mortality rates (down to only 2–3% per year), the prevalence of CML has increased considerably {22294282}.

Etiology

The predisposing factors for CML are largely unknown. Acute radiation exposure has been implicated based on reports of increased incidence of CML among survivors of atomic bombings {5932020; 7880928}. Unlike with other myeloproliferative neoplasms, there is little, if any, inherited predisposition {18451307; 23094849}.

Pathogenesis

At diagnosis, 90–95% of cases of CML have the characteristic t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) reciprocal translocation that results in the so-called Philadelphia (Ph) chromosome. This translocation fuses sequences of the BCR gene on chromosome 22 with regions of ABL1 on chromosome 9. The remaining cases have either variant translocations that involve a third or even fourth chromosome (in addition to chromosomes 9 and 22) or a cryptic translocation of 9q34 and 22q11.2 that cannot be identified on routine cytogenetic analysis. In such cases, the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene is present and can be detected by FISH analysis and/or molecular diagnostics.

It is generally accepted that the increa­sed tyrosine kinase activity of BCR::ABL1 is necessary and sufficient to cause CML-CP through constitutive activation of proteins in several signal transduction pathways. Among the many pathways affected are the JAK/STAT (cell growth and survival), PI3K/AKT (cell growth, cell survival, and inhibition of apoptosis), and RAS/MEK/MAPK (activation of transcription factors, including NF-κB) pathways.

The understanding of the abnormal signalling in CML cells led to the design and synthesis of small molecules that target the tyrosine kinase activity of BCR::ABL1, of which imatinib was the first to be successfully used to treat CML. Imatinib competes with ATP for binding to the BCR::ABL1 kinase domain, thus preventing phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on its substrates. Interruption of the oncogenic signal in this way is effective for control of the disease, particularly when used early in CP. However, the emergence of subclones of leukaemic progenitor cells that have ABL1 point mutations that alter amino acids and prevent the binding of the inhibitor to the BCR::ABL1 kinase domain can lead to drug resistance {37567965; 35906470}. The second and third generations of TKIs (i.e. nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib, and ponatinib) can circumvent this form of drug failure in the presence of most kinase domain mutations. Recently, a new class of small molecules called “specifically targeting the ABL1 myristoyl pocket” (STAMP) inhibitors has been introduced for the treatment of CML, with the first of these being asciminib {31826340; 34407542}.

Transformation to BP is usually associated with clonal evolution, with 70–80% of cases demonstrating additional chromosomal aberrations (ACAs) besides the Ph chromosome {33990592}. Such ACAs include 3q26.2 rearrangements, monosomy 7, trisomy 8, isochromosome 17q, trisomy 19, trisomy 21, an additional Ph chromosome, and complex karyotypes {29296906; 27560111; 27006386}. Controversy remains as to the prognostic impact of ACAs present at the time of diagnosis in CP. There is general agreement that 3q26.2 (MECOM) rearrangements, monosomy 7, isochromosome 17q, and complex karyotypes (high-risk ACAs) are associated with an increased risk of progression to BP {22460758; 25931274; 26243778; 27006386; 29296906}. Others have also identified an increased risk of progression if trisomy 8, trisomy 19, trisomy 21, a second Ph chromosome, or involvement of 11q23 is present at diagnosis {26385387; 33616651}. A large study of > 1500 patients recently showed that at low (1–15%) blast levels, patients with a high-risk ACA showed an increased risk of death, as compared with patients without an ACA {32382082}. No effect was observed at blast levels of 20–30%. Each individual abnormality is rare at diagnosis, which adds to the difficulty in assessing their biological significance. In contrast, the acquisition of ACAs during the course of the disease is clearly associated with an increased risk of progression to BP.

Genes reported to be altered in the transformed stages include TP53, RB1, MYC, CDKN2A, NRAS, KRAS, RUNX1, TET2, CBL, ASXL1, IDH1, and IDH2, but their exact role in transformation is unknown {33990592; 21346257; 26297264}.

Histopathology

Chronic phase

The peripheral blood shows leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 12–1000 × 109/L, median: ~80 × 109/L) due primarily to neutrophils in various stages of maturation, with peaks in the proportions of myelocytes and segmented neutrophils {10674901}. Children often have a higher white blood cell count (median: ~250 × 109/L) than adults {25891192; 31330093}. Granulocytic dysplasia should be absent. Absolute basophilia and eosinophilia are common {25783795}. Absolute monocytosis may be present, but the proportion of monocytes is usually < 3%, except in rare cases with the p190 BCR::ABL1 isoform, which often mimic chronic mye­lomonocytic leukaemia. Blasts typically account for < 2% of the white blood cells. Platelet counts are normal or increased to > 1000 × 109/L, and marked thrombocytopenia is uncommon in CP {25783795}. Atypical presentations include marked thrombocytosis without leukocytosis that mimics essential thrombocythaemia or other types of myeloproliferative neoplasms {25025015; 29668550}.

The bone marrow is hypercellular, with marked granulocytic proliferation at all stages of differentiation. Dysplastic changes should be absent. Erythroid precursors are typically decreased. Megakaryocytes are increased in number in more than half of the cases, typically with altered morphology that includes small size and hyposegmented nuclei (referred to as dwarf megakaryocytes) {10674901}. Eosinophils and basophils are usually increased in number, and pseudo-Gaucher cells are common. These features are mirrored in trephine biopsy sections, in which a layer of immature granulocytes (5–10 cells in thickness) is common around bone trabeculae, in contrast to the normal thickness of 2–3 cells.

Reticulin fibrosis of the bone marrow may be seen in as many as 30% of cases at diagnosis, often in proportion to the number of megakaryocytes {14562117}. This finding does not correlate with response to imatinib {16019549}.

Blast phase

The criteria defining BP are listed in #33603Box 2.01 . Peripheral blood and bone marrow evaluation typically reveals increased blasts. In bone marrow biopsy specimens, sheets of blasts that occupy focal but substantial areas of the bone marrow (e.g. an entire intertrabecular space or more) can be considered presumptive evidence of BP even if the rest of the marrow shows CP.

Extramedullary BP may be an initial manifestation of transformation. Extramedullary blast proliferations are most common in the skin, lymph nodes, bone, and CNS, but they can occur anywhere; they may be of myeloid, lymphoid, or mixed-lineage immunophenotype {23171293; 3862425}.

In most cases, BP is myeloblastic in nature and consists of blasts that have myeloid morphology and express a myeloid immunophenotype. In such cases, blasts should constitute at least 20% of cells on differential counts in peripheral blood and/or bone marrow.

In a minority of cases, BP is lymphoblastic in nature and consists of blasts that express a precursor B immunophenotype (see below). The detection of bona fide lymphoblasts in the peripheral blood or bone marrow (even if < 10%) is generally considered consistent with the diagnosis of BP {25387987; 25916436; 33030302}.

In myeloid BP, blasts have strong, weak, or no myeloperoxidase activity but express one or more antigens associated with granulocytic, monocytic, megakaryocytic, and/or erythroid differentiation, such as CD33, CD13, CD14, CD11b, CD11c, KIT (CD117), CD15, CD41, CD61, and glycophorins A and C. However, in many myeloid BP cases, the blasts also express one or more lymphoid-related antigens {9872654; 18760473; 3165486}. A recent study showed a higher frequency of unusual blast types and immunophenotypes (e.g. basophil blasts or megakaryoblasts) after the introduction of TKI therapy, although this may be due in part to improved immunophenotyping technology {25511149}.

Most lymphoblastic BP blasts are of precursor-B origin and express TdT in addition to B cell–related antigens (e.g. CD19, CD10, CD79a, PAX5, and CD20), but a minority of lymphoblastic BP blasts are of T-cell origin and express T cell–related antigens (e.g. CD3, CD2, CD5, CD4, CD8, and CD7). Expression of one or more myeloid-related antigens by the blasts is common in both B- and T-cell–derived blast transformations. Bilineage cases (i.e. with distinct populations of myeloid and lymphoid blasts) also occur {23171293; 3165486}, and sequential lymphoblastic BP and myeloblastic BP have been reported {24371788}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular diagnostics

BCR::ABL1 exists in several different isoforms depending on the precise position of the t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) genomic breakpoints. The two most common isoforms are known as e13a2 and e14a2, i.e. BCR exon 13 and BCR exon 14, respectively, spliced to ABL1 exon 2. Historically, these two fusions were referred to as b2a2 and b3a2, with b2 (BCR exon 13) and b3 (BCR exon 14) corresponding to the second and third exons within the classically defined 5.8 kb major breakpoint cluster region within BCR.

Collectively, e13a2 and e14a2 BCR::ABL1 account for 98% of CML cases, with the majority of these expressing e14a2. About 10% of cases express both isoforms {30675008}. A large international survey of CML cases found small but significant differences in the relative prevalence of the two fusions according to age and sex, with e13a2 being more frequent in male patients and less common in the elderly {30675008}. Several studies have shown that patients carrying e13a2 BCR::ABL1 have inferior molecular responses at various time points after starting imatinib therapy than those carrying e14a2, but the underlying reason for this remains unclear; only one study to date has shown any impact on overall survival {19713230; 24837466; 26729897; 28466557; 28083711}, and it is possible that difference in the levels of the two transcripts is an artefact of the RT-PCR technology {34997766}.

The remaining 2% of patients carry atypical BCR::ABL1 transcripts that arise from BCR breakpoints outside the major breakpoint cluster region or downstream of ABL1 exon 2. The most common are e1a2, e19a2, e13a3, and e14a3, i.e. mRNA fusions of BCR exons 1 or 19 to ABL1 exon 2, or of BCR exons 13 or 14 to ABL1 exon 3. Several less common recurrent fusions have also been described (e.g. e6a2 {8822944}), as well as patient-specific variants with unique fusion exons and/or incorporation of exons from other genes as a result of complex genomic rearrangements {10233416}. Case reports have associated atypical BCR::ABL1 fusions with an unusually aggressive or benign clinical course, but these are probably subject to substantial ascertainment or publication bias, and in general atypical variants are not considered to be markers of prognosis. An exception appears to be the e1a2 transcript, which encodes p190 BCR::ABL1, the predominant isoform in Ph chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukaemia that is also seen in 1% of CML cases. Recent data on CML with p190 have indicated a relatively poor response to imatinib, possibly associated with frequent mutations in epigenetic modifier genes {19531657; 28708130; 33168949}.

The BCR::ABL1 transcript type in any given patient is stable over time because it is determined by the position of the t(9;22) genomic breakpoints. However, the sensitivity and specificity of qRT-PCR may occasionally cause some confusion. For example, rearrangements that give rise to e13a2 and e14a2 BCR::ABL1 have the capability to generate e1a2 mRNA by alternative splicing. Indeed, very low levels of e1a2 transcripts can be detected in most cases of CML before treatment but are generally believed to be of no clinical significance {8652835}. These cases should not be considered as p190 CML, because the level of e1a2 expression is typically 100–1000 times lower than that of the predominant isoform, and clonal evolution of p210 to p190 is at best exceedingly rare {8652835}.

Detection of resistance-associated mutations in the BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase domain is recommended in patients showing signs of resistance to TKI therapy {37794101; 32127639}. Several mutations between amino acids 217 and 514 have been identified in imatinib-resistant patients.

Cytogenetic analysis and FISH

A bone marrow aspirate is preferred for cytogenetic analysis at presentation and can be performed on the same sample taken for morphological analysis to diagnose disease phase. Cytogenetic evaluation identifies t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) and the Ph chromosome in nearly 85–90% of cases. A variant translocation is detected in 5–10% of cases. Rare cases of CML (~1%) may lack visible chromosomal alterations involving chromosome 9 or 22 on conventional karyotyping. A diagnosis of CML in such cases necessitates the use of FISH and/or molecular diagnostics.

Additional chromosomal abnormalities (ACAs) in the CML clone that are unrelated to the t(9;22) are detected in up to 30% of CP {22349721; 12651263; 27006386}. They may be acquired during the disease course and are identified at a higher frequency in BP. An extra Ph chromosome, isochromosome 17q, and gain of chromosome 8 or 19 are referred to as major route karyotypic abnormalities. Minor route ACAs may include balanced translocations or chromosomal gains/losses rarely observed in CML.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: peripheral blood neutrophilic leukocytosis; detection of the Ph chromosome and/or BCR::ABL1 by cytogenetic and/or other molecular genetic techniques.

Desirable: bone marrow evaluation at diagnosis or at suspected progression for assessment of disease phase; evaluation for ACAs.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

In the current era of TKI therapy, the most important prognostic indicator is response to treatment at the haematological, cyto­genetic, and molecular levels. Therapeutic milestones have been developed by the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) to categorize treatment response during treatment as “optimal”, “warning”, or “failure” {32127639}. At diagnosis, the risk scores based on clinical and haematological findings (Sokal {6584184} and EUTOS long-term survival [ELTS] {32601376}) are also useful, with low-risk patients responding to TKIs significantly better than high-risk patients and having a lower probability of dying of CML-related causes {23803709}. Overall, the complete cytogenetic response rate to imatinib is 70–90%, with 5-year progression-free and overall survival rates of 80–95%. Beyond cytogenetics, follow-up is recommended using a standardized quantitative RT-PCR approach adjusted to an international scale (IS) {37794101}. The achievement of a stable deep molecular response, defined as a 4-log reduction in tumour load as measured by qRT-PCR adjusted to the international scale (IS) (BCR::ABL1IS ≤ 0.01%) and sustained for at least a year, is a prerequisite for trials of treatment discontinuation. Several studies have confirmed that about 50% of patients can then remain off treatment indefinitely; this is known as treatment-free remission {20965785; 29735299; 33707652}. Major molecular responses (BCR::ABL1IS ≤ 0.1%) and deeper molecular responses (BCR::ABL1IS ≤ 0.01% {25652737}) are achieved faster and more frequently with second-generation TKIs, predicting that treatment-free remission will be achieved in more patients than was possible with imatinib. However, the progression-free survival rate is only marginally improved, and overall survival is the same irrespective of the TKI used as first-line therapy.

Recognition of disease progression (phases) is important for treatment and prognostic purposes, but the clinical and morphological boundaries between CP, AP (formerly), and BP are not always sharp, and the parameters used to define them have differed between investigators. Furthermore, gene expression studies of CP, AP, and BP suggest that progression of CP to AP and/or BP is more consistent with a two-step process, with new gene expression profiles occurring early in AP (or late in CP), before the accumulation of blasts and other features often used to define AP {16477019; 19837975}. Abrupt onset of BP, directly from CP, has long been recognized but is more frequent in the TKI era.

Recognition of CML phases was of substantial prognostic importance in the pre-TKI era when effective treatment without allogeneic transplant was not available, but the efficacy of TKIs has blurred the lines between the phases as defined traditionally {22228624}. For example, studies show that some newly diagnosed patients who initially present in AP may have similar outcomes when treated with TKIs as patients with newly dia­gnosed CP {24332214; 22460758}. A recent population-based study from the Kingdom of the Netherlands showed that patients with blast counts of 10–15%, defined as AP by previous WHO criteria, have an outcome equivalent to those with lower blast counts at diagnosis {28718971}. In clinical practice, newly presenting patients satisfying the previous definition of AP are treated just as patients in CP, with treatment modifications made on the basis of response or lack thereof. BP continues to have a very poor outcome, even with TKI therapy {22653972}.

In this edition, AP at diagnosis or during treatment has been omitted, with only CP and BP now recognized as CML phases. Instead, this new paradigm emphasizes high-risk features associated with CP progression and resistance to TKIs. When such features are detected, the qualifier “chronic phase with high-risk features” should be used, and the additional clarification “(formerly accelerated phase)” is permissible and may be considered in accordance with local practice. High-risk features include 10–19% blasts in bone marrow or peripheral blood, ≥ 20% basophils in the peripheral blood, and/or an additional major route ACA (see #33602Box 2.02). Some of these features are incorporated into risk scores such as Sokal and ELTS, but others are not. For instance, the poor prognostic impact of ACAs emerging on treatment is well recognized, but the role of individual ACAs at diagnosis (as discussed above) is less clear, as reflected by their variable inclusion in previous definitions of AP. Emerging data suggest that the presence of mutations other than BCR::ABL1 might also carry prognostic impact {31209280; 31073075; 27733357}. If and when validated, new genetic information can be incorporated into this simplified framework of CML phases and used to support management decisions.

In BP, the increase in blasts not only indicates a loss of response to therapy but also signifies that the disease has acquired characteristics of acute leukaemia. Some investigators and the majority of clinical trials of TKIs have used a threshold of ≥ 30% blasts in the peripheral blood and/or bone marrow to define BP. Data from the EUTOS registry showed that patients with blast percentages of 20–29% at diagnosis had an inferior survival, more similar to those with > 30% blasts than to those with < 20% blasts {31456269}.
