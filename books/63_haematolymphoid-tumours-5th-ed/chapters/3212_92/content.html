3.2.1.2 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Definition

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a clonal neoplasm of myeloid dendritic cells that have a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a and CD207 expression) and lack high-grade cytological features.

ICD-O coding

9751/1 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

9751/3 Langerhans cell histiocytosis, disseminated

 

ICD-11 coding

2B31.2 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: Langerhans cell granulomatosis; histiocytosis X; eosinophilic granuloma (solitary lesions); Hand–Schüller–Christian disease (multifocal); Letterer–Siwe disease (disseminated or visceral involvement).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Single-system LCH (SS-LCH): Occurs in one organ or one system and can be unifocal or multifocal. Commonly there is skin, pituitary, or bone involvement {11496348}.

Multisystem LCH (MS-LCH): Occurs in two or more organ systems. Commonly there is bone, skin, liver, and spleen involvement, with additional extraosseous involvement, including lymph node involvement, visceral mass lesions, and cystic pulmonary nodules. MS-LCH with bone marrow, spleen, or liver involvement is considered high-risk {23589673}.

In both SS-LCH and MS-LCH, the most commonly involved bones include the craniofacial bones, femur, spine, pelvis, and/or ribs.

Clinical features

SS-LCH in older children and adults manifests with painless to painful osteolytic lesions of the bone cortex and/or vertebra plana of the spine. Adults presenting with cutaneous SS-LCH may go on to develop systemic LCH in 20% of cases {34119604}. Multifocal involvement of a single system (i.e. skin or bone) may occur.

MS-LCH typically arises in neonates and infants, manifesting with multiple bony lesions, soft tissue masses, skin rash (vesicles/bullae in early infancy, dermatitis until late infancy, and nodules). MS-LCH includes fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly with jaundice, increased direct hyperbilirubinaemia, and hypoalbuminaemia. Active sites of disease are often PET-avid. Endocrine dysfunction and diabetes insipidus may occur with solitary CNS LCH involvement and in MS-LCH disease. Long-term consequences of LCH-associated neurodegeneration may manifest years later, including progressive ataxia, dysarthria, dysmetria, and learning and psychosocial/behavioural problems, with typical bilateral and symmetrical MRI signal intensity alterations, often in the cerebellum, brainstem, and basal ganglia {23109216; 30157397; 18571550; 30421536}.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence rate in the paediatric population is 5–9 new cases per 1 million population {18260117; 32106306}, and in adults it is 1–2 new cases per 1 million population. SS-LCH disease of the bone usually occurs in patients aged > 1 year, whereas MS-LCH disease with high-risk organ involvement often occurs in the youngest patients (aged < 1 year), with nearly all cases arising before the age of 5 years. There is a slight male predominance {18260117}. The disease is more common among individuals of European descent and Hispanic individuals. LCH can also be associated with Erdheim–Chester disease, either concomitantly or preceding it; in such cases, shared molecular alterations have been reported {26966089}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

MAPK pathway gene mutations are present in the majority (> 85%) of cases, with near-universal expression of phosphorylated ERK. The BRAF p.V600E mutations are the most prevalent and carry prognostic significance, especially in MS-LCH {30157397; 27382093}. MAP2K1 mutations are the second most frequent, and ARAF mutations are rare; ERBB3, NRAS, and KRAS mutations have been reported in adults {30157397}.

The cell of origin of LCH is closer to a bone marrow–derived myeloid dendritic cell than to an epidermal Langerhans cell, and MAPK pathway activation plays a central role in LCH pathogenesis {20220088; 25646268; 28566492; 28512190; 32750121; 30157397; 31899802; 34620874; 33901419}.

A misguided myeloid differentiation model has been proposed, in which activating MAPK mutations at a pluripotent haematopoietic, tissue-restricted, or local precursor level give rise to high-risk multisystem, multifocal low-risk, or unifocal LCH, respectively {25646268; 30157397}. These mutations, especially BRAF p.V006E, seem to confer an advantage for tissue site accumulation via disrupted cell migration, apoptosis inhibition, and oncogene-induced senescence, rather than proliferation {29263218; 33958797}. Late permanent tissue consequences appear related to clonal myeloid-derived histiocytes inciting inflammation and fibrosis at the site (i.e. biliary cirrhosis in the liver, neurodegeneration/gliosis in the brain in older children) and/or activating a hyperinflammatory pathway, with macrophage activation (in the youngest patients with MS-LCH) {11910507; 32092141}. In adults, SS-LCH of the lung is almost universally related to smoking, and MAPK mutations are prevalent {31806714}; a new murine model suggests a possible link between smoking and MAPK mutation in the pathogenesis of LCH {31961828}. Rare familial cases have also been reported {11452942}.

Rare cases of LCH occur in association with or follow follicular lymphoma or lymphoblastic leukaemia (B or T lineage). This is considered a transdifferentiation phenomenon and may be associated with more aggressive behaviour {23759932; 15925822; 19642834; 20551822}. A small subset of LCH cases, without a known prior lymphoma, have shown clonal IGH, IGK, and/or TR gene rearrangements {20551822}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not clinically relevant

Histopathology

LCH is characterized by an accumulation of large (10–25 µm), round to oval histiocytes with a complex nuclear contour that often assumes a nuclear groove, and moderately abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Nuclear atypia is minimal, and mitotic activity is variable but without atypical forms. The inflammatory milieu contains eosinophils, lymphocytes, and multinucleated histiocytes (both LCH-type and osteoclastic-type, especially in bone), with rarer neutrophils and plasma cells. Central necrosis is occasionally found and is of no prognostic significance. In active lesions, LCH cells predominate, often with eosinophils. In late lesions, diagnostic cells are decreased and fibrosis is increased.

Pathological descriptions of the neurodegenerative manifestations in LCH are limited {15705614; 29624648}. Reports describe inflammatory infiltrates (either foamy cell collections containing CD1a+ cells or perivascular infiltrates of mononuclear cells carrying the BRAF p.V600E mutation), and there may be associated neurodegeneration or demyelination {15705614; 29624648}.

In patients with a previous diagnosis of LCH, tissue sampling demonstrating only histiocytes with a monocyte–macrophage immunophenotype should not be considered burnt-out if BRAF p.V600E mutations are detected in tissue cells or blood, even in the absence of phenotypic LCH cells, as documented in the bone marrow {28194436} and CNS {29624648}.

Electron microscopy

The diagnostic ultrastructural hallmark is the Birbeck granule, which is a 200–400 nm long and 33 nm wide cytoplasmic structure with a tennis-racket shape and a zipper-like appearance [[Birbeck MS, Breathnach AS, Everall JD. An electron microscope study of basal melanocytes and high-level clear cells (Langerhans cells) in vitiligo. J Invest Dermatol. 1961 Jul;37(1):51–64. Doi:10.1038/jid.1961.80.]].

Immunohistochemistry

LCH cells express CD1a (membranous), S100 (nuclear and cytoplasmic), CD207 (langerin; granular cytoplasmic), and CD68 (Golgi dot-like staining). CD207 (langerin) immunohistochemical stain is the surrogate for Birbeck granules {15630529; 26461144}. CD45 expression is low. The Ki-67 proliferation index is variable (typically < 10% by double-staining) {12121233; 29263218}. Downstream markers of MAPK activation include cyclin D1 and phosphorylated ERK in LCH cells {28622183}.

Differential diagnosis

It is important to distinguish clonal histiocytic lesions from accumulations of reactive histiocytes in other conditions. LCH cells should be distinguished from the inflammatory CD1a+ dendritic cells that have branching morphology. The morphology, immunophenotype, and correct pattern/distribution of involvement at each site or organ system are all important considerations {26461144}. The differential diagnosis is site-dependent and includes other histiocytic neoplasms, reactive lesions (such as dermatopathic lymphadenopathy for lymph node), osteomyelitis and chondroblastoma (for bone), and other reactive and neoplastic disorders containing high numbers of dendritic/Langerhans cells.

 

Cytology

Cytological diagnosis of histiocytic neoplasms should be approached with caution, particularly in the lymph node, because the architecture or pattern of involvement is critical for distinguishing a clonal histiocytic disorder from reactive paracortical histiocytic accumulations. In specific settings, the assessment of cytological samples by FNA may represent a useful additional tool when integrated with other appropriate investigations, including immunostaining and molecular investigations, and interpreted in the correct clinical and radiographic setting. The longitudinal nuclear grooves of the LCH cells are best appreciated with Pap staining {28407623}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Properly validated mutation-specific immunohistochemistry (anti-BRAF VE1 antibody clone) may be used in conjunction with sensitive nucleic acid–based assays for detecting BRAF p.V600E {30923995; 28219109}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: large, round to oval histiocytes with grooved to convoluted nuclei and a Langerhans cell phenotype (CD1a+, CD207+) in the correct histological/anatomical context.

Desirable: mutation analysis in the MAPK pathway, with sensitive testing for low-frequency allele detection.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Diagnostic and staging evaluation should document the extent of systemic disease involvement after tissue biopsy. Radiographic skeletal survey, 99mTc bone scan, and brain MRI with gadolinium are used in diagnostic staging, but FDG PET-CT imaging is increasingly used for the detection of active lesions, especially in follow-up {25827831; 33442960; 32106306}. SS-LCH and MS-LCH may be distinguished, to assist in the choice of treatment {34620874; 33901419}.

The prognosis is largely dependent on the stage at presentation. SS-LCH has a 99% survival rate, although a subset of patients may subsequently develop permanent consequences or MS-LCH, including hepatic failure and hypothalamic pituitary involvement {11496348}. MS-LCH with risk-organ involvement has a variable course, with only about 60% of patients achieving no evidence of disease after 1 year on standard LCH-III therapy. In MS-LCH without high-risk organ involvement, there is still about a 37% relapse/reactivation rate after 1 year of standard therapy with vinblastine and prednisone {23589673}.

Although there has been no formal recommendation set forth for upfront molecular testing, emerging evidence shows that BRAF p.V600E is highly associated with an increased risk of relapse, development of MS-LCH {24638167; 27382093}, and adverse clinical outcomes. Irreversible clinical consequences such as diabetes insipidus and neurodegenerative complications may develop, especially in the case of involvement of the base of the skull vault and craniofacial bones {27382093; 23109216}. Therapy targeted at BRAF p.V600E has shown efficacy as a single agent, and its role in treatment is being further explored {31513482; 30718231; 32092141; 32985015}.
