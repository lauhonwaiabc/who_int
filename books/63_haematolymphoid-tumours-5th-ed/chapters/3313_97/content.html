3.3.1.3 Erdheim-Chester disease

Definition

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a neoplasm characterized by an accumulation of mature histiocytes in many different organs and associated with inflammation and fibrosis.

ICD-O coding

9749/3 Erdheim–Chester disease

ICD-11 coding

2B31.Y & XH1VJ3 Other specified histiocytic or dendritic cell neoplasms & Erdheim–Chester disease

Related terminology

Not recommended: lipogranulomatosis; lipoid granulomatosis; polyostotic sclerosing histiocytosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ECD is a multisystem disease and can affect virtually any organ, although the lymph nodes, spleen, and liver are generally spared {32187362; 29396850; 33901419}. Bilateral symmetrical involvement of the long bones is characteristic and detected in 80–95% of cases. Perinephric (58%) and periaortic (46%) infiltrations are also frequent. Pituitary and neurological involvement occurs in 20–50% of cases. Infiltration of the lungs, heart, serosa, and skin (xanthelasma or papular lesions) can also occur.

Clinical features

ECD may be asymptomatic, but more often patients present with systemic symptoms such as fatigue or fever, and/or with site-related symptoms, most of which are considered to be related to the inflammation and fibrosis associated with histiocytic infiltration of the tissues. Bone pain (in 37–50% of cases), exophthalmos (in 25%), xanthelasma (in 19–26%), and diabetes insipidus (in 28%) are frequently reported {32187362; 26966089}. CNS disease manifests either with a tumour mass or with neurodegenerative-type symptoms. Renal dysfunction is usually secondary to ureteric compression. Rare cases manifest with cardiac tamponade or a painful abdominal mass {31423997; 34020934}.

About 3–15% of ECD cases are associated with different myeloid neoplasms, including chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia. ECD can be associated with Langerhans cell histiocytosis in > 10% of cases (known as mixed histiocytosis) {24894769; 32969291}, with Rosai–Dorfman disease {31123032}, or with autoimmune-related symptoms {30923093}.

Imaging

Symmetrical osteosclerosis of the leg bones is highly suggestive of ECD. It is best visualized by PET with bilateral uptake of FDG but can also be seen on CT or MRI, whereas it is often missed on plain X-rays. Bilateral perinephric infiltration, responsible for a hairy-kidney aspect on CT, is frequent and sometimes associated with hydronephrosis. Imaging may also show a coated aorta, corresponding to a thickening of soft parts around the abdominal or thoracic aorta. Detection of mass-like infiltration of the right atrium on MRI (in 33% of cases) or sclerosis of sinuses of the face on CT are also suggestive of ECD. CT of the chest may show pleural and septal thickening, as well as serous effusions.

Epidemiology

ECD is a rare but probably underdiagnosed disease. The median age at diagnosis is 55 years, and the M:F ratio is 3:1. ECD is reported as very rare in children < 15 years of age {28214412}. However, cases may be misdiagnosed as disseminated juvenile xanthogranuloma due to substantially overlapping features.

Etiology

ECD is a clonal neoplastic disorder without a known genetic predisposition {22879539; 26566875; 31768065}. An underlying clonal haematopoiesis has been described in 42% of cases in a reported series {33067622; 31221777}, which is significantly more frequent than in the age-matched general population.

Pathogenesis

Most cases have a gain-of-function somatic alteration activating the MAPK cell signalling pathway. BRAF p.V600E mutations are present in 50–60% of cases {22879539}. Patients without BRAF p.V600E mutations frequently have other mutations (also activating the MAPK pathway) in BRAF, ARAF, NRAS, KRAS, or MAP2K1 {26566875}. PIK3CA may also be mutated {25150293}.

The MAPK pathway gene mutations may also be present within bone marrow progenitors {28566492; 28679734}, and some cases of ECD can result from tissue accumulation of histiocytes derived from mutated bone marrow progenitors {28566492}.

A model with sequential acquisition of driver mutations leading to the development of myeloid neoplasms and ECD from underlying clonal haematopoiesis has been proposed {28566492; 28679734; 31624111}. However, some ECD cases can occur in the absence of clonal haematopoiesis {33067622}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most of the samples obtained for diagnosis correspond to biopsies of perirenal fat, bone, or skin. They are usually firm and sclerotic.

Histopathology

Lesional tissue demonstrates infiltration of foamy, lipid-laden, and/or small mononuclear histiocytes associated with variable proportions of Touton giant cells, small lymphocytes, plasma cells, and/or neutrophils {26966089; 29192649}. The histology can be indistinguishable from that of xanthogranuloma. Fibrosis is usually present and sometimes predominant, leading to interpretation of the lesion as a reactive fibroinflammatory process. Admixed Langerhans cell histiocytosis may be present in the same biopsy. Some cases may show at least partial morphological and immunophenotypic overlap with Rosai–Dorfman disease or with reticulohistiocytosis {31123032; 31955466}.

Immunohistochemistry

ECD histiocytes have an immunohistochemical profile similar to that of xanthogranuloma, being positive for CD163, CD68, CD14, and CD4, and negative for CD1a and CD207. They may also be positive for factor XIIIa, fascin, and (less commonly) S100. Expression of phosphorylated ERK is frequent, and diffuse strong cytoplasmic staining with VE1 monoclonal antibody may signal BRAF p.V600E mutation, but this should always be confirmed with molecular analysis.

 

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of a corroborating genomic alteration may aid in diagnosis and treatment {33901419}.

The allele frequency of BRAF p.V600E mutations may be < 5% in a quarter of the cases and thus not detectable by less sensitive molecular methods {30262559}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: collections of bland foamy histiocytes in the appropriate clinical and radiological context.

Desirable: mutation analysis of the MAPK pathway.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis depends on the site and extent of involvement. CNS involvement is a poor prognostic factor {29396850}. Baseline assessment includes CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis; FDG PET of the entire body; MRI of the brain, with gadolinium; and cardiac MRI. Most patients require systemic therapy. Patients with ECD obtain clinical benefit with interferon alfa-2a or pegylated interferon alfa {32187362; 33901419}. However, in recent years, MAPK pathway alterations have been recognized, and targeted therapies with kinase inhibitors are frequently used {25422482; 28667012}. Recent studies showed a 43–100% response rate, and almost no disease progression, with the use of BRAF and/or MEK inhibitors {30867592; 29188284}.
