2.2.1.4 Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia

Definition

Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (CEL) is a mye­loproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by an autonomous, clonal proliferation of eosinophil precursors, resulting in persistent eosinophilia in the blood and bone marrow.

ICD-O coding

9964/3 Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia

ICD-11 coding

2A20.3 Chronic eosinophilic leukaemia

Related terminology

Acceptable: chronic eosinophilic leukaemia NOS.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

CEL is a systemic multiorgan disorder. The peripheral blood and bone marrow are always involved; splenic and hepatic involvement is common. Tissue infiltration by eosinophils and the release of cytokines and humoral factors from the eosinophil granules may lead to tissue damage in a number of organs, particularly the heart, lungs, CNS, skin, and gastrointestinal tract {22473587; 32243620}.

Clinical features

Eosinophilia is sometimes detected incidentally in patients who are otherwise asymptomatic. In other cases, patients experience constitutional symptoms such as weight loss, night sweats, and fever, as well as a variety of other symptoms including fatigue, cough, angio-oedema, muscle pain, pruritus, and diarrhoea. The most serious clinical findings relate to endomyocarditis, which results in endomyocardial fibrosis and ensuing restrictive cardiomyopathy. Scarring of the mitral and tricuspid valves leads to valvular regurgitation and the formation of intracardiac thrombi, which can embolize to the brain or elsewhere. Cardiac failure can occur. Peripheral neuropathy, CNS dysfunction, and pulmonary symptoms due to lung infiltration may also ensue.

Hypereosinophilia (HE) is defined as peripheral blood eosinophilia > 1.5 × 109/L on at least two occasions over an interval of at least 4 weeks, but it is recognized that some cases with life-threatening organ damage may require urgent therapy without waiting for confirmation of sustained eosinophilia {22460074; 28028030}.

Epidemiology

Because of the rarity of CEL and the difficulty in distinguishing it from non-clonal HE, the true incidence is unknown. CEL is more common in men, with a median age of presentation in the seventh decade of life {27174585; 22473587; 27174585; 32243620}. In one study, only 1.2% of cases with peripheral blood eosinophilia fulfilled the criteria for CEL {32243620}.

Etiology

No known risk factors or genetic predisposition

Pathogenesis

For most cases, the specific driver of HE is unclear {30656986; 32243620}. Some cases have oncogenic mutations in genes encoding signal transduction components, but these are frequently seen in cases without pronounced eosinophilia; for example, JAK2 p.V617F and KIT p.D816V are seen in 3–4% of cases with HE {26017288} but are much more common in MPNs and systemic mastocytosis, respectively. JAK2 exon 13 indels have been shown to activate the β cytokine receptor common chain in the absence of ligand, with the maximum effect observed for IL-5R, consistent with a clinical phenotype of eosinophilia with or without polycythaemia vera {31697804}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Peripheral blood

The peripheral blood smear shows HE (eosinophils > 1.5 × 109/L) and usually leukocytosis, although the latter is not required to diagnose CEL. Eosinophils are mostly mature but usually show a range of abnormal morphological features including abnormal granulation (hypogranulation or uneven granulation), cytoplasmic vacuoles, abnormal nuclear lobation (non-lobated or multilobated), or increased size. Of note, isolated cytological abnormalities of eosinophils are not specific to CEL or related neoplastic conditions and can also be seen in reactive conditions. However, the absence of eosinophil dysplasia generally favours reactive eosinophilia {27174585}. Normocytic or macrocytic anaemia is present in the majority of patients and thrombocytopenia is present in nearly half of patients {22473587; 27174585; 33367563}. Rare cases show increased platelets on peripheral blood smears. Some cases show mild monocytosis but do not meet all the criteria for chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia.

Bone marrow

Bone marrow smears show an elevated myeloid-to-erythroid ratio with increased eosinophils. Morphological abnormalities in lineages beyond eosinophils are present {28495918; 32243620; 33367563}, with hypercellularity and megakaryocytic dysplasia being the most common findings. Megakaryocytes predominantly show changes associated with myelodysplastic neoplasia, such as a small size with hypolobated/non-lobated nuclei or separated nuclear lobes. Granulocytic dysplasia (hypogranular, hypolobated), erythroid dysplasia (including ring sideroblasts), and significant marrow fibrosis (≥ MF-2) are also present in some cases. Charcot–Leyden crystals may be present, and eosinophils show morphological abnormalities similar to those described in the blood. The bone marrow core biopsy shows a hypercellular marrow with eosinophilic infiltrates invariably present. CEL should not be diagnosed if criteria for another specific myeloid neoplasm are fulfilled; for example, cases that fulfil the criteria for chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia would be classified as chronic mye­lomonocytic leukaemia with eosinophilia, and cases with ≥ 20% myeloblasts in the blood or bone marrow should be considered as acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with eosinophilia.

Recent studies have demonstrated that most CEL cases have abnormal marrow morphology, and such a finding is a strong indicator of clonality {28495918; 32243620}. This is the basis for adding abnormal bone marrow morphology as a requirement for a diagnosis of CEL. Notwithstanding, in rare cases where evidence of clonality in a patient with HE is detected without definitive morphological abnormalities, a diagnosis of CEL may still be rendered if differential diagnostic considerations are excluded (see below).

Additionally, in the previous edition, criteria allowed for an excess of blasts (≥ 2% in peripheral blood or 5–19% in bone marrow) as an alternative to clonality; however, subsequent studies have found that only a very small proportion of cases were diagnosed with CEL on this basis. Given the advances in genomics and the new requirement for abnormal bone marrow morphology, an excess of blasts as a criterion for CEL is no longer required. This definition recognizes the fact that CEL is a poor-prognosis disorder and thus an indication for allogeneic stem cell transplantation {28028030}.

Differential diagnosis

A diagnosis of CEL requires the integration of morphology, genetics, clinical features, and other laboratory parameters. The distinction between neoplastic HE (which includes CEL and other myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms), reactive HE, and other forms of HE for which the underlying pathogenesis is unknown may be challenging, and the working diagnosis may change as new information emerges {22460074; 28028030; 30105844}. Cases that do not fulfil the criteria for neoplastic HE or reactive HE are diagnosed as hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) if organ damage is present and HE of uncertain significance in the absence of organ damage. Patients with HES and HE of uncertain significance should be regularly monitored, because evidence may subsequently emerge that the condition is leukaemic in nature and treatment may be necessary; this may be particularly important for cases with suggestive evidence for clonality or abnormal bone marrow morphology. Identification of the presence, pattern, degree, and extent of eosinophil-mediated tissue damage may be challenging, and although it is not required for a diagnosis of CEL, it is very important for case management {33714638}.

Exclusion of secondary causes should consider parasitic infections, drug reactions, allergic disorders, gastrointestinal disease, non-haematological malignancies, and other conditions {28112388; 30105844}. Flow cytometric analysis to demonstrate abnormal T-cell populations (often sCD3−/CD4+) may identify lymphocytic variant HES, in which cytokine release from a clonal circulating T-cell population with a T helper 2 (Th2) profile results in secondary non-clonal eosinophilia {10511609; 32157815; 33545400}. Conversely, the demonstration of immunophenotypic aberrancy in the blast population favours the diagnosis of neoplastic HE. Patients with clonal/neoplastic HE frequently present with constitutional symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and night sweats, whereas symptoms related to eosinophilic activation such as allergy, urticaria, oedema, asthma, and eosinophil-mediated organ injury are less frequently seen. Patients with CEL are older than those with HES and have higher leukocyte and eosinophil counts and a higher eosinophil percentage {30105844; 32243620}.

Eosinophilia at HE levels may be present in other myeloid neoplasms. Some cases with a very high eosinophil percentage and clinical manifestations of eosinophilic infiltration may be more appropriately considered as CEL even if the criteria for another myeloid neoplasm are technically fulfilled; however, the possibility of coexisting disorders should also be considered. Nonetheless, cytogenetic and molecular studies should be used to exclude chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), AML with CBFB::MYH11, and myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (MLN-TK). Tests for MLN-TK should not be limited to FIP1L1::PDGFRA but should cover other PDGFRA fusions, ETV6::ABL1, and fusions involving PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, or FLT3. Eosinophilia may also be secondary to T-cell lymphoma, classic Hodgkin lymphoma, and B-lymphoblastic leukaemia with IGH::IL3. The finding of KIT p.D816V or JAK2 variants should prompt consideration of the possibility of mastocytosis or MPN, respectively {26017288}. Markedly elevated serum tryptase levels also point to the possibility of mastocytosis.

Demonstration of clonality by cytogenetic or molecular studies is required but is not sufficient for a diagnosis of CEL. A major caveat is that CEL tends to occur in elderly patients who may have clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential rather than a myeloid neoplasm. In general, mutations with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of < 2% should not be used to support clonality in the context of a diagnosis of CEL. Abnormalities in DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 should be considered with caution, and age-related loss of the Y chromosome as the sole cytogenetic abnormality should not be considered as clear evidence of clonality in the absence of supporting mutation data. Mutations in other genes associated with myeloid neoplasms tend to be more specific indicators of a neoplastic process, particularly those with a higher VAF (e.g. > 10%) and in combination with other mutations {27174585; 30656986; 33367563}, but there is no definitive boundary that distinguishes between clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential and myeloid neoplasia. For a diagnosis of CEL this uncertainty is mitigated by the additional requirement of abnormal marrow morphology consistent with a myeloid neoplasm.

 

Cytology

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A combination of approaches is required, to exclude other haematological neoplasms associated with fusion genes and other defined abnormalities and to provide evidence for clonality. Standard approaches include chromosome banding, FISH, RT-PCR, and gene panels, but broad genomic screening by RNA sequencing or whole-genome sequencing are expected to become more mainstream approaches. Although the direct demonstration of clonal eosinophils is not feasible on a routine basis, clonality may be inferred by the finding of somatic mutations in myeloid neoplasia–associated genes, or by the finding of a clonal cytogenetic abnormality associated with myeloid neoplasia. Molecular analysis should include KIT p.D816V, JAK2 p.V617F, JAK2 exon 13 indels, and STAT5B p.N642H, each of which are seen in 1–4% of cases with HE {26017288; 30656986; 30573779; 31697804}. Other evidence for clonality may be provided by somatic variants in other genes, including but not limited to DNMT3A, ASXL1, TET2, EZH2, SRSF2, SETBP1, and CBL {27174585; 30656986; 32243620}; however, consideration needs to be given to the pathogenicity of any variant, as well as the possibility of clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, especially in elderly patients. In many cases, genetic analysis will suggest or demonstrate an alternative diagnosis to CEL.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: HE, defined as peripheral blood eosinophilia > 1.5 × 109/L on at least two occasions over an interval of at least 4 weeks; evidence of clonality; abnormal bone marrow morphology; not meeting diagnostic criteria for other myeloid or lymphoid neoplasms.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of CEL is generally poor. The median survival time is about 2 years, with death occurring from infection, bleeding, or disease-related organ failure. Transformation to AML may occur {22473587; 28495918; 30105844; 32243620}. Individuals with STAT5B p.N642H as the sole abnormality or in combination with SF3B1 seem to have a markedly longer overall survival (median: 65 months) than those with STAT5B p.N642H combined with other additional somatic mutations (median survival time: 14 months) {30573779}. A population-based study has suggested that overall survival may not be as poor as that suggested from small case series {32533865}; however, such studies suffer from a lack of clear diagnostic criteria, which makes them difficult to interpret.

Response to imatinib is uncommon but has been reported {22473587}. One study noted that HE in association with KIT p.M541L responded to imatinib {25015329}; however, this variant is a recognized polymorphism, and the robustness of the association has been disputed {29393093}. Similarly, point mutations in PDGFRA have been identified in cases of unexplained HE, some of which were sensitive to imatinib in model systems {21224473}, but no clinical value of PDGFRA mutation screening has emerged. Other factors such as an abnormal karyotype, atypical megakaryocytes, thrombocytopenia, and bone marrow fibrosis have been reported as potential adverse prognostic markers {28495918; 32243620}, but larger case series are needed to identify robust predictive markers for this very rare disorder.
