2.4.1.3 Myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition

Definition

Myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition include acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDSs), myeloproliferative neoplasms, and myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms that arise in individuals with genetic conditions associated with increased risk of myeloid malignancies.

ICD-O coding

Code as condition.

ICD-11 coding

Code as condition.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

See #31696Table 2.21 . In addition, there are several conditions that have been occasionally associated with myeloid neoplasms (see #31697Table 2.22), as well as emerging disorders that require additional research (see #31698Table 2.23).

Localization

These conditions manifest in the blood and bone marrow. Depending on the syndrome, benign and/or malignant manifestations may affect other organs. See #31696Table 2.21 , #31697Table 2.22 , and #31698Table 2.23 .

Clinical features

This heterogeneous group of diseases is caused by deleterious germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants {25741868} in genes that affect critical biological pathways leading to varying abnormalities of benign, malignant, haematopoietic, or/and non-haematopoietic disorders.

Because of the increasing number of relevant syndromes, it is beyond the scope of this section to provide detailed descriptions of each of them; they are summarized in #31696Table 2.21 , #31697Table 2.22 , and #31698Table 2.23 . Below, details are provided on CEBPA-associated familial AML and myeloid leukaemia predisposition associated with thrombocytopenia, to highlight these as common prototypes of myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition.

CEBPA-associated familial AML

In approximately 10% of patients with favourable-prognosis biallelic CEBPA-mutant AML, one of those mutated alleles is due to a germline mutation (MIM number: 116897), typically the 5′ mutation {28637622}, although rare 3′ germline mutations have been described {26721895}. The penetrance of germline 5′-end CEBPA mutations is virtually 100% {28637622}, in contrast to germline 3′-end mutations, which show a lower penetrance of leukaemia development. Because of the high penetrance of leukaemia development in those with 5′-end germline mutations, some have argued for consideration of pre-emptive allogeneic stem cell transplantation {31170028}. When outcomes for patients with biallelic CEBPA-mutant AML have been compared, survival appears to be longer for those with a germline mutation than for those with two acquired mutations {28637622}. The presence of the acquired CEBPA mutation serves as a molecular marker of AML, and these acquired 3′ mutations are distinct in AML that re-emerges in germline CEBPA-mutation carriers, suggesting that these are independent second and even third primary AML clones rather than recurrences of the original clone {26162409}. Therefore, it would appear that if individuals with germline CEBPA mutations develop AML and are treated only with chemotherapy, they are at risk in subsequent years of developing independent AML clones, because their germline mutation remains. Some have used this observation to argue for allogeneic stem cell transplantation in first remission for these patients.

Myeloid leukaemia predisposition associated with thrombocytopenia

Currently, there are three genes in which heterozygous deleterious variants confer lifelong thrombocytopenia with variable degrees of platelet dysfunction as well as risk for myeloid malignancies: RUNX1 (MIM numbers: 151385, 601399), ETV6 (MIM numbers: 600618, 616216), and ANKRD26 (MIM numbers: 188000, 610855). Among individuals with these mutations, those with germline ANKRD26 mutations generally have the lowest platelet counts, and all show phenotypic variability even within the same family. Germline RUNX1 mutations mainly cause myeloid malignancies, and to a lesser extent also cause T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma and B-cell malignancies {31648317; 32165484}. Germline ETV6 mutations cause B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma mainly, and myeloid malignancies to a lesser extent {28555414}. Germline ANKRD26 mutations have been associated only with myeloid malignancies to date {29927566}.

 

Epidemiology

With the exception of Down syndrome (prevalence: 1 in 319–1000 live births), Noonan syndrome (~1 in 2000 live births), and neurofibromatosis type 1 (1 in 3000 live births), syndromes predisposing to myeloid neoplasms are rare individually, but in aggregate they contribute to a growing subset of patients with myeloid neoplasms. About 5–10% of children with AML have Down syndrome, and increasing attention to the diagnosis of germline predisposition alleles has led to the recognition that germline P/LP alleles in SAMD9, SAMD9L, and GATA2 are the most frequent causes of MDS in children and adolescents, especially those with monosomy 7 {33038986}. In adults aged 18–40 years, germline mutations in DNA repair genes and telomere biology disorders cause MDS in 19% of cases and aplastic anaemia in 15% of cases {33510405}. Among adults diagnosed with AML, germline predisposition alleles are found in ≥ 5% of individuals {34482403}. Recognizing that germline DDX41 mutations typically cause MDS in elderly patients {34723452}, we are gaining the appreciation that germline mutations cause myeloid malignancies throughout the age spectrum, with age at presentation signalling the biological pathway involved.

Pathogenesis

In general, individuals with myeloid neoplasms with germline predisposition acquire additional somatic genetic events in the haematopoietic system, eventually leading to myeloid neoplasms. Notably, not all acquired anomalies are associated with transformation (see #31700Table 2.24).

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The histopathology of myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition is not pathognomonic of any of the specific subtypes {30573048}. The ultimate diagnosis is provided by the clinicopathological presentation combined with the molecular findings. The somatic mutation spectrum and cytogenetic clonal abnormalities may suggest the presence of an underlying syndrome; for example, the presence of an isochromosome 7 may suggest Shwachman–Diamond syndrome (see #31700Table 2.24).

Patients may present with severe aplastic anaemia, MDS/AML, or normal bone marrow findings, but certain subtypes have characteristic morphological findings that can suggest germline predisposition. Many entities are associated with a bone marrow that is hypocellular for age with abnormal-appearing megakaryocytes. Typical morphological features are illustrated in the figures. For example, familial platelet disorder with associated myeloid malignancy shows dysmegakaryopoiesis that is characterized by > 10% of the megakaryocytes having a small size and nuclear hypolobation {28659335; 30600763; 32238878}. Severe congenital neutropenia caused by pathogenic variants of ELANE shows increased peritrabecular myeloid precursor expansion, promyelocyte arrest with decreased to absent mature neutrophils, and increased eosinophils {20727024}. Shwachman–Diamond syndrome shows hypocellularity with incomplete nuclear segmentation and variable hypogranulation of neutrophils {31879230}, and variable degrees of low-level megakaryocytic and (less commonly) erythroid dysplasia. Fanconi anaemia shows hypocellularity with low-level baseline dysplasias. RASopathies with leukocytosis (as seen in some cases of Noonan syndrome) show hypercellular marrow with myeloid hyperplasia. Patients with GATA2-related MDS have hypocellular marrow with increased dysplastic megakaryocytes, characterized by separated and peripheralized nuclear lobes as well as frequent reticulin fibrosis, whereas asymptomatic family members with GATA2 mutation have normal marrow {25359990}. Some inherited bone marrow failure syndromes and germline predispositions without onset of MDS can mimic bone marrow findings of childhood MDS with low blasts. In such cases, the diagnosis of myeloid neoplasm should be made on the basis of increased blasts (≥ 10%) or MDS-associated cytogenetic changes (e.g. monosomy 7 or complex karyotype in a patient with Fanconi anaemia) {34196171}.

Immunophenotype

Flow cytometry analysis of MDS with GATA2 germline mutation shows frequently altered leukocyte subset frequency in adults {25359990} and frequent asynchronous maturation of granulocytes in children {27013649}. Immunophenotypic abnormalities related to other germline mutations are likely given their presence in paediatric MDS, which shows altered subset leukocyte frequency, asynchronous maturation of antigen expression, and lineage infidelity antigen expression {19061215; 2542568}. However, caution is needed as these studies are few, abnormalities are age-dependent {23493026}, and immunophenotypic abnormalities can be seen in patients with severe aplastic anaemia and predisposing germline mutations without MDS/AML {2542568; 25402872; 26800764; 28659335}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

For some syndromes, specific functional tests exist (e.g. telomere length measurement for telomere biology disorders, or chromosomal breakage analysis for Fanconi anaemia). Sequencing-based determination of the precise underlying genetic lesion is best made from DNA derived from cultured skin fibroblasts or capillary bulbs, given the relatively high frequency of somatic reversion in haematopoietic tissues for some of these disorders, such as Fanconi anaemia {15383454}, telomere disorders {22341970}, or germline SAMD9 and SAMD9L {33038986}. Care must be taken to employ testing platforms that are comprehensive for the genes and mutation types that confer germline risk, which should include the capacity to diagnose single-nucleotide and copy-number variants {32807974}.

On occasion, underlying germline predisposition disorders can be incidentally uncovered during somatic mutation profiling of a myeloid malignancy at presentation or follow-up {34028637}. Mutation patterns observed in germline predisposition–associated genes such as RUNX1 and CEBPA that may warrant further consideration and germline workup include (1) biallelic gene alterations (two P/LP variants in the same gene, a pathogenic variant with either a copy-number change or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity involving the other allele of the same gene, or a pathogenic variant with an allele frequency consistent with homozygosity; (2) the presence of certain variants of unknown significance; and (3) persistence of the gene mutation on follow-up studies while the primary disease is in clinical remission {34028637}.

In the presence of increased blasts or complex karyotype, the diagnosis of MDS/AML is straightforward. In contrast, because of the presence of baseline dysplasia in patients with germline predisposition, the diagnosis of MDS with low blasts can be challenging, and care should be taken not to overcall MDS. Therefore, in the setting of germline predisposition, the diagnostic criteria for MDS need to be more stringent than for sporadic MDS {28659335}. Whether or not certain types of somatic clonal abnormalities are indicative of MDS progression transformation depends on the underlying predisposition disorder. Patients with germline predisposition also have an increased frequency of clonal haematopoiesis {26492932}. Hence, diagnosis of MDS based on the acquisition of somatic clonal alterations should be made with caution and interpreted within the context of the specific germline predisposition. The somatic alterations typically associated with MDS progression are included in #31700Table 2.24 {10887102; 17494858; 18768433; 19357396; 22138511; 22371884; 25920683; 26162409; 26316320; 26492932; 27112265; 28346228; 28659335; 29217778; 30046003; 30963592; 32208489; 32770553; 33328584}. These can be particularly helpful for MDS diagnosis in the setting of low blasts. Conversely, the presence of these additional somatic alterations should trigger further studies for the diagnosis of MDS and/or closer follow-up.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Note: The diagnostic criteria for the various specific myeloid neoplasms that are diagnosed in the setting of a germline predisposition take precedence over the broad essential and desirable criteria listed below.

Essential: detection of a germline mutation associated with increased risk of myeloid malignancies; myeloid neoplasm meeting the diagnostic criteria of a specific disease type.

Desirable: clonal molecular and/or cytogenetic abnormalities in addition to the germline mutation; a positive family history as determined by formal genetic counselling.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis depends both on the specific germline predisposition and on the characteristics of the myeloid malignancy itself. Myeloid malignancies arising in most predisposition conditions require stem cell transplantation for cure. Patients with underlying germline predisposition syndromes associated with bone marrow failure and multiorgan dysfunction (e.g. Fanconi anaemia, telomere disorders) who develop advanced MDS/AML have a poor prognosis due to an intolerance of intensive chemotherapy cycles before transplant. Transplantation strategies including conditioning regimens have to be adapted in these patients. Myeloid malignancies in other predisposition syndromes are not associated with a worse prognosis than are sporadic myeloid neoplasms (e.g. juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia in patients with CBL syndrome, germline CEBPA, or DDX41 P/LP variants).
