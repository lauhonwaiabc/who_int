2.5.1.3 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB rearrangement

Definition

Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB rearrangement includes myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms and is frequently associated with eosinophilia. This entity is characterized by chromosomal rearrangements involving 5q32, leading to the formation of a PDGFRB fusion gene. The most common partner gene is ETV6, although other partner genes have been described.

ICD-O coding

9966/3 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB rearrangement

ICD-11 coding

2A51 Myeloid neoplasm associated with PDGFRB rearrangement

Related terminology

Acceptable: myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with PDGFRB rearrangement; chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia with eosinophilia associated with t(5;12); myeloid neoplasms associated with PDGFRB rearrangement.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The peripheral blood and bone marrow are invariably involved. Splenic involvement is common. Other extramedullary sites of involvement include the skin and heart. Tissue infiltration by neoplastic eosinophils and the release of eosinophilic granules and related cytokines and other humoral factors may contribute to organ damage.

Clinical features

Most cases manifest with myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative-type features with prominent eosinophilia, although eosinophilia is not universal. Cases often resemble chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia with eosinophilia or (less commonly) myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with neutrophilia (formerly known as atypical chronic myeloid leukaemia) with eosinophilia {17508004}, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia, or other myeloproliferative neoplasms with eosinophilia {11919393; 17868855}. Leukocytosis and peripheral blood and/or bone marrow eosinophilia are typical {11919393; 28725989}. Anaemia and variable degrees of neutrophilia and monocytosis may be present, but thrombocytopenia is uncommon. Basophilia is rare but has been reported {17296564}. Splenomegaly is common, and hepatomegaly may be present. B symptoms are common. Cardiac failure may occur as a result of direct tissue damage. Serum tryptase may be mildly or moderately elevated {32083752}.

Cases with ≥ 20% blasts at initial presentation {17122866} or during the course of the disease are best classified as blast-phase (either myeloid or lymphoid lineage) myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB fusion gene. Several PDGFRB fusions, including ETV6::PDGFRB, have been described in BCR::ABL1-like B-lymphoblastic leukaemia (B-ALL). If there is evidence of a coexisting myeloid neoplasm, then a diagnosis of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB fusion gene in lymphoid transformation is appropriate. Cases lacking eosinophilia and monocytosis, with no evidence of dysplasia and myelofibrosis and histologically resembling other forms of B-ALL, are best classified as BCR::ABL1-like B-ALL.

 

Epidemiology

The median age at initial diagnosis is in the fifth decade of life (range: 8–80 years). There is a striking male predominance (males: 81–91%) {28725989}.

Etiology

The etiology of this disease remains unknown.

Pathogenesis

The postulated cell of origin is a pluripotent haematopoietic stem cell that can give rise to myeloid blasts, neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, mast cells, and B-cell lineage lymphoblasts. The various chromosomal translocations/rearrangements involving PDGFRB lead to the formation of an aberrant, constitutively activated tyrosine kinase. The fusion gene retains the C-terminal intracytoplasmic kinase domain and contains the N-terminal portion of the partner gene. All the fusion partners described to date contain a dimerization motif, resulting in forced dimerization and reorientation of the receptor, leading to constitutive activation {17587879}.

Although ETV6 is the most common partner gene, more than 30 additional partner genes have been identified {28028030; 29169164; 19006078; 20085582; 17296564; 18566537; 15492236; 15087377; 12542482}. In a subset of cases, PDGFRB fusion may be acquired later in the disease course or at relapse {25606057}. Although such cases do not fall into this diagnostic category, the therapeutic implications of the acquired PDGFRB fusion gene remain important; therefore, development of prominent eosinophilia should prompt further investigation for identification of a potential PDGFRB fusion gene.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Bone marrow is typically hypercellular, with an increased myeloid-to-erythroid ratio and variably increased neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes, and their precursors. Abnormal megakaryocytes with myelodysplastic-type and/or myeloproliferative-type features and significant fibrosis, as well as (less frequently) dysgranulopoiesis, dyserythropoiesis, and abnormal eosinophils, are typical histological features {32083752}. Mast cells may be increased and may include atypical (spindled) forms {17133421; 17296564; 32083752; 33022638}. Rare cases may fulfil the histological diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis {32083752}, but if PDGFRB fusion is detected, classification as myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRB fusion gene is more appropriate. Myelofibrosis is common {17122866; 17296564}.

Myeloid progenitors may show little to no immunophenotypic alterations in the chronic phase of disease {17296564; 33022638}. However, only limited information is available on the immunophenotypic features of these neoplasms. Mast cells in the context of this disease may show aberrant immunophenotype, including the expression of CD2 and CD25 {17296564; 32083752; 33022638}.

Cytology

See Histopathology.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The most common cytogenetic alteration is t(5;12)(q32;p13.2), resulting in ETV6::PDGFRB {8168137}. Other variant translocations have also been reported, resulting in the same fusion gene {16960151; 17574967}. Chromosomal breakpoints in the proximity of 5q32, including those involving 5q31 and 5q33, should prompt additional molecular workup in patients with a presumptive diagnosis of a myeloid neoplasm, particularly if associated with eosinophilia. This may include RT-PCR, FISH with break-apart PDGFRB probes, or RNA sequencing. Rarely, FISH may fail to detect PDGFRB rearrangement, even when such rearrangement is detectable on Southern blot analysis {11919393}. In myeloid neoplasms with eosinophilia with a chromosomal breakpoint in the vicinity of 5q32 and a high index of suspicion for PDGFRB fusion but unavailable molecular confirmation, a trial of imatinib may be justified, if feasible. Complex chromosomal rearrangements are common {11919393}.

Not all t(5;12)(q31-33;p12) translocations, or other translocations involving 5q32, lead to the formation of a PDGFRB fusion gene, even in cases with a clinical phenotype consistent with PDGFRB involvement; therefore, molecular confirmation is highly recommended. Cases without a PDGFRB fusion gene are excluded from this diagnostic category and are very unlikely to respond to imatinib. Therefore, break-apart FISH for PDGFRB, RT-PCR, or RNA-sequencing is strongly recommended to confirm the presence of a PDGFRB fusion gene {17508004}. Although identification of the partner gene by RNA sequencing or alternative approaches is optimal and required for subsequent molecular monitoring, it may not be practically feasible in many laboratories, because of the presence of numerous partner genes. Quantitative RT-PCR remains the gold standard for molecular monitoring of measurable residual disease during and after therapy. Although the majority of cases of PDGFRB gene fusions are detected by routine karyotyping studies, rare cryptic lesions have also been reported {28751768; 32083752; 32442889; 33022638; 33367495}.

Additional somatic mutations may be identified by next-generation sequencing. Patients with mutations tend to be older. There are limited studies addressing the prognostic effects of additional mutations in this context; however, available data suggest that additional mutations are not associated with outcome {29567772}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid or lymphoid neoplasm, often with prominent eosinophilia with varying degrees of neutrophilia or monocytosis; presence of a PDGFRB fusion gene; exclusion of BCR::ABL1-like B-ALL without evidence of an associated myeloid neoplasm.

Desirable: cytogenetic and/or molecular identification of the PDGFRB rearrangement partner chromosome/gene.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

This disease is extremely sensitive to tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib {12094244; 12506022; 12181402; 16960151; 17305499; 18566537}. Nearly all patients presenting with chronic-phase disease achieve complete haematological remission after treatment with imatinib, with complete cytogenetic remission and complete molecular remission rates of 92% and 86%, respectively {28725989}. The reported 10-year overall survival of patients treated with imatinib is 90%, with a 6-year progression-free survival rate of 88% {24687085}. Patients achieving complete cytogenetic or molecular remission with imatinib typically do not relapse and do not progress to the blast phase {24687085}.

Complex karyotype is associated with adverse outcomes {28725989}. Patients presenting with blast-phase disease have a poor prognosis despite therapeutic intervention and allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation {15477214; 23615556; 28725989}. In a subset of patients with blast-phase disease, imatinib may still be effective {23615556; 34533850}.
