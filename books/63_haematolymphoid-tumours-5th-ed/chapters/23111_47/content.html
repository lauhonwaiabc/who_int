2.3.1.11 Acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia-related

Definition

Myelodysplasia-related acute myeloid leukaemia (AML-MR) is a myeloid neoplasm harbouring specific cytogenetic and/or molecular abnormalities associated with myelodysplastic neoplasia, arising de novo or after a known diagnosis of myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN).

ICD-O coding

9895/3 Acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia-related

ICD-11 coding

2A60.1 Acute myeloid leukaemia with myelodysplasia-related changes

Related terminology

Acceptable: acute myeloid leukaemia post myelodysplastic neoplasm; acute myeloid leukaemia post myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm.

Not recommended: acute myeloid leukaemia with myelodysplasia-related changes; secondary acute myeloid leukaemia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The peripheral blood and bone marrow are always involved. Rarely, AML-MR may manifest as myeloid sarcoma {28801374}.

Clinical features

AML-MR patients often present with severe pancytopenia and symptoms related to cytopenias.

Epidemiology

AML-MR is estimated to account for 24–35% of all cases of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) {12525517; 15813916; 23690127; 24861848; 32864703}, notwithstanding changes in diagnostic criteria over time. Patients have a median age of about 70 years (range: 18–94 years), and there is a male predominance {31977035; 32112433}.

Etiology

AML-MR arises from antecedent MDS or MDS/MPN. The etiology is unknown for cases presenting as de novo disease.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenetic mechanisms that underpin the development and progression of myeloid neoplasms with myelodysplastic biology characterize AML-MR. Exclusion of cases classified as AML-MR based solely on multilineage dysplasia is anticipated to mitigate pathogenetic heterogeneity. Mechanistic associations related to specific numerical and unbalanced structural chromosomal alterations that define MDS and MDS/MPN have been well studied {27834397}. Balanced translocations are no longer considered defining for AML-MR. Translocations involving t(v;5q33) are very rare {26297286}, limiting definitive associations with MDS-related biological features. Additionally, the pathogenetic impact of other balanced translocations previously used to define this entity is often distinct and in line with other AML types. For example, in t(1;3)(p36.3;q21.2), PRDM16 hijacks the GATA2 enhancer to cause overexpression of PRDM16 and functional haploinsufficiency of GATA2, which mirrors the mechanism of t(3;3)(q21.3;q26.2) {24703711}.

Recent findings have demonstrated that certain somatic mutations are highly associated with AML arising in a background of MDS, providing further insight into the cellular mechanisms that underlie the biology of AML-MR {22535592; 25550361; 32112433; 33570629}. Such mutations involve chromatin modifiers (ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2), the splicing complex (SF3B1, SRSF2, U2AF1 and ZRSR2), and the cohesin complex (STAG2) {25550361}. This reflects the pathogenic factors shared across MDS, MDS/MPN, and secondary AML {27834397}. Several studies have demonstrated a high frequency of TP53 mutations in AML-MR, likely reflecting an association with complex karyotype {25860933}. Conversely, FLT3 and DNMT3A mutations are uncommon {25550361}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Most cases have morphological evidence of multilineage dysplasia, which must be assessed on well-stained smears of peripheral blood and bone marrow. Dysgranulopoiesis is characterized by neutrophils with hypogranular cytoplasm, hyposegmented nuclei (pseudo–Pelger–Huët), or unusually segmented nuclei. Dyserythropoiesis is characterized by megaloblastosis and karyorrhexis, as well as nuclear irregularity, fragmentation, or multinucleation. Ring sideroblasts and cytoplasmic vacuoles are additional features of dyserythropoiesis. Dysmegakaryopoiesis is characterized by micromegakaryocytes and normal-sized or large megakaryocytes with non-lobated or multiple separate nuclei.

Immunophenotype

Increased numbers of blasts can be identified by multiparameter flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry. Blasts often express CD34, KIT (CD117), and HLA-DR, as well as myeloid markers such as myeloperoxidase and CD13. Expression of such antigens is usually at aberrantly high or low levels. Maturing monocyte, myeloid, and/or erythroid lineages can also show immunophenotypic aberrations {34897979}.

Differential diagnosis

In patients diagnosed with MDS or MDS/MPN, the diagnosis of AML-MR is usually straightforward if the blast count is demonstrated to be ≥ 20%.

The differential diagnosis of de novo AML-MR may be broader, and its consideration should be informed by the AML diagnostic hierarchy (see section Acute myeloid leukaemia: Introduction). Patients should not have a history of myeloproliferative neoplasm or prior cytotoxic therapy for an unrelated disease. In addition, no genetic fusion or mutation defining a specific AML type must be present. Cytomorphological dysplasia may be present but is not sufficient for classification as AML-MR {15813916; 20581309; 32112433; 32798550; 34864369}. Rather, AML defined by differentiation should be a differential diagnostic consideration if criteria for AML-MR are not met. It should be noted that the diagnosis of acute erythroid leukaemia (formerly pure erythroid leukaemia) supersedes AML-MR in view of its distinct clinicopathological features.

In paediatric patients, myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition should be considered in the differential diagnosis of AML-MR.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A list of chromosomal abnormalities and somatic mutations that define AML-MR is provided in #31404Box 2.25 . Chromosomal abnormalities that define AML-MR often involve the gain or loss of major segments of certain chromosomes, with complex karyotype, loss of chromosome 7, 7q deletion, 5q deletion, and unbalanced translocations involving 5q being the most common. Although monosomy 5 can be identified on conventional karyotyping, bona fide loss of chromosome 5 is uncommon, as remnant 5p material is often detected on ring or marker chromosomes. These defining cytogenetic abnormalities should be identified by chromosome banding analysis or any other technique capable of assessing structural and copy-number variants genome-wide. Balanced translocations and other rearrangements that fulfil the diagnostic criteria for AML with defined genetic alterations (e.g. MECOM or KMT2A rearrangements) are excluded from AML-MR.

Detection of somatic mutations in SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2, ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR, or STAG2 is considered defining of AML-MR, because > 95% are present specifically in AML arising after MDS or MDS/MPN {25550361; 34664628}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: ≥ 20% blasts in blood or marrow; presence of at least one of the following two criteria: (1) a history of MDS or MDS/MPN, (2) one or more cytogenetic or molecular abnormalities listed in #31404Box 2.25 ; absence of the following: a history of exposure to cytotoxic therapy, a history of myeloproliferative neoplasm, criteria for AML with defining genetic abnormalities, and criteria for myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

AML-MR generally has a poor prognosis, with a lower rate of complete remission than other AML subtypes {12760285; 15813916}. TP53 is frequently mutated in AML-MR and is strongly associated with a complex karyotype; several studies have suggested that it may independently impart a worse prognosis in this group {22535592; 25412851; 25860933}.

Some cases in patients with prior MDS and < 30% bone marrow blasts (oligoblastic AML-MR) may behave in a manner more similar to MDS than other AML types and may be managed accordingly {34111285; 35133004}. Managing cases in patients with oligoblastic AML-MR and a stable clinical course for ≥ 2 months akin to high-risk MDS has been proposed {34111285}. Several findings support this recommendation: (1) patients with oligoblastic AML-MR have shown response on therapeutic trials for MDS {19230772; 20940414}; (2) their overall and leukaemia-free survival times are similar to those of patients classified as having MDS with increased blasts {22740453; 35133004}; and (3) the molecular and cytogenetic features of these two patient groups are very similar {21494258}.

Defining cytogenetic abnormalities:

·         Complex karyotype (at least three abnormalities)a

·         5q deletion or loss of 5q due to unbalanced translocation

·         Monosomy 7, 7q deletion, or loss of 7q due to unbalanced translocation

·         11q deletion

·         12p deletion or loss of 12p due to unbalanced translocation

·         Monosomy 13 or 13q deletion

·         17p deletion or loss of 17p due to unbalanced translocation

·         Isochromosome 17q

·         idic(X)(q13)

Defining somatic mutations:

·         ASXL1

·         BCOR

·         EZH2

·         SF3B1

·         SRSF2

·         STAG2

·         U2AF1

·         ZRSR2

aThe International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN) should be used to determine the number of chromosomal abnormalities necessary to meet the criteria for a complex karyotype [[McGowan-Jordan J, Hastings RJ, Moore S, editors. ISCN 2020: an international system for human cytogenomic nomenclature (2020). Basel (Switzerland): Karger Publishers; 2020.]], using the following guidelines:

1.    Only clonal abnormalities are counted; abnormalities present in only one metaphase are ignored.

2.    Numerical gains and losses, simple and complex balanced translocations, unbalanced aberrations involving one chromosome, and multiplication of a complete chromosome set are counted as one abnormality.

3.    Unbalanced aberrations involving two or more chromosomes, tetrasomy of the same chromosome, triplication or quadruplication, and isoderivative chromosomes are counted as two abnormalities.

4.    Constitutional abnormalities are not counted.

5.    In the case of multiple clones (subclones or independent clones), chromosomal abnormalities in each clone/subclone are counted separately, and the number of chromosomal abnormalities is defined by the clone with the highest number of abnormalities.

6.    For composite karyotypes, clonal chromosomal aberrations are counted in metaphases with the highest number of abnormalities.
