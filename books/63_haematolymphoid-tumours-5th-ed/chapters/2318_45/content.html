2.3.1.8 Acute myeloid leukaemia with BCR::ABL1 fusion

Definition

Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with BCR::ABL1 fusion is a de novo AML with BCR::ABL1 detected at initial diagnosis and where no evidence of chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) can be established.

ICD-O coding

9912/3 Acute myeloid leukaemia with BCR::ABL1 fusion

ICD-11 coding

2A60.0 & XH6FZ7 Acute myeloid leukaemia with recurrent genetic abnormalities & Acute myeloid leukaemia with BCR-ABL1

Related terminology

Acceptable: acute myeloid leukaemia with t(9;22)(q34;q11.2).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The bone marrow and blood are always involved. Rarely, extramedullary sites may be involved {18308388}.

Clinical features

Patients frequently present with leukocytosis and variable degrees of anaemia and/or thrombocytopenia. Compared to patients with the myeloid blast phase of CML, patients with AML with BCR::ABL1 tend to have a higher percentage of blasts (median: 47% vs 13%), a lower percentage of basophils (median: 0% vs 2.5%) and absolute basophil count, and a lower frequency of splenomegaly (25% vs 65%) {17369142; 22691121}.

Epidemiology

AML with BCR::ABL1 is rare and accounts for < 0.5% of all cases of AML and < 0.5% of all cases of acute and chronic leukaemia with BCR::ABL1. It occurs mainly in adults, with a male predominance {17369142; 22691121; 33433047}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The chimeric BCR::ABL1 fusion protein is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. About 70–80% of cases of AML with BCR::ABL1 harbour p210 transcripts, and 50–60% of cases demonstrate additional chromosomal alterations {17369142; 22691121; 23521501; 33433047}. Additional chromosomal alterations typical of the myeloid blast phase of CML are less common in AML with BCR::ABL1.

Cryptic deletions within IG and TR genes are nearly universal, and they are frequently accompanied by losses of the IKZF1 and/or CDKN2A/B genes detected by array comparative genomic hybridization. Although not seen in the myeloid blast phase of CML, these deletions are recurrent in the lymphoid blast phase of CML and in mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia with BCR::ABL1 {20082691; 23521501}. RUNX1 mutation is common in AML with BCR::ABL1 and occurs in about 40% of cases. Other mutated genes include ASXL1, BCOR, IDH1/IDH2, and SRSF2, and these each occur in 10–15% of cases. This mutation profile appears similar to that of the myeloid blast phase of CML {28321123; 33433047}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Bone marrow

The morphological spectrum of AML with BCR::ABL1 is broad and can encompass AML with minimal differentiation, without maturation, or with monocytic differentiation {8952155; 9844918; 17369142; 22691121; 23521501; 27297971}. Compared with the myeloid blast phase of CML, AML with BCR::ABL1 shows a lower myeloid-to-erythroid ratio (median: 2.0 vs 4.8) and a lower frequency of > 2% basophils (13% vs 53%) in the bone marrow {17369142}. Dwarf megakaryocytes are rare {22691121}.

Immunophenotype

Most cases express CD34, HLA-DR, and myeloid antigens (CD13, CD33, and KIT [CD117]). Expression of CD7, CD19, and TdT is not uncommon {8952155; 9844918; 17369142}.

Differential diagnosis

Given that AML with BCR::ABL1 may aberrantly express lymphoid antigens, mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia with BCR::ABL1 should be considered in the differential diagnosis. The co-occurrence of a genetic abnormality with t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) that defines another de novo AML subtype would take precedence over the diagnosis of AML with BCR::ABL1 {21275954; 22691121}. BCR::ABL1 may be acquired secondarily in AML transforming from a previous myelodysplastic neoplasm or in relapsed/refractory AML previously lacking BCR::ABL1 {29449681}. These cases should not be included in this category. Distinguishing AML with BCR::ABL1 from the initial myeloid blast phase of CML is often challenging.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Karyotype analysis identifies the t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) translocation that defines this subtype of AML; t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) may be the sole finding or occur with additional chromosomal abnormalities {17369142; 22691121}. Additionally, molecular methods to detect the resulting BCR::ABL1 fusion, with primers/probes designed to identify all common breakpoint variants when applicable, include dual-colour dual-fusion FISH, RT-PCR, qPCR, and RNA or DNA sequencing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid neoplasm with > 20% blasts expressing a myeloid immunophenotype in the bone marrow and/or peripheral blood; detection of BCR::ABL1 at initial diagnosis; lack of features of CML before or at diagnosis or after therapy.

Desirable: presence of t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) on conventional karyotyping; determination of the BCR::ABL1 transcript subtype and establishment of a baseline level of BCR::ABL1 transcript for monitoring treatment response.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Response to standard AML induction chemotherapy is poor, with a median survival time of < 9 months {8952155; 17369142}. Treatment with single-agent tyrosine kinase inhibitors does not seem to be effective, but there is a single case report of a patient achieving molecular remission with dasatinib and chemotherapy {17369142; 30383645}. Survival after allogeneic transplantation appears similar to that for intermediate-risk AML, with one report demonstrating a 3-year overall survival rate of 73% {32737447}.
