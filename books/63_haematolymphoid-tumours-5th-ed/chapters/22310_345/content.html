2.2.3.10 Myelodysplastic neoplasm, hypoplastic

Definition

Hypoplastic myelodysplastic neoplasm (hMDS) is a myeloid neoplasm with cytopenia and dysplasia, characterized by significantly decreased age-adjusted bone marrow cellularity as determined on a trephine biopsy.

ICD-O coding

9985/3 Myelodysplastic neoplasm, hypoplastic

ICD-11 coding

2A4Y Other specified myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative neoplasms

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Bone marrow and peripheral blood

Clinical features

hMDS is characterized by significantly decreased marrow cellularity, a feature shared with aplastic anaemia. Clinical symptoms are typically due to cytopenias {30940907}. Compared with patients with other MDS types, patients with hMDS tend to have more profound cytopenias at diagnosis, with a lower median white blood cell count (2.4 vs 3.7 × 109/L, P = 0.002) and a lower absolute neutrophil count (1.1 vs 1.7 × 109/L, P = 0.01) {18094713; 26273060}.

Epidemiology

hMDSs represent about 10–15% of all MDSs. Patients are usually younger than those with other MDS types but older than those with aplastic anaemia {30940907}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

hMDS is driven by a selective growth advantage of somatically mutated clonal haematopoietic stem cells or progenitor cells. Hypoplasia may be driven by a T cell–mediated immune attack on haematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, with oligoclonal expansion of CD8+ T cells overproducing IFN-γ and/or TNF-α. Significant overlap with paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria and aplastic anaemia is reported. When PIGA mutation is demonstrated in the absence of bona fide features of MDS, classification as paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria is preferred. Individuals with germline mutations of GATA2, DDX41, Fanconi anaemia genes, or telomerase complex genes can present with hypoplastic bone marrow and develop hMDS and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). These patients do not usually respond to immunosuppressive treatment {34404528; 30670443; 21097671}.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Bone marrow cellularity, as assessed on a trephine biopsy and/or an adequate-quality clot preparation, must be significantly decreased (by definition below 30% of normal cellularity in patients younger than 70 years and below 20% in patients aged ≥ 70 years) {30940907; 15965280}. Hypocellularity is usually diffuse but may be patchy. Dysplastic features are identified in one or more haematopoietic lineages. Megakaryocytes may be scattered or form loose clusters, with usual features of megakaryocytic dysplasia, including small size and nuclear hypolobulation. The presence of micromegakaryocytes is rare. Blast counts are variable, and clustering of blasts on trephine biopsy may be observed in a subset of cases {30940907}. CD34 or KIT (CD117) immunostains are helpful for recognizing microclusters of blasts or immature cells on a core biopsy. CD61 and/or CD42b may be helpful for highlighting megakaryocytes. Reticulin fibrosis is rare {30940907}.

Granulocytic dysplasia is identified in peripheral blood and bone marrow smears. Iron stain on the bone marrow aspirate is helpful to identify an increased proportion (> 5%) of ring sideroblasts. If the percentage of ring sideroblasts exceeds 15%, then MDS with low blasts and SF3B1 mutation should be considered. When cellularity is extremely low, it may be virtually impossible to distinguish hMDS from aplastic anaemia by cytomorphology {12446422; 9052376}. Peripheral blood and marrow may also show an accompanying large granular lymphocyte expansion {31808900; 15965280}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnosis is with aplastic anaemia, which typically shows a marked decrease in megakaryocytes and haemoglobin F–containing erythroblasts {4066981; 2788455; 11999352}. Aplastic anaemia may be associated with dyserythropoietic changes and congenital bone marrow failure; hence, morphological support for a diagnosis of hMDS requires identification of dysplastic features in myeloid and/or megakaryocytic lineages {31808900; 30940907; 15965280}.

 

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Cytogenetics

Chromosomal abnormalities are detected in 25–40% of cases {8471656; 30940907; 18094713}. The number of aberrations may be lower than in other MDS types, with more low- and intermediate-risk cytogenetic changes such as trisomy 8 and del(20q). Deletion of 7q and monosomy 7 are associated with an increased risk of AML transformation {32066866; 22553315}. When cytogenetic analysis fails, FISH may be used to detect MDS-associated alterations {22537394}, which further facilitates distinction from aplastic anaemia.

Molecular pathology

There are no specific mutations or patterns of mutations in hMDS. Mutations are usually detected at lower variant allele frequencies (VAFs) {30940907; 18094713; 26273060; 27527853; 33401595}.

Because there is a significant overlap with paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria, somatic mutation of the X-linked gene PIGA should be excluded {30940907}. A genetic predisposition to bone marrow failure, with germline mutations of GATA2, DDX41, Fanconi anaemia genes, or telomerase complex genes, may be suspected and should be excluded, especially in younger individuals, on the basis of comorbidities and family history {34404528; 30670443; 21097671}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: cytopenia involving one or more lineages; hypocellular bone marrow (assessed on a trephine core biopsy, adjusted for patient age) not explained by non-neoplastic bone marrow failure conditions; dysplasia involving the granulocytic and/or megakaryocytic lineage; < 5% blasts in bone marrow and < 2% blasts in peripheral blood; not fulfilling criteria for MDS with defining genetic abnormalities or MDS with increased blasts.

Desirable: detection of a clonal cytogenetic and/or molecular abnormality.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with hMDS have a significantly worse overall survival than those with aplastic anaemia {30940907}. In comparison with those that have other MDS types, patients with hMDS have a better prognosis {3409176; 15965280; 35597805}. Notably, patients with hMDS, particularly those with low blast counts, have a favourable response to immunosuppressive therapy. In fact, patients with hMDS who are treated with immunosuppressive therapy can achieve higher rates of transfusion independence than those without hMDS {30037803}. When patients with hMDS were stratified according to International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) risk, the 5-year overall survival (OS) and AML progression–free survival rates were, respectively, 81% and 80% for the low and intermediate-1 risk groups combined, and 37% and 30% for the intermediate-2 and high-risk groups combined {28891083}. These OS and AML progression–free survival rates in the low and intermediate-1 risk groups in the hMDS cohort (81% and 80%, respectively) were significantly higher than those in the non-hMDS cohort (which were 57% and 49%, respectively) {28891083}. Similarly, the low and intermediate-1 risk groups of the hMDS cohort had significantly longer OS than their non-hMDS counterparts (58 vs 24 months). However, no differences in survival were observed between the hMDS and non-hMDS cohorts in the intermediate-2 and high-risk groups {18094713}. There remains some ambiguity in the outcome of hMDS because of limited datasets. In fact, one study detected only a marginally better outcome for patients with hMDS than for their non-hMDS counterparts {30940907}.
