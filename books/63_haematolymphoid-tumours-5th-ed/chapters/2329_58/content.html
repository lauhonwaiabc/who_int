2.3.2.9 Acute erythroid leukaemia

Definition

Acute erythroid leukaemia (AEL) is a neoplastic proliferation of erythroid cells with features of maturation arrest (increased proerythroblasts) and a high prevalence of biallelic TP53 alterations.

ICD-O coding

9840/3 Acute erythroid leukaemia

ICD-11 coding

2A60.35 Acute erythroid leukaemia

Related terminology

Acceptable: pure erythroid leukaemia.

Not recommended: Di Guglielmo disease; acute myeloid leukaemia, M6.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

This leukaemia predominantly involves the peripheral blood and bone marrow.

Clinical features

Patients may present with de novo AEL, but more often the disease arises after exposure to cytotoxic therapy or progression of a prior myeloid neoplasm, particularly myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) {21102413; 28006859; 28246192; 29327715}.

Epidemiology

AEL is extremely rare and represents < 1% of all cases of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Patients are elderly, often in their late sixties. Men are affected more commonly.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Aberrant expression of key transcriptional regulators, including SKI, ERG, and CBFA2T3 (ETO2), appears to mediate the blockade of erythroid differentiation in murine erythroid progenitors and may play a role in the pathogenesis of AEL {32350520}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

On bone marrow smears, there is an overall erythroid predominance (usually ≥ 80% of marrow cellularity), which includes increased (≥ 30%) immature erythroid cells (proerythroblasts, or pronormoblasts) that are medium-sized to large with a round central nucleolus, dispersed to finely reticulated chromatin, one or several distinct nucleoli, and deeply basophilic and agranular cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic blebs and vacuoles are commonly seen. PAS stain shows a coarsely globular staining pattern in the cytoplasm. Erythroid precursors at later stages are often present and display prominent dysplastic features, including ring sideroblasts. Myeloid and megakaryocytic cells are significantly decreased. Dysmegakaryopoiesis is frequently present.

Bone marrow core biopsy is hypercellular and shows sheets of immature proerythroblasts that are large, with round nuclei, pale chromatin, one to several distinct nucleoli, and thin but distinct nuclear membranes. The nucleoli exhibit variable shapes, and some can be located at the periphery of the nucleus, touching the nuclear membrane. Occasionally, an intrasinusoidal growth pattern may be present {26071461}.

Immunophenotype

CD71 is a pan-erythroid marker that stains nucleated erythroid precursors at all stages {21415701}, while mature anucleate red blood cells are negative. Thus, CD71 is an ideal marker for assessing the percentage of the erythroid component on bone marrow sections. Of note, although erythroid precursors have high levels of CD71 (an iron transporter), its expression is not specific and can be detected by flow cytometry in non-erythroid cells. KIT (CD117) and E-cadherin often only highlight proerythroblasts at early maturation stages {21102413; 29327715}. Glycophorin A and haemoglobin only highlight late erythroid cells, including mature anucleate red blood cells. Other markers such as ferritin {23611361} and α-haemoglobin–stabilizing protein {22982896; 25611244} may also be used to highlight pronormoblasts. All these markers cannot distinguish neoplastic from reactive erythroid cells. Detection of mutant p53 patterns (overexpression or complete loss) by immunohistochemistry is a helpful adjunct to the presence of TP53 mutations {28006859}. CD34-positive blasts are not increased in AEL.

By flow cytometry, proerythroblasts are positive for CD36, CD71, and KIT (CD117) (often in a subset of tumour cells only), and negative for CD34 and myeloperoxidase. CD235a (glycophorin A) is positive in a subset of cells that often corresponds to the KIT (CD117)-negative subset. CD45 expression is variable but often dimly positive. HLA-DR is negative or partially positive. Neoplastic proerythroblasts may show aberrant CD13, increased CD4, or decreased CD38 expression. Although not increased in number, CD34-positive myeloblasts in AEL frequently show an aberrant immunophenotype.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of AEL is broad, including both reactive and neoplastic conditions associated with proerythroblast proliferation. Reactive erythroid hyperplasia with a prominent proerythroblast component can be seen in nutritional (vitamin B12 or folate) deficiency and haemolytic anaemia. Laboratory studies, TP53 mutation analysis, and cytogenetic studies are helpful, because reactive proerythroblast proliferation shows no TP53 mutation and a normal karyotype.

Exuberant erythroid hyperplasia is also present in some MDS cases. Erythroid cells in MDS often show sequential maturation with later-stage proerythroblasts as a predominant component, whereas in AEL, erythroid cells have maturation arrest at the proerythroblast stage. AEL shares morphological, molecular, cytogenetic, and prognostic features with myeloid neoplasms post cytotoxic therapy. Cases of AEL arising after cytotoxic therapy should be designated as such. Similarly, AEL shows overlap with myelodysplasia-related AML on the basis of complex karyotype. However, myelodysplasia-related AML requires the presence of ≥ 20% blasts, a criterion that would not typically be met in AEL, because myeloblasts in bona fide cases are generally below this threshold. Additional studies are needed to better characterize rare cases with ≥ 30 pronormoblasts and ≥ 20% myeloblasts.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Biallelic (or multi-hit) TP53 loss of function is characteristic of AEL, which often shows mutation in one allele and deletion in another allele, or two or more TP53 mutations in cases without TP53 deletion {28246192}. Missense TP53 mutations are most common, followed by deletion and nonsense mutations. Mutations at splice sites are also rarely seen. TP53 abnormalities are associated with complex karyotype, frequently with losses of 17/17p, 5/5q, and 7/7q. Biallelic TP53 alterations are not unique to AEL and are seen in other myeloid neoplasms, including MDS {32747829}, with and without a history of exposure to cytotoxic therapy.

Detection of TP53 mutation and the presence of a complex karyotype provides supportive data.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: erythroid predominance, usually ≥ 80% of bone marrow elements, of which ≥ 30% are proerythroblasts.

Desirable: evidence of TP53 mutation.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with AEL share distinctive histopathological and molecular genetic features and extremely poor outcomes. The prognosis is dismal, with a median survival time of 2–4 months.
