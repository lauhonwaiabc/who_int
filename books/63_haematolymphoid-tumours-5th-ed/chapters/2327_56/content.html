2.3.2.7 Acute monocytic leukaemia

Definition

Acute monocytic leukaemia is an acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with monocytic differentiation that lacks defining genetic abnormalities.

ICD-O coding

9891/3 Acute monocytic leukaemia

ICD-11 coding

2A60.34 & XH9NE2 Acute monoblastic or monocytic leukaemia & Acute monocytic leukaemia

Related terminology

Acceptable: acute monoblastic leukaemia.

Not recommended: acute myeloid leukaemia, M5; acute monoblastic leukaemia, M5a; acute monocytic leukaemia, M5b.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These leukaemias predominantly involve the blood and bone marrow. Extramedullary disease is common, particularly involving the gingiva, skin, and CNS.

Clinical features

Patients commonly present with symptoms related to cytopenias and/or extramedullary disease. Some patients present with bleeding disorders or symptoms related to hyperleukocytosis.

Epidemiology

All age groups may be affected. The immature form (monoblastic) is more common in children. There are limited data using the current definition criteria.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Bone marrow biopsy shows extensive infiltration by immature cells that are intermediate-sized to large, with eosinophilic cytoplasm, high N:C ratios, irregularly lobulated to reniform nuclear contours, fine chromatin, and distinct nucleoli.

Typical monoblasts are medium-sized or large, with abundant, moderately to intensely basophilic cytoplasm and round to oval nuclei. The nuclei have delicate, lace-like chromatin and one or more prominent nucleoli. Blasts may also show scattered fine azurophilic granules and cytoplasmic vacuoles {19535346; 32297416}. The presence of ≥ 80% monoblasts has been used to define acute monoblastic leukaemia, but although such a distinction is acceptable, it is not required.

Promonocytes have a delicately folded, convoluted nuclear configuration with fine, lace-like chromatin, and they usually show inconspicuous nuclei. The cytoplasm is blue-grey, with variable azurophilic granules and sometimes vacuoles. More mature monocytes also show abnormalities such as convoluted nuclei with less clumped nuclear chromatin and minute nucleoli, more cytoplasmic vacuolization, and/or granularity {19535346; 32297416}.

Monocytes and promonocytes are not always readily distinguishable from maturing myeloid cells in routinely stained bone marrow smears; cytochemistry may be useful in such cases. The peripheral blood typically shows an increase in monocytes, which are often more mature-appearing than those in the bone marrow. The monocytic component may be more evident in the peripheral blood than in the bone marrow.

Immunophenotype

By flow cytometry, blasts commonly express myeloid-associated antigens such as CD13, CD15, CD33 (bright), CD65, and CD117. Blasts may express CD34 and usually express HLA-DR. Markers of monocytic differentiation may be expressed by promonocytes and more mature monocytes (CD4 [dim], CD11b, CD11c, CD14, CD36 [bright], CD64 [bright], CD163, LILRB1, and LILRB4) {26282340; 32918786}. Coexpression of two or more monocytic markers is characteristic of monocytic differentiation. The expression of the lymphoid-associated markers CD7 and CD56 is frequent. Immature and mature monocytes may show asynchronous expression of antigens associated with monocytic differentiation. Blasts with monocytic differentiation and immature/mature monocytes can be identified in tissue sections by immunohistochemistry with CD68R (as recognized by PGM1) and CD163 {17041567; 19880784}. Lysozyme and CD68 (KP1) are also positive but are not specific for monocytic differentiation.

Cytochemistry

The monoblasts, promonocytes, and monocytes are typically positive for nonspecific esterase. Staining is usually strong and diffuse but may occasionally be weak or absent; that does not exclude this diagnosis if immunophenotypic markers are detected. Myeloperoxidase may be positive in a subset of blasts.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of AML should primarily include microgranular acute promyelocytic leukaemia, AML with NPM1 mutation, AML with KMT2A rearrangements, and chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia (CMML). Occasionally, these cases resemble plasmablastic myeloma. Immunophenotyping with an adequate panel of markers, along with cytochemistry, can readily distinguish acute monocytic (monoblastic) leukaemia from other entities. Extramedullary myeloid (monoblastic) sarcoma may be confused with malignant lymphoma or soft tissue sarcomas and can be distinguished with CD45 (LCA), CD43, CD68, and CD163 on immunohistochemistry, or by a flow cytometry panel with myeloid markers. The differential diagnosis between acute monocytic leukaemia and CMML and may be difficult. Proper identification of promonocytes and their designation as blast equivalents is key in distinguishing between acute monocytic leukaemia and CMML, but this is difficult to reproduce. Bone marrow evaluation is critical for blast and promonocyte counts {31048353}, because the peripheral blood component can appear more mature.

Flow cytometry immunophenotyping can help in the quantitation of the proportion of immature and mature forms of monocytes and blasts {31048353}. It usually helps in identifying the proportion of precursor monocytes equivalent to promonocytes, which can be identified with characteristic immunophenotypes such as weak or negative CD14, weak and variable CD36, strong CD64, and strong HLA-DR expression {17658407; 31676343}. In addition to a higher percentage of blasts and promonocytes, the proportion of monocytic cells is usually significantly higher than that of granulocytic cells in acute monocytic leukaemia, and lower in CMML {21219126}.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Most cases show normal karyotypes, single trisomies, or rare structural abnormalities. No specific molecular genetic profile is described {14970186; 27285584}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: ≥ 20% blasts and blast equivalents (promonocytes) in bone marrow and/or blood; ≥ 80% of the leukaemic cells are monocytes and their precursors, including monoblasts and promonocytes; < 20% maturing granulocytic cells; not fulfilling criteria for AML types with defining genetic abnormalities; not fulfilling diagnostic criteria for myeloid neoplasm post cytotoxic therapy.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

There are limited data specifically on prognosis and prediction in patients classified as having acute monocytic leukaemia using the current definition criteria.
