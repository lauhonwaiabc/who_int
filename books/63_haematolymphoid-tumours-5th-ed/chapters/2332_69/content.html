2.3.3.2 Myeloid sarcoma

Definition

Myeloid sarcoma is a tumour mass involving any anatomical site other than bone marrow (i.e. extramedullary) that effaces tissue architecture and is composed of myeloid blasts, with or without maturation.

ICD-O coding

9930/3 Myeloid sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2A60.39 Myeloid sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: chloroma; granulocytic or monocytic sarcoma; extramedullary myeloid tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Almost any site in the body can be involved. The most frequently affected sites are the skin, soft tissue, lymph nodes, gastrointestinal tract, bones, and head and neck region {23530613; 25213180}; less frequently involved sites include the reproductive organs, brain, and lungs {29789584; 33232718; 33213985; 34380367}.

Clinical features

Myeloid sarcoma is reported in 2–9% of patients with acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) and in 5–12% of patients who have undergone allogeneic stem cell transplantation. It most often occurs concurrently with or as a relapse of AML. Rarely, myeloid sarcoma can precede the onset of AML. Myeloid sarcoma may also present as the transformation of other myeloid neoplasms, including myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS), myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN), myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN), or myeloid neoplasm secondary to prior cytotoxic therapy {17170724; 31467130; 32577912}. De novo isolated myeloid sarcoma with morphologically normal-appearing bone marrow is rare, occurring in about 1% of patients with AML {34346084; 34380367}. The clinical manifestations of myeloid sarcoma are diverse, because of the wide variety of sites in which myeloid sarcoma can develop, and depending on whether the bone marrow is involved by AML with associated cytopenias.

Epidemiology

The epidemiological features are similar to those of AML. Myeloid sarcoma is a rare disease and can occur at any age, with reported median ages ranging from 46 to 59 years. The M:F ratio is 1.2:1 {25213180; 33213985; 34346084}.

Etiology

The etiology is similar to that of AML. Adhesion molecules (e.g. ITGA7), chemokine receptor–ligand interactions, extracellular matrix interactions, and perturbation of the RAS pathway have been suggested to underly presentation as an extramedullary mass {20582977; 22388727; 32033262}.

Pathogenesis

Myeloid sarcoma shares cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities with bone marrow AML. Chromosomal abnormalities assessed by conventional cytogenetics and/or FISH are detected in about 50% of cases. These abnormalities include t(8;21)/RUNX1::RUNX1T1, inv(16)/CBFB::MYH11, trisomy 4, trisomy 8, monosomy 7, and deletions of 5q and 20q {17170724; 23530613}. Some of these abnormalities seem to be associated with particular sites of involvement; for example, inv(16)-associated myeloid sarcoma is more common in the abdominal region, whereas myeloid sarcoma associated with t(8;21) often involves the orbital region in children {17170724; 23530613; 31671434}.

In recent years, next-generation sequencing studies have elucidated the molecular landscape of myeloid sarcoma cases (see #28942Table 2.18). Molecular abnormalities include somatic mutations in NPM1 (~20–30%) and KMT2A rearrangement (~10%); these alterations tend to be present in cases with monocytic differentiation. Other recurrent mutations include genes involved in the RAS pathway (~50%), epigenetic pathways, signalling, transcription, and tumour suppression (see #28942Table 2.18) {27659839; 28633614; 29789584; 33232718; 34380367}.

Macroscopic appearance

Involved organs are commonly enlarged and have a fish-flesh cut surface. Neoplasms with granulocytic maturation may appear green because the cells express myeloperoxidase.

Histopathology

Myeloid sarcoma forms a mass that diffusely replaces the tissue architecture. A single-file pattern is common in cases with monocytic differentiation. Mitotic figures are often numerous. The neoplastic cells are intermediate to large in size, with scant to moderate amounts of pale or eosinophilic cytoplasm, round to oval nuclei or irregular nuclear contours, fine (blastic) chromatin, and usually small nucleoli {17170724; 23530613; 26185307}. Tumours with granulocytic differentiation occasionally show a subset of maturing neoplastic cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm (known as eosinophilic myelocytes and metamyelocytes). Tumours with monocytic differentiation often have a subset of cells with folded nuclei known as promonocytes. Some tumours exhibit myelomonocytic lineage with proliferation of granulocytic and monocytic precursors. Tumours with erythroid or megakaryocytic differentiation are rare.

Evaluation of peripheral blood and bone marrow for AML or other myeloid neoplasms (e.g. MDS, MPN, MDS/MPN) is mandatory.

Immunophenotype

The immunophenotypic profile in myeloid sarcoma is highly variable, given the broad spectrum of myeloid lineage differentiation and maturation {17170724; 26185307} (see #28943Table 2.19). The antigens frequently expressed include CD13, CD33, CD43, CD68, and myeloperoxidase; CD45 expression is positive in 60% of cases. Tumours with an immature granulocytic profile express CD34 and KIT (CD117), whereas CD34 is negative in tumours with a more mature profile. Tumours with immature monocytic cells / monoblasts express CD11b, CD11c, CD15, CD64, KIT (CD117) (~30%), and lysozyme but often lack CD14, CD34, and myeloperoxidase. CD14, CD68, and CD163 are variably expressed in tumours with a more mature monocytic profile {7668936; 11698000; 17170724; 26185307; 28574302}. CD61 and glycophorin A are expressed in tumours with megakaryocytic and erythroid differentiation, respectively. Myeloid sarcoma may express B-cell markers (e.g. CD19 in cases with t(8;21)), T-cell markers (e.g. CD4, CD7), or NK-cell markers (e.g. CD56). CD123, CD99, and TdT (often weak or only in a subset of cells) can be expressed in rare cases {17170724; 26185307}. Cases that meet the criteria for mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia are not classified as myeloid sarcoma.

Additionally, aberrant cytoplasmic NPM1 expression, indicative of NPM1 mutation, is detected in a subset of myeloid sarcoma, particularly those cases with negative CD34 or with monocytic differentiation {17443224}. Myeloid sarcoma carrying IDH1 p.R132 mutation is expected to be immunoreactive with an IDH1 p.R132 mutation–specific antibody {29635257}. Patients with myeloid sarcoma carrying IDH1 or IDH2 mutations may have an increased serum level of D-2-HG {29479068}.

Cytochemistry

Unstained touch imprints can be assessed by cytochemical stains for myeloperoxidase, nonspecific esterase, and naphthol AS-D CAE. These stains are often useful for distinguishing granulocytic cells from monocytic cells (positive for nonspecific esterase).

Differential diagnosis

The major differential diagnoses of myeloid sarcoma include lymphomas, small blue round cell tumours, and neoplasms of histiocytic/dendritic derivation, such as blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm {24390220; 26185307; 31261288; 32591154}. Myeloid sarcoma must be distinguished from myeloid blasts infiltrating any site of the body, without architectural effacement, in a patient with systemic myeloid neoplasm. Myeloid sarcoma also needs to be distinguished from extramedullary haematopoiesis; the latter can produce pseudotumoural masses virtually anywhere in the body {9760159; 17170724; 17334344}. The presence of trilineage haematopoiesis, a lack of significant increase in blasts or dysplasia, and an absence of p53 or cytoplasmic NPM1 expression by immunohistochemistry support extramedullary haematopoiesis.

 

Cytology

Giemsa touch imprints or smears highlight large cells with cytoplasmic granules in maturing neoplastic cells {31051716}. Auer rods or morphological features of myelodysplasia in the background may be identified.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The detection of myeloid sarcoma is equivalent to a diagnosis of AML {26185307; 31682773}. A comprehensive evaluation that includes cytogenetic analysis and molecular assessment for translocations and gene mutations is needed for correct classification, prognostication, and therapy planning.

Genetic alterations in myeloid sarcoma and concurrent bone marrow AML are concordant in 60–80% of patients, suggesting that myeloid sarcoma is derived from a common haematopoietic stem cell or precursor. Relevant gene mutations are detected in a subset of patients with morphologically normal-appearing bone marrow, suggesting low-level clonal myeloid disease or clonal haematopoiesis in the bone marrow {31866570; 33108522}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: effacement of tissue architecture by a mass composed of myeloid blasts, with or without maturing elements; positive immunophenotyping for granulocytic and/or monocytic markers.

Note: A complete workup including flow cytometry immunophenotyping, conventional cytogenetics, and molecular analyses is suggested in patients with de novo myeloid sarcoma. Although this workup is not essential for diagnosis, it is needed for prognostication and targeted therapies.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The recommended therapy for patients with myeloid sarcoma, as recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), is AML-type chemotherapy should the patient be otherwise fit and eligible, guided by targets identified by cytogenetic and molecular analyses. Identification of specific genetic abnormalities not only improves diagnosis (e.g. immunohistochemical staining for mutant NPM1 and IDH1 p.R132) and prognostic stratification (e.g. FLT3 internal tandem duplication [ITD]) but also provides therapeutic targets (e.g. FLT3 and IDH). Radiation therapy can be used to treat myeloid sarcoma in selected cases.

The presence of myeloid sarcoma at diagnosis of AML does not seem to influence prognosis {25213180; 28556258; 29043233}. However, patients with concurrent myeloid sarcoma and AML with molecularly discordant profiles likely have a poorer prognosis than those with concordant molecular profiles {17170724; 31866570; 33232718}. Patients who present with isolated myeloid sarcoma appear to have a better prognosis; long-term remission has been achieved in a subset of patients with disease involving the head/neck and skin {18623376; 29043233; 34346084; 34380367}.
