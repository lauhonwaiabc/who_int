2.5.1.4 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with FGFR1 rearrangement

Definition

This is a heterogenous group of neoplasms with FGFR1 rearrangement, arising from a pluripotent haematopoietic stem cell that may manifest as chronic myeloid neoplasms or blast-phase disease of B-cell, T-cell, myeloid, or mixed-phenotype origin, typically with associated eosinophilia. The phenotype may change throughout the disease course.

ICD-O coding

9967/3 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with FGFR1 rearrangement

ICD-11 coding

2A52 Myeloid or lymphoid neoplasms with FGFR1 abnormalities

Related terminology

Not recommended: 8p11.2 myeloproliferative syndrome; 8p11.2 stem cell syndrome; stem cell leukaemia/lymphoma syndrome; haematopoietic stem cell neoplasm with FGFR1 abnormalities; myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms with FGFR1 abnormalities.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most common sites of involvement include the bone marrow, peripheral blood, lymph nodes, liver, and spleen. Other extramedullary sites may be involved.

Clinical features

Patients can present with various types of acute leukaemias, myeloproliferative neoplasms, or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms with associated eosinophilia, with the associated characteristic clinical and haematological symptoms. B symptoms are frequently seen. Patients typically present with cytopenias or cytoses {11919391; 26276769}. Eosinophilia is very common but not universally present {26276769}. Most patients present with hepatosplenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy can be present.

The diagnosis is typically made in the chronic phase. Cases with ≥ 20% blasts at initial presentation or during the course of the disease are best classified as blast phase of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with FGFR1 fusion gene.

Epidemiology

The median age at presentation is in the fourth decade of life (range: 3–84 years). There is a mild male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.5:1) {26276769; 20226962}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

FGFR1 fusion genes lead to the formation of chimeric proteins that can self-dimerize and constitutively activate the tyrosine kinase FGFR1 and associated downstream signalling pathways. The function of the various partner genes is to foster FGFR1 dimerization and activation, thereby promoting activation of downstream pathways involved in oncogenesis {20226962}. Distinct FGFR1 fusion partners and their effect on different intracellular signalling pathways may contribute to the heterogeneous clinical/immunophenotypic presentation. For example, ZMYM2::FGFR1 is associated with a high incidence of T-lymphoblastic leukaemia; BCR::FGFR1 and TPR::FGFR1 have been associated with myeloproliferative neoplasms with basophilia, morphologically resembling chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) {15050920; 22619110}; and CEP43::FGFR1 and CNTRL::FGFR1 frequently impart a chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia–like phenotype. An association with a polycythaemia vera–like picture has been reported with CEP43::FGFR1 {9949182; 15570299}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Cases present as acute lymphoblastic (B- and T-), myeloid, or mixed-phenotype leukaemias. Bone marrow may be normocellular or hypercellular with eosinophilia. The immunophenotype, particularly in the chronic phase of disease, has not been extensively characterized; however, immunophenotyping studies are essential in the characterization of disease lineage. These neoplasms are commonly bilineal or multilineal, although only one component may predominate. Flow cytometric immunophenotyping may be more useful than immunohistochemistry in identifying small subclonal populations. Involved lymph nodes typically show diffuse effacement or interfollicular expansion with preservation of residual germinal centres. The neoplastic cells tend to localize to perivascular regions, often accompanied by mature-appearing eosinophils and increased high endothelial venules {1599015; 18162765}. Lymph nodes are typically involved by lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma, most often reported as being of T-cell lineage, but myeloid/T-cell neoplasms and (less often) myeloid sarcoma may be a manifestation. The association with lymphoblastic lymphoma/leukaemia of B lineage is rare {26276769}.

Cytology

Because of the heterogeneous nature of these neoplasms, a spectrum of cytological features can be observed, with proliferation of immature myeloid cells of intermediate size with irregular nuclear outlines often accompanied by small lymphoblasts. The accompanying eosinophils are often mature in appearance.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although RNA-sequencing methods are the gold standard for detecting the fusion gene product, these techniques are not readily available in many laboratories. Conventional karyotyping is the most readily available technique for identifying alterations of FGFR1 at 8p11.2. The diagnosis is then confirmed using FISH with break-apart probes for FGFR1. Alternatively, the fusion products may be detected by RT-PCR; however, the rarity of this entity has not fostered routine testing in most molecular diagnostic laboratories to date. The most common partner gene is ZMYM2 (ZNF198) / 13q12.11 {9425908; 9499416; 9716603}, followed by CNTRL and CEP43, but at least 14 partner genes have been described {9499416; 17868855; 20226962; 25873510; 28028030; 31802477}. The most common secondary cytogenetic abnormality is trisomy 21. Next-generation sequencing may identify additional somatic mutations {28495918}. Concurrent mutations involving RUNX1 are common and proposed to increase proliferation of the clone with FGFR1 fusion and contribute to disease progression and poor outcome {29119847; 29567772}. Monoclonal TR rearrangements are identified in a subset of cases.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: myeloproliferative or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm with prominent eosinophilia, with or without neutrophilia or monocytosis or with increased blasts of myeloid or T- or B-cell lineage, or of mixed phenotype; presence of an FGFR1 fusion gene by t(8;13)(p11.2;q12.1) or an alternative translocation partner.

Desirable: molecular identification of the partner gene of FGFR1.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The natural history of the disease follows an aggressive course, typically terminating in blast-phase transformation of either myeloid or T-cell phenotype in 1–2 years {34533850}. Intensive chemotherapy is of limited efficacy, with allogeneic stem cell transplantation offering the best chance of long-term remission. Limited but encouraging results have been seen with targeted therapy, but this is generally considered a bridge to stem cell transplantation {15448205}. Isolated incidences of the successful use of other small molecule inhibitors, such as ponatinib and futibatinib, resulting in haematological, cytogenetic, and molecular remissions, have been reported {22781593; 22875613; 26055304; 29767670; 32649766; 32923913}. Pemigatinib is currently undergoing evaluation, with preliminary phase II trial data showing a complete cytogenetic response rate of 76% [[Gotlib J, Kiladjian J-J, Vannucchi A, et al.
