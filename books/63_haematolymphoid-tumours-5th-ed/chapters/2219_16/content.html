2.2.1.9 Myeloproliferative neoplasm NOS (unclassifiable)

Definition

Myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) not otherwise specified (NOS) is a diagnosis of exclusion for cases that have definite clinical, laboratory, morphological, and molecular features of an MPN but fail to meet the diagnostic criteria for any of the specific types in this disease category.

ICD-O coding

9975/3 Myeloproliferative neoplasm, NOS (unclassifiable)

 

ICD-11 coding

2A4Z & XH5HH7 Myelodysplastic and myeloproliferative neoplasms, unspecified & Myeloproliferative neoplasm, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: myeloproliferative neoplasm, unclassifiable.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The blood and bone marrow are the major sites of involvement, but in advanced stages the spleen and liver as the major sites of extramedullary haematopoiesis may be also affected.

Clinical features

The clinical features are similar to those of other MPNs. In the early stages, organomegaly may be minimal or absent, but splenomegaly and hepatomegaly can be massive in advanced cases in which bone marrow specimens are characterized by marked myelofibrosis and/or increased blasts {27739437; 16781481}. The haematological values are also variable, ranging from mild leukocytosis to moderate or marked thrombocytosis, with or without accompanying anaemia. Prominent cytopenia or myelodysplastic features should always prompt the definitive exclusion of myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN) {26718907; 24146204}. Discrepancies between morphological and clinical features are particularly common in cases presenting with otherwise unexplained portal or splanchnic vein thrombosis {25863408; 25840747; 31721282}.

Epidemiology

The exact incidence of MPN-NOS is unknown, given that this is largely a diagnosis by exclusion. Therefore, thorough synthesis of clinical, morphological, and molecular features limits the frequency to ≤ 5% of all cases of MPN {26718907; 22990042; 27739437; 28109016; 24146204; 23897670; 33751548}.

Etiology

Like for other MPNs, the etiology is largely unknown. Some patients have an underlying hereditary predisposition to MPN {19287385; 19287384; 19287382; 34477817; 26574608; 26280900; 34172895; 33560389}.

Pathogenesis

Like in specific MPN types, driver mutations involving JAK2, CALR, or MPL are often also present in MPN-NOS. An increase in JAK/STAT signalling is the common denominator of the pathophysiology of MPN, including in patients who are triple-negative, i.e. do not show any of these three identified driver gene mutations. Mutations in epigenetic regulators, transcription factors, and signalling components are instrumental for disease initiation and contribute to disease progression, although no single mutation is pathognomonic. Furthermore, particularly in older patients, it is important to consider the possibility that any one of these (and other) mutations may be age-associated and cannot per se serve to label the disease as an MPN {25426837}. Nevertheless, the mutation landscape, including the three driver mutations and mutations in genes such as ASXL1, TET2, EZH2, or IDH1/IDH2, indicative of clonal haematopoiesis, provide clues to the disease mechanism {29212804}. It may be challenging to dissect the mechanisms in the infrequent cases where patients present with both JAK2 mutations and BCR::ABL1 fusion {27739437}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not applicable

Histopathology

Most cases that are diagnosed as MPN-NOS constitute very early stage disease, in which the differentiation between essential thrombocythaemia, primary myelofibrosis (PMF), and polycythaemia vera may be very difficult {24146204; 17587877; 19844986}. Bone marrow biopsy specimens frequently show hypercellularity and prominent megakaryocytic proliferation, with variable degrees of granulocytic and erythroid proliferation {24146204; 19844986; 16210032; 11064463; 11325641}. Occasional cases with normal blood counts but not meeting diagnostic criteria for other MPN types, and incidental isolated unexplained splenomegaly with characteristic MPN-associated driver mutations, may also be classified as MPN-NOS {24907356}. These cases are best classified as MPN-NOS until careful follow-up or additional laboratory studies provide sufficient evidence for a more precise diagnosis {24146204; 19844986}.

In late-stage disease, bone marrow specimens may show dense fibrosis and/or osteosclerosis, indicating a terminal burnt-out stage; every possible effort should be made to identify the specific type of MPN. If no previous history or histology is available, it may be impossible to distinguish between the postpolycythaemic stage of polycythaemia vera (post–polycythaemia vera myelofibrosis) or, rarely, essential thrombocythaemia (post–essential thrombocythaemia myelofibrosis) and the overt fibrotic/osteosclerotic stage of PMF {23787440; 15578448; 17587880; 16210032; 16781481}. Differential diagnosis with myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS), or MDS/MPN-NOS, associated with severe bone marrow fibrosis may be challenging in cases with multilineage dysplasia because of overlapping clinical and morphological features {23693053; 21282718; 19103730; 19605823}. In these cases, meticulous investigation with respect to splenomegaly, blood cell count, peripheral blood and bone marrow findings, presence or absence of MPN-associated mutations (JAK2, MPL, or CALR) and other mutations, and karyotypic findings may facilitate the diagnosis {24186132; 19605823}.

The presence of ≥ 10% blasts in the peripheral blood or bone marrow and/or the finding of significant dysplastic changes generally indicates a transition to a more aggressive, often terminal phase of the disease {23018876; 26336886}. Cases initially diagnosed as MPN-NOS in which 10–19% blasts are found in the peripheral blood or bone marrow are considered to be in the accelerated phase. It is important to rule out oligoblastic acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with defining genetic abnormalities, especially AML with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusion. The blast phase is defined on the basis of ≥ 20% blasts in the peripheral blood and/or bone marrow in a patient previously diagnosed with MPN-NOS. In most of these advanced-stage cases, fibrosis can cause dilution of bone marrow aspirates; therefore, immunohistochemical staining of the bone marrow biopsy sections for CD34 provides diagnostic value by demonstrating increased numbers and/or clusters or aggregates of blasts {16210032; 16781481}.

Common pitfalls include MPN-like presentations of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with FGFR1 rearrangements, particularly those showing minimal involvement by T or B lymphoblasts, and cases with ABL1 rearrangements should be diagnosed using morphological, immunophenotypic, cytogenetic, and molecular genetic analysis as appropriate. It is also necessary to rule out an underlying plasma cell neoplasm with a paraneoplastic leukaemoid reaction mimicking MPN {26218186}.

 

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

There is no cytogenetic or molecular genetic finding specific to this disease type. There is no rearrangement of PDGFRA, PDGFRB, or FGFR1, and no fusions involving JAK2 or ABL1, emphasizing the utility of comprehensive profiling for chromosomal alterations. The presence of a phenotypic driver mutation in JAK2, CALR, or MPL supports the diagnosis of MPN. Cases that do not meet the clinical and/or morphological criteria for a specific MPN type or any other specific disease category are best categorized as MPN-NOS. MPN-NOS is a diagnosis of exclusion and hence requires the presence of morphological changes of MPN on bone marrow examination, and a clonal genetic marker {33751548; 33144355}. The term MPN-NOS should not be used in instances where a full diagnostic workup was not performed. MPN-NOS is genetically heterogeneous despite improvements and refinements in the classification criteria for definitive subtypes of MPN, such as prefibrotic PMF and masked polycythaemia vera {28109016}. Studies have demonstrated that the majority of cases have mutations in MPN drivers: mostly JAK2, followed by CALR {27739437; 33751548; 25873496}. The remaining cases have mutations in one or more non-driver genes such as TET2, ASXL1, SRSF2, RUNX1, and DNMT3A {33751548}. Nearly 90% of cases show normal cytogenetics. The presence of certain genetic alterations such as MDS-defining cytogenetic abnormalities or the presence of isolated isochromosome 17q at diagnosis should prompt a thorough search for concurrent dysplastic changes, rule out secondary causes of proliferation, and prompt consideration of MDS or MDS/MPN {22331955; 22740453; 22038701; 26883102; 26883102}. The triple-negative nature of essential thrombocythaemia–like presentations should be confirmed with sensitive mutation assays {33144355}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of MPN-NOS is dependent upon the mutation landscape, cytogenetic abnormalities, patient age, comorbid conditions, and (presumably) factors not yet recognized as contributory. In cases that present very early in the disease course, before clinical and laboratory characteristics have fully developed, additional observation is generally needed to assess prognosis as indicated by the evolution into one of the well-characterized entities such as PMF, polycythaemia vera, or essential thrombocythaemia {34298741}. Some cases will have a worse prognosis than those that present with classic features of essential thrombocythaemia or polycythaemia vera (e.g. cases with non-driver ASXL1 mutations), so careful diagnostic annotation is important. For patients who present with advanced disease with an increase in myeloblasts, extensive fibrosis (often not restricted to the bone marrow), and extramedullary haematopoiesis, the prognosis is poor {32726410; 21531982}. Currently, the only strategy that may lead to long-term survival is allogeneic haematopoietic cell transplantation.
