2.4.1.5 Myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome

Definition

Myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome encompass two clonal conditions that arise in children with constitutional trisomy 21: transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM) and myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome (ML-DS).

ICD-O coding

9898/3 Myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome

9898/1 Transient abnormal myelopoiesis

ICD-11 coding

2A60.4Z Myeloid proliferation associated with Down syndrome, unspecified

2A60.4Y Other specified myeloid proliferation associated with Down syndrome

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Myeloid leukaemia associated with Down syndrome; transient abnormal myelopoiesis

Localization

TAM manifests mainly in the peripheral blood. Severe cases affect the liver, spleen, pleura, and pericardium {18182574; 21849481}. Bone marrow involvement is usually mild.

ML-DS manifests mainly in the bone marrow but can also involve the liver and spleen. CNS involvement is rare {18182574; 21849481}.

Clinical features

Clinically, TAM ranges from a life-threatening disease to an asymptomatic incidental finding. Most patients present within 7 days of birth. Severe disease manifests with hepatomegaly (40%), splenomegaly (30%), vesiculopapular skin rash (11%), and pleural and/or pericardial effusion (9%) {29916557}. Cholestatic jaundice is common (70%) but may not be specific to TAM. Leukocytosis with increased blasts is common and may be extreme (> 100 000 × 109/L). Anaemia is uncommon, and platelet counts may be normal, reduced, or increased {18182574; 21849481; 24021668}. Mortality in severe cases approaches 20%, usually from progressive liver failure. Asymptomatic disease is clinically silent but may manifest with peripheral blood blasts {24021668}.

For ML-DS, the median age of onset is 1.6 years {25092781; 28389462}. Overt ML-DS is preceded by a myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) phase with pancytopenia, which may last for several months {22392565; 25092781; 28400376}. Nearly half of all patients with ML-DS have a history of TAM {28389462; 22392565}.

Epidemiology

About 25–30% of newborns with Down syndrome have TAM (GATA1 mutation present) {24021668}. Clinical TAM occurs in 10% of newborns with Down syndrome, and another 15% have silent TAM (no clinical or haematological features) {24021668}. TAM is the most common leukaemia in neonates.

The incidence of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) is 150-fold higher in young children with Down syndrome than in the overall paediatric population {10675114}. ML-DS almost always presents before 4 years of age {18182574; 21849481; 28389462}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

TAM

In virtually all cases where a sensitive test is used, neonatal peripheral blood cells harbour one or more hemizygous, prenatally acquired somatic mutations in exon 2 or 3 of GATA1 {24021668; 12586620}. Somatic mutations in exon 2/3 of GATA1 encode a shorter GATA1 protein (GATA1s) lacking the N-terminal activation domain {12172547}. GATA1s is leukaemogenic only in trisomy 21 haematopoietic cells and thus causes TAM in newborns with Down syndrome {18182574; 16783379}. GATA1s disrupts fetal and early postnatal haematopoiesis, causing blast cell accumulation in the blood, liver, and other tissues {34244384}.

ML-DS

Exon 2/3 somatic GATA1 mutations identical to those in the neonatal period are present in all cases of ML-DS {24056718; 31303423; 14656875}. ML-DS is caused by additional acquired mutations, usually in cohesin, epigenetic regulator, or tyrosine kinase genes {24056718; 31303423}. Although ML-DS is caused by the combination of trisomy 21, GATA1s, and secondary mutations in other genes, the exact mechanism of ML-DS development is unknown.

 

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

TAM

Increased peripheral blood blasts are seen in all symptomatic cases. Blast morphology varies from undifferentiated myeloblasts to typical small megakaryoblasts with cytoplasmic blebbing or large, partially differentiated megakaryoblasts. TAM blasts usually express KIT (CD117) and show variable expression of CD34, CD7, CD36, CD41/42b, and/or CD235a (glycophorin A) {15858703}. Peripheral blood may show erythroid precursors with abnormal features and circulating megakaryocytes. Peripheral blood blasts almost always exceed bone marrow blasts.

ML-DS

Blast morphology and immunophenotype is the same as in TAM. Fibrosis may result in non-diagnostic aspirate samples. Bone marrow biopsy shows infiltration with blasts, which often (in 39–97% of patients) express megakaryocytic markers {18182574; 21849481; 29916557}. Bone marrow blasts may be < 20% in ML-DS, and reticulin fibrosis is frequent. Some cases show prominent proliferation of abnormal megakaryocytes.

 

Cytology

See above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

TAM

Diagnosis requires detection of exon 2/3 GATA1 mutations by DNA sequencing of peripheral blood cells {29916557}. Nearly all (> 95%) of the mutations are in exon 2, but large deletions may be difficult to detect {14656875; 21715302; 24056718; 31303423}. Cells with mutated GATA1 may be infrequent, so the rate of false negative results may be impacted by the sequencing platform (e.g. Sanger sequencing vs next-generation sequencing). GATA1 mutation analysis should be performed in the first week after birth, while peripheral blood blasts are present, to avoid false negatives {29916557}.

ML-DS

DNA sequencing for exon 2/3 GATA1 mutations should be performed in all cases of suspected ML-DS. This should be combined with mutation analysis using a myeloid gene panel that includes genes commonly mutated in ML-DS, such as cohesin complex genes (STAG2, RAD21, SMC1A, CTCF), EZH2, KANSL1, and JAK3 {24056718; 31303423}. Low blasts in bone marrow may cause false negative results. AML in children with Down syndrome who are aged > 4 years is rare and may represent an unrelated AML {18059480}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

TAM

Essential: confirmation of constitutional trisomy 21; peripheral blood leukocytosis with increased blasts; detection of exon 2/3 GATA1 mutation (GATA1 exon 2/3 sequencing should be performed in all cases with > 10% peripheral blood blasts).

ML-DS

Essential: confirmation of constitutional trisomy 21; a myeloid neoplasm with persistent increased peripheral blood and/or bone marrow blasts (may be < 20%); detection of exon 2/3 GATA1 mutation.

Desirable: mutation profiling and detection of mutations in other genes, e.g. cohesin complex, EZH2, KANSL1, and/or JAK3.

 

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

TAM resolves in 2–3 months without treatment in > 90% of patients. However, 20% of patients with clinical TAM later develop ML-DS {18182574; 21849481}. No clinical, haematological, or molecular features reliably predict which patients with TAM will develop ML-DS {18182574; 21849481; 24021668; 29916557}. Risk factors for early death in severe TAM include preterm delivery, ascites, leukocyte count > 100 × 109/L, hepatomegaly, and coagulopathy {18182574; 21849481; 29916557}.

ML-DS has a better prognosis than AML in children without Down syndrome, with event-free and overall survival rates of 87–89% and 89–93%, respectively, despite lower chemotherapy doses {28389462; 28400376}. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is reserved for relapse and is rarely performed.
