2.5.1.2 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement

Definition

Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement includes myeloid and lymphoid neoplasms and is frequently associated with eosinophilia. The majority of cases are characterized by cytogenetically cryptic deletion of 4q12, resulting in formation of the FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion gene, although other partner genes have been described.

ICD-O coding

9965/3 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA rearrangement

ICD-11 coding

2A50 Myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm associated with PDGFRA rearrangement

Related terminology

Not recommended: hypereosinophilic syndrome; chronic eosinophilic leukaemia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The peripheral blood and bone marrow are invariably involved. The lungs, heart, central and peripheral nervous system, skin, and gastrointestinal tract are among other frequently involved organs.

Clinical features

The most common symptoms include weakness and fatigue; pruritus; cough; dyspnoea; and other respiratory, cardiac, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Serious and irreversible complications (e.g. endomyocardial fibrosis, cardiomyopathy, cardiac valve injury, venous and arterial thromboembolic events, and pulmonary fibrosis) may occur as a result of tissue infiltration by eosinophils and/or cytokine-induced injury {12660384; 14973504}. Hepatosplenomegaly is common. Prominent eosinophilia is common, although rare cases without prominent eosinophilia have been reported {12660384; 12944919; 17377585; 22224867; 24203930}. Serum tryptase is often elevated (≥ 12 ng/mL) and can be a useful surrogate marker for FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion in the absence of appropriate molecular methods for detection of the fusion product {12676775}. Serum vitamin B12 levels are markedly increased {14973504; 31423623}. Relative and absolute neutrophilia may be seen; however, relative basophil and monocyte counts are typically within the normal range {16409293}. Anaemia and thrombocytopenia are variably present.

The majority of cases manifest as myeloproliferative neoplasms or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms, typically in the chronic phase and less frequently in the blast phase of myeloid or lymphoid lineage {17377585; 26797429}. The predominant lineage should be specified when possible.

Epidemiology

The overall incidence of this disease is rare. There is a strong male predominance (M:F ratio: ~7:1). The median age at presentation is in the fifth decade of life (range: 7–77 years). However, the vast majority of patients present between the ages of 25 and 55 years.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The formation of a PDGFRA fusion gene results in the neoplastic transformation of a pluripotent haematopoietic stem cell that can give rise to eosinophils, neutrophils, monocytes, mast cells, B cells, and T cells {16409293}. In vitro studies have suggested that FIP1L1::PDGFRA in human haematopoietic progenitors induces a myeloproliferative phenotype via the activation of multiple signalling molecules, including PI3K, ERK1/2, and STAT5 {17440089}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

The bone marrow is hypercellular and typically shows increased eosinophils and mast cells. Unlike the dense clusters of mast cells seen in the bone marrow of patients with systemic mastocytosis associated with the KIT p.D816V mutation, the mast cells associated with FIP1L1::PDGFRA are typically scattered or form loose clusters {14504092; 15284118}. Tissue infiltration by eosinophils with associated Charcot–Leyden crystals may be seen. Bone marrow reticulin fibrosis is common {14504092}. Bone marrow or tissue necrosis may be seen.

Eosinophils may show expression of activation markers such as CD23, CD25, and CD69 {14504092}. Increased mast cells can be highlighted using immunohistochemical stains for KIT (CD117) and tryptase. Like the mast cells of systemic mastocytosis, the mast cells in this disease often (but not always) exhibit aberrant expression of CD25 {12676775; 17377585}, but unlike in the mast cells of systemic mastocytosis, CD2 expression is typically negative {25658984}.

Bone marrow and peripheral blood blast counts are < 20% in most cases (chronic-phase disease). In cases with ≥ 20% blasts, a diagnosis of blast phase of myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with PDGFRA fusion is appropriate.

Cytology

Eosinophils may exhibit various cytological abnormalities, including increased size, abnormal distribution of cytoplasmic granules, cytoplasmic vacuolation, abnormal granule size or colour, and/or abnormal nuclear lobation {12660384; 14973504}. Eosinophils are predominantly composed of mature forms with a small proportion of eosinophil precursors (myelocytes/promyelocytes). Cytological atypia of eosinophils is not specific to this entity and may be seen in other neoplastic (i.e. chronic eosinophilic leukaemia) and reactive conditions {28495918}. In some cases, cytological atypia may be subtle or non-appreciable. Notably, abnormal cytological features of eosinophils may result in underestimation of the peripheral eosinophil count by automated cell counters. Mast cells may include increased atypical and spindle-shaped forms {15284118}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Laboratory evaluation for patients presenting with unexplained eosinophilia must include evaluation for PDGFRA rearrangement. Different modalities can be employed, but the most common form of testing includes nested RT-PCR {12660384} for FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion or interphase/metaphase FISH for PDGFRA rearrangement. Routine karyotype is typically diploid unless a rare, non-FIP1L1::PDGFRA fusion gene is suspected/identified. The FISH probes that hybridize to the region between the FIP1L1 and PDGFRA are used to detect a cytogenetically cryptic 800 kb deletion including the region containing CHIC2 located at 4q12 that results in the formation of FIP1L1::PDGFRA {12660384; 12842979}. Importantly, FISH or nested RT-PCR alone may miss occasional cases because of variant breakpoints or small clone size, and therefore both approaches should be considered in cases with a high index of suspicion {18987650; 31854104}. RNA sequencing is emerging as a robust alternative diagnostic approach. Monoclonal TR rearrangements are frequently identified {23982058}. Chromosomal breakpoints in the proximity of 4q12 are indicative of PDGFRA fusion genes with alternative partners and should prompt additional molecular workup. Additional cytogenetic abnormalities such as trisomy 8 tend to be associated with disease evolution {17377585}. Partner genes include but are not limited to KIF5B {16498388}, CDK5RAP2 {16845659}, STRN, ETV6 {17555450}, BCR {12023981; 15034867}, and TNKS2 {25658984}.

 

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a myeloid or (less frequently) lymphoid neoplasm, usually with prominent peripheral and/or tissue eosinophilia; presence of a PDGFRA fusion gene, usually with FIP1L1.

Desirable: in the absence of molecular demonstration of the fusion gene, the diagnosis should be suspected if there is a BCR::ABL1-negative myeloproliferative neoplasm with prominent eosinophilia associated with splenomegaly; marked elevation of serum vitamin B12, increased serum tryptase, and increased bone marrow mast cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The overall prognosis appears to be favourable, because the multikinase inhibitor imatinib has shown remarkable efficacy and is considered the definitive first-line therapy in patients with myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with a PDGFRA fusion gene {12660384; 15034867}. Imatinib has been shown to be effective in both the chronic phase and the blast phase of disease {17377585; 24669761}. Initial presentation with blast-phase disease and complex karyotype, as well as cardiac involvement, is associated with a poor prognosis. Acquired imatinib resistance is rare but has been reported {15618966; 19212337}, mostly resulting from the PDGFRA p.T674I point mutation involving the ATP-binding domain, and has been associated with the blast phase of the disease {16772610; 19210352}. Primary resistance to imatinib is exceedingly rare, with a single case report showing tandem PDGFRA mutations (p.S601P and p.L629P) in a patient with a chronic myeloid neoplasm with FIP1L1::PDGFRA {16772610; 19210352}. Alternative tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as midostaurin and sorafenib have shown efficacy in this setting and in patients with acquired imatinib resistance due to the p.T674I mutation, although their long-term effectiveness is unknown {12781364; 16030188}. FISH for PDGFRA rearrangement or nested RT-PCR can be used to assess response to therapy. Most patients have undetectable disease after a few months of imatinib therapy, and treatment-free remission may be achievable in select patients {31995156; 32720700}.
