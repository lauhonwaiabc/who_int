2.4.1.1 Myeloid neoplasms and proliferations associated with antecedent or predisposing conditions: Introduction

Myeloid neoplasms that arise secondary to exposure to cytotoxic therapy or germline predisposition are grouped in this category. The framework of the secondary myeloid neoplasms category was redesigned with two important aims: (1) providing a scalable structure for incorporating novel discoveries in germline predisposition to myeloid neoplasia; and (2) recognizing the dual value of cataloguing myeloid neoplasms that arise after exposure to cytotoxic therapies for clinical purposes as well as population health purposes. The latter factor is gaining increasing recognition as cancer survival is prolonged and the incidence of late complications of therapy, such as secondary myeloid neoplasia, is increased. An overarching principle in this context is the requirement to consider “post cytotoxic therapy” and “associated with germline [gene] variant” as disease attribute qualifiers that should be appended to the names of relevant myeloid disease types whose criteria are fulfilled as defined elsewhere in the classification; for example, acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) with KMT2A rearrangement post cytotoxic therapy, or MDS with low blasts associated with germline RUNX1 variant. A recommended workup is listed in #30677Box 2.26 . It should be noted that AML as transformation of myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) is retained in the MPN family, whereas AML as transformation of myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS) and myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN) are classified as a separate entity within the family of AML with defining genetic abnormalities (see section Acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia-related).

Myeloid neoplasms post cytotoxic therapy (MN-pCTs): These are defined as AML, MDS, and MDS/MPN that arise in patients exposed previously to cytotoxic (DNA-damaging) therapy for an unrelated condition. The terminology and definitions of this disease group have been modified slightly to reflect an improved understanding of the role that clonal haematopoiesis plays as a risk factor for myeloid neoplasia, related particularly to the expansion of pre-existing clones secondary to selection pressures of cytotoxic therapy agents in an altered marrow environment. Thus, the diagnosis of MN-pCT entails the fulfilment of criteria for a myeloid neoplasm in addition to a documented history of chemotherapy treatment or large-field radiation therapy for an unrelated neoplasm. This would exclude clonal cytopenia of unknown significance, which by definition is insufficient to support a finding of morphological dysplasia. Cases with a de novo molecular signature (such as NPM1 mutation) and core-binding factor (CBF) leukaemias should still be assigned to MN-pCT, because the “post cytotoxic therapy” designation is based on the medical history, and the indication of the most specific diagnosis in the pathology report is recommended when possible. Exposure to PARP1 inhibitors is added as a qualifying criterion for MN-pCT, whereas methotrexate exposure has been excluded. It is recommended that specification of the type of myeloid neoplasm is made, when possible, with “post cytotoxic therapy” appended (e.g. chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia post cytotoxic therapy).

Myeloid neoplasms associated with germline predisposition: These are defined as AML, MDS, MPN, and MDS/MPN that arise in individuals with genetic conditions associated with an increased risk of myeloid malignancies. Myeloid neoplasms arising in individuals with Fanconi anaemia, Down syndrome, and RASopathies are discussed in separate dedicated sections. These diseases are now classified using a formulaic approach that couples the myeloid disease phenotype with the predisposing germline genotype (e.g. AML with germline pathogenic variants in RUNX1). The clinical manifestations of these diseases are grouped into three subtypes under which most germline predisposition conditions can be assigned. Genetic counselling and evaluation of family history is an expected component of the diagnostic evaluation of index patients. Myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome, typically associated with somatic exon 2 or 3 GATA1 mutation, continue to encompass two clonal conditions that arise in children with constitutional trisomy 21: transient abnormal myelopoiesis, which is confined to the first 6 months of life, and myeloid leukaemia of Down syndrome. This model is designed to be scalable and adaptable to expansion as additional germline predisposition conditions are discovered in the future.
