14.0.6.2 Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2

Definition

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by activating mutations in the RET proto-oncogene, usually associated with medullary thyroid carcinoma, often with parathyroid proliferations, phaeochromocytomas, and mucosal neuromas.

Related terminology

Acceptable: MEN2 syndrome.

Not recommended: Sipple syndrome; multiple endocrine neoplasia type 3 (MEN3); Gorlin syndrome; Wagenmann–Froboese syndrome; mucosal neuroma syndrome; Farndon syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A); multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B (MEN2B; formerly MEN3)

Localization

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), which is the hallmark of this disorder, involves the thyroid gland; specifically, it arises most often at the junction of the upper third and lower two thirds of the lateral lobes of the gland. The disease begins as microscopic proliferations (classified as nodular C-cell hyperplasia) in this location, usually bilaterally, and progresses to multifocal invasive carcinoma.

Parathyroid proliferations arise in the parathyroid glands beside and below the thyroid in the neck. They are bilateral in 70% of patients.

Mucosal neuromas in MEN2B can involve the oropharynx, lips, and eyelids, with the addition of thickened corneal nerves and ganglioneuromatosis of the gastrointestinal tract with neuroma or diffuse ganglioneuromatosis. Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis involves the skin of the chest.

Clinical features

The disorder has several subtypes. MEN2A is the classic form, characterized by the development of MTC often associated with phaeochromocytoma and/or parathyroid proliferations. MEN2B, originally called MEN3, is a rare subtype characterized by a highly aggressive early-onset MTC associated with a high risk of phaeochromocytoma, oral and intestinal ganglioneuromas, and a marfanoid body habitus.

MTC usually presents as one or more neck masses; occasional patients have diarrhoea and flushing caused by calcitonin excess. Rarer presentations are due to phaeochromocytoma. Patients with Hirschsprung disease present in childhood with constipation, diarrhoea, vomiting, and abdominal distention. Patients with MEN2B have a marfanoid habitus, and ganglioneuromas may be seen on oral examination.

Increasingly, MEN2 is being diagnosed early, owing to the genetic screening of known kindreds.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of MEN2 (all subtypes) is about 1 per 30 000 population. The incidence of MEN2A is about 1 in 1 973 500 people; MEN2B is much rarer, at 1 in 38 750 000 people. The M:F ratio is about 1:1, but some studies have shown a slight female predominance.

Etiology

MEN2 is caused by monoallelic gain-of-function constitutional pathogenic sequence variants of RET. A negative family history does not exclude germline disease, because the disorder can skip generations or be misdiagnosed.

MEN2B is more often due to a de novo constitutional pathogenic sequence variant.

Pathogenesis

The RET proto-oncogene encodes a tyrosine kinase receptor that is activated by GDNF family ligands bound to GFRαs {32816064}. Intracellular RET signalling is needed for the development of the enteric nervous system, kidney, and urinary tract {32816064}.

MEN2A mutations mainly alter cysteines of codons 609, 611, 618, 620 (exon 10), or 634 (exon 11) of the extracellular domain {8918855; 21552134; 7907913}. RET p.C634R is the most frequent mutation and is associated with the classic triad of MTC, phaeochromocytoma, and hyperparathyroidism. Familial MTC mutations do not affect cysteines in exons 8, 13, 14, or 15, or codons 609–620. The p.A883T mutation only induced MTC in a family in a homozygous setting only. In about 2% of patients with MEN2, no mutation in the RET proto-oncogene can be detected {20516206}.

The vast majority of patients with MEN2B carry the RET p.M918T mutation in the kinase domain leading to a constitutive autophosphorylation. RET p.A883P is found in 2–3% of patients with MEN2B {9360560}.

Macroscopic appearance

MTC in this disorder is typically bilateral and multicentric. The cut surface of the tumours is white to yellow and homogeneous.

Mucosal neuromas in MEN2B can involve the oropharynx, lips, and eyelids.

Histopathology

MTC in MEN2 appears histologically indistinguishable from sporadic MTC, but familial cases are more often multifocal, bilateral, and associated with nodular C-cell hyperplasia.

Parathyroid proliferations occur in 15–30% of individuals with MEN2A but are not seen in MEN2B. They have been classified as adenomas and as so-called hyperplasia when there is multiglandular disease that is more likely multiple neoplasms involving single or multiple glands.

Mucosal neuromas in MEN2B can involve the oropharynx, lips, and eyelids, with the addition of thickened corneal nerves and ganglioneuromatosis of the gastrointestinal tract with neuroma or diffuse ganglioneuromatosis {4957444; 5660196; 11356339; 8880581}.

Cytology

Not applicable

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of MEN2 is confirmed by the identification of a constitutional pathogenic sequence variant in the RET gene.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a documented pathogenic sequence variant of the RET gene; and/or MTC associated with any of the other features of MEN2.

Desirable: nodular C-cell hyperplasia.

Staging

There is no uniformly accepted syndrome-specific staging system for MEN2. For the malignant neoplasms associated with this syndrome, staging generally follows the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis in MEN2 is determined by MTC and is superior to that of sporadic MTC, with 10-year survival rates depending on disease stage (currently 100%, 93%, 71%, and 21% for stages I, II, III, and IV, respectively) {9578814}. The mutation risk profile (see #26829Table 16.04) has led to recommendations for prophylactic thyroidectomy in mutation carriers in families with MEN2, with surgery performed before the age of 1 year for the highest-risk group, before the age of 5 years for those in the high-risk group with codon 634 mutations in MEN2A, and after the age of 10 years for those in the moderate-risk group {25810047; 28609830}.
