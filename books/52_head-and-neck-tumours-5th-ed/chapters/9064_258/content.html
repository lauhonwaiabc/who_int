9.0.6.4 Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Definition

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a clonal neoplastic proliferation of myeloid dendritic cells expressing the Langerhans cell phenotype.

ICD-O coding

9751/1 Langerhans cell histiocytosis, NOS

9751/1 Single-system Langerhans cell histiocytosis, with one organ/system involved (unifocal or multifocal)

9751/1 Multisystem Langerhans cell histiocytosis, with two or more organs/systems involved

9751/3 Langerhans cell histiocytosis, disseminated

ICD-11 coding

2B31.2 & XH1J18 Langerhans cell histiocytosis & Langerhans cell histiocytosis, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: Langerhans cell granulomatosis; histiocytosis X; eosinophilic granuloma; Hand–Schüller–Christian disease; Letterer–Siwe disease.

Subtype(s)

Single-system Langerhans cell histiocytosis, with one organ/system involved (unifocal or multifocal); multisystem Langerhans cell histiocytosis, with two or more organs/systems involved

Localization

Single-system LCH of the head and neck affects the cranial and facial bones, skin, gingiva, or cervical lymph nodes. In multisystem LCH, other organs (liver, spleen, and/or bone marrow) are also involved {25200852; 29164473}.

Clinical features

Unifocal LCH preferentially affects older children and adults; multifocal LCH affects young children; and multisystem LCH affects infants. Oral lesions represent the commonest manifestation, mainly in the form of an intraosseous, poorly defined, unilocular radiolucency with gingival involvement and tooth mobility. Diabetes insipidus occurs in cases of hypothalamic–pituitary involvement. Other findings include facial swelling and cervical lymphadenopathy. In multisystem LCH, cutaneous lesions may precede visceral compromise {25200852; 29164473}.

Epidemiology

The annual incidence rates in children and adults are, respectively, about 0.5 and 0.1–0.2 cases per 100 000 population {18260117}. The M:F ratio is 1.2:1 {30157397}. The disease is more common among people of European descent and Hispanic people. LCH can be associated with Erdheim–Chester disease {26966089}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The cell of origin is closer to a myeloid dendritic cell than to an epidermal Langerhans cell {20220088}. MAPK pathway mutations at a pluripotent haematopoietic, tissue-restricted, or local precursor level might give rise to multisystem, multifocal, or unifocal LCH, respectively {30157397}. These mutations may favour tissue-site accumulation via disrupted cell migration and apoptosis inhibition {29263218}. Rare familial cases are reported {11452942}. About 30% of cases show clonal IGH, IGK, and/or TR gene rearrangements {20551822}.

Macroscopic appearance

Not clinically relevant

Histopathology

Diagnostic LCH cells are oval (10–15 μm in diameter) with grooved, folded, indented, or lobed nuclei; fine chromatin; inconspicuous nucleoli; and thin nuclear membranes {12121233}. Nuclear atypia is minimal, but mitotic activity and the Ki-67 proliferation index are variable and can be high without atypical forms {12121233}. The cytoplasm is moderately abundant, slightly eosinophilic, and devoid of dendritic processes {12121233}. The characteristic milieu includes variable numbers of eosinophils, histiocytes, neutrophils, small lymphocytes, and plasma cells {12121233}. Occasionally, eosinophilic microabscesses with central necrosis are found. LCH cells predominate in early lesions but decrease in late lesions, with increased foamy macrophages and fibrosis. The ultrastructural hallmark is the cytoplasmic Birbeck granule (200–400 nm long and 33 nm wide, with a tennis-racket shape and a zipper-like appearance) {12121233}. LCH cells express CD1a, CD207 (langerin), S100, CD68, and HLA-DR {30157397}. BRAF p.V600E mutations can be identified by a specific antibody {28219109; 30923995}. Rare cases associated with follicular lymphoma or with B- or T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma are considered a transdifferentiation phenomenon and behave more aggressively {23759932; 15925822; 19642834}.

Cytology

LCH cell clusters are diagnostic, but caution is needed in lymph nodes because paracortical dendritic/Langerhans cells may be indistinguishable from sinus-based LCH cells.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In > 85% of cases, MAPK pathway gene mutations are recorded, affecting BRAF or alternatively MAP2K1, or (much more rarely) ARAF. ERBB3, NRAS, and KRAS mutations can also occur in adults {30157397}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: typical Langerhans cell morphology; CD1a, S100, and CD207 (langerin) positivity.

Desirable: detection of MAPK pathway mutations (in selected cases).

Staging

Staging is based on the subtypes and whether there is risk-organ involvement.

Prognosis and prediction

Stage is a more important prognosticator than age at presentation, with ≥ 99% survival for unifocal disease and < 20% mortality in risk-organ multisystem LCH with prolonged therapy {23589673; 12376981; 11496348; 30157397}. Progression from unifocal to multisystem LCH can occur, most commonly in infants with hypothalamic–pituitary involvement. Multisystem LCH can be complicated by macrophage activation syndrome {11910507}. BRAF p.V600E mutations correlate with an increased risk of relapse, resistance to first-line therapy, and neurodegenerative CNS LCH {24638167; 27382093}. In previously diagnosed LCH patients with BRAF p.V600E mutations, additional lesions with BRAF-positive, CD1a/CD207-negative histiocytes may still represent active disease {28194436}.
