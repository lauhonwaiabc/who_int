13.2.0.6 Vestibular schwannoma

Definition

Vestibular schwannoma is a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumour arising from the eighth cranial nerve, composed nearly entirely of differentiated neoplastic Schwann cells.

ICD-O coding

9560/0 Schwannoma, NOS

9560/0 Ancient schwannoma

9560/0 Cellular schwannoma

9560/0 Plexiform schwannoma

9560/0 Epithelioid schwannoma

9560/0 Microcystic/reticular schwannoma

ICD-11 coding

2F00.Y & XH98Z3 Other specified benign neoplasm of middle ear or respiratory system & Schwannoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: acoustic neuroma.

Not recommended: neurilemoma; neurinoma.

Subtype(s)

Ancient schwannoma; cellular schwannoma; plexiform schwannoma; epithelioid schwannoma; microcystic/reticular schwannoma

Localization

Vestibular or acoustic schwannoma derives from the vestibular division of the eighth cranial (vestibulocochlear) nerve {33826821} and accounts for > 90% of cerebellopontine angle tumours; cochlear division tumours are uncommon. Bilateral tumours are strongly associated with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) {19545378; 19652604}.

Clinical features

The majority of acoustic schwannomas are unilateral, but when bilateral, acoustic schwannoma defines NF2 {19652604; 33445724}. Symptoms relate to the specific nerve branch involved, mass effect, and/or tumour progression, usually combined. The majority (> 90%) of patients present with ipsilateral sensorineural hearing loss, frequently unrecognized by the patients. Other symptoms include vertigo, loss of balance (66%), and asymmetrical tinnitus (70%) {8971818; 9007856}. Facial numbness, pain, hydrocephalus, and cerebellar tonsil herniation are rare, usually seen in longstanding, large tumours {8971818; 9007856; 20844798; 20042905}. Multiple schwannomas are a feature of NF2 and schwannomatosis. In contrast to NF2, schwannomatosis is not associated with vestibular schwannomas {22210082}.

Imaging

Imaging studies must be performed to document tumour size, location, and extent, and they usually show a funnel-shaped widening of the internal auditory canal {29764781; 29764780; 31469788; 31504802; 32234257}.

Epidemiology

Acoustic schwannoma is the most common tumour occurring at the cerebellopontine angle, with an incidence of 1 case per 100 000 person-years. The incidence is lowest in Black populations and highest in Chinese populations {14967754; 16443943; 24786799; 30032646}, and the incidence has increased over time, reflecting improved imaging, improved kindred screening, and more rigorous protocols for the investigation of unilateral hearing loss {31504802; 33826821}. Patients commonly present in their fifth or sixth decade of life, but they are generally aged < 30 years when NF2 is associated. There is a slight female predominance {28441508; 28045630; 29315189; 30032646; 31504802}.

Etiology

Exposure to ionizing radiation may increase the risk of tumour development {3173432}, with conflicting data about occupational or recreational exposure to loud noise over extended periods {23725662; 24786799; 26996581; 2736213; 28213724}. Mutations in the tumour suppressor gene NF2 (somatic or germline) are common.

Pathogenesis

There is a causal relationship between schwannoma tumorigenesis and loss of merlin (NF2, schwannomin) expression, the protein product of the gene NF2, located at 22q12 {9403715}. The types of mutation in NF2 appear to affect disease severity and mortality {33445724}. Inactivating mutations have been detected in 50–75% of sporadic cases {8889506; 23921927; 26360373; 28409725}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumour size is variable, with most measuring < 15 mm. The tumour appears as a globular to mushroom-shaped mass, frequently attached to the nerve, with a smooth surface. Stretched nerve may be seen on the surface. Sectioned tumours reveal firm, light-tan, glistening tissue; cystic spaces; white/yellow areas; and/or patchy haemorrhage.

Histopathology

Tumours are encapsulated, showing compact hypercellular Antoni A areas with occasional nuclear palisading, alternating with loosely arranged Antoni B foci, although Antoni A areas are more common in vestibular schwannoma. Cellular zones demonstrate spindled cells with modest eosinophilic cytoplasm, no discernible cell borders, and elongated tapered to buckled nuclei. Microcystic to reticular areas may be seen. Mitoses are inconspicuous. Perivascular hyalinization is characteristic. Extensive, multifocal longitudinal nerve involvement is a clue to NF2 association {18422762; 28365909}. The subtypes are described in detail in the CNS volume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Central nervous system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 6). https://publications.iarc.who.int/601.]], with ancient schwannoma diagnosed when there are scattered atypical, pleomorphic, often hyperchromatic nuclei. Cellular schwannoma shows more closely packed cells with occasional mitoses. The remaining subtypes are rare in this site.

Immunohistochemistry

There is strong diffuse S100 and SOX10 immunoreactivity {22495377; 25724000}.

Differential diagnosis

Separation from meningioma, neurofibroma, solitary fibrous tumour, and paraganglioma is usually straightforward.

Cytology

Squash preparations are typically cellular with cohesive syncytial fragments of spindle cells. Within the fragments, variably wavy and bent tumour cell nuclei with tapered edges and fibrillary cytoplasm are seen {2677811; 22297471; 17922589}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not routinely performed

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a tumour arising from the eighth cranial nerve; dense spindle cell areas with loose hypocellular myxoid areas; spindled cells with buckled, elongated, fusiform nuclei, occasionally palisaded.

Desirable: S100/SOX10 in selected cases.

Staging

Staging is not applicable for tumour classification, but clinical tumour staging may be useful in management {29204572}.

Prognosis and prediction

Vestibular schwannomas are indolent tumours. Recurrence may develop in as many as 10% of cases, often due to incomplete removal. Long-term sequelae include meningitis, facial nerve paralysis, bilateral hearing loss, and vestibular dysfunction, and these complications are more frequent in patients aged > 65 years {9092842; 9092841; 8971819; 9007856; 31129783; 33826821}.
