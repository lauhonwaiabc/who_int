9.0.1.1 Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia

Definition

Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia is a non-neoplastic proliferation of lymphoid tissue in nodal or extranodal sites.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

MA01.Z Enlarged lymph nodes, unspecified

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia may involve the cervical lymph nodes, Waldeyer ring, salivary glands, and/or mucosal surfaces. Localization may vary by etiology.

Clinical features

Patients present with lymphadenopathy, enlarged tonsils or salivary glands, and/or mucosal ulceration. Symptoms vary by cause. Manifestations may be more severe in immunodeficient patients.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology varies according to the underlying disease (see #24644Table 10.01).

Etiology

Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia and lymphadenitis in the head and neck can be caused by viruses (EBV {31347374; 30773381; 20154586}, CMV {30773381; 26857321}, herpes simplex virus {27905140}, HIV {31060959; 32171271}), bacteria (Bartonella henselae {26551620}, pyogenic bacteria {29594802; 30626342; 26857321}), mycobacteria {31060959; 32171271; 31002716; 29594802}, autoimmune diseases (systemic lupus erythematosus {8853165}, Sjögren syndrome {11130833}, rheumatoid arthritis {17064872}), and diseases of unknown etiology (e.g. IgG4-related disease {20061932}, histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis [Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease] {3217625}, Kawasaki disease {25882057; 23020240}, Kimura disease {8936520; 15087670}, and atypical marginal zone hyperplasia of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue {15256428; 26794707; 21956819}).

Pathogenesis

Exposure to the inciting agent causes reactive changes in lymphoid tissue or the mucosa.

Macroscopic appearance

Reactive lymphoid hyperplasia produces enlarged lymph nodes, expanded extranodal lymphoid tissue, or ulceration. In salivary glands, small or large cysts may form due to ductal obstruction.

Histopathology

Depending on the etiology and disease phase, lymphoid tissue may show follicular hyperplasia, paracortical hyperplasia, monocytoid B-cell hyperplasia, immunoblastic reaction, granuloma formation (caseous or non-necrotizing), and/or suppurative inflammation. Certain reactive conditions, especially infectious mononucleosis {22627742}, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease {3217625}, and atypical marginal zone hyperplasia {15256428}, can mimic lymphoma. EBV+ mucocutaneous ulcer is a neoplastic rather than reactive lesion, but it is an important differential diagnosis for reactive lymphoid hyperplasia and lymphoma {20154586}.

Cytology

Aspiration biopsies of reactive lymphoid proliferations show a mixture of lymphoid cells, mimicking the reactive germinal centre, including immunoblasts and normal-appearing lymphocytes. Tingible-body macrophages may also be present. Occasional reactive lymphoid proliferations may show clonal B-cell populations by flow cytometry and should be interpreted with caution {15080297}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Occasional cases may show such distortion of normal architecture or cytological atypia as to raise the question of lymphoma. In such cases, gene rearrangement studies can be useful to show the absence of a clonal B- or T-cell population. EBV+ mucocutaneous ulcer can show B- and/or T-cell gene rearrangements, and the results should be interpreted in the context of other clinicopathological features.

Genetic techniques can assist with the identification of certain infectious agents {29594802}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: reactive lymphoid tissue changes with no evidence of lymphoma or other neoplasm.

Desirable: identification of an infectious agent by immunohistochemistry, special stains, microbiology culture, or molecular techniques; recognition of an underlying autoimmune disease, immunodeficiency, or other disease by clinical or laboratory criteria.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Outcome is dependent on the underlying disease and available therapy, but most cases due to infection resolve spontaneously or respond to antibiotics. Outcome in patients with autoimmune diseases or immunodeficiency is variable.

Bacterial infections

 

 

Pyogenic bacteria (Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, others)

Mainly children

Acute onset of lymphadenopathy

Acute inflammation +/− suppurative necrosis

Excellent prognosis with antibiotics

 

Bartonella henselae

Most patients < 30 years of age

Papule at inoculation site; tender, localized lymphadenopathy 1–2 weeks later +/− systemic symptoms; severe manifestations are rare

Classic histological features:

Early stage: Florid follicular and monocytoid B-cell hyperplasia with early necrosis among monocytoid B cells

Later stage: Stellate microabscesses

Complete recovery without specific therapy in nearly all cases; antibiotics can be given

 

Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Mainly adults, both immunocompetent and immunodeficient

Non-tender lymphadenopathy +/− draining sinuses, fever, fatigue, weight loss, pulmonary involvement

Necrotizing granulomatous lymphadenitis with caseous necrosis +/− suppurative necrosis; few AFB in immunocompetent patients; granulomas may be poorly formed with many AFB in immunodeficient patients

Favourable outcome with appropriate antibiotics in immunocompetent patients; worse outcome with drug resistance

 

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria, often Mycobacterium avium complex

Immunocompetent patients are children or adults; immunodeficient patients more often adults

Unilateral, non-tender lymphadenopathy; systemic symptoms and pulmonary involvement uncommon in immunocompetent patients; disease can be disseminated in immunodeficient patients

Necrotizing granulomatous lymphadenitis; necrosis is caseous or suppurative, with few AFB in immunocompetent patients; poorly formed granulomas or diffuse histiocytic infiltrates +/− necrosis with many AFB in immunodeficient patients

Excellent prognosis in immunocompetent patients; guarded prognosis in immunodeficient patients

Parasitic disease

 

Toxoplasma gondii

Mainly children and young adults

Unilateral or bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy; other forms of disease include encephalitis, chorioretinitis, disseminated infection, and congenital infection

Florid follicular and monocytoid B-cell hyperplasia with clusters of epithelioid histiocytes surrounding and infiltrating follicles; organisms only rarely found

Patients with lymphadenopathy: Complete recovery without specific therapy in nearly all cases; other forms of infection have worse outcome

Autoimmune diseases

 

Sjögren syndrome

Adults > 40 years; F > M

Dry eyes and dry mouth; some also have rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus

Lymphoepithelial sialadenitis: Follicular hyperplasia and prominent lymphoepithelial lesions

Increased risk of EMZL and DLBCL

 

Rheumatoid arthritis

Adults, F > M

Destructive arthritis +/− haematological, cardiovascular, neurological, and pulmonary abnormalities; lymphadenopathy is common

Lymph nodes typically show follicular hyperplasia and plasmacytosis and may show nodal hyalinization or sinus histiocytosis with neutrophils and/or iron deposition

Lymphadenopathy typically requires no specific therapy; other manifestations can be treated, but disease is often progressive, with significant morbidity

 

Systemic lupus erythematosus

Young and middle-aged adults; F > M; more common in African-American, Hispanic, and Asian populations

Lymphadenopathy can be localized (most often cervical) or generalized; fatigue, joint pain or stiffness, facial rash, fever, shortness of breath, chest pain, headaches, dry eyes, confusion, memory loss

Lymph nodes: Paracortical expansion by immunoblasts, histiocytes, apoptotic and amorphous debris; neutrophils and granulomas are absent; prominent plasma cells

Variable symptoms with periodic flares; treated with NSAIDs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biologicals; end organ damage in kidney, brain, lung, heart, and blood vessels

Diseases of unknown etiology

 

IgG4-related disease

Middle-aged and older adults, M > F

Enlargement of salivary glands (submandibular > parotid) and/or lacrimal gland and/or orbital soft tissue masses +/− lymphadenopathy +/− lesions beyond head and neck

Extranodal sites: Follicular hyperplasia, mixed infiltrate of eosinophils and many plasma cells; obliterative phlebitis; storiform fibrosis; increased number and percentage of IgG4+ plasma cells

Lymph nodes: Follicular and/or paracortical hyperplasia, some with PTGCs, some with sclerosis; some cases reminiscent of Castleman disease; increased number and percentage of IgG4+ plasma cells

Good response to steroids and/or rituximab in most cases

 

Histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis (Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease)

Adolescents and young adults, F > M

Cervical lymphadenopathy, fever, skin rash, +/− arthritis, fatigue, rare hepatosplenomegaly

Lymph nodes: Paracortical expansion by T-lineage immunoblasts, histiocytes, apoptotic and amorphous debris; neutrophils and granulomas are absent

Complete recovery without specific therapy in nearly all cases; rare recurrence

 

Kimura disease

Broad age range but mostly adolescents and young adults, M > F; more common in people of Asian descent

Unilateral, non-tender subcutaneous swelling with or without regional lymphadenopathy, parotid swelling, pruritis, peripheral blood eosinophilia

Extranodal sites: Eosinophil infiltrates with eosinophilic microabscesses, hyperplastic vascular structures

Lymph nodes: Reactive follicular hyperplasia, polykaryocytes, IgE on follicular dendritic cell meshworks, vascular hyperplasia

Serum: Elevated IgE

Resolution with surgery followed by corticosteroids; low-dose radiation for recurrences; monitor for glomerulonephritis (rare)

 

Kawasaki disease

Infants and young children, M > F

Acute onset cervical lymphadenopathy; fever; bilateral conjunctival injection; rash; swollen tongue, oral mucosa, palms, soles; irritability; peeling skin on hands and feet; joint pain

Lymph nodes: Non-suppurative lymphadenopathy (limited data)

Skin: Polymorphous exanthem, desquamation

Vessels: Medium-calibre vessel vasculitis, mixed inflammation, aneurysm formation, scarring

IVIG; echocardiogram mandatory for detecting coronary artery changes and myocardial dysfunction

 

Atypical marginal zone hyperplasia

Rare disorder of children aged 3–11 years

Unilateral or bilateral tonsillar enlargement or pharyngeal mass +/− dyspnoea or apnoea; other patients have involvement of appendix

Expanded marginal zones with numerous B cells invading crypt epithelium +/− invasion of germinal centres, composed of CD20+ B cells that are usually CD43+, CD21+, IgM+, IgD+, monotypic light chain +, IRF4 (MUM1)−, IRTA1+, high proliferation index; absent clonal IGH or Ig light chain by gene rearrangement studies

Patients alive and well after excision of lesion

 

AFB, acid-fast bacilli; CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; DLBCL, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma; EMZL, extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue; Ig, immunoglobulin; IVIG, intravenous immunoglobulin; OI, opportunistic infection; PTGC, progressively transformed germinal centres.
