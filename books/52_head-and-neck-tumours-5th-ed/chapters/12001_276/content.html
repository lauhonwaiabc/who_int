12.0.0.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasms and paraganglioma: Introduction

In this fifth-edition WHO classification of head and neck tumours, the classification of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) has been revised to reflect both the WHO / International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) unified terminology framework {30140036} and progress made in diagnostic biomarkers. The upper aerodigestive tract and salivary gland NENs are discussed as a group, but invasive/ectopic pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET) / pituitary adenoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, and paraganglioma are each presented in separate sections because of their distinct clinical and pathological characteristics. Previously classified as middle ear adenoma, middle ear neuroendocrine tumour (MeNET) is formally recognized as a NEN {34041698} but is discussed in the chapter on ear tumours because of its unique anatomical considerations.

In this new edition of the classification, the term “neuroendocrine carcinoma” (NEC) is applied only to poorly differentiated NENs including small cell and large cell NECs. Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) represent well-differentiated epithelial NENs and are assigned to three proliferative tumour grades (see #28634Table 15.01). Importantly at present, the grade 3 (G3) NET category remains provisional. Grade 2 (G2) NETs may exhibit necrosis and/or 2–10 mitoses/2 mm2, whereas grade 1 (G1) NETs lack necrosis and have < 2 mitoses/2 mm2. The Ki-67 proliferation index for NETs is generally < 20% {22082601; 26830400}, but the optimal Ki-67 cut-off level for the biological distinction between G1 and G2 NETs remains to be determined {34401980}.

Immunohistochemical biomarkers that are useful in the diagnostic workup of NENs are discussed in each section. Given the complexity of NENs, it is often insufficient to classify a tumour by chromogranin and synaptophysin alone. The importance of keratin immunohistochemistry and the immunohistochemical confirmation of diffuse neuroendocrine differentiation, including the emerging role of the transcription factor INSM1, is emphasized in the diagnostic workup of NECs.

The current WHO classification of PitNETs / pituitary adenomas requires the routine use of transcription factors (PIT1, SF1, TPIT, GATA3, and ERα) and antibodies against adenohypophysial hormones, as well as other biomarkers including but not limited to keratins. GATA3 expression alone should not be used to confirm PitNETs / pituitary adenomas, because GATA3 is also expressed in various other neoplasms including paragangliomas, the latter potentially arising from dispersed paraganglia that are closely aligned with components of the autonomous nervous system. The use of tyrosine hydroxylase may help distinguish paraganglioma in a keratin-negative NEN {27529763}.

Paraganglioma is the most heritable human neoplasm, with a reported rate of ≥ 40% genetic predisposition and a high probability of synchronous/metachronous multifocal disease {28162975; 33433885}. The value of molecular biomarkers and the Ki-67 proliferation index in predicting metastasis is highlighted. The significant proportion of head and neck region germline disease is linked to pathogenic variants in the SDH genes {29299512}. The paraganglioma section particularly expands on the role of molecular immunohistochemistry, which can be used as a screening tool to predict the pathogenetic basis of paragangliomas, including SDHB (SDH-related pathogenesis), α-inhibin (including the SDH-related and VHL-related pseudohypoxia pathway) {33826547}, and CAIX (VHL-related pseudohypoxia pathway) {23257898; 31383958; 33826547}.

Routine HPV testing in NECs is not recommended, because of a lack of proven prognostic benefit. The role of p53 and RB1 immunohistochemistry is discussed in the distinction between NET and NEC, especially G3 NET from NEC. Further work is ongoing to determine whether IDH2-mutated tumours are unique or represent a common pathway in several high-grade poorly differentiated malignancies of the sinonasal tract {31934917; 34265800}.

Merkel cell carcinoma is a high-grade cutaneous NEC that is distinguished from other NECs. The role of Merkel cell polyomavirus positivity and/or other immunohistochemical features supportive of Merkel cell carcinoma is discussed.
