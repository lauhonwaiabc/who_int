9.0.3.5 Plasmablastic lymphoma

Definition

Plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) is an aggressive large B-cell lymphoma composed of a diffuse proliferation of large cells resembling immunoblasts or plasmablasts, lacking mature B-cell markers and expressing plasma cell differentiation antigens.

ICD-O coding

9735/3 Plasmablastic lymphoma

ICD-11 coding

2A81.2 Plasmablastic lymphoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The most frequent site of involvement in the head and neck region is the oral cavity, although cases in the nasal and respiratory sinuses have been reported. Nodal presentations (< 10% overall) are reported more frequently in posttransplant settings (30%) {12786898; 15166665; 16327436; 9242574}.

Clinical features

A tumour mass in the oral cavity is the most common presentation. However, patients usually show advanced-stage disease at diagnosis. Disseminated disease is seen in 75% of HIV-positive patients, 50% of posttransplant patients, and 25% of patients without apparent immunodeficiency {15166665; 25636338}. Some cases in HIV-positive patients may have overlapping features with multiple myeloma, such as lytic bone lesions and paraprotein {20348882}.

Epidemiology

PBL is an uncommon type of large B-cell lymphoma that occurs predominantly in adults and with increased frequency in immunocompromised states (e.g. HIV infection, posttransplant patients, autoimmune diseases, and immunosenescence) {17944927; 9028965; 22958176}.

Etiology

In HIV-positive and posttransplant patients, EBV, with type I latency and occasional LMP1 expression, is frequently observed {25636338}. By definition, KSHV/HHV8 is absent {9028965; 15166665; 16327436; 9242574}.

Pathogenesis

PBL originates from a terminally differentiated B cell that has switched to a plasma cell programme. MYC rearrangement is detected in 50% of cases, and these cases are commonly associated with EBV infection (74%) {23599149; 20962620}. PBL has a mutation profile that differs from that of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of the activated B-cell subtype and multiple myeloma, with frequent mutations in genes of the MAPK (25–50%) (NRAS, KRAS) and JAK/STAT (40%) (STAT3, JAK2, SOCS1) signalling pathways {27687004; 33225311; 33951889}. The microRNA expression profile of PBL has revealed a number of EBV-encoded microRNAs that suppress the transcription of tumour suppressor genes (PTEN, PBX2) and lipid metabolism controller genes (PPARGC1A; PLIN2, encoding adipophilin) suggesting a pathogenetic role of EBV-encoded microRNA in the lymphomagenesis of EBV-positive cases {29296171}. EBV-positive PBLs have molecular properties and pathogenetic mechanisms distinct from those of EBV-negative PBL, similar to those observed in other lymphoma types {28419429; 31403034; 26468873}.

Macroscopic appearance

Involved organs are replaced by masses with a fish-flesh appearance, often associated with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis.

Histopathology

PBL may display a variety of morphological features. Monomorphic PBL shows diffuse, cohesive sheets of large cells, most resembling immunoblasts with inconspicuous plasmacytic features and sometimes with a starry-sky pattern. Some cases may have cells with more overt plasmacytic differentiation, showing round, eccentric nuclei; coarser chromatin; ample cytoplasm; and paranuclear hof. Areas of geographical necrosis are seen with low numbers of tumour-infiltrating T cells.

PBL shows a terminally differentiated B-cell/plasma-cell phenotype (CD38+, CD138+, IRF4 [MUM1]+) without expression of common B-cell markers (CD45, CD20, PAX5), although CD79a positivity is reported in 40% of cases {25636338; 20418245; 23599149}. In oral cavity PBL, CD56 is usually negative, but in other sites it is frequently positive (25%). In situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is commonly observed (60–75%).

Differential diagnosis

Key features distinguishing between PBL and plasma cell neoplasms are the clinical presentation, high proliferation index, and EBV positivity in the former. Possible transformation of plasmacytoma into PBL has been hypothesized to be triggered by EBV reactivation, and this event demands a precise diagnosis {25193957}. When a clear distinction may be difficult, a descriptive diagnosis such as “plasmablastic neoplasm, indeterminate between PBL and anaplastic plasmacytoma” can be used. The key feature to discriminate PBL from large B-cell lymphomas (e.g. diffuse large B-cell lymphoma with plasmablastic features and EBV+ diffuse large B-cell lymphoma NOS) is essentially the maintenance of the B-cell programme, namely the strong expression of CD20, CD79a, and PAX5, in the latter. The cohesive growth, starry-sky pattern, and high proliferation index can suggest Burkitt lymphoma, but PBL can be distinguished by CD20 and PAX5 negativity.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a lymphoma with immunoblastic or plasmablastic morphology; immunophenotypic features of terminal B-cell differentiation (loss of conventional B-cell markers; expression of plasma cell markers); lack of ALK expression; KSHV/HHV8 negativity.

Staging

The predominantly extranodal distribution and high stage at presentation of PBL makes Lugano staging suboptimal.

Prognosis and prediction

Overall survival is short (6–11 months) despite therapeutic intervention. The presence of MYC translocation has been associated with an inferior prognosis {25636338; 24832164}.
