13.2.0.2 Cholesteatoma

Definition

Cholesteatoma is a non-neoplastic but destructive middle ear accumulation of keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium and keratinous debris with fibrosis and an inflammatory reaction.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

AB12 Cholesteatoma of middle ear

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Congenital cholesteatoma; acquired cholesteatoma

Localization

Congenital cholesteatoma arises unilaterally in the middle ear, medial to an intact tympanic membrane in the anterosuperior quadrant closely followed by the posterosuperior quadrant {31955213}. Acquired forms present in a posterior mesotympanic or posterior epitympanic location {26747599}. Bilateral disease is rare. External auditory canal presentation is reported but more likely represents a distinctly different entity: keratosis obturans {25422282; 23235551; 21839307}.

Clinical features

Patients present with unrelenting or recurrent foul-smelling otorrhoea, and tinnitus with progressive hearing impairment (due to eroded ossicles) {28059056}. Petrosal involvement can result in facial paralysis, vertigo, diplopia, and blurred vision {31876216}.

Imaging

Imaging studies (CT and diffusion-weighted – especially non-echoplanar – MRI) are complementary studies that demonstrate a non-enhancing soft tissue mass in the Prussak space with scutum erosion (in ~80% of cases) or an erosive mass in the posterior mesotympanum, medial to the ossicles (in ~15%). Involvement of ossicles, tegmen tympani, mastoid, or facial nerve canal may be seen. Importantly, surrounding granulation tissue may enhance {28969854; 28634512; 10845037}.

Epidemiology

The worldwide prevalence is about 5 million cases and is decreasing, owing to the widespread use of ventilation tubes {21739009; 25866816}. The reported annual incidence is 3–14 cases per 100 000 children and 9.2 cases per 100 000 adults, with an M:F ratio of 1.4:1 {10478597; 12835944}. White populations show the highest prevalence, and the lowest prevalence is in Inuit, Native American, and Asian populations {20360335}. Patients with syndromes involving dysmorphic ears and/or cleft palate develop cholesteatoma disproportionately more often than the general population {23860365; 23931986; 25151219; 28941966}.

Etiology

Congenital cholesteatoma develops medial to an intact tympanic membrane from persistent embryonic epithelial rests that proliferate {30069838; 27171804}. Inferred to form via multiple mechanisms, acquired cholesteatoma results from traumatic or iatrogenic tympanic membrane perforation and subsequent migration of squamous epithelium into the middle ear. Retraction pockets caused by negative pressure in the middle ear and subsequent accumulation of desquamated keratin results in cholesteatoma {25866816; 11078064}. Metaplastic transformation of middle ear mucosa into keratinizing epithelium is another suggested pathway to development {25123251}.

Pathogenesis

Congenital cholesteatoma is thought to develop from the fetal epidermoid formation or persistent embryonic epithelial rests. Pathogenesis of acquired cholesteatoma is incomplete, with the most widely accepted theory being that of tympanic membrane retraction into the middle ear space, resulting in an accumulation of desquamated keratin with trapped bacteria, leading to infection, increased inflammation, and the release of cytokines that results in epithelial proliferation {17097438; 17697435; 19467409; 2926259}.

Macroscopic appearance

When intact, there is a white, ovoid, compressible mass surrounded by a thin wall. Disrupted lesions show an irregular, shaggy, friable surface with oedematous edges. Spilled sac contents display flaky, greasy, or cheese-like keratinaceous debris {30069838}.

Histopathology

Keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium, granulation tissue, and keratinaceous debris characterized as perimatrix, matrix, and cystic contents are noted. The peripheral perimatrix consists of granulation tissue, with the subepithelial area consisting of cellular, thickened, dense fibrous connective tissue. The epithelial matrix consists of keratinizing, cytologically bland, thin to atrophic stratified squamous epithelium arranged in convoluted formations, lacking rete ridges, and showing a prominent granular cell layer with abundant keratohyalin granules. The cystic contents comprise abundant layers of laminated, free-flowing or aggregated, desquamated, anucleate keratin. Haemorrhage, haemosiderin-laden macrophages, and (when ruptured) a foreign body giant cell reaction are noted {30069838}.

Differential diagnosis

Separation from cholesterol granuloma, otic polyp, and squamous cell carcinoma is generally straightforward.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

None

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: keratinizing squamous epithelium with keratin debris, a prominent granular layer, and inflammation.

Desirable: origin from the middle ear.

Staging

Clinical staging reflects severity, difficulty of complete excision, and restoration of normal function, and is defined as follows {28059056}:

Stage I: Cholesteatoma localized in the primary site such as the attic or tympanic cavity Stage II: Involvement of ≥ 2 sites Stage III: Cholesteatoma with extracranial complications or pathological conditions Stage IV: Cholesteatoma with intracranial complications

Prognosis and prediction

Long-term follow-up is necessary because recurrences are common {28953605}, especially with ossicular involvement or mastoid bone disease {28543174; 24882923; 27565391; 28274504}.
