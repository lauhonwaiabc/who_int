14.0.6.3 Cowden syndrome

Definition

Cowden syndrome (CS) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by multiple hamartomas involving organs derived from any of the three germ layers, with a predisposition to cancer, frequently associated with a constitutional pathogenic variant in PTEN.

Related terminology

Acceptable: PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome; Cowden disease; multiple hamartoma syndrome.

Subtype(s)

PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS) is a heterogeneous group of disorders with autosomal dominant inheritance, caused by constitutional pathogenic variants of the PTEN gene. PHTS also includes Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome and Proteus syndrome; however, most cases of PHTS correspond to CS, and the terms “PHTS” and “CS” are often used interchangeably {19668082}.

Localization

Oral papillomas are a major diagnostic criterion for CS and are present in virtually all patients with CS. Papillomas are usually present on the lips but are also common on the tongue, buccal mucosa, and gingivae. Mucocutaneous neuromas have also been reported in patients with CS. Facial trichilemmomas are a major diagnostic criterion for CS. Trichilemmomas are typically multiple and found in the central portion of the face, around the eyes, nose, mouth, and forehead {26975628; 27889943; 24560406}.

Clinical features

CS is characterized by mucocutaneous lesions (multiple facial trichilemmomas, acral keratoses, papillomatous papules, and mucosal lesions are considered pathognomonic), an increased cancer risk, benign hamartomatous overgrowth of tissues (including gastrointestinal polyposis), and macrocephaly (see #25223Box 16.02) {24136893; 26975628}. Diffuse oesophageal glycogenic acanthosis in combination with colonic polyposis may be diagnostic of CS {21437855; 23238744}. Cancer risk is relatively broad, with a risk of breast, thyroid, endometrial, renal cell, and colon cancers; melanoma; and other cancers (see #25224Table 16.02) {20301661}.

Trichilemmomas present in the central portion of the face, including the eyes, nose, mouth, and forehead, and mucocutaneous papillomatous papules are one of the first signs of the disease. Facial lesions occur in the majority of patients. The most distinctive and peculiar facial features of CS consist of multiple small and keratotic papules concentrated around the orifices. A trichilemmoma is rarely a sporadic feature, and the literature regularly reports numerous lesions at presentation in individuals with a clinical and/or genetic diagnosis {24136893}.

Consequently, trichilemmoma is a clinically significant sign of CS when seen in multiplicity (≥ 3), but ≥ 1 lesion should be biopsy-proven given the difficulty with clinical diagnosis. Early recognition of these skin lesions may help in diagnosing an underlying malignancy and early cancer screening {26975628}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence has been estimated at about 1 case per 200 000–250 000 individuals in a European population. This may be an underestimate, given the difficulty in diagnosing this syndrome {10234502}.

Etiology

CS is an autosomal dominant disorder with age-related penetrance and variable expression. Constitutional pathogenic sequence variants in PTEN (10q23.3) are found in about 85% of CS cases, as well as in subsets of other PHTS disorders (in 65% of Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome cases, 20% of Proteus syndrome cases, and 50% of Proteus-like syndrome cases) {19668082; 18794875; 9140396; 8673088}. Individualized risk calculation may aid in the assessment of a patient’s risk of carrying a constitutional pathogenic PTEN sequence variant based on clinical features {21194675}.

Constitutional pathogenic succinate dehydrogenase sequence variants have been found in about 5% of PTEN mutation–negative CS / CS-like individuals and are associated with increased frequencies of breast, thyroid, and renal cancers beyond those conferred by constitutional pathogenic PTEN variants {18678321}.

Pathogenesis

PTEN is a virtually ubiquitously expressed tumour suppressor and a dual-specificity lipid and protein phosphatase that regulates cell proliferation, cell migration, and apoptosis through the inhibition of AKT via the PI3K/AKT pathway {23613428}. Inactivation of the second copy of the gene allows deregulation of the AKT pathway.

Macroscopic appearance

Mucosal papillomas present as small, smooth, whitish papules that often coalesce, resulting in the characteristic cobblestone appearance on the lips, gingiva, buccal mucosa, or tongue mucosa. The presence of a fissured tongue is also considered a characteristic feature of CS {26975628; 27889943; 24560406}.

Histopathology

Oral papillomas are histologically nonspecific and indistinguishable from irritation fibroma. They are composed of fibrous connective tissue that may contain some dilated blood vessels and inflammatory infiltrate, and lined by hyperplastic non-keratinizing squamous epithelium. The skin lesions and other tumours associated with CS lose PTEN staining by immunohistochemistry {30730313}.

Clinically, trichilemmomas are often indistinguishable from other verrucous skin lesions, and therefore histopathological confirmation is necessary. Trichilemmomas are typically associated with pre-existing hair follicles and characterized by thickening of the follicular infundibulum, with superficial hypergranulosis and hyperkeratosis and focal trichilemmal differentiation. Trichilemmomas can be difficult to distinguish from basal cell carcinoma with trichilemmal differentiation, and the surface of trichilemmoma may be indistinguishable from verruca vulgaris.

One of the characteristic findings in Cowden syndrome is the presence of multiple thyroid adenomatous nodules and hyperplastic nodules.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with PHTS are at increased risk of several types of cancer, and interval screening is recommended (see #25224Table 16.02) {22252256; 23613428; 23335809}.
