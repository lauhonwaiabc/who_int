11.0.0.1 Tumours and tumour-like lesions of the neck and lymph nodes: Introduction

The neck is divided into the anterior and posterior triangles by the sternocleidomastoid muscle. The contents of the neck include lymph nodes, soft tissues, vascular/lymphatic structures, and nerves. Excluding cutaneous lesions/neoplasms, owing to the various tissue types, the neck is host to a wide variety of primary non-neoplastic lesions and primary neoplasms. The spectrum of haematolymphoid and mesenchymal neoplasms is beyond the scope of this chapter. This chapter will focus on select tumours and tumour-like lesions. Noteworthy is the branchioma, previously referred to as ectopic hamartomatous thymoma. Whether lymphoepithelial cyst is an independent entity, separate from branchial cleft cysts and HIV-related or HIV-unrelated cystic lesions of the parotid gland, is debatable, but it has been added to this chapter. Heterotopia-associated carcinoma, an entity introduced in the prior edition, primarily relates to papillary thyroid carcinoma arising in association with heterotopic thyroid tissue in the neck, the latter associated with thyroglossal duct cyst remnants. A less well recognized phenomenon is the intranodal origin of salivary gland lesions/neoplasms (benign and malignant) wholly without involvement of the salivary gland parenchyma.

The most common malignancies of the neck are metastases from primary head and neck cancers, in particular squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma or, less commonly, from non–head and neck primary cancers. However, the head and neck may harbour a small, clinically occult primary carcinoma with numerous positive neck nodes. Among such carcinomas of unknown primary include oropharyngeal HPV-associated carcinomas, oropharyngeal neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) {21997688; 22301491; 30468701}, and nasopharyngeal EBV-related carcinomas. Pathologists should have an understanding of the topographical anatomy of the cervical neck lymph node levels, as well as an understanding of lymphatic drainage from site-specific mucosal sites of the head and neck, which can provide a possible clue in identifying the primary source of the metastasis (see #26425Table 12.01). It is equally imperative that clinicians provide the exact localization of the involved nodes, as opposed to “neck mass”, in order to assist the pathologist in determining the origin of the metastasis.
