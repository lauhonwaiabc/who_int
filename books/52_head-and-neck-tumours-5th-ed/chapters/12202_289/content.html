12.2.0.2 Head and neck paraganglioma

Definition

Head and neck paragangliomas are non-epithelial neoplasms derived from paraganglion cells of the autonomic nervous system.

ICD-O coding

8693/3 Head and neck paraganglioma

8693/3 Composite paraganglioma

8692/3 Carotid body paraganglioma

8693/3 Vagal paraganglioma

8690/3 Middle ear paraganglioma

ICD-11 coding

2D12.Y & XH5LK3 Other specified malignant neoplasms of other endocrine glands or related structures & Parasympathetic paraganglioma

Related terminology

Acceptable: paraganglioma; SDH-deficient paraganglioma.

Not recommended: glomus tumour; carotid body tumour; glomus caroticum; glomus jugulare; glomus tympanicum; chemodectoma.

Subtype(s)

Composite paraganglioma; carotid body paraganglioma; vagal paraganglioma; middle ear paraganglioma; laryngeal paraganglioma

Localization

Tumours are typically associated with the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves. Although the terms “parasympathetic” and “head and neck” are generally considered synonyms, head and neck lesions may include sympathetic paragangliomas associated with the cervical sympathetic chains {11400237; 16782577; 20310044; 24652566; 33100776} or arise from paraganglia with mixed sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation {11889402}.

Tumours develop most often around the carotid body (40–60%), middle ear (jugulotympanic; 30%), vagal nerve trunk in or near the nodose ganglion (10%), and sympathetic chain (4%); rarely, they develop in the larynx, thyroid gland, parathyroid gland, mandible, parotid gland, nasopharynx and paranasal sinuses, sella turcica, orbit, and clivus {9236830; 20237987; 28299533; 30217041}. Bilateral/multifocal paragangliomas account for 10–40% of cases {28321772; 29299512}.

Clinical features

Carotid body paragangliomas present as asymptomatic mass lesions. Middle ear paragangliomas present with pulsatile tinnitus, hearing abnormalities, or a sensation of aural fullness. Large or infiltrative tumours and most vagal paragangliomas are associated with cranial nerve symptoms {28321772}. Horner syndrome and first bite syndrome are associated with sympathetic paragangliomas. Only about 4% of head and neck paragangliomas are biochemically functional, producing predominantly dopamine and/or 3-methoxytyramine. Occasionally norepinephrine or normetanephrine is elevated {17400487; 19328652; 22431860; 23526121; 24119888; 31168271}, but elevated metanephrine/epinephrine levels usually indicate a concurrent sympathetic paraganglioma/phaeochromocytoma {29299512}. Increasingly, tumours are discovered by screening families with known genetic predisposition syndromes (see #27513Table 16.03, in section Familial paraganglioma syndromes). These patients require lifelong surveillance and management tailored to specific genotypes.

Imaging

Paragangliomas are detected with the highest sensitivity by PET-CT {29204718}; 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT is most commonly available in North America, and other methods use 68Ga-DOTATOC or 68Ga-DOTANOC. This technique has the added advantage of predicting the efficacy of somatostatin-based therapies including medical therapy {25554089} and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT). Other techniques (iobenguane [metaiodobenzylguanidine; 131I-MIBG], SSTR scintigraphy with 111In-pentetreotide) have significant limitations but are useful if other methods are unavailable {14602858; 18632829; 19190077; 22399235}. FDG PET-CT is not very sensitive for sporadic disease but is valuable in patients with SDHB-related disease {25873086}.

Epidemiology

Paragangliomas represent < 0.5% of head and neck tumours, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 100 000 person-years {29299512}. They can occur from childhood to old age, with a mean age at presentation in the sixth decade of life {29299512}. In contrast to their sympathetic counterparts, they show a definite female predominance (M:F ratio: 1:1.7) {11701678; 19223516}. Paediatric cases reported in the teenage years are rare, with 9–23% of paragangliomas affecting the head and neck in this population {32432211; 32491270}.

Etiology

Paragangliomas have a hereditary predisposition in ≥ 40% of patients, approaching 85% of patients of all ages tested in some series {29299512}. Consequently, there is a high probability of synchronous or metachronous multicentric paragangliomas, which may be confined to the head and neck or occasionally include sympathetic paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas {15774781; 29299512}.

Pathogenesis

Genetic factors

The most common germline mutation in patients with head and neck paragangliomas is in SDHD (47%), followed by SDHB (30%) and SDHC (16%) {29299512}. SDHD mutation is associated predominantly with single or multifocal tumours in the head and neck, whereas SDHB mutation is more often associated with thoracoabdominal tumours {22584701}. Paragangliomas in the head and neck are rarely associated with mutations of VHL, RET, or NF1 {22584701}. A rare, syndromic but non-hereditary association is Carney triad, where most cases show downregulation of SDH through site-specific hypermethylation of SDHC {24859990; 25540324}.

Risk factors

For carotid body paragangliomas in particular, tumour incidence is increased and there is a female predominance in some populations living at high altitudes, probably reflecting the effects of hypoxia as a phenotypic modifier in patients with SDHD {12811540; 29681642} or SDHB {20592014} mutations. An association with congenital cyanotic heart disease has also been reported {26961564}. Although the SDHD mutation can be transmitted by either a male or a female carrier, tumours usually occur only after paternal transmission, causing skipped generations {26067997}.

Macroscopic appearance

Head and neck paragangliomas are typically encapsulated, pink to tan, firm nodules. There may be areas of haemorrhage, especially after preoperative embolization (frequently employed in head and neck sites).

Histopathology

Tumours exhibit a variably present Zellballen architecture of chief cells with abundant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm with slightly to moderately atypical nuclei, and peripherally located sustentacular cells, usually difficult to detect in H&E sections. The stroma is highly vascular. Mitotic figures are usually rare.

Composite paraganglioma, composed of paraganglioma admixed with ganglioneuroma, is extremely rare in the head and neck.

Chief cells in head and neck paragangliomas usually show nuclear staining for the transcription factors INSM1 and GATA3 {29438167; 32813226}, whereas cytoplasmic functional markers of neuroendocrine differentiation, including synaptophysin and chromogranin A, are highly variable. The catecholamine-synthesizing enzymes tyrosine hydroxylase and DBH are expressed in approximately 30% and 10%, respectively, of head and neck paragangliomas {25677368; 34743284}. A recent study showed expression of choline acetyltransferase (an enzyme involved in acetylcholine synthesis) in virtually all head and neck paragangliomas, even in the absence of expression of catecholamine-synthesizing enzymes {34743284}. However, the specificity of this biomarker remains to be validated in additional studies. Chief cells are usually non-reactive for keratins {33098891}. Sustentacular cells express S100, SOX10, and (uncommonly) GFAP {32548761}. Loss of reactivity for SDHB is associated with mutations of SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, and SDHAF2 {19576851}. α-Inhibin is expressed in pseudohypoxic tumours (including SDH-related and VHL-related types) {33826547}, and CAIX is expressed strongly in VHL-associated tumours {23257898; 31383958; 33826547}. Paragangliomas express SSTR {14602858}, and SDH-deficient paragangliomas have been reported to demonstrate stronger staining for SSTR2A and SSTR3 {19936639}.

Differential diagnosis

Numerous primary and metastatic tumours constitute the differential diagnosis in head and neck regions, including epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the larynx {28247224}, parathyroids {27406876}, pituitary {30217041}, middle ear and temporal bone, and thyroid gland (medullary thyroid carcinoma) {28493102}. Olfactory neuroblastoma, Merkel cell carcinoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma may also be considerations.

Negativity for keratins distinguishes paragangliomas from most but not all epithelial NENs {33098891}. Although GATA3 is a useful biomarker of paragangliomas, it is also expressed in normal and neoplastic parathyroid and pituitary {24145643; 27529763; 30217041; 32193825; 32303954}. Paragangliomas can also express peptide hormones including calcitonin {27529763; 28493102; 29744727}. Although medullary thyroid carcinomas can also focally stain for tyrosine hydroxylase, they are typically negative for GATA3 and positive for monoclonal CEA and thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) {28493102; 29744727}. Sustentacular cells identified by S100 or SOX10 {32548761} are not specific for paraganglioma {27529763; 32632839}.

Cytology

FNA biopsy is generally not advocated because of potential complications, but if inadvertently sampled, smears usually contain blood and isolated tumour cells or groups forming rosettes. The cells are often large and polygonal, ovoid, or elongated. The abundant granular/dense cytoplasm appears eosinophilic on H&E and pale on Pap. The nuclei are centrally or eccentrically located and show variation in size and shape. Intranuclear cytoplasmic pseudoinclusions may be seen. Pleomorphic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, clumped chromatin, intranuclear pseudoinclusions, and granular cytoplasm may cause misdiagnosis. Cell block material can be used for immunohistochemistry to aid in diagnosis {10945911; 20715670; 31168271}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a nested/Zellballen pattern; round, hyperchromatic nuclei with stippled chromatin; confirmation of negativity for keratin and positivity for neuroendocrine differentiation markers.

Desirable: GATA3 expression; positivity for one of the catecholamine-synthesizing enzymes (e.g. tyrosine hydroxylase, DBH); SDHB immunohistochemistry or relevant genetic evaluation to exclude hereditary risk (as available).

Staging

There is no eighth-edition Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system for head and neck paragangliomas, but International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) and other expert-initiated reporting guidelines have been introduced {24476517; 32407815}.

Prognosis and prediction

Head and neck paragangliomas have low metastatic potential, with only a 4–6% risk of metastasis for those in the carotid body and 2% in the middle ear {28321772}. Although few or no metastases are reported in paediatric patients in the short term {32432211}, they can occur after many years, especially in patients harbouring germline SDHB mutations {24169168; 31194233}. The main putative risk factor for metastasis is hereditary mutation of SDHB {24169168; 31194233}. However, locally invasive tumours can have a very aggressive course if unresectable {32548761}. Familial cases require lifelong surveillance to evaluate for multicentric disease.
