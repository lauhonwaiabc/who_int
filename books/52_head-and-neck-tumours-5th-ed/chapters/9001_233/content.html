9.0.0.1 Haematolymphoid proliferations and neoplasia: Introduction

With the abundance of lymph nodes and other lymphoid structures (including those of the Waldeyer ring) in the head and neck, it is not surprising that this anatomical region is one of the most common sites where nodal and extranodal haematolymphoid neoplasms are first detected in adults as well as children {26107683; 34388659; 31187530}. Although many such neoplasms are systemic, some have a strong predilection to arise primarily in the head and neck region. Of these, we note in particular paediatric-type follicular lymphoma, large B-cell lymphoma with IRF4 rearrangement, plasmablastic lymphoma, and extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma.

This chapter starts with a number of reactive haematolymphoid lesions and related disorders that may mimic haematolymphoid neoplasia. EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer is now regarded as a self-limited lymphoproliferative disorder that arises in immunocompromised individuals. A new entity included in this category is IgG4-related disease, which often presents in this region of the body. Listing IgG4-related disease in this volume is anticipated to help standardize its diagnostic criteria worldwide.

The majority of lymphomas of the head and neck are of B-cell lineage {14631680}. Differential diagnostic considerations for these should rest on combinations of morphological and immunophenotypic findings, often diverging on the basis of whether the neoplastic cells are small/intermediate in size or large. For tumours that do not fall into this group, the possibility that they may be myeloid sarcoma, extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma, or any of the rare histiocytic or dendritic cell tumours should be entertained.
