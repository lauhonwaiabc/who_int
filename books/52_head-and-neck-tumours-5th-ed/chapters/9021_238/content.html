9.0.2.1 Myeloid sarcoma

Definition

Myeloid sarcoma is an extramedullary tumour mass composed of myeloid blasts and often maturing myeloid elements that efface tissue architecture.

ICD-O coding

9930/3 Myeloid sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2A60.39 & XH3l40 Myeloid sarcoma & Myeloid sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: chloroma; granulocytic or monocytic sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Myeloid sarcoma in the head and neck region is rare, occurring in 0.1–1% of patients with acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) and accounting for 5–10% of all cases of myeloid sarcoma {17170724; 23530613; 25213180}. Any site in this region can be involved, but the oral cavity is most common {23530613}.

Clinical features

Patients usually have a history or coexistent evidence of AML or (less often) other myeloid neoplasms. Rarely, myeloid sarcoma is the initial manifestation of the disease. Patients may present with a mass, painful throat, bleeding from the nose or mouth, jaw pain, or disturbed vision.

Epidemiology

The epidemiological features are similar to those observed among patients who develop de novo AML or patients who develop a blast phase of an underlying myelodysplastic neoplasm, myeloproliferative neoplasm, or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm.

Etiology

The etiology is similar to that of AML. The specific reasons for its presentation as an extramedullary mass are unknown, but adhesion molecules (e.g. ITGA7) and extracellular matrix interactions are suggested as being involved {32033262}.

Pathogenesis

Myeloid sarcoma involving the head and neck region shares cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities with AML, such as monosomy 7, t(8;21), inv(16), KMT2A rearrangement, and NPM1 mutation {23530613; 28574302; 31682773}.

Macroscopic appearance

Involved organs are commonly enlarged, have a fish-flesh cut surface, and may appear green when the neoplastic cells exhibit granulocytic maturation and express myeloperoxidase.

Histopathology

Myeloid sarcoma forms a mass that diffusely replaces tissue architecture. A single-file pattern is common, particularly for cases with monocytic differentiation. Mitotic figures are often numerous. The neoplastic cells have thin nuclear membranes, fine (blastic) chromatin, and usually pinpoint nucleoli. Tumours with granulocytic differentiation often show a subset of partially mature neoplastic cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm (e.g. eosinophilic myelocytes and metamyelocytes). Tumours with monocytic differentiation often have a subset of cells with folded nuclei (e.g. promonocytes).

Immunophenotypic analysis using flow cytometry and/or immunohistochemistry shows that the neoplastic cells are often positive for CD11c, CD13, CD33, CD34 (often absent in immature monocytes), CD43, CD68, KIT (CD117), myeloperoxidase, and lysozyme {17170724; 23530613; 26185307}. CD4, CD19 (in cases with t[8;21]), CD56, CD99, CD123, CD163 (in more mature neoplastic cells), and TdT (often weak or only in a subset of cells) can be expressed in subsets of cases {17170724; 26185307}.

Cytology

Wright–Giemsa or Giemsa touch imprints or smears highlight cytoplasmic granules in maturing neoplastic cells {31051716}. Auer rods or morphological features of myelodysplasia in the background may be identified. Unstained touch imprints can be assessed by traditional cytochemistry methods for myeloperoxidase and esterases.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Detection of myeloid sarcoma is equivalent to a diagnosis of AML {26185307; 31682773}. A comprehensive evaluation that includes conventional cytogenetic analysis and molecular assessment for translocations and gene mutations is needed for the correct classification and for planning therapy.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: effacement of tissue architecture by a mass composed of myeloid blasts, with or without maturing elements, that are positive for multiple granulocytic and/or monocytic markers.

Desirable: cytogenetic and molecular analysis (essential for de novo myeloid sarcoma).

Staging

Evaluation of peripheral blood and bone marrow for AML or other myeloid neoplasms (e.g. myelodysplastic neoplasm, myeloproliferative neoplasm, or myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm) is mandatory.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with myeloid sarcoma require AML-type therapy, guided by targets identified by cytogenetic and molecular analysis. The prognosis is similar to that for patients with AML without evidence of myeloid sarcoma {29043233}. Patients who present with isolated myeloid sarcoma appear to have a better prognosis {18623376; 25213180}. Long-term remission has been achieved in a subset of patients {29043233}.
