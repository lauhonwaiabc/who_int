14.0.6.4 Tuberous sclerosis

Definition

Tuberous sclerosis is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder resulting from a constitutional pathogenic sequence variant of TSC1 (chromosome 9q34) or TSC2 (chromosome 16p13). The disorder is characterized by a variety of hamartomas and benign neoplasms affecting many organ systems, including the skin, soft tissues, CNS, heart, kidneys, and lungs.

Localization

Major manifestations include cutaneous angiofibromas, shagreen patches, subungual fibromas, cardiac rhabdomyomas, pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis, and renal angiomyolipomas. CNS involvement is characterized by cortical tubers, white matter glioneuronal hamartomas, subependymal nodules, and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas.

Clinical features

Cardiac rhabdomyomas are often a presenting feature of tuberous sclerosis in newborns and infants aged < 2 years, and more than half of all cardiac rhabdomyomas are associated with tuberous sclerosis. Cutaneous manifestations include hypomelanotic nodules evident from birth, facial angiofibromas, shagreen patches, ungual/subungual fibromas, renal angiomyolipomas, renal cysts, and lymphangioleiomyomatosis. It is important to highlight that the individual phenotypic features of tuberous sclerosis can also occur sporadically.

Head and neck manifestations

Manifestations of tuberous sclerosis in the head and neck are predominantly cutaneous and oral. Angiofibroma has a predilection for the face and manifests as benign neoplasms typically arising in the centrofacial cheek areas, nasolabial folds, and chin {30246432}. Other non-neoplastic cutaneous manifestations include shagreen patches and poliosis, i.e. patches of white hair involving the scalp or eyelids. Fibrous cephalic plaques appear as rubbery or firm red-brown plaques typically involving the forehead or scalp. Oral manifestations are frequent and specifically include angiofibromas and dental enamel pits. Angiofibromas are more common in the anterior gingiva, lips, tongue, and palate. Dental enamel loss on the incisal border and tooth wear are also observed {24310804; 32644893; 21055980}.

Epidemiology

The disorder affects 25 000–40 000 individuals in the USA and about 1–2 million individuals worldwide, with an estimated prevalence of 1 case per 6000–10 000 live births and a population prevalence of about 1 in 20 000 {24053982}.

Etiology

Most tuberous sclerosis cases (~60%) result from sporadic (de novo) constitutional pathogenic sequence variants in TSC1 or TSC2 {2918523}. In affected kindreds, the disease follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, with a high penetrance but with considerable phenotypic variability {7670658}.

Pathogenesis

The gene TSC1 maps to chromosome region 9q34 {2889832} and contains 23 exons {9242607}. The TSC1-encoded protein is hamartin (molecular weight: 130 kDa), which is strongly expressed in brain, kidney, and heart {10349994}. The most common TSC1 mutations are small deletions and nonsense mutations (each accounting for ~30% of all mutations in the gene) {11030407; 10227394}. Virtually all mutations result in a truncated gene product. Large deletions of the TSC1 gene are rare.

The TSC2 gene maps to chromosome region 16p13.3 {1303246} and contains 42 exons. The TSC2 transcript shows widespread expression in many tissues, including the brain and other organs affected in tuberous sclerosis. Tuberin is the protein product (molecular weight: 180 kDa) and bears significant homology with the catalytic domain of RAP1GAP, a member of the RAS family. The mutation spectrum of TSC2 is wider than that of TSC1, including large deletions, missense mutations, and splice junction mutations {9863590; 11112665; 10205261}. Large deletions in the TSC2 gene may extend into the adjacent gene PKD1, with the resulting phenotype of tuberous sclerosis and polycystic kidney disease {17185137; 10227394}.

Tuberin and hamartin form a heterodimeric complex that integrates growth factor and stress signals from the PI3K/AKT pathway and transmits signals downstream to coordinate multiple cellular processes, including cell proliferation and cell size {26893383; 2889832; 1303246; 9809973}. The heterodimer complex negatively regulates the mTOR pathway {16288294; 12172555; 12271141}. The understanding of the basic mechanism of mTOR pathway activation in tuberous sclerosis lesions has led to the use of mTOR inhibitors in the treatment of manifestations of tuberous sclerosis.

Histopathology

Angiofibromas form well-circumscribed nodules composed of bland oval cells, with delicate collagen deposition and evenly distributed small to medium-sized vessels {27140177}. Fibrous cephalic plaques are characterized by thickened collagen bundles with a decrease or absence of elastic fibres. They may have dilated vessels, resembling angiofibromas {29258863}.

Cytology

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In the revised diagnostic criteria, a constitutional pathogenic sequence variant of TSC1 or TSC2 is an independent diagnostic criterion and is sufficient for a definitive diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis {24053983}. Genetic testing is recommended for family members of an affected patient, especially in infants, and may also be offered as a preimplantation or prenatal test.

Prognosis and prediction

Tuberous sclerosis tends to shorten the lifespan slightly (as compared with a non-syndromic White control population) {1861550}. The most common causes of death in the second decade of life are brain tumours and status epilepticus, followed by renal abnormalities {24053982; 27935023}. In patients aged > 40 years, mortality is most commonly associated with renal complications or lymphangioleiomyomatosis.
