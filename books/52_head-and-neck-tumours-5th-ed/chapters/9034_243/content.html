9.0.3.4 Plasmacytoma

Definition

Solitary plasmacytoma is a localized, mass-forming intramedullary or extramedullary tumour composed of monoclonal plasma cells, without evidence of plasma cell myeloma or additional plasma cell tumours.

ICD-O coding

9731/3 Plasmacytoma of bone

ICD-11 coding

2A83.2 & XH4BL1 Solitary plasmacytoma & Plasmacytoma, NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: extramedullary plasmacytoma; plasmacytoma of bone.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Solitary extraosseous plasmacytoma (SEP) typically infiltrates the mucosal tissues of the upper respiratory tract (nasopharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, oropharynx, larynx, and oral cavity) {10357398; 15586381}, whereas solitary bony lesions affect the mandibular marrow-rich areas (body, angle, ramus) more than the maxilla {22134311}. Cervical lymph node dissemination occurs in about 15% of cases {11190797}.

Clinical features

Patients with SEP most often present with a soft tissue mass (80%) {9852535}, followed by airway obstruction (35%), epistaxis (35%), local pain (20%), proptosis (15%), nasal discharge (10%), and cranial nerve palsy (5%). Jaw and tooth pain, tooth migration, and swelling are seen in solitary bony lesions {9117760}. Approximately 20% of patients have a detectable, low-quantity monoclonal serum or urine paraprotein (M component), most commonly IgA {10629591; 12780789; 15009059}. Multifocal bone involvement is absent by imaging studies {19421229; 8315427; 21757591}.

Epidemiology

About 80% of all SEPs arise in the head and neck; SEP accounts for 4% of all non-epithelial tumours in the head and neck {10357398}. There is a male predominance, and the peak incidence is in the sixth decade of life {6180279; 12780789; 10357398; 9852535}. Rare cases have been reported in a posttransplant setting {19762536}.

Etiology

Etiology is unknown, although in 10% of SEP cases, EBV has been detected in immunocompetent patients {7675768; 25556356; 16308165}.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis and genetic features appear to be similar to those of other plasma cell neoplasms but have not been extensively studied in head and neck sites {23368088; 18326524; 20880115}.

Macroscopic appearance

SEP form soft, gelatinous, tan to yellow and sometimes haemorrhagic soft tissue masses.

Histopathology

SEP shows a subepithelial diffuse infiltrate of neoplastic plasma cells combined with a scant vascularized stroma, haemorrhagic areas {7675768; 6180279}, and, in a few cases, amyloid or immunoglobulin deposits. For solitary bone plasmacytoma and SEP, clonal plasma cells can be classified on the basis of their mature and immature features. Immature plasma cells (plasmablastic or anaplastic features) usually exhibit a higher N:C ratio, dispersed chromatin (often with prominent nucleoli), more abundant cytoplasm, and a hof region.

The immunophenotype is similar to that of other plasma cell neoplasms, with CD38, CD138, and IRF4 (MUM1) positivity, and tumour cells can be differentiated from reactive plasma cells by light chain restriction. Occasionally, they may display positivity for CD20 with negativity for most B-cell markers. Rare positive staining for cytokeratin and EMA is reported {1469918}, potentially leading to misdiagnosis as carcinoma. Unlike solitary bone plasmacytoma and plasma cell myeloma, SEP usually lacks cyclin D1 expression and shows a weaker positivity for CD56 {15586381}. The Ki-67 (MIB1) proliferation index is usually low; if elevated, it may help in predicting a more aggressive course {25842207}.

Differential diagnosis

Large B-cell lymphoma, carcinoma, melanoma, extramedullary myeloid sarcoma, and olfactory neuroblastoma can be challenging to distinguish from cases of moderately immature SEP. In EBV-positive plasmacytoma, it is mandatory to exclude plasmablastic lymphoma, which has a high Ki-67 proliferation index (often > 60%) {25193957} and is usually associated with an immunocompromised state. When a clear distinction may be difficult, a descriptive diagnosis such as “plasmablastic neoplasm, indeterminate between plasmablastic lymphoma and anaplastic plasmacytoma” is allowed. In the differential diagnosis between SEP and lymphomas with extensive plasma cell differentiation (e.g. mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue marginal zone lymphoma, lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma), the finding of aggregates of small B lymphocytes, clonal B cells (if flow cytometry is performed), remnants of germinal centres, and expression of IgM favour B-cell lymphoma.

Cytology

Cytology specimens may include plasma cells with variable degrees of immaturity and anaplasia.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular subtyping follows that for multiple myeloma {23368088; 18326524; 20880115}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a mass-forming infiltrate of plasma cells, which may exhibit variable degrees of immaturity and anaplasia; immunohistochemical demonstration of light chain restriction; no evidence of plasma cell myeloma or plasma cell lesions elsewhere.

Staging

Thorough staging is required to rule out evidence of other plasmacytomas or plasma cell myeloma.

Prognosis and prediction

About 25% of patients experience local recurrence or spread to regional lymph nodes {1272069}. Many patients have a relatively indolent course, and the 10-year disease-free survival rate is about 70% {12780789; 12057071}. Possible EBV-driven transformation of plasmacytoma into plasmablastic lymphoma has been reported {25193957}. About 10% of patients progress to plasma cell myeloma {10357398; 25439696}.
