11.1.0.2 Branchial cleft cyst

Definition

Branchial cleft cyst is a developmental anomaly that arises from the first, second, third, or fourth pharyngeal/branchial clefts and manifests clinically as a lateral neck mass.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

DA05.Y Other specified cysts of oral or facial-neck region & Branchial cleft cyst

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Location depends on the branchial cleft of origin. The most common location is the second branchial cleft, along the anterior surface of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, lateral to the carotid space, and posterior to the submandibular gland {9925396}.

Clinical features

Patients present with a painless mass in the neck, which may acutely increase in size after an upper respiratory tract infection {16616308; 22494467}. Bilateral branchial cleft cysts may be associated with branchio-oto-renal syndrome {29500469}.

Epidemiology

Branchial cleft cysts have an equal sex distribution and a bimodal age distribution that peaks during early childhood and between the third and fifth decades of life {16616308; 21680029; 22494467}. Second branchial cleft cysts are most common (> 95%); the remainder are rare.

Etiology

The most common theory proposes that branchial cleft cysts derive from incomplete involution of branchial cleft structures during embryogenesis {1529135; 16616308}.

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

A cyst with a smooth surface lining, containing brown mucoid material {25421295}.

Histopathology

Branchial cleft cyst is usually unilocular and lined with stratified squamous epithelium lacking cytomorphological evidence of malignancy. Columnar respiratory epithelial lining may be seen. Lymphoid tissue is typically present in the cyst wall. In inflamed or ruptured cysts, an admixture of acute inflammatory cells, fibrosis, and granulation tissue may predominate.

Differential diagnosis

Importantly, the differential diagnosis of branchial cleft cyst includes a cystic lymph node metastasis from an HPV-associated squamous cell carcinoma, particularly for patients aged > 40 years {18383529; 26457358; 29983308}. In this setting, p16 should be interpreted with caution, because 30–40% of branchial cleft cysts overexpress this marker {19365840; 20004950}. In such situations, more specific high-risk HPV testing (PCR-based assay or RNA in situ hybridization) should be performed, because high-risk HPV is not an established etiology of these cysts {19365840; 20004950; 29764277}. Notably, most tumours previously reported as carcinoma arising in branchial cleft cysts represent cystic metastasis of HPV-associated squamous cell carcinoma {23328541}.

Cytology

Aspirates may show acute inflammatory cells and macrophages admixed with mature squamous cells and keratinous debris {22507494}. It is unusual to see many nucleated squamous cells; rather, keratinous debris (i.e. dead squamous cells) is prominent.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: lateral neck location; bland cytomorphology.

Desirable: negative HPV status (in selected cases).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Surgical excision is curative. Incomplete resection may lead to recurrence, and unresected cysts have a risk of infection {17346566}. There is essentially no risk of carcinomatous transformation {23328541}.
