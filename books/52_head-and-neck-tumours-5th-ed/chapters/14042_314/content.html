14.0.4.2 Fanconi anaemia

Definition

Fanconi anaemia (FA) is a heterogeneous disorder resulting from constitutional pathogenic sequence variants in one of the 22 genes involved in DNA repair and the maintenance of genomic stability. It is characterized by cellular hypersensitivity to DNA cross-linking agents, physical abnormalities, bone marrow failure and an increased risk for haematological and solid malignancies.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Fanconi anaemia complementation groups A–W (FANC-A to FANC-W)

Localization

The most common solid cancer in patients with FA is head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Other solid tumours may occur in the upper digestive and urogenital tracts or in breast (breast cancer only in FANC-D1/BRCA2, FANC-S/BRCA1, and FANC-N/PALB2).

Clinical features

FA is one of the most common hereditary cancer syndrome, described first by the Swiss paediatrician Guido Fanconi in 1927 {12525534}. FA is characterized by cellular hypersensitivity to DNA cross-linking agents, physical abnormalities, bone marrow failure, and an increased risk of haematological and solid malignancies {20507306; 12525534}.

The clinical manifestations of FA are pleotropic, without genotype–phenotype correlation {12525534}. FA is characterized by premature ageing, short stature, microcephaly, hypoplastic thumb, multiorgan disorders, cutaneous pigmentation, hearing loss, and intellectual disability {12525534}. Patients with FA present with progressive bone marrow failure and a predisposition to haematological and solid malignancies {12525534; 12393516; 25307146}. FA patients with bone marrow failure are treated with allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation {24144640}.

Epidemiology

FA is a rare inherited disorder with a prevalence of 3 cases per 1 million people and a carrier frequency of 1:300 in Europe and the USA {12525534}. Some ethnic groups, such as Ashkenazi Jews, Afrikaners, and Tunisians, have the highest carrier frequency (1:80–90) due to founder effects and isolation {12525534; 33679882}. FANC-A is the most common type, accounting for 65% of all FA cases, followed by FANC-C (12%) and FANC-G (8%) (see #24688Table 16.05) {29254745}. The most common solid cancer in patients with FA is HNSCC {12525204; 22504776; 15331448; 18831513}, the risk of which is increased by 500- to 800-fold compared with the general population {12525204; 15331448; 18831513; 22504776; 23558727; 26567103}.

Etiology

FA is a heterogeneous genetic chromosomal instability disorder resulting from pathogenic inactivating constitutional sequence variants of 1 of the 22 genes involved in DNA repair and maintenance of genomic stability {29254745; 16675878; 26593718}. The majority of FA subtypes have autosomal recessive inheritance, except for FANC-B and FANC-R, which have X-linked and autosomal dominant modes of inheritance, respectively {29254745}.

Pathogenesis

Proteins encoded by FANC genes orchestrate a common signalling pathway (FA/BRCA) that is critical for interstrand DNA crosslink repair and genomic stabilization {29254745; 16675878; 26593718}. The increased risk for HNSCC among patients with FA is not linked to any specific FA genotype {32480311}. Defective DNA damage response predisposes patients with FA to HNSCC {25307146}. The increased risk for HNSCC in FA occurs without exposure to tobacco and alcohol {15331448; 26567103}. Oral HPV prevalence was higher in patients with FA than in their unaffected family members and in unrelated controls {33803570}. However, the etiological role for HPV in FA-associated HNSCC remains unproven {23558727; 15735012}. Patients with FA who undergo allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation have a higher risk of developing HNSCC than non-transplanted FA patients, which is attributed to the conditioning regimen and the occurrence of chronic oral graft-versus-host disease {22504776}. Some patients with FA have reversion of genomic duplication to the wildtype allele in the haematopoietic tissue, leading to spontaneous correction of bone marrow failure {32480311}. These patients are still at risk for solid malignancies because they harbour the biallelic FA gene mutations in non-haematopoietic tissue (mosaicism) {32480311}. In these patients, the diagnosis of HNSCC often precedes the diagnosis of FA {22504776; 32480311}. FA-related HNSCCs frequently occur as multifocal disease, preceded by an oral potentially malignant disorder presenting as lichenoid keratosis or leukoplakia {25662766; 26276748; 29481998}. FA-related oral potentially malignant disorders have a higher risk of malignant transformation than those of the general population {29481998}, and they must be biopsied and kept under close surveillance, even if they have a low-risk clinical appearance.

Macroscopic appearance

None

Histopathology

The typical histological features of FA-related and sporadic HNSCCs are similar (see #24689Table 16.06). FA-related HNSCCs frequently present as poorly differentiated tumours with epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and they exhibit a higher proportion of cancer stem cells {21138479; 25340704}.

Cytology

See HNSCC and other tumour types for specific details.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of FA is established in a proband by diepoxybutane (DEB)- or mitomycin C–induced formation of triradial and quadriradial figures in chromosome spreads from lymphocytes of patients with FA. Molecular testing options include single gene testing, a multigene testing panel, and more comprehensive genomic sequencing if serial single gene and multigene panel analyses fail to confirm the diagnosis in a patient with clinical features of FA.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

See #31915Box 16.04 .

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

FA-related HNSCCs have an aggressive biological behaviour, characterized by poor survival and high relapse rates {26484938; 32480311}. Patients with FA exhibit higher rates of toxicity from radiation and chemotherapy because of DNA repair defects; therefore, surgery remains the preferred treatment of choice for FA-related HNSCC {26484938; 32480311}.
