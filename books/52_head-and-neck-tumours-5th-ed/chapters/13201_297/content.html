13.2.0.1 Otosclerosis

Definition

Otosclerosis is usually an inherited disturbance in bone metabolism affecting the otic capsule (or bony labyrinth) of the inner ear.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

AB33 Otosclerosis

Related terminology

Acceptable: localized bone dysplasia.

Subtype(s)

Clinical otosclerosis; histological otosclerosis.

Localization

Bony changes can occur at many locations in the inner ear. Plaques of softer, more vascular bone replace endochondral bone of the otic capsule, frequently affecting more than one location, located anterior to the oval window and on the stapes footplate (80%), at the round window (30%), in the pericochlear region (21%), and in the anterior part of the internal auditory canal (19%) {26276418}.

Clinical features

Otosclerosis is one of the commonest causes of conductive hearing loss (when involvement of the annular ligament of the stapes causes fixation) {20195188}, although patients may have sensorineural hearing loss (with involvement of the cochlea and part of the labyrinth) or mixed hearing loss. It is usually bilateral (80%) but with initial asymmetrical hearing loss in one ear before the other is affected. It is traditionally regarded as a middle ear disease, although inner ear involvement is recognized with sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and vertigo {30540696}. Otoscopy may reveal circumscribed reddening on the promontory {32215901}.

Imaging

Demineralized otospongiotic bone adjacent to the cochlea and anterior to the stapes footplate is identified on CT {32215901}.

Epidemiology

Clinical otosclerosis mainly affects White people (prevalence: 0.3–0.4%) {11568664}, with a reported frequency of 0.1–2.1% in White Europeans {30540696}; it is rare in African and Asian people (0.03–0.1%). The incidence in Japanese and South American populations is half that in White populations {19318139}. It accounts for as many as 9% of all cases of hearing loss and 22% of cases of conductive hearing loss. The prevalence of histological otosclerosis in large autopsy series is 8–11%. Age at presentation ranges from the first to the sixth decade of life but is typically in the third decade {5298472; 2692453; 14414297}. Patients are predominantly female (M:F ratio: 2:1) {2825424; 16985478; 30540696}.

Etiology

Otosclerosis is considered an autosomal dominant disease with variable penetrance (average: 40%); a family history is reported in as many as 70% of cases {5298472; 9102432; 13242969; 14414297; 30540696}. Several gene loci on different chromosomes have been identified, but without an established causal link {24215213; 30968248}. Although its role is unclear, measles virus has been identified, with inflammation postulated as a trigger {3054069; 18235207}. Estrogen deficiency may cause otosclerosis in menopausal women, and hormone replacement has been shown to improve symptoms {26276418}. Worsening of symptoms during pregnancy has been reported {30540696}. Studies investigating autoimmune causes, HLA association, and the role of inflammation have been inconclusive and contradictory.

Pathogenesis

Unknown

Macroscopic appearance

Otosclerosis is rarely biopsied. Specimens include all or part of the stapes bone, which may show plaque tissue in the anterior footplate.

Histopathology

Histologically active and inactive/sclerotic areas are seen. Active areas (otospongiosis) have zones of active bone resorption composed of spongy immature bone with numerous enlarged plump osteoblasts, osteoclastic giant cells, and dilated vascular marrow spaces containing cellular connective tissue {20195188}. Otospongiotic bone contrasts with the well-developed lamellar bone beneath the outer periosteum, endochondral middle layer, and endosteal layer of the otic capsule. A sharply demarcated edge between normal and otosclerotic bone is a prominent feature. Otosclerotic bone sometimes reaches the endosteum of the cochlear capsule. Sometimes it may lead to a fibrous reaction deep to the spiral ligament.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

None

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: well-demarcated plaques of immature bone from the inner ear.

Staging

Not performed

Prognosis and prediction

Stapedectomy with prosthetic implant aids with hearing restoration, although progressive loss is seen in untreated cases.
