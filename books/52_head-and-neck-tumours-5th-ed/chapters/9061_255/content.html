9.0.6.1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Definition

Juvenile xanthogranuloma (JXG) is a clonal expansion of non–Langerhans cell histiocytes that share a dermal macrophage phenotype.

ICD-O coding

9749/1 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

ICD-11 coding

2B31.0 Juvenile xanthogranuloma

Related terminology

Acceptable: xanthogranuloma; solitary xanthogranuloma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

JXGs are generally confined to the skin, with a predilection for the head and neck (42%), upper trunk, and proximal extremities {12717244; 15613853; 32468628}. Solitary extracutaneous JXGs are rare, with 40–50% being localized in the head and neck, mostly the eye and ocular adnexa (iris, conjunctiva, eyelid, choroid, orbit), oral cavity (gingival, buccal mucosa, midline hard palate), nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, subglottis, and ear {12717244; 26744443; 26189188}. Solitary spinal or intracranial JXGs (mostly extra-axial, in the skull base) or within skeletal muscles, are rare {25281434; 29488239; 26744443}. Systemic JXG shows multivisceral involvement {16462582; 19515049}.

Clinical features

Cutaneous JXG is a papulonodular lesion, measuring from about 5–10 mm to as large as 100 mm {7577591; 12717244; 29488239}. JXG is generally asymptomatic and evolves from a raised, pink to darker-brown lesion with progressive flattening. JXG may disappear within months or years with scarring or anetoderma {12717244; 15613853; 32468628}. In adults, lesions are large, solitary, and persistent {16319479}. Solitary ocular JXGs and those associated with skin lesions (reported in < 0.4% of cutaneous JXG cases) are generally unilateral {8609257; 26189188; 29488239}. CNS JXG can cause seizures, hydrocephalus, or diabetes insipidus (in case of pituitary involvement) {8765620; 12717244; 15613853}. JXG arising within the upper respiratory tract or oral cavity may appear as a verrucous, pedunculated, or fibroma-like lesion.

Epidemiology

JXG is a rare disease typical of young children. The incidence is unknown and is probably underestimated. According to the Kiel tumour registry, JXGs represent 0.5% of all paediatric tumours {12717244; 32468628}. About 5% of patients show multiple cutaneous lesions {12717244}. Systemic JXG occurs in < 5% of cases {12717244; 15613853; 32468628}. More than 90% of JXGs arise within the first year of life (20% are congenital) and show a male predilection (M:F ratio: 1.4:1) {15613853}. JXG in adults is rare. JXGs may be present in 13–15% of patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 {15078345}. Whether there is a higher prevalence of juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 and JXG is debated {7632061; 28189268}. JXG is also reported in Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome {23157619}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Pathogenetic mechanisms in JXG are poorly understood. Rare cases with mixed Langerhans cell histiocytosis and JXG phenotype suggest one or more common cells of origin, similar to Langerhans cell histiocytosis and Erdheim–Chester disease {28566492; 28512190}. The expression of fascin and factor XIIIa support a histogenetic link with dermal macrophages {17786242; 34620874}. Recent studies suggest a pathogenetic role of the MAPK/ERK pathway. Activating mutations of NRAS, KRAS, ARAF, and MAP2K1, as well as an RNF11::BRAF fusion, have been reported in 7 out of 12 systemic JXGs {28752840}. Further somatic activating mutations, including CSF1R mutations (in 10%) or NTRK1 fusions (in 12%) and, less frequently, mutations in CSF3R, KIT, ALK, MET, JAK3, and RAF1, as well as RAF gene fusions, have been reported in 55 JXGs (20 involving the head and neck area) {31768065}. PIK3CD mutations, identified in a few cases, suggest that the PI3K pathway plays a role in JXG pathogenesis {25202140; 26637772}. The recent finding of BRAF p.V600E mutations in cases of systemic JXG with CNS involvement raises the possibility that such cases might represent variants of Erdheim–Chester disease {31685033}. JXG in monozygotic twins suggests genetic transmission {21091658; 31768065}.

Macroscopic appearance

JXG appears as a pale-yellow to tan, dome-shaped lesion. Early lesions appear as pink macules.

Histopathology

JXGs are unencapsulated, well-circumscribed lesions centred in the papillary dermis, with larger lesions extending into the subcutis. Overlying skin may ulcerate. The infiltrate is composed of histiocytes intermixed with variable numbers of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells, neutrophils, and mast cells. Dilated vascular channels may be present. The histological features may vary according to the temporal evolution. In early lesions, small, oval to spindle histiocytes with eosinophilic cytoplasm predominate, whereas large xanthomatous histiocytes and multinucleated Touton giant cells, characterized by foamy cytoplasm and multiple nuclei in a ring-like arrangement, are more frequent in older JXGs. Mitoses may be seen {12717244; 24582118}.

Histiocytes are positive for CD68, CD163, factor XIIIa, CD4, CD14, and fascin. CD1a and CD207 (langerin) are negative; S100 is often negative but may show variable light staining.

Differential diagnosis

Early JXGs with rare xanthomatous cells are differentiated from Langerhans cell histiocytosis by negative immunostaining for S100 and CD1a. In older children, negative staining for ALK differentiates JXG from ALK-related histiocytosis and epithelioid fibrous histiocytoma, whereas a lack of characteristic folliculosebaceous induction, peripheral entrapment of collagen, and haemosiderin deposits exclude a lipidized fibrous histiocytoma.

Benign cephalic histiocytosis is a rare, self-healing, asymptomatic papular eruption, predominantly in the head and neck of young children, that is histologically and immunohistochemically undistinguishable from JXG {15613853; 23551579}.

Cytology

Cytology is rarely performed. Smears show mononuclear or multinucleated histiocytes with reniform/oval nuclei intermixed with variable numbers of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils. Touton giant cells or foreign body cells may be present. In older lesions, fibroblasts are also seen {21491610}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

None

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: histiocytes intermixed with variable numbers of inflammatory cells; positivity of histiocytes for CD4 and CD68/CD163 and negativity for CD1a / CD207 (langerin).

Desirable: Touton giant cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

JXG has an excellent prognosis and may resolve spontaneously. Ocular JXG may be complicated by glaucoma, uveitis, and blindness. JXG in the CNS and systemic JXG may behave aggressively, with an overall mortality rate of 5–10% in systemic JXG {12717244; 26637774; 32383820}.
