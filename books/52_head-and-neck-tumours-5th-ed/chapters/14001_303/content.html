14.0.0.1 Genetic tumour syndromes involving the head and neck: Introduction

For more than a century it has been recognized that hereditary factors can contribute to the development of tumours involving the head and neck. Clinically recognizable tumour-predisposing syndromes have been more widely recognized during the past few decades because of the rapidly increasing use of sequencing of DNA in both diagnostic pathology and clinical genetics. What has appeared to be a sporadic tumour is becoming less often so with more intense investigation, and new syndromes and tumours associated with these syndromes are being reported more often.

The fifth edition of the WHO classification of head and neck tumours has a newly created chapter dedicated to heritable syndromes with tumours in the head and neck region (listed in #28600Table 16.01). This initiative was needed to better understand the tumours, diseases, and associated syndromes, as well as to establish recommendations for monitoring and treating these patients.

As an example, middle ear endolymphatic sac tumour (ELST) is a well-recognized tumour associated with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome in over one third of the cases. Data from the International Endolymphatic Sac Tumor Registry {25867206} show a prevalence of ELST of 3.6% in von Hippel–Lindau syndrome. VHL constitutional pathogenic variants were present in almost 40% of ELSTs, with an apparent sporadic presentation. These findings support genetic testing in all patients with ELSTs {20351605}.

Naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) is a cancer predisposition syndrome associated with basal cell carcinomas, odontogenic keratocysts, and medulloblastomas, related to a constitutional pathogenic variant of a gene in the sonic hedgehog pathway (PTCH1 or SUFU) {8326488; 31725470}. Odontogenic keratocyst screening should begin at 2 years of age, with annual orthopantomography beginning at about 8 years of age for carriers of the PTCH1 pathogenic variant only {28620006; 30826393}.

Brooke–Spiegler syndrome, also known as familial cylindromatosis or CYLD cutaneous syndrome, is an inherited autosomal dominant disease characterized by multiple spiradenomas, cylindromas, spiradenocylindromas, trichoepitheliomas, and salivary gland tumours. Salivary gland involvement by membranous basal cell adenoma {33526221} is characteristic of this syndrome. This tumour primarily involves the parotid gland. These tumours are usually bilateral/multicentric and well circumscribed.

Genodermatosis affecting the skin and mucosa of the head and neck has been well characterized {26975628}. PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome, caused by mutation of the gene PTEN, includes Cowden syndrome as the principal PTEN-related disorder. This syndrome is characterized by multiple neoplasms and hamartomas, mucosal papillomatosis, and skin lesions such as trichilemmomas. Multiple trichilemmomas are a clinically significant sign of Cowden syndrome. The most distinctive and peculiar facial features of Cowden syndrome consist of multiple small and keratotic papules concentrated around the orifices. Early recognition of these skin lesions may help with diagnosing an underlying malignancy and early cancer screening {26975628}.

Although cancers of the head and neck region are uncommon, the diagnosis of multicentric head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC; occurring in the larynx, pharynx, and oral cavity) in particular can be seen in Li–Fraumeni syndrome {27328919; 11120478}. This syndrome is associated with many tumours, but this chapter focuses specifically on head and neck tumours.

The most common solid cancer in patients with Fanconi anaemia is also HNSCC. Compared with the general population, risk of HNSCC in these patients is increased by 500- to 800-fold, and the diagnosis of HNSCC often precedes the diagnosis of Fanconi anaemia {22504776}. Fanconi anaemia–associated HNSCCs frequently occur as multifocal disease, preceded by oral potentially malignant disorders presenting as lichenoid keratosis or leukoplakia.

Patients with familial adenomatous polyposis, which is characterized by the presence of > 100 adenomatous colorectal polyps, may also present with multiple extracolonic manifestations. Desmoid tumours may arise in soft tissue of the head and neck but may also originate in the maxillary sinus, nasopharynx, and oral cavity {27387679}. Other features involving the head and neck are osteomas {19157925; 22010067} and supernumerary teeth.

Manifestations of tuberous sclerosis in the head and neck are predominantly cutaneous and oral. Angiofibroma has a predilection for the face, typically arising in the centrofacial cheek areas, nasolabial folds, and chin {30246432}. Oral manifestations are frequent and specifically include angiofibromas in the anterior gingiva, lips, tongue, and palate; dental enamel pits on the incisal border and tooth wear are also observed {24310804; 30246432}.

Neurofibromatosis type 1 is recognized in patients with orbitofacial neurofibromas and numerous neurofibromas at other sites. This syndrome is now described in the Head and neck tumours volume, focusing on the head and neck pathology findings. Other important syndromes with involvement of the head and neck, such as those with familial paragangliomas, jaw tumours (hyperparathyroidism–jaw tumour syndrome), oral mucosa leukoplakia (dyskeratosis congenita), and oropharyngeal carcinoma (Bloom syndrome), are described in detail.

Pathologists have a very important role in recognizing tumours of the head and neck associated with the heritable syndromes addressed in this chapter. Pathologists must be aware of the morphological features associated with distinct syndromes that should prompt consideration of germline screening. The routine use of next-generation sequencing will undoubtedly continue to widen the spectrum of known predisposing genetic variants among patients with tumours of the head and neck. The identification of syndromes has made possible the identification of at-risk individuals in affected families, who can then be offered genetic counselling. Information on underlying genetic variants can also have an important impact on the choice of therapy and surveillance guidelines. Virtually all of these disorders have now been characterized at the molecular level; patients with these disorders may also require distinct forms of therapy and personalized follow-up.
