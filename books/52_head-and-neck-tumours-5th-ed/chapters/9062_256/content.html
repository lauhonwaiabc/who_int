9.0.6.2 Erdheim-Chester disease

Definition

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a systemic histiocytic neoplasm characterized by multiorgan proliferation of mature histiocytes in a background of fibrosis.

ICD-O coding

9749/3 Erdheim–Chester disease

ICD-11 coding

2B31.Y & XH1VJ3 Other specified histiocytic or dendritic cell neoplasms & Erdheim–Chester disease

Related terminology

Not recommended: lipogranulomatosis; lipoid granulomatosis; lipid (cholesterol) granulomatosis; polyostotic sclerosing histiocytosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

ECD is a multisystem disease and can affect virtually any organ system, but the lymph nodes, spleen, and liver are generally spared {32187362; 28553668; 29396850; 31472931} (see #26435Table 10.06). Bilateral symmetrical osteosclerosis of the lower-extremity long bones is characteristic of ECD and occurs in 80–95% of cases. Perinephric (55–65%) and periaortic (50–80%) infiltrations are also frequent. ECD involvement of the head and neck is characterized by xanthelasma-like lesions around the eyes, face, and neck (33%); unilateral or bilateral orbital infiltration (30%); and sclerosis of facial sinuses (~47% by imaging studies). Rare cases with intraoral involvement have been described in the course of the disease process rather than at initial presentation {29192649; 29097410; 29188284; 28553668; 33582038}.

Clinical features

Symptoms are variable depending on site and extent of multisystem involvement. Patients mostly present with lower-extremity bone pain and fatigue. ECD can manifest with multisystem involvement, with cerebellar signs, diabetes insipidus, panhypopituitarism, pulmonary and cardiac disease, urinary tract obstruction, and retroperitoneal fibrosis {32187362; 28553668; 29396850; 31472931}. Patients with involvement of the maxilla/mandible may show tooth loss {33582038}, whereas sinus involvement may mimic chronic sinusitis with thickening of the walls of the paranasal sinuses and facial pain. Orbital lesions may present as exophthalmos or diplopia {30337283}. Underlying clonal haematopoiesis and overt myeloid neoplasms (mostly chronic myelomonocytic leukaemia) can be present in as many as 42.5% and 15.8% of cases, respectively {28679734; 31221777; 31624111; 31475353; 33067622}. Patients with cases associated with clonal haematopoiesis and/or myeloid neoplasms present at older ages than those without. ECD can be associated with Langerhans cell histiocytosis or Rosai–Dorfman disease, as well as autoimmune diseases {24894769; 26966089; 30923093; 31123032}.

Epidemiology

ECD is a rare, but it is probably an underdiagnosed disease. It is a disease of middle-aged adults (mean age: ~50 years) with an age range of 15–80 years at diagnosis and a male predominance (~70%) {32187362; 28553668; 29396850; 31472931; 29192649}. It is extremely rare in children, in whom it is frequently associated with Langerhans cell histiocytosis {19755920; 26466952; 30265230}.

Etiology

ECD is a clonal neoplastic disorder with no known genetic predisposition {22879539; 26566875; 29192649; 31768065}, although an underlying clonal haematopoiesis has been described in over two thirds of cases, a much higher rate than that reported in the general population {33067622}.

Pathogenesis

Gain-of-function alterations activating mostly the MAPK pathway and cell proliferation–related pathways other than MAPK have been identified in nearly all cases {22879539; 26566875; 29192649; 30262559; 31768065}. Additionally, a characteristic cytokine signature responsible for the local activation and recruitment of histiocytes has been identified {21205927}.

Functional studies have shown that some cases derive from haematopoietic/myeloid progenitors {28566492}. The high frequency of myeloid neoplasms and clonal haematopoiesis associated with ECD further supports the clonal myeloid origin in at least a proportion of patients with ECD. A model with sequential acquisition of driver mutations leading to the development of ECD and myeloid neoplasms from underlying clonal haematopoiesis has been proposed. However, ECD can occur irrespective of the presence of clonal haematopoiesis {33067622; 29192649; 28679734; 31624111}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most of the samples obtained for diagnosis correspond to biopsies of skin or bone. They are usually firm and sclerotic. The cut surface of lesions may be yellow in appearance.

Histopathology

The infiltrate is composed of histiocytes with foamy and/or eosinophilic cytoplasm admixed with chronic inflammatory cells and marked fibrosis. The lesional histiocytes may be sparse and may have distorted shapes. Eosinophils are rare to absent. Touton giant cells can be seen but are not always present {29192649}.

Histological features of ECD xanthelasma-like lesions can closely resemble classic xanthelasma. ECD xanthelasma-like lesions infiltrate the reticular dermis more frequently, display more multinucleated and Touton giant cells, and show less extensive fibrosis than classic xanthelasma lesions {26785805; 29192649}.

Immunohistochemistry

The immunophenotype is nonspecific, with the expression of CD163, CD68, CD14, factor XIIIa, and CD4. CD1a and CD207 (langerin) stains are consistently negative. There is variable staining for S100 in as many as one third of the cases, although extensive expression is not seen {29192649}. Immunostaining with BRAF p.V600E antibody for the detection of BRAF p.V600E mutations can be useful, although some cases showing discordant results with the molecular techniques have been reported {29192649}. Histiocytes are positive for phosphorylated ERK in most cases, thus confirming activation of the MAPK pathway; however, this staining is sensitive to fixation conditions and may have false negativity.

Cytology

Cytology samples are mostly of low cellularity due to associated fibrosis. They may show bland foamy histiocytes, including occasional binucleated and multinucleated cells without grooves admixed with chronic inflammatory cells. However, the diagnosis of ECD typically requires a biopsy of involved tissues {22279491; 23447119}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of a corroborating genomic alteration may aid in diagnosis and treatment. BRAF p.V600E mutations are present in 50–60% of cases of ECD {22879539; 26566875; 29192649; 31768065}. Patients without BRAF p.V600E frequently have other mutations, mostly on MAP2K1, KRAS, or NRAS {22879539; 26566875; 29192649; 31768065}. BRAF indels or BRAF fusions may also be present, which may require RNA sequencing in patients without point mutations in kinases {31768065}. It should be noted that mutant allele frequency is low (< 5%) in a quarter of cases, which may pose a diagnostic difficulty {30262559}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: collections of histiocytes with bland cytological features, foamy and/or eosinophilic cytoplasm, and non-Langerhans cell phenotype in the appropriate clinical and radiological context.

Desirable: corroborating genetic alteration (in selected cases).

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Most patients require systemic therapy. Traditionally, drugs such as peginterferon and cladribine have been used as initial treatments. However, in recent years, MAPK pathway alterations have been recognized, and targeted therapies with kinase inhibitors are used more frequently {29396850; 22300602; 32187362}. Recent studies reported a 43–100% response rate and almost no disease progression with the use of BRAF and/or MEK inhibitors {30867592; 28667012; 29188284}. Prognosis depends on the site and extent of involvement. CNS involvement and multisystemic disease are poor prognostic factors {21239701}.
