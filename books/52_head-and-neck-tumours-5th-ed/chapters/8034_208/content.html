8.0.3.4 Kaposi sarcoma

Definition

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a locally aggressive vascular endothelial proliferation associated with KSHV/HHV8.

ICD-O coding

9140/3 Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Classic indolent Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Endemic African Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 AIDS-associated Kaposi sarcoma

9140/3 Iatrogenic Kaposi sarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B57.Z & XH36A5 Kaposi sarcoma of unspecified primary site & Kaposi sarcoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: angiosarcoma multiplex; granuloma multiplex haemorrhagicum.

Subtype(s)

Classic indolent KS; endemic African KS; AIDS-associated KS; iatrogenic KS

Localization

Within the head and neck, the oral cavity is the most common site for AIDS-associated KS, and 70% of patients with cutaneous KS also have oral lesions. Oral lesions arise at the initial site of infection in 20% of cases, affecting the hard palate more commonly than the gingiva or tongue {21644423; 23008762}. Involvement of other head and neck sites is rare {10030245; 10618601; 16496108; 18605999; 27178524; 29508130}.

Clinical features

KS forms multiple reddish to purple macules that progress to plaques or nodules, which may ulcerate.

Epidemiology

Oral KS is the most common tumour identified in patients with HIV/AIDS {21644423; 23008762}. It is most common in men who have sex with men {32862546}. Although KS can develop at any stage of HIV infection, it is more common in patients with advanced immunosuppression {11423260; 25709361; 26261737}, with a 500-fold elevated risk of KS in patients with HIV compared with the general population {32051269}. AIDS-associated KS develops in the fourth or fifth decade of life and is seen in primarily HIV-positive men who have sex with men, especially in North American and European countries, whereas a similar male predominance is not seen in African or Chinese patients {22807383; 21644423; 23008762; 19404695; 32862546}.

Etiology

KSHV/HHV8 is found in KS endothelial cells in all disease forms {7997879; 7700310}.

Pathogenesis

A serological correlation exists between KSHV/HHV8 infection and KS, with infection required but insufficient for disease induction, which develops by a complex interplay of genetic, immunological, and environmental factors {11423257; 11536246}.

Macroscopic appearance

KS forms purplish, haemorrhagic nodules, which are frequently coalesced. See the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series for additional clinical details.

Histopathology

Early lesions are characterized by a subtle proliferation of small, slit-like vascular spaces with extravasated erythrocytes and a lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate. The plaque stage demonstrates an increased vascular proliferation along with tumour cell spindling and prominent intracellular and extracellular hyaline globules. The advanced nodular stage shows unencapsulated infiltrating fascicles of spindle cells with atypia and easily identified mitoses. Rare histological patterns include solid, lymphangioma-like, telangiectatic, desmoplastic, lymphangiectatic, ecchymotic, and anaplastic patterns {19839366; 23312917; 24034072}. Nearly all cases show a strong and diffuse nuclear KSHV/HHV8 reaction {11474291; 14990970; 15023037; 18494611; 19874352; 22314185; 22372906}. The four KS subtypes show identical histological features and are distinguished by epidemiological and etiological factors.

The differential diagnosis includes lobular capillary haemangioma, kaposiform haemangioendothelioma, angiosarcoma, and spindle cell carcinoma, all of which are KSHV/HHV8-negative.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant, although reported {22807383}

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a proliferation of small slit-like vessels lined by mildly atypical cells, with surrounding bland spindle cells; extravasated erythrocytes and lymphoplasmacytic patchy infiltrates.

Desirable: hyaline globules; nuclear KSHV/HHV8 expression by immunohistochemistry in selected cases.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis is immune status–dependent. Combined highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) and systemic chemotherapy reduces morbidity and mortality {15280789}. Intralesional therapies do not seem to affect the disease course {25345840}.
