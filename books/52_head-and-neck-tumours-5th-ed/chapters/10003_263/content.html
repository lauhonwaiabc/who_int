10.0.0.3 Mucosal melanoma

Definition

Mucosal melanoma (MM) is a malignant neoplasm of mucosal melanocytes.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Desmoplastic mucosal melanoma; mucosal lentiginous melanoma; nodular melanoma

Localization

About 80% of head and neck MMs involve the nasal cavity or septum and the maxillary sinus {31670075}. In the oral cavity, most cases arise on the maxillary gingiva and palate {26753505}. Tumours arising in other paranasal sinuses, the pharynx, and the larynx are rare {28325255; 25242350}.

Clinical features

Sinonasal MM presents with nonspecific symptoms simulating inflammatory conditions, which can delay diagnosis for months and sometimes years. Symptoms include nasal obstruction, sinus congestion, rhinorrhoea including melanorrhoea, and epistaxis. Endoscopy shows a polypoid, fleshy or pigmented mass. Oral MM is often asymptomatic, with dark-brown to black macules, plaques, or nodules {26753505}. Some patients have a long history of mucosal melanosis. Ulceration is common. Laryngeal MM may present with hoarseness or breathing problems. Lymph node and distant metastases at presentation may be seen in approximately 20% and 10% of cases, respectively {31670075}.

Imaging

Radiological imaging reveals the extent of invasion of the primary tumour, including destruction of cartilage and bone, and orbital and intracranial extension. It also demonstrates lymph node and distant metastases for staging purposes {21295201; 10720207}.

A combination of CT and MRI is used to assess the primary tumour. CT demonstrates bone erosion and is useful for surgical planning. MRI better differentiates tumour from retained secretions and demonstrates intracranial and intraorbital invasion more effectively.

MRI with contrast is the modality of choice for detecting cerebral metastases with higher sensitivity and specificity than contrast-enhanced CT.

With regard to nodal metastases, these are frequently hyperintense on T1-weighted imaging and low-signal on T2-weighted imaging, unlike metastases from other primaries. Melanin content reduces T1 relaxation times, leading to the T1-weighted high signal. Postcontrast heterogeneous enhancement is often noted.

CT is the modality of choice for detecting distant metastases. The lungs and pleura are most frequently affected, followed by the liver. Lesions may be hypervascular on CT in the arterial phase and hypodense in the portal phase. Rim enhancement and central necrosis are common. High signal is often noted on non-contrast T1-weighted liver MRI.

When combined with dedicated CT, PET-CT is 98% sensitive and 94% specific for detecting metastatic melanoma, with sites normally being FDG-avid {17641374}.

Epidemiology

Head and neck MMs represent < 1% of all melanomas and affect the sinonasal and oral mucosa more frequently than other mucosal sites {25242350}. MMs constitute about 4% of all sinonasal tumours and 0.26% of all oral malignancies {12717245; 22024859; 20198705; 18675580}. Tumours affect older adults, with a peak incidence in the seventh decade of life, and are rare in the first three decades of life {31670075; 26753505}. A predilection for sex or race has not been conclusively demonstrated {32057276; 28092366; 31670075; 25242350}.

Etiology

MMs are biologically distinct from cutaneous melanomas, with no definitive precursor lesions, but they may be associated with mucosal melanosis {26399561}. There is no known correlation with exposure to environmental carcinogens (e.g. tobacco, chemicals, or viruses) {28092366; 28325255}.

Pathogenesis

The genomic landscape of MM is distinct from that of cutaneous and uveal melanomas {31655118; 31358010; 31320640; 25147369}. Compared with cutaneous melanomas, MMs lack the ultraviolet (UV) radiation signature, have a significantly lower somatic mutation burden, and show higher rates of KIT mutations and a lower prevalence of NRAS and BRAF mutations (see #25622Table 11.01) {31655118; 31320640; 25147369; 23860532; 35978013}. Mutation profiles vary even across mucosal subsites {31320640; 31655118; 28296713}. More recent studies show co-mutations of NF1 and KIT {28296713}, mRNA splice factor SF3B1 alterations {31655118}, and cell-cycle aberrations {31358010}.

Macroscopic appearance

Sinonasal MMs are polypoid, exophytic nodules that may be tan-grey, firm to friable, or deeply pigmented. Oral MMs tend to be pigmented flat lesions. Surface ulceration is frequent.

Histopathology

Tumour cells are polymorphic, displaying spindled, epithelioid, plasmacytoid, or rhabdoid, round, clear, and undifferentiated morphology, often within the same tumour. Epithelioid tumour cells show amphophilic hyaline cytoplasm and atypical nuclei with a high N:C ratio and prominent macronucleoli arranged in solid sheet-like or nested growth patterns. Spindled tumour cells form intersecting fascicles. Invasive MM is primarily submucosal but can invade the underlying bone and cartilage. Mitoses are readily apparent and often atypical. Necrosis and discohesion frequently lead to a pseudopapillary or peritheliomatous pattern. A pseudoalveolar pattern has been described {26753505}. Lymphovascular and perineural invasion are common. The intact respiratory or squamous epithelium may show a pagetoid or lentiginous pattern of involvement with contiguous or separate intraepithelial malignant melanocytes, singly or in clusters. A lentiginous growth pattern is more common in oral MM. A nodular subtype may be seen, but due to fragmentation artefacts, this subtype is more challenging to document.

As many as 50% of sinonasal MMs are amelanotic, resulting in a broad differential diagnosis that includes small blue cell tumours (olfactory neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma), high-grade carcinomas (sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma, poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, NUT carcinoma, SMARCB1-deficient carcinoma), neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, myeloma, and epithelioid sarcoma. Tumours with a predominantly spindle cell morphology must be differentiated from various spindle cell carcinomas and sarcomas.

Desmoplastic melanoma affects the epidermis adjacent to the oral cavity mucosa, usually the lip, and the nasal vestibule where exposure to sun occurs. The tumours usually comprise amelanotic spindle cells in a collagenous matrix, which may be paucicellular and slightly atypical and may not show mitosis, necrosis, or lymphovascular invasion or express melanocytic markers, but which frequently shows perineural invasion {30312883}. The presence of junctional activity in the overlying epithelium may aid in their distinction from other low-grade spindle cell tumours {15054741}. In the absence of an epithelial component and melanin pigment in a biopsy, an adequate panel of immunohistochemical markers is necessary for diagnosis {32068045; 30312883}.

Immunohistochemistry is essential for diagnosing amelanotic tumours. S100 and melanocytic markers (HMB45, tyrosinase, melan-A [MART1], MITF, and SOX10) show variable sensitivity depending on tumour cell morphology, with robust expression in epithelioid/undifferentiated cells and weak and focal expression in spindled cells {11395556; 12717245}. Individual markers can be negative, and if melanoma is suspected, a single marker should not be relied on for screening purposes.

Cytology

FNAC, frequently obtained from metastatic sites, shows features similar to those of cutaneous melanomas, e.g. large discohesive epithelioid or spindled cells with or without melanin pigment.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A minor subset will have a targetable molecular alteration by molecular evaluation, with a low yield.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: highly atypical or undifferentiated malignant cells demonstrating melanocytic differentiation (production of melanin or expression of melanocytic markers by immunohistochemistry) involving epithelium and/or submucosa in the absence of any evidence of metastasis from a cutaneous primary.

Desirable: pagetoid or lentiginous involvement of epithelium; evidence of melanin production.

Staging

All head and neck mucosal MMs are T3–4 and stage III–IV in the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year overall survival rate of head and neck MM ranges from 20% to 50% {24548569; 31670075; 28325255}. Large tumour size and advanced age and stage, including distant metastasis, portend a poor prognosis {24548569; 31077407}. MMs of the oral and nasal cavity tend to be localized (T3) and resectable and have better outcomes than tumours involving paranasal sinuses {12717245; 31670075; 31077407}. Cutaneous melanoma prognostic factors (e.g. Clark level of invasion and Breslow tumour thickness) do not apply. Surgical resection remains the mainstay of therapy. Adjuvant radiotherapy improves local control but not survival {27043023}. In a small number of cases, targeted therapy and immunotherapy appear to be promising {31172258}.
