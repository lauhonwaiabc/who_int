14.0.1.1 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Definition

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a pathogenic constitutional variant of the gene NF1, which encodes neurofibromin (NF1), characterized by at least two of eight diagnostic criteria.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

162200 Neurofibromatosis, type I; NF1

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.10 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Related terminology

Not recommended: von Recklinghausen disease; peripheral neurofibromatosis.

Subtype(s)

Mosaic neurofibromatosis type 1, including segmental neurofibromatosis type 1; spinal neurofibromatosis; neurofibromatosis–Noonan syndrome; 17q11.2 microdeletion syndrome

Localization

NF1 affects many different cell types and tissues in the body, predominantly localizing to central and peripheral nervous system sites.

Clinical features

Multiple café-au-lait macules are usually present at birth, and they increase in number during the first 2 years of life. Skinfold freckling in the axillary, inguinal, and submammary regions occurs in > 80% of adults with NF1. Lisch nodules and skinfold freckling are usually detectable before puberty. Cutaneous neurofibromas typically develop during puberty and are identified in > 85% of adults {28230061}. In contrast, plexiform neurofibromas are considered congenital, developing in 30–50% of children with NF1 {28230061}. Individual plexiform neurofibromas have variable growth rates, which may remain stable over time, but exhibit the highest growth potential during infancy and childhood. A subset of plexiform neurofibromas transform into atypical neurofibromas / atypical neurofibromatous neoplasms of uncertain biological potential, which are associated with an increased risk of progression to high-grade malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) {28551330; 21987445}. Specific bone abnormalities include severe scoliosis, sphenoid wing dysplasia, non-ossifying fibromas, and congenital tibial bowing. Progressive tibial bowing can result in pathological fracture, with the development of pseudarthrosis {18248783}. Other neoplasms observed at increased frequency are optic pathway glioma / pilocytic astrocytoma and other gliomas {32300062}, juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia, rhabdomyosarcoma, glomus tumours of the digits, gastrointestinal stromal tumours, phaeochromocytoma, female breast cancer, and duodenal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs; somatostatinomas) {19410195}.

Orbitofacial neurofibromas

Orbitofacial NF1 has been historically studied as a clinical subtype of NF1 {22193879}. Orbitofacial NF1 is often characterized by progressive, large, disfiguring tumours involving the orbit and periorbital tissues and associated with the remodelling of adjacent skull bones. The rate of recurrence after excision is relatively high, but tumour growth may stabilize as the individual ages.

Epidemiology

NF1 has a birth incidence of approximately 1 in 3000 {25354145}.

Etiology

NF1 is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in NF1, encoding neurofibromin (NF1) {2134734; 2114220; 1694727}. Half of the individuals with NF1 have unaffected parents and represent de novo mutations.

Pathogenesis

Neurofibromin (NF1) primarily functions as a GAP for the RAS proto-oncogene {8499945}. The EVH1 domain of SPRED1 (the protein implicated in Legius syndrome) binds to neurofibromin (NF1) on both sides of the GAP-related domain and recruits neurofibromin (NF1) to the membrane, where it can accelerate RAS inactivation {22751498; 26635368; 27313208}.

Most clinical features of NF1, including café-au-lait macules, bony abnormalities, and benign tumours result from complete loss of neurofibromin (NF1) function (biallelic NF1 inactivation), leading to increased RAS and RAS effector (MEK/ERK or AKT/mTOR) signalling. Atypical neurofibromas / atypical neurofibromatous neoplasms of uncertain biological potential harbour additional genetic alterations, including loss of the CDKN2A/CDKN2B and SMARCA2 regions {21987445; 30722027; 18984156}. MPNSTs frequently show additional aberrations of genes encoding components of the PRC2 complex, and to a lesser extent, TP53 genes {25119042; 25240281; 25305755}. In both neurofibromas and optic pathway gliomas, murine studies have revealed that non-neoplastic (stromal) cells with a heterozygous NF1 mutation, including mast cells, macrophages, neurons, T cells, and microglia, are critical for tumorigenesis and continued growth.

Novel NF1 mutations associated with orbitofacial neurofibromatosis have been detected in some Chinese families, and more severe ocular phenotypes have been observed in successive generations (i.e. anticipation) {31533651}. Differential promoter methylation and expression of the HOX family genes has also been reported in orbitofacial neurofibromas developing in patients with NF1 when compared with neurofibromas developing at other sites {32366326}.

Histopathology

Nerve sheath tumours in NF1 are predominantly neurofibromas, which resemble their sporadic counterparts. Cells of the haematopoietic lineage, including histiocytes and mast cells, are also encountered, which contribute to the tumour microenvironment {28001089}. Recently proposed criteria for atypical neurofibromatous neoplasm of uncertain biological potential are used to describe premalignant or potentially early malignant changes in neurofibromas and include at least two of the following: cytological atypia, hypercellularity, loss of neurofibroma architecture, and increased mitotic rate (< 1.5 mitoses/mm2, equating to 3 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.51 mm in diameter and 0.20 mm2 in area {28551330}). Most MPNSTs in individuals with NF1 are high-grade malignant spindle cell neoplasms with brisk mitotic activity and necrosis, which either develop in a pre-existing plexiform neurofibroma or arise de novo.

Cytology

Similar to sporadic counterparts

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although no mutation hotspots have been identified, a pathogenic NF1 variant is detected in 95% of people with NF1 {10862084}. Nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site mutations, as well as small insertions, small deletions, and small duplications, all result in NF1 inactivation.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

A clinical diagnosis of NF1 requires the presence of at least two of the following features: (1) six or more café-au-lait macules (diameter > 5 mm in children, > 15 mm in adults); (2) two or more neurofibromas of any type, or one plexiform neurofibroma; (3) axillary/inguinal freckling; (4) optic pathway glioma; (5) two or more iris hamartomas (Lisch nodules); (6) a distinctive bony abnormality (tibial dysplasia, pseudarthrosis, orbital dysplasia); (7) a first-degree relative with NF1 (by the above criteria).

Recently these criteria have been revised to include genetic testing and other common but newly recognized features, particularly choroidal abnormalities {34012067}. It must be highlighted that some of these clinical features may be seen in other genetic syndromes including Legius syndrome (MIM number: 641331), Noonan syndrome, and constitutive mismatch repair deficiency {30415209}, making molecular analysis of the NF1 gene increasingly more important for diagnostic purposes {19920235; 25074460; 26337637}.

Staging

Staging for NF1-associated MPNSTs is identical to that for their sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

Individuals often have a shortened lifespan, mainly due to malignant disease and stroke {25354145; 21694737; 11283797}. The standard mortality ratio for many complications is higher in women aged < 50 years {25354145}. Genetic counselling is recommended and includes the possibility of prenatal and/or preimplantation genetic testing.
