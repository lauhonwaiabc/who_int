14.0.0.2 Naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome)

Definition

Naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (NBCCS) is an autosomal dominant disease with a complex multiorgan syndrome caused by constitutional sequence variants in genes involved in the hedgehog signalling pathway (most commonly PTCH1). The most common head and neck pathology finding is odontogenic keratocyst (OKC).

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

109400 Basal cell naevus syndrome; BCNS

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.4 Gorlin syndrome

Related terminology

Acceptable: Gorlin syndrome; Gorlin–Goltz syndrome.

Not recommended: basal cell naevus syndrome; fifth phakomatosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

NBCCS manifests in the skin (basal cell carcinomas, sebaceous cysts), jaw (OKC), bone (congenital anomalies), and brain (macrocephaly, falx calcification, medulloblastoma), as well as in the eyelid, mouth (cleft lip/palate), and ovaries (ovarian fibromas).

Clinical features

The most frequent manifestations of NBCCS are multiple basal cell carcinomas, as well as OKC of the jaw. In one study, basal cell carcinomas and OKC were found together in > 90% of affected individuals by the age of 40 years {8326488}. Other major diagnostic criteria include calcification of the falx cerebri, palmar and plantar pits, and bifid or fused ribs {9096761; 8042673} (see #26375Box 16.01). Clinical features manifest at different times of life. Macrocephaly and rib anomalies can be detected at birth, whereas medulloblastoma typically develops within the first 3 years of life. OKCs are not usually evident before 8 years of age, whereas basal cell carcinomas are usually found 10 years later {25131638}. In one series of 50 children with OKCs, 36% were associated with NBCCS and over half of those had no known family history; therefore, genetic testing is often required to establish the diagnosis {30826393}. Radiation exposure induces multiple basal cell carcinomas of the skin, as well as various other tumour types (e.g. meningioma) {9010431; 33125361}. Rare examples of odontogenic myxoma have also been reported {24163866}.

Epidemiology

The syndromic prevalence has been estimated to be between 1:31 000 and 1:256 000 {27054559}. The overall prevalence of OKC in NBCCS is 66–86% {8326488; 8042673; 9096761; 22246785}, but OKCs have an age-dependent onset, and by the age of 40 years the prevalence is reported to be 91% {8326488}. Cysts commonly arise at a relatively early age, with the mean age of onset estimated at 15.5 years {8042673}, but they may occur much later in life {8326488}.

Some differences have been noted between sporadic and syndromic OKCs, such as a higher frequency of epithelial rests/islands and microcysts in the cyst wall {2441019}. However, these can also occur in non-syndromic cysts and do not represent diagnostic criteria for syndromic cysts.

Etiology

NBCCS results from inactivating constitutional sequence variants in PTCH1 on 9q22 {8681379; 8658145}, its paralog PTCH2 on 1p34 in rare cases {18285427; 23479190}, or the SUFU gene on 10q24 {25403219}. However, patients with germline SUFU mutation do not develop OKC, with most OKCs associated with PTCH1 inactivation instead {29356994; 31725470}.

Numerous PTCH1 constitutional pathogenetic sequence variants have been reported {25131638; 16419085}, although variants were historically detected in only 50–60% of cases {16088933}. That detection rate has increased significantly (to as high as 93%) due to recent testing advances {25131638}. The mostly truncating mutations are distributed over the entire PTCH1 coding region, with no mutation hotspots {8981943}. Missense mutations cluster in a highly conserved region (the sterol-sensing domain), and particularly in transmembrane domain 4. SUFU pathogenic variants are detected in only 5–6% of tested probands and are mostly truncating {25403219}. It has been estimated that a high proportion (14–81%) of cases are the result of new pathogenic variants {8326488; 3547011; 8042673; 9415689}.

Pathogenesis

The PTCH1 gene encodes a 12-transmembrane protein expressed on many progenitor cell types. It functions as a receptor for members of the secreted hedgehog protein family of signalling molecules (SHH, IHH, and DHH) {8906794; 8906787}. PTCH1 controls another transmembrane protein, SMO {8906787; 9278137}. In the absence of ligand, PTCH1 inhibits the activity of SMO {8906787; 9278137}. Hedgehog signalling takes place in the primary cilium {25172843}. Binding of hedgehog proteins to PTCH1 can relieve its inhibition of SMO, allowing its translocation to the tip of the primary cilium, which results in the activation and translocation of GLI transcription factors into the cell nucleus and the transcription of a set of specific target genes controlling the survival, differentiation, and proliferation of progenitor cells. SUFU has been found to directly interact with GLI proteins and is a negative regulator of hedgehog signalling {10564661}. The inactivation of PTCH1 or SUFU leads to the pathological activation of the sonic hedgehog signalling pathway.

Macroscopic appearance

NBCCS tumours have the same features as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

All subtypes of basal cell carcinoma including the pigmented variant can be seen. NBCCS-associated OKCs resemble their sporadic counterparts.

Cytology

Not applicable

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A comprehensive mutation analysis of PTCH1 and SUFU by DNA sequencing and search for deletions/duplications by appropriate methods (e.g. multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, targeted array-based analysis) is able to identify most NBCCS-related constitutional pathogenic variants {20301330}. To detect more complex alterations, additional analysis at the transcript level is occasionally necessary. Despite testing advances, no PTCH1 or SUFU pathogenic variant is identified in 15–27% of index cases {28596197}.

Genetic counselling should be offered to patients with NBCCS and their families. The detection of a germline condition is important in tumour treatment planning (e.g. avoiding radiation therapy) {19276247; 29186568; 33125361}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Major and minor diagnostic criteria are listed in #26375Box 16.01 . The diagnosis of NBCCS requires two major and one minor diagnostic criteria, or one major and three minor diagnostic criteria {8326488; 20301330}.

In cases with inconclusive clinical presentation, the identification of a heterozygous constitutional pathogenic variant of PTCH1 or SUFU is required.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The life expectancy of patients with NBCCS (73.4 years) is only slightly reduced from that of unaffected populations when compared with other genetic tumour syndromes; however, there is a considerable impact on quality of life {22362873}. Surveillance guidelines are well established {28620006}. For carriers of the SUFU pathogenic variant, these include a recommendation for repeated brain MRI screening for medulloblastoma development within the first 5 years of life {28620006}.

Major criteria:

·     Lamellar (sheet-like) calcification of the falx

·     Jaw keratocyst

·     Palmar/plantar pits (≥ 2)

·     Multiple BCCs (> 5 in a lifetime) or a BCC before age 30 years

·     First-degree relative with naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome

Minor criteria:

·     Childhood medulloblastomaa

·     Lymphomesenteric or pleural cysts

·     Macrocephaly (occipitofrontal circumference > 97th centile)

·     Cleft lip/palate

·     Vertebral/rib anomalies

·     Preaxial or postaxial polydactyly

·     Ovarian/cardiac fibromas

·     Ocular anomalies
