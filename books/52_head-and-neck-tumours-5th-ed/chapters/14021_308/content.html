14.0.2.1 Familial adenomatous polyposis, extraintestinal manifestations

Definition

Classic familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant syndrome caused by pathogenic APC variants, characterized by > 100 adenomatous colorectal polyps. More than 70% develop multiple extraintestinal manifestations.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

175100 Familial adenomatous polyposis 1; FAP1

ICD-11 coding

2B90.Y Other specific malignant neoplasms of colon

Related terminology

Not recommended: Gardner syndrome; Turcot syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Attenuated familial adenomatous polyposis

Localization

The bulk of FAP manifestations are colorectal, with gastric and duodenal polyps also common. Desmoid tumours mostly occur in the small bowel mesentery, abdominal wall, or extremities, and less frequently in the head and neck, mostly arising in soft tissue {975005; 8154779} but also in the maxillary sinus, nasopharynx, and oral cavity.

Less frequent extraintestinal malignancies involve the thyroid, biliary tree, pancreas, liver, and brain. Frequent benign extraintestinal features involve bone (osteomas) and dental structures (supernumerary teeth and odontomas).

Clinical features

Classic FAP is characterized by numerous (usually > 100, sometimes thousands) adenomatous polyps of the large intestine, usually occurring in the second decade of life. Without colectomy there is a near-100% risk of colorectal adenocarcinoma by 45 years of age {18194984}.

Attenuated FAP is distinguished from classic FAP by fewer (1–100) colorectal adenomas and a slightly reduced risk (80%) and later onset (56 years) of colorectal cancer {16461775; 20105204}.

Most patients with FAP develop duodenal adenomas, mostly in the periampullary region and distal duodenum.

The most common head and neck manifestations are desmoid-type fibromatosis, thyroid carcinoma, osteomas, and dental anomalies.

Desmoid-type fibromatosis, a locally infiltrative, non-metastasizing (myo)fibroblast neoplasm, occurs in 10–15% of cases, mostly in the small intestinal mesentery, abdominal wall, or extremities, and less frequently in head and neck soft tissues, where it may cause an enlarging painless neck mass, nasal obstruction or epistaxis in the sinonasal tract, facial deformity, proptosis, or dysphagia. The risk of desmoid-type fibromatosis in FAP is increased by prior surgery and certain APC mutations {27387679}. Although it has no metastatic potential, it can cause severe morbidity {10732754; 9014661}.

Multiple osteomas occur due to constitutional APC mutations in 50–90% of patients {19157925}. Such osteomas increase in size over time. They are benign neoplasms composed of mature cortical or cancellous lamellar bone, mostly in the craniofacial bones, most commonly the mandible {18602294}. They often present as a painless swelling on the bone surface, although they may be asymptomatic {22010067}. Radiographically, osteomas form a well-demarcated radiopaque mass (< 20 mm) on the cortical surface.

The thyroid carcinomas are usually multifocal and bilateral in this syndrome and are specifically of the cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma type {29239041; 21455198; 20878367}. The cribriform component is usually admixed with morulae and in some there is a cribriform-predominant architecture {29239041; 29785019; 34019236}.

Multifocal odontomas or supernumerary teeth may occur. Odontomas can occur in any tooth-bearing area and are mixed epithelial and mesenchymal tumour–like hamartomatous malformations, composed of dental hard and soft tissues, that can be either compound odontomas mainly located in the anterior maxilla, or complex odontomas mostly in the posterior mandible or anterior maxilla.

FAP is associated with an increased risk of carcinoma of the thyroid (~15%, cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma) {34019236}, hepatobiliary tree, and pancreas; childhood hepatoblastoma (1%); adrenocortical adenomas/adenocarcinomas; medulloblastoma (< 1%), and (rarely) adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of FAP is 1 in 8000–10 000. It has an equal sex distribution and accounts for < 1% of all colorectal cancers {18194984}.

As many as 30% of FAP-associated desmoids occur in children {975005; 24206198; 23331794; 24290952}; there is no sex predilection, and syndromic desmoids may be multifocal.

Etiology

FAP is a Mendelian autosomal dominant syndrome caused by constitutional pathogenic variants in the APC gene (chromosome 5q22.2) that result in a truncated or absent APC protein. Disease severity varies with the mutation position in the APC gene {10470088; 12057910; 20105204}. Desmoid tumours are associated with constitutional pathogenic variants in APC involving codons 1310–2011 in the mid- to C-terminal portion of the encoded protein {26179480}.

APC acts as a classic tumour suppressor gene and a tumour phenotype arises when the non-mutant allele is spontaneously lost or mutated as a somatic event.

Pathogenesis

The classic APC protein isoform is 2843 amino acids long (among other isoforms) and regulates WNT signalling. Normal APC restrains colorectal epithelial cell proliferation through its role as a scaffold protein that binds critical components that tag (by phosphorylation and ubiquitination) the WNT effector protein β-catenin for proteasomal destruction {10733430}. With APC protein truncation (or absence), the loss of critical APC functions (especially its binding of AXIN proteins and β-catenin) results in greatly impaired degradation of β-catenin with nuclear translocation and upregulation of WNT signalling {23589686}.

Macroscopic appearance

FAP-associated lesions grossly resemble their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

The histopathology of FAP-associated lesions is indistinguishable from that of their sporadic counterparts, with the exception of thyroid carcinoma. Thyroid tumours are those of the cribriform-morular thyroid carcinoma type, which is characteristic of this syndrome {18948764; 20966648; 21455198; 29239041; 34019236; 29785019}. The cribriform component is positive for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and nuclear β-catenin (strong immunopositivity) and negative for thyroglobulin {21455198; 29239041}. PAX8 is absent in half of the tumours and focal in the remainder {34019236}. Morulae are positive for CK5 (KRT5) and CD5, and negative for p63, p40, TTF1, and PAX8 {29239041; 34019236}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

APC mutation is the tumour-initiating event for most colorectal adenomas {8861899}. Similarly, FAP-associated desmoid tumours are caused by APC rather than CTNNB1 mutation, the latter being more common in their sporadic counterparts; nevertheless, they also show nuclear β-catenin immunoreactivity secondary to WNT pathway activation {22419028}. Germline testing and genetic counselling for possible FAP should be considered for patients with head and neck desmoids driven by APC inactivation.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The presence of > 100 colorectal adenomas indicates a diagnosis of classic FAP. Given the phenotypic variability of FAP, several other polyposis conditions can have near-identical features. The essential molecular criterion is the presence of a pathogenic constitutional (germline) variant in APC.

Staging

FAP-associated tumours are staged in the same way as equivalent sporadic tumours at each site.

Prognosis and prediction

The management of tumours is guided by their clinical presentation and severity. Colorectal screening by endoscopy and chemoprevention are in use {28674119; 22122775; 26315524}. Colectomy is often required to prevent the development of colorectal adenocarcinoma. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is guided by the Spigelman stage of duodenal polyposis {2571019; 15951555}. In addition, screening for extraintestinal manifestations is recommended {28674119; 25645574}.

Desmoid tumours generally have a good prognosis {25434922}; however, positive surgical margins and young age are associated with recurrence {20085516; 23913621; 25341748}.
