14.0.4.3 Dyskeratosis congenita

Definition

Dyskeratosis congenita is an inherited syndrome due to defective telomere maintenance characterized by a triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate hyperpigmentation.

Subtype(s)

Autosomal dominant dyskeratosis congenita (DKCA); autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB); X-linked dyskeratosis congenita (DKCX); Zinsser–Engman–Cole syndrome; Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome; Revesz syndrome

Localization

The disease involves the skin as hyperpigmentation, the nail as dystrophy, and the oral mucosa as leukoplakia.

Clinical features

Clinical features are characterized by a triad of oral leukoplakia (80%), nail dystrophy (90%), and reticulate hyperpigmentation/poikiloderma (80–90%) (see #27193Table 16.07). Oral leukoplakia is most commonly seen on the tongue. Signs of dystrophic nails, typically longitudinal ridging, are seen at the ages of 5–13 years {19327580}. Reticular hyperpigmentation is typically a fine, lace-like pattern, frequently on the face, upper trunk, and upper arms. Other mucocutaneous findings include adermatoglyphia (absence of fingerprints), palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, early greying, scalp or eyelash hair loss, and epiphora (watering eyes). Bone marrow failure is seen in the second decade of life in 90% of cases. The patients have an increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck; upper aerodigestive tract cancers (oesophageal carcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma); and haematological malignancy including myelodysplasia, acute myelogenous leukaemia, and Hodgkin lymphoma.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is approximately 1 in 1 million.

Etiology

Dyskeratosis congenita is caused by constitutional pathogenic sequence variants in genes important in telomere biology. Inheritance is most commonly X-linked recessive (DKC1). Other modes of inheritance include autosomal dominant (TERC, TINF2 mutations), autosomal recessive (NOP10, NHP2, WRAP53, CTC1), and autosomal dominant or recessive (TERT). De novo / sporadic mutation can occur with TINF2 {30047419; 33482595}.

Pathogenesis

Telomeres are non-coding sequences at chromosome ends. Telomerase adds repeat nucleotides to the 3ʹ end of DNA after replication, to prevent the shortening of telomeres with each cell division. A number of genes have been implicated in dyskeratosis congenita, most commonly defects in DKC1, encoding dyskerin, which is necessary for telomere maintenance. Without dyskerin, telomeres progressively shorten, which causes cellular apoptosis or senescence, DNA replication problems, and replicative senescence {33482595; 19327580; 17785587; 20008900}. For telomere elongation, DKC1, TERC, TERT, NOP10, and NHP2 encode components of the telomerase complex responsible. DKC1 is the most commonly involved gene. For telomere protection, TINF2 and ACD encode components of the shelterin complex. For the recruitment and docking of telomerase onto the telomere, CTC1 encodes part of the CST complex. WRAP53 is involved in telomere trafficking. RTEL1 and STN1 are involved in telomere replication. PARN is involved in the control of mRNA stability.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Cutaneous findings are nonspecific, comprising epidermal atrophy, dermal telangiectasia, and pigment incontinence {26089061}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clinical diagnosis may be confirmed by telomere length testing. Testing of peripheral blood leukocytes shows the telomere lengths to be below the first percentile {19327580}. Sequencing of affected genes may be helpful in confirming the exact diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of telomere shortening in peripheral blood; clinical findings in the skin, nail, or mouth.

Desirable: demonstration of mutations of affected genes, such as DKC1, TERC, TERT, and others.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis varies depending on both genetic and environmental factors. Patients with a mild form of the disease can present with one or more clinical features, whereas others can develop bone marrow failure by 20 years of age. By the age of 40 years, 40% of patients experience bone marrow failure.
