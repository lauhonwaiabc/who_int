4.5.1.2 Haemangiomas

Definition

Haemangiomas are benign tumours consisting of a small blood vessel proliferation.

Clinical features and imaging

Haemangiomas are benign vascular tumours that occur more commonly in children than in adults, and predominantly in superficial locations of the head and neck region. Deep tumours involving the bones, liver, and other visceral sites can also occur {17113009; 26839221}. The International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classifies vascular anomalies as tumours that can be benign, locally aggressive or borderline, or malignant, or as malformations that are simple, combined, or “other” [[International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies. ISSVA Classification of Vascular Anomalies, 2018 Classification (approved at the May 2018 General Assembly in Amsterdam, the Netherlands); 2018. Available from: https://www.issva.org/classification.]]. Some subtypes of haemangioma are based on the type of vessels involved, such as capillary, venous, arteriovenous, lymphatic, or littoral cell; others are based on the morphology, including hobnail, papillary, and glomeruloid, or on the location, such as synovial haemangioma and intramuscular angioma {17113009}. Clinically, these lesions present as raised papules with a red or violaceous colour in superficial sites, and in deep locations they present with haemoptysis and pressure on adjacent structures or as incidental findings on imaging. With rapid growth, these lesions may cause thrombocytopenia due to consumption {32281140}.

Infantile haemangiomas represent the most common soft tissue tumours in the first months of life, with growth until about 1 year of age and spontaneous regression by 4 years of age {31303649}, whereas congenital haemangiomas are largest at birth and usually show partial or complete regression {33324762}. Synovial haemangiomas occur within the joint space of young patients and may be associated with pain or osteoarthritis.

Benign vascular tumours show vascular flow on Doppler ultrasound imaging or postcontrast enhancement peripherally on CT {10741493}. They are thought to arise due to endothelial proliferation with uncontrolled angiogenesis due to abnormalities in the HIF1α, VEGF, or PI3K/AKT pathways. There is an association between vascular tumours and malformations such as in Sturge–Weber syndrome, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome, and PIK3CA-related overgrowth syndromes (e.g. Klippel–Trénaunay syndrome) {31095066; 29478617; 16049535; 28654575}. Treatment decisions depend on the age of the patient, rate of tumour growth or regression, potential complications of the lesion (e.g. scarring), tumour location, risks and benefits of treatment, and availability of medical expertise. Some treatments include steroids, other systemic medications, laser therapy or sclerotherapy, embolization, and/or surgical resection {32238724; 29376569; 31807282}. The lesions can recur if incompletely excised, but malignant transformation to angiosarcoma is rare and primarily reported in adults with deep lesions {12360047}.

Histopathology

Haemangiomas are well-circumscribed, lobular proliferations of capillary-sized blood vessels with a single layer of endothelium, usually arising from a larger feeding vessel. Epithelioid haemangiomas tend to have plump and rounded endothelial cells, and not the standard spindle cell. Cavernous haemangiomas show dilated vascular channels. Congenital haemangiomas show a solid pattern with increased mitoses in the rapid growth phase, and more well-defined vascular channels as they mature. Infantile haemangioma has an early proliferative phase with immature dendritic-type cells, then an early regressive phase where capillaries dilate and start to show apoptotic debris in the basement membrane, followed by late regression with ghosts of capillaries {32281140}. Phleboliths may be seen in vascular channels of spindle cell haemangiomas.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB is not typically performed, because of the characteristic imaging findings, a high “Insufficient” rate, and a potential increased risk of bleeding complications. Subclassification into the type of vascular proliferation can be challenging based on the limited material, leading to a somewhat vague diagnosis of a “benign vascular tumour or proliferation favouring a haemangioma”.

Benign vascular tumours, such as haemangiomas, can be challenging to diagnose on small biopsies and require clinical and imaging correlation. They can mimic vascular malformations and granulation tissue with angiogenesis, and the history can be critical, because a history of trauma raises the possibility of reactive granulation tissue. Haemangioendotheliomas are typically more epithelioid (with intracellular vascular lumina containing red blood cells) and infiltrative, with an indeterminate biological behaviour ranging between that of haemangiomas and angiosarcomas {23288560; 16470861}. Pulmonary sclerosing pneumocytoma, which was previously referred to as sclerosing haemangioma of the lung, shows positivity for TTF1, EMA, and cytokeratin in the surface cells {27761142}. Kaposi sarcoma tends to have more pointed slit-like spaces with cytoplasmic eosinophilic globules, occurs most commonly in HIV-positive patients, and has KSHV/HHV8 positivity that is not seen in haemangiomas.

Ancillary testing

Haemangiomas are positive for vascular ICC markers, which include CD31, CD34, von Willebrand factor, thrombomodulin, FLI1, and ERG, and they are usually negative for cytokeratins. GLUT1 is positive in infantile haemangioma and negative in congenital haemangioma. D2-40 (podoplanin) is positive in lymphatic proliferations {15810951}. The key negative stains in these lesions include KSHV/HHV8 to exclude Kaposi sarcoma and TFE3, which is seen in epithelioid haemangioendothelioma. Cytokeratin can show some weak staining in vascular tumours, but this is more common in epithelioid angiosarcoma {22406364}.

Molecular studies are increasingly being used, particularly in challenging cases; mutations in RASA1, GNAQ, and PDGFRB have been reported in haemangiomas {23656586}, but molecular abnormalities become more important in supporting the diagnosis of an intermediate to malignant vascular lesion. The recent discovery of YAP1, TFE3, CAMTA1, and MAML2 rearrangements in epithelioid haemangioendotheliomas has made the definitive diagnosis of these neoplasms easier on small biopsies {32991341; 34381186; 26414223}.
