6.2.1.8 Myoepithelial neoplasms

Definition

Myoepithelial neoplasms of soft tissue share morphological, immunophenotypic, and genetic features with their counterparts in salivary gland. The group includes benign myoepithelioma and mixed tumour, as well as malignant myoepithelial carcinomas.

Clinical features and imaging

Myoepithelial neoplasms of soft tissue can affect patients of all ages, with a peak incidence in early adulthood to middle age; they show an equal sex distribution {8990137; 10389622; 12960802; 18043035}. Approximately 20% of cases occur in the paediatric population, and most of those cases are malignant {18043035}.

Most patients present with a palpable, often painless mass. Tumours most commonly arise in the limbs and limb girdles (particularly the lower rather than the upper) and trunk, and they are more frequently subcutaneous than deep-seated. Rarely, tumours arise in the head and neck, bone, and visceral organs {10389622; 12960802; 18043035; 20815032; 24585572; 23681076}. Some present as primarily cutaneous lesions {14745720; 21725291; 23588365}.

The overall behaviour is indolent. Benign tumours show a 20% risk of recurrence and rarely metastasize, but myoepithelial carcinomas show recurrence and distant metastasis in 40–50% of cases {12960802; 18043035}.

Histopathology

Myoepithelial tumours are morphologically heterogeneous; they characteristically show a wide spectrum of architectural and cytopathological features both within a given lesion and between different tumours {12960802; 18043035; 20815032}. Growth patterns vary, from reticular to trabecular, nested, or solid. Myxoid or hyalinized stroma is common. Tumour cells can be ovoid or epithelioid, usually with uniform nuclei and eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm. Some cases may show abundant cytoplasmic vacuolization, whereas others can have predominant spindle or plasmacytoid forms with hyaline cytoplasmic inclusions {7541966; 8990137; 12960802}. Osseous and/or cartilaginous differentiation is seen in as many as 15% of cases, with squamous or adipocytic metaplasia seen less frequently. Approximately 10% of benign myoepithelial tumours are mixed tumours, defined by the presence of ductal differentiation.

Myoepithelial carcinomas show appreciable moderate to severe nuclear atypia – the most reliable criterion for malignancy – often with high mitotic activity and necrosis. Malignant tumours in children often show an undifferentiated round cell component {12960802; 18043035}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis is broad and depends on the clinical presentation and anatomical site. In somatic soft tissue, diagnostic considerations include extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, and ossifying fibromyxoid tumour.

In adult patients, myoepithelial carcinoma of soft tissue should be separated from metastatic carcinoma and melanoma, which requires clinical correlation and ICC. In paediatric patients, myoepithelial carcinomas can show predominant round cell morphology, and a wide range of round cell sarcomas may need to be excluded. Ancillary testing is useful in resolving most diagnostic considerations.

Ancillary testing

The diagnosis of myoepithelial neoplasms typically requires confirmation of myoepithelial differentiation with PLAG1 ICC positivity and PLAG1 rearrangements – a feature of mixed tumours. EWSR1 gene rearrangements are common in myoepitheliomas and myoepithelial carcinomas {22038920; 23630011}. Occasionally, FISH to detect EWSR1 rearrangement is required. Definitive classification is typically challenging on cytopathological material, and the diagnosis is suggested on FNAB in less than half of all cases {34649776}. However, in rare cases FISH alone may not be sufficient, because EWSR1 fusions are common in a wide range of mesenchymal tumours, and NGS is required to identify characteristic specific fusion genes.

Most myoepithelial tumours express keratins, EMA, and S100 {18043035; 12960802; 10389622}. SOX10 is positive in 80% of cases, although less commonly in malignant tumours, where it is positive in 30% {25724000}. Other commonly positive markers are GFAP (in 50%) and p63 (in 40%) {18043035; 12960802; 22031315}. Staining for myogenic markers is variable and less diagnostically useful, but calponin is commonly positive in 90% of cases, SMA in 60%, and desmin in 20% {8990137; 12960802}.

In the differential diagnosis, extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma can show rare staining for S100, but it is negative for keratins and EMA; FISH for EWSR1::NR4A3 is diagnostically specific, but EWSR1 FISH is not. Epithelioid sarcoma shows positivity for keratin and EMA, but it shows frequent staining for CD34 and is negative for S100 and SOX10, with SMARCB1 loss as a consistent feature although it is present in a small subset of myoepithelial carcinomas. Ossifying fibromyxoid tumour can also show variable staining for S100, keratin, and GFAP, but desmin is more frequently positive in ossifying fibromyxoid tumour (in as many as 50% of cases) than in myoepithelial tumours. PHF1 rearrangements are a feature of ossifying fibromyxoid tumour.

In the paediatric age group, the most common differential diagnoses include Ewing sarcoma, which is positive for CD99 and NKX2-2 and shows the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion; alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, which is positive in desmin and myogenin ICC and has the FOXO1 rearrangement; poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma showing SS18::SSX; high-grade myxoid liposarcoma, which is positive for DDIT3 or DDIT3 FISH; and CIC-rearranged sarcoma, positive for WT1, ETV4, and the CIC rearrangement.

Extrarenal malignant rhabdoid tumour may also be considered; it shows variable staining for keratin, EMA, and S100 but lacks EWSR1 rearrangement and shows SMARCB1 loss.

FISH can be used to detect EWSR1 rearrangements, which are present in approximately half of all myoepitheliomas and myoepithelial carcinomas of soft tissue, as well as in skin and bone lesions {20815032; 21725291; 23588365; 25231231; 31135487}. Fusion partners are heterogeneous and include POU5F1, PBX3, PBX1, ZNF444, KLF15, KLF17, and ATF1, and rarely there are alternative FUS gene rearrangements {20815032; 25706482; 25231231; 22154050; 31994243}. Some genotype–phenotype associations have been suggested; for example, the most common fusion, EWSR1::POU5F1, is found in as many as 28% of cases, and it tends to be associated with malignant deep-seated tumours in the limbs and nested and sheet-like growth of epithelioid cells with clear cytoplasm {20815032; 31994243}.

A small subset (< 10%) of myoepithelial carcinomas show loss of SMARCB1 expression, in most (if not all) cases occurring secondary to homozygous deletions of SMARCB1 {18043035; 19033866; 24585572}.
