8.2.3.1 Leiomyosarcoma

Definition

Leiomyosarcoma (LMS) is a malignant neoplasm composed of cells showing smooth muscle differentiation.

Clinical features and imaging

LMS accounts for approximately 10% of all primary soft tissue sarcomas and for 10–15% of limb sarcomas; it typically occurs in adults, with a peak incidence in the sixth decade of life, although it may develop in younger individuals as well {7998525; 7272926; 35436892; 10403297}. LMS occurs most often in the extremities and retroperitoneum, but also in other locations such as visceral organs and bones {23354568; 7272926; 17154171}. Many subcutaneous and deep soft tissue LMSs originate from a small to medium-sized vein, and a subgroup of these neoplasms originate from large blood vessels such as the vena cava and other large veins {11756765; 6488138}. The imaging features of LMS are nonspecific. The signal on T1-weighted MRI is isointense to muscle, and there is an intermediate or high signal on T2-weighted images. CT generally shows heterogeneous soft tissue masses {17681989}.

Histopathology

LMS shows diffuse cellularity and a predominantly fascicular pattern of growth, with the tumour bundles intersecting each other at wide angles. Storiform, palisaded, and aimless patterns may be present in some tumours or in different areas of the same tumour {7272926}. Most LMSs show moderate cellular and nuclear pleomorphism and consist of spindle cells with plump, blunt-ended nuclei and moderate to abundant, pale to brightly eosinophilic fibrillary cytoplasm. The degree of cellular and nuclear atypia varies in different cases and in different areas of the same case {24384850}. The majority of LMSs are of high grade and show high mitotic counts, including atypical mitoses, and necrosis is often present in larger tumours {11756765; 7272926}. Pleomorphic variants of LMS are predominantly composed of pleomorphic spindle and polygonal cells admixed with varying large, bizarre, atypical cells and multinuclear giant cells, with or without abundant eosinophilic or fibrillary cytoplasm {11474287}. Other findings occasionally seen include focal fibrosis, myxoid change, and hyalinized hypocellular areas {11756765}, while epithelioid morphology, multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells, and areas with osseous or chondro-osseous metaplasia are rare findings.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

LMS smears are usually moderate to highly cellular and show distinctive cytoarchitectural patterns at low-power microscopy, which in most cases is a characteristic fascicular/spindle cell/pleomorphic pattern. LMS shares many cytopathological features with other spindle cell lesions, as well as with high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas of other lineage and epithelioid malignancies. In two large series of LMS cytopathology, only 24% and 34.8% of cases, respectively, were specifically diagnosed as LMS in FNAB {12619091; 16900474}.

The cytopathological differential diagnosis of LMS includes benign spindle cell lesions, such as leiomyoma, nodular fasciitis, desmoid fibromatosis, and schwannoma. Both leiomyoma and LMS are positive for SMA and desmin. Increased cellularity, pleomorphic nuclei, and mitoses help to distinguish leiomyoma and LMS. The clinical presentation of nodular fasciitis differs from that of LMS in that nodular fasciitis commonly presents as a rapidly growing and occasionally painful or tender superficial nodule that regresses spontaneously in most cases {6942611; 8519200}. Smears from fibromatoses are usually of lower cellularity, showing less pronounced pleomorphism and nuclear atypia, compared with LMS {17477374}. Smears from schwannoma show cohesive fascicles and sheets of variable size in a fibrillary background matrix and few dispersed cells, and the tumour cells have elongated, wavy, occasionally comma- or boomerang-shaped nuclei that are often pointed {16680779; 16850489; 25641870}. Schwannoma shows diffuse S100 positivity and stains negatively for smooth muscle markers.

Malignant entities such as monophasic synovial sarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour, and high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of LMS. A distinctive pattern of cohesive tissue fragments of bland spindle cells alternating with dispersed cells and a pericapillary arrangement of tumour cells suggests monophasic synovial sarcoma {12722117; 12203876; 14696140; 9500652; 19267417}. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour occurs commonly in the setting of neurofibromatosis. Differentiation of LMS from myofibroblastic sarcoma and high-grade pleomorphic sarcomas can be difficult in individual cases, apart from blunt-ended nuclei favouring LMS, and the diagnosis relies on ICC.

Ancillary testing

LMS stains for SMA, desmin, and caldesmon, although sometimes only focally. Focal cytokeratin and EMA positivity has been reported. Synovial sarcomas show TLE1 positivity in most cases, and molecular testing with the presence of SS18 fusion confirms the diagnosis. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours express S100 and SOX10 focally, with a subset of cases showing loss of H3 p.K28me3 (K27me3); they do not express smooth muscle markers.
