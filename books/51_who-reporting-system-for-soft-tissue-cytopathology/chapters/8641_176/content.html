8.6.4.1 Chordoma

Definition

Chordoma is a malignant tumour that shows notochordal differentiation, usually arises in the axial skeleton, and is characterized by brachyury immunoexpression and loss of SMARCB1 expression secondary to SMARCB1 deletion in poorly differentiated chordoma.

Clinical features and imaging

The majority of chordomas arise in bones of the axial skeleton, with relatively equal distribution to the base of skull (in 32%), mobile spine (in 32.8%), and sacrum/coccyx (in 29.2%) {11227920; 21184634}. The presentation is with pain and site-related symptoms. Most poorly differentiated chordomas arise in paediatric patients, most commonly in the base of skull {29483606}. Poorly differentiated chordoma and dedifferentiated chordoma that shows transition to a high-grade sarcoma are associated with more aggressive behaviour and poor prognosis {29483606}.

Imaging typically demonstrates a destructive, large mass that expands the bone, often with extension into soft tissue. Tumours appear lytic on CT, while MRI often shows a lobulated, septate, and heterogeneous mass, usually T1-hypointense and T2-hyperintense. Dedifferentiated chordomas may present with rapid growth and rapid worsening of symptoms, as well as more T2-hypointense areas on MRI {18450635}.

Histopathology

Chordoma shows a lobulated architecture separated by fibrous septa. Large epithelioid cells with variably clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm are arranged in nests, cords, or sheets, embedded in the abundant myxoid extracellular matrix. Tumour cells have round nuclei with small or inconspicuous nuclei. Some tumour cells show abundant bubbly cytoplasm, the so-called physaliphorous cells. Occasionally the matrix may appear chondromyxoid and mimic hyaline cartilage. Poorly differentiated chordoma is a poorly differentiated neoplasm with notochordal differentiation showing more solid growth, often with rhabdoid or signet-ring cells and a scarcity of physaliphorous cells, with minimal myxoid stroma and extensive necrosis.

Dedifferentiated chordoma is a biphasic neoplasm characterized by a high-grade sarcoma with spindle and/or pleomorphic cells, either admixed with or abruptly transitioning from conventional chordoma. The dedifferentiated component may show osteosarcomatous and rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Variable cellularity Round, cuboidal, and epithelioid cells arranged in cords within an abundant myxoid matrix forming discohesive tissue fragments Extracellular matrix is granular, fibrillary, and metachromatic (Giemsa) Round nuclei and occasional binucleation Varying amounts of cytoplasm, which is granular or variably vacuolated, ranging from “signet ring” (single large eccentric vacuole impinging the nucleus) to abundant and multivacuolated physaliphorous cells Cytopathological atypia and pleomorphism can be more pronounced in some cases Poorly differentiated chordoma shows scarce matrix and rhabdoid and/or more uniform epithelioid cells with central large round nuclei and large nucleoli, with some cells showing eccentric cytoplasm Dedifferentiated chordoma shows a dedifferentiated component of nonspecific high-grade spindle cell or pleomorphic malignant neoplasm, with frequent necrosis, with chordoma usually present focally

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Clinical correlation is necessary to exclude benign notochordal cell tumour, which is usually found incidentally and, when presenting as a large tumour, may be challenging to distinguish from chordoma. Both entities express brachyury, but even with larger size, benign notochordal cell tumour does not show destructive growth or invasion into soft tissue, and it is composed of solid sheets of vacuolated tumour cells that resemble adipocytes, often admixed with physaliphorous cells {20121620}. Microcyst formation with colloid-like extracellular material may be seen, but myxoid stroma is absent in most cases of benign notochordal cell tumour. Some notochordal tumours have indeterminate features and show permeative growth, soft tissue extension, and myxoid change, and have been designated “atypical notochordal cell tumour” {27819871}, but standardized criteria have not been established for this provisional entity.

The differential diagnosis for conventional chordoma also includes chondrosarcoma with myxoid stroma, myxopapillary ependymoma, soft tissue myoepithelioma and myoepithelial carcinoma, pleomorphic adenoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, and renal cell carcinoma. Correlation with clinical history and imaging is helpful. Brachyury reliably distinguishes chordoma from these mimics, which are all negative.

Ancillary testing

Chordoma is positive for epithelial markers, including cytokeratin and EMA, and variably positive for S100. All cases show expression of brachyury (encoded by TBXT), a transcription factor required for notochordal development {21171078; 16538613}. Nuclear staining for brachyury is highly sensitive and specific for chordoma and reliably distinguishes it from cytomorphological mimics {24554551}. Brachyury is positive in poorly differentiated chordoma, but expression is absent in the dedifferentiated component in dedifferentiated chordomas, and these regions may show focal keratin expression {16538613; 26099010}, necessitating correlation with clinical and imaging and a broad ICC panel if conventional chordoma is not sampled. Poorly differentiated chordoma shows SMARCB1 loss secondary to SMARCB1 deletions {21057957; 31135077}.

Myoepithelial tumours also show positivity for S100 and keratins, but they additionally show frequent staining for GFAP, SOX10, and p63/p40, which are negative in chordoma. Most cases of metastatic renal cell carcinoma do not show the fibrillary metachromatic stroma of chordomas and are positive for PAX8. Metastatic adenocarcinomas may show 3D overlapping tumour cell tissue fragments and gland formation. Myxopapillary ependymoma shows GFAP immunopositivity {24554551; 18301055; 19521276}.
