4.6.1.1 Tenosynovial giant cell tumour

Definition

Tenosynovial giant cell tumour (TGCT) is a neoplasm with synovial differentiation arising from the synovium of joints, bursae, and tendon sheaths, involving the articular and/or extra-articular soft tissues.

Clinical features and imaging

TGCTs encompass a group of lesions most often arising from the synovium of joints, bursae, and tendon sheaths, and they are subdivided, based mostly on their growth characteristics, into localized TGCT, which is also known as giant cell tumour of tendon sheath and forms a discrete mass, and diffuse TGCT, which is also known as pigmented villonodular synovitis and displays an infiltrative and multinodular growth pattern. Clinical and imaging features correlate with the specific subtype. The localized form is most commonly observed in adults aged 30–50 years, arising most frequently in the hands (especially the fingers), followed in decreasing order of frequency by the toes, ankle and foot, knee, and wrist {3943019}. Clinical symptoms may include a painless to painful soft tissue swelling, often present for years prior to diagnosis, and limitation of movement. On imaging, localized TGCTs form well-circumscribed masses, frequently overlying a joint and sometimes associated with underlying bone erosion.

Diffuse TGCT most commonly occurs in adults aged 20–40 years. Although virtually any joint may be affected, the larger joints, most commonly the knee, followed by the ankle and foot, hip, and shoulder, are most frequently involved. Extra-articular examples are more often seen arising in the knee or wrist {10757395}. Clinical symptoms include localized pain (often of a long duration), tenderness, swelling, joint effusion, haemarthrosis, and limitation of motion. Given the tendency of TGCT to involve intra-articular spaces, MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for detection. Imaging features include soft tissues masses, sometimes extending outside of the joint, and single to multiple cortical bone defects, rarely large and mimicking a primary bone neoplasm {26440548}.

Histopathology

Localized TGCTs form well-circumscribed, lobulated masses, which may be connected to the underlying synovium or tendon sheath. Collagenous septa divide the tumour into lobules, composed of variable numbers of predominantly smaller histiocyte-like cells with pale cytoplasm and round to reniform nuclei and larger polygonal to epithelioid cells with amphophilic cytoplasm and a peripheral rim of haemosiderin deposition. Aggregates of foamy histiocytes, osteoclast-type giant cells, and haemosiderin deposits are virtually always admixed at least focally. The background stroma may be significantly collagenized. Identical histological features are observed in diffuse tenosynovial giant cell tumours, but the growth pattern is infiltrative and expansile, often causing significant and grossly visible enlargement of the synovial fronds. Osteoclast-type giant cells tend to be less common and may be absent in up to 20% of cases. Tissue tears and cyst-like spaces, sometimes filled with blood, are observed in a minority of examples {10757395}. Infarct-like necrosis, leaving residual “tombstones” of osteoclast-type giant cells, is not infrequently observed and may encompass virtually the entire tumour, presumably the result of torsion of the tumour nodule(s). Mitotic activity, especially among the diffuse form of tenosynovial giant cell tumour, may exceed 10 mitoses per 10 HPF, but in the absence of significant nuclear pleomorphism or frank sarcoma-like changes, this has no prognostic relevance.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for TGCT is largely dependent on the site and growth pattern (localized vs diffuse), encompassing both neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions made up of a predominantly histiocytoid to epithelioid cell population, with or without abundant osteoclast-type giant cells. Correlation with imaging studies, when available, considerably narrows the differential, helping to provide a more definitive diagnosis.

In the extremities, especially the hand, it is important to distinguish localized TGCT from epithelioid sarcoma, which usually lacks osteoclast-type giant cells, admixed inflammatory cells, and haemosiderin pigment deposition. Giant cell tumours of soft parts are another differential diagnosis of localized TGCT, and they arise in the superficial soft tissues of the dermis or subcutis, primarily in the extremities, and are less polymorphous than TGCT, with uniform ovoid to slightly spindle mononuclear cells, a prominent fibrovascular stroma, and frequently secondary haemorrhagic and cystic changes {10716152; 10680893}.

Revision arthroplasty rarely undergoes FNAB, but the clinical setting, with polarizable foreign material and histiocytic giant cell reaction, is diagnostic {3554529}.

Intra-articular haemorrhage can produce haemosiderin deposits and villous hyperplasia of the synovium.

Giant cell tumour of bone (GCTB) should only rarely be a problem for the differential diagnosis from TGCT when clinical and imaging correlation is carried out. GCTBs generally affect patients between 20 and 40 years of age, usually arising in the epiphyseal regions of long bones, with less frequent involvement of the sacrum and vertebral body, pelvis, and small tubular bones of the hands and feet. In the vast majority of cases, these tumours remain confined to the bone, and soft tissue extension is the exception {16311110}. Locally recurrent GCTBs presenting in the soft tissue are a differential diagnosis of giant cell tumour of soft tissue and TGCT. GCTBs produce hypercellular smears that are more cohesive, with a dual population of uniform oval to slightly spindle mononuclear cells admixed with usually distinctly larger osteoclast-type multinucleated giant cells. Like TGCT, infarct-like necrosis may be present and sometimes extensive, and foamy histiocytes are not uncommon.

High cellularity and the presence of mitotic activity may raise the differential of malignant TGCT, but these extraordinarily rare tumours demonstrate overtly malignant cells with high nuclear grade, tumour necrosis, atypical mitotic figures, and areas of overt pleomorphic spindle cell sarcomatous differentiation {30206409}. Most resemble undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas or high-grade myxofibrosarcomas. As mentioned previously, mitotic activity, especially in the diffuse form of an otherwise typical TGCT, may be brisk, and this finding alone does not equate to “malignant” TGCT.

Ancillary testing

ICC is generally not required to establish the diagnosis of TGCT. Clusterin is frequently positive in the larger mononuclear cells, some of which may also show desmin reactivity. Histiocytic markers (CD68 and CD163) are diffusely expressed by the predominant smaller histiocyte-like cells and osteoclasts. Cytokeratins, EMA, S100, SOX10, CD21, and CD35 are uniformly negative. Cytogenetic analysis has revealed that 1p13 (CSF1) is most frequently involved by structural aberrations, most often fused with 2q37 (COL6A3), in a minority of the lesional cells {17918257; 10392632}. GCTB also demonstrates a different molecular pathogenesis with H3-3A (H3F3A) mutations, readily assessed by the diagnostic ICC surrogate H3 p.G34W {14696139; 29757500}.
