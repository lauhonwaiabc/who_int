6.5.5.3 Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma

Definition

Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma (AFH) is a rare neoplasm of intermediate (rarely metastasizing) malignant potential, occurring mostly in the subcutis and characterized by varying proportions of epithelioid, ovoid, or spindle cells arranged in a nodular and often syncytial growth pattern, with haemorrhagic pseudovascular spaces and frequently a peripheral fibrous pseudocapsule with a prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate.

Clinical features and imaging

AFH presents as a slow-growing, superficial, painless, 20–40 mm mass, which may simulate a haematoma or a haemangioma. It occurs most commonly in the deep dermis or subcutis of the extremities, preferentially in areas where lymph nodes are found, such as the popliteal fossa, axilla, inguinal, and neck regions {228836; 2174650; 25927151}. Unusual visceral, intramammary, and intracranial locations have also been increasingly reported {21822206; 31236300; 28281318; 33141488}. MRI shows a predominantly well-circumscribed, primarily cystic mass with a double rim and marginal infiltration {26919861}.

Histopathology

The classic histopathology of AFH is distinctive, with characteristic features including a well-circumscribed and lobulated margin at lower magnification; a thick fibrous pseudocapsule with variable amounts of peripheral lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate; multinodular growth in sheets, short fascicles, and sometimes whorls of uniform, bland, ovoid to spindle to histiocytoid neoplastic cells; and pseudoangiomatoid spaces filled with blood and lined by tumour cells {228836; 25927151; 17724745; 11063792}. The presence of nodules or sheets of histiocytoid cells is a consistent feature, but myxoid, reticular, small round cell, and pleomorphic subtypes have also been described {24503754; 18422688}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate to high cellularity or hypocellular and bloody due to blood-filled cystic component Small and large cellular tissue fragments, some containing vessels Cohesive cellular tissue fragments, often associated with thin vessels, forming a whorl or “ball”-like structure Ovoid to spindle histiocytoid cells with a moderate amount of vacuolated cytoplasm and an ovoid or folded nucleus with a small inconspicuous nucleolus and perinucleolar clearing (Pap, “vesicular” nucleus) Occasional nuclear atypia and pleomorphism Variable numbers of single histiocytoid cells, haemosiderin-laden cells, lymphocytes, and plasma cells in the background

Reference(s): {2170972; 16007669; 22045622}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of AFH is wide, depending on its morphology and location. For a superficial lesion, considerations include haemangioma, organizing haematoma, aneurysmal fibrous histiocytoma, and nodular fasciitis. In cases with a prominent lymphoid component resembling lymphoid tissue, considerations that enter the differential diagnosis include metastatic disease, Kaposi sarcoma, follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, and intranodal palisaded myofibroblastoma. AFH can also be confused with Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour due to the frequent expression of CD99, desmin, and ALK, respectively {29877921; 10571514; 27166030}. In addition, pleomorphic AFH may be misdiagnosed as pleomorphic sarcoma {18422688}.

Ancillary testing

The tumour cells are positive for desmin in 50–60% of cases and show variable, nonspecific staining for EMA, CD68, and CD99 {10571514; 27166030}. In ICC, ALK, especially clone D5F3, is positive in the majority of AFHs {29877921}. AFH is most commonly characterized by recurrent chromosomal translocations resulting in EWSR1::CREB1 (seen in most cases), followed by EWSR1::ATF1, and rarely FUS::ATF1 gene fusions, which can confirm the diagnosis of AFH {19801966; 18094413}.
