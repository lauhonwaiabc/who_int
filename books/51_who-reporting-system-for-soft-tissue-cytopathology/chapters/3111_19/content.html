3.1.1.1 Introduction

A categorization of “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” is used to convey to the patient care team that the specimen provided no useful information concerning the targeted lesion and that additional sampling is necessary. The precise terminology for this category has been controversial, and the terms provided have been used interchangeably and inconsistently among institutions, which has resulted in all three terms being offered as options for this category. An institution should choose one preferred term and use it consistently.

An “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” categorization may result from a variety of technical, preparation, and sampling issues and is defined in relation to the clinical history, physical examination and imaging findings, and ultimately the material for assessment on the direct smears or in LBC preparation. This is why a descriptive definition of this category is preferred over a definition based on the number of cells.

Any specimen is regarded as adequate if it demonstrates a pathological process consistent with the clinical setting and imaging findings. If there are any atypical features in the cells or the background, even if there is suboptimal preparation or degenerative limitations, the case is categorized as “Atypical” rather than “Insufficient”. When only benign cells, such as adipocytes or skeletal muscle fibres, are present, some cytopathologists regard this as “Non-diagnostic” if the FNAB target was regarded as a suspicious mass or lesion; others describe and categorize the material on the smears as “Benign” and add a mandatory caveat that “the material may not represent the targeted lesion”. In this the first edition of the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System), both approaches are acceptable; an institution should choose which approach to take, stick to it, and clearly communicate this approach to their clinicians. In both scenarios, a repeat biopsy, most commonly a repeat FNAB or a CNB, is required.

The FNAB sample may be “Insufficient”, but a later cell block or other ancillary test may provide sufficient material for a diagnosis. An institution or cytopathologist can decide whether to issue the FNAB report and then later a supplementary report where the diagnostic category is changed, or to report the FNAB only once all cell block and ancillary tests are available.

The reasons why the FNAB is categorized as “Insufficient” should be stated in the report, such as because of obscuring blood or ultrasound gel, poor smearing of the material on the slides, slow air-drying artefact in Giemsa stains, air-drying artefact in alcohol-fixed Pap-stained slides, crush artefact, lysis of cells due to contamination with local anaesthetic, formalin vapour effect, or low cellularity of the sample due to technical difficulties in obtaining material. The nature of the specimen and poor FNAB technique by the proceduralist also have an effect on adequacy.
