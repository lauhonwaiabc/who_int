6.2.1.4 Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour

Definition

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour (IMT) is a distinctive, rarely metastasizing neoplasm composed of myofibroblastic and fibroblastic spindle cells accompanied by an inflammatory infiltrate of plasma cells, lymphocytes, and/or eosinophils.

Clinical features and imaging

IMT typically affects children and young adults, although it may occur in older adults {17414097; 7611533; 15602716; 17938159}. Clinical presentation varies by site of origin, and the anatomical distribution of IMT is wide, with frequent occurrence in the abdominopelvic and retroperitoneal regions, lung, and mediastinum. The head and neck, visceral organs, and somatic soft tissues are less common sites {7611533; 17938159; 16967468; 34001695}. Systemic symptoms (e.g. fever and malaise) and laboratory abnormalities may be present {15602716; 7611533; 11381424; 20730898}.

Epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma (EIMS) is a distinct clinicopathological subtype presenting almost exclusively as an intra-abdominal mass in male patients, and it is associated with an aggressive clinical course {21164297; 26139079; 24034896}.

IMT demonstrates variable imaging findings. Lesions are lobulated to infiltrative, and they enhance after contrast administration – frequently heterogeneously {27224222; 27153788}. Calcifications may be present {11381424}.

Histopathology

Variable histopathological features characterize IMT and can be conceptualized as three main but often overlapping patterns: myxoid granulation tissue–like, hypercellular spindle cell, and hypocellular fibrous. Each pattern is characterized by different proportions of cytopathologically bland spindle fibroblastic/myofibroblastic cells, myxoid matrix, vascularity, and collagen {17414097; 7611533; 17938159; 31950474}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The overall cytomorphological features of IMT are inconsistent and nonspecific, owing to its varied histopathology. EIMS demonstrates features distinct from those of classic IMT, reflecting its unique histopathological features.

The cytopathological diagnosis is challenging, and the differential spans reactive and reparative processes as well as malignancy. The differential is shaped by the predominant histopathological pattern and anatomical site of involvement. Having a high index of suspicion and applying ancillary testing judiciously improves the diagnostic accuracy {20730898}.

For classic IMT, reactive fibrosis with background inflammation is a common diagnostic consideration or misdiagnosis {11391606; 20730898; 15452903}. IgG4-related sclerosing disease or haematopoietic malignancies may be considered in cases where the inflammatory component predominates {15452903}. In the hypercellular spindle cell pattern of IMT, a range of benign and malignant spindle cell tumours enters the differential, including spindle cell or embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, smooth muscle tumours, nerve sheath tumours, and gastrointestinal stromal tumour {29673102; 34001695}.

For EIMS, histiocytic sarcoma is a differential {28805986}, along with anaplastic large cell lymphoma, epithelioid gastrointestinal stromal tumour, dedifferentiated liposarcoma, and various carcinomas. In ascites or pleural effusion samples, mesothelial proliferations (both reactive and malignant) are diagnostic considerations {26139079}.

Ancillary testing

A confident, specific diagnosis of IMT or EIMS is generally not possible without ancillary studies, which show ALK and ROS1 to be positive. FISH and NGS for ALK or other relevant kinase gene fusion can help with making the diagnosis {11406658; 26647767}.
