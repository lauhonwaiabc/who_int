7.1.1.3 Discussion and background

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category should have a high positive predictive value for malignancy, because the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) includes a separate “Atypical” category for atypical FNAB samples, which are most likely benign, with a lower ROM. The category reduces the risk of false positive diagnoses and ensures a high positive predictive value in the “Malignant” category, close to 100%.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to show a degree of interobserver variability due to the significant heterogeneity of tumours arising in the soft tissue, as well as the variation in diagnostic threshold among pathologists for the diagnosis of malignancy.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cellular smears showing nuclear enlargement, anisonucleosis, irregularity of nuclear membranes, and coarse chromatin, possibly with prominent nucleoli and mitotic figures. The background features may include necrosis, myxoid material, and inflammatory cells. Many cases are from entities that show a range of cytopathological appearances, which require specific histopathological features, such as mitotic count, infiltrative growth, or focal necrosis, for a specific diagnosis. Typical examples are smooth muscle or neural neoplasms and spindle cell lesions such as gastrointestinal stromal tumour.

This category is particularly dependent on the expertise of the FNAB operator, the quality of material provided, the quality of the smear preparation, the availability of material for cell block and ancillary testing, and the inherent nature of the lesion. Many soft tissue neoplasms are of low cellularity, such as lipomatous tumours, and it may be difficult to obtain sufficient atypical cells in a cytopathology preparation to allow a diagnosis of malignancy. Other tumours show considerable intratumoural variation in cellularity, which may be reflected in hypocellular smears.

The rate of “Suspicious for malignancy” cases varies in published studies. In a review of 1114 bone and soft tissue lesions, 1% showed cells suspicious for malignancy, of which 5 of the 7 with follow-up (71%) were confirmed as malignant on histopathology {20091838}. One text [[Cibas ES, Ducatman BS. Cytology: diagnostic principles and clinical correlates. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 2009.]] suggests using atypical and suspicious categories for FNAB, but most studies do not provide ROM for atypical/suspicious FNAB and only indicate overall sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant. Several other studies have reported the proportion of cases in the atypical/suspicious category as 1–2%, usually representing only 1 or 2 cases {24876987; 11038426}.

Ancillary techniques, such as ICC on cell block material, can assist in confirming a specific tumour type, but in many soft tissue tumours the cellularity is insufficient for definitive assessment, or the ICC may suggest a cell lineage but not define the malignant nature of the neoplasm. Molecular studies in a few specific tumours, such as MDM2 FISH analysis for liposarcoma, may allow a definitive diagnosis; if one of these tumours is suspected on the FNAB, such ancillary tests can be requested. In all cases, the tumour type suspected, or a differential diagnosis, should be stated in the cytopathology report.
