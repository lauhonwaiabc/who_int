4.9.1.1 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a neoplasm showing neuroectodermal differentiation, composed of epithelioid to polygonal cells with copious, distinctively granular cytoplasm.

Clinical features and imaging

Granular cell tumour occurs in soft tissue and other body sites, including breast, tongue, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory tract. The vast majority of granular cell tumours are benign, with very rare instances of malignant behaviour. Granular cell tumours most commonly occur in adults, with a female predominance. Soft tissue–based granular cell tumours involve the dermis and subcutis. They manifest as slow-growing, small (< 30 mm) lumps or ill-defined firm lesions that may be painful. In the gastrointestinal tract or other visceral organs, they may be incidental findings or may lead to obstructive symptoms. Approximately 10% of patients with granular cell tumours have multiple lesions. It is estimated that < 1–2% of all granular cell tumours behave in a malignant fashion. Malignant tumours tend to be larger (> 40 mm) and grow rapidly, and they can recur and metastasize {30196562; 32089415} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. On imaging, granular cell tumours may have irregular borders and can appear spiculated and denser than normal host tissue. On MRI, granular cell tumours have distinctive signal characteristics {16003548}.

Histopathology

In the dermis and other mucosal sites such as the tongue and oesophagus, granular cell tumour is often accompanied by pseudoepithelial hyperplasia, which can mimic invasive squamous carcinoma. The underlying tumour is most often infiltrative and ill-defined. Tumour cells are arranged in nests, trabeculae, or cords. Tumour cells are often very infiltrative and can occasionally cause a stromal fibrosis. Individual cells are large, epithelial, or polygonal in shape, and they have abundant, faintly granular to eosinophilic cytoplasm. Cytoplasmic eosinophilia is attributable to an abundance of lysosomes and accumulation of intracytoplasmic granules.

Histopathological features associated with malignancy include necrosis, individual cell spindling, vesicular nuclei with large nucleoli, increased mitotic rate (> 2 mitoses per 2 mm2), high N:C ratio, and pleomorphism {9669341}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Moderate cellularity Small, loosely cohesive tissue fragments or sheets and single cells Epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm Uniform, round nuclei with a single small nucleolus Granular debris in the background with stripped nuclei, indicating disruption of individual cells Features that suggest malignancy include hyperchromasia, coarse chromatin, increased N:C ratio, nuclear pleomorphism, perinucleolar clearing (“vesicular” in Pap), and enlarged nucleoli

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Alveolar soft part sarcoma displays some similarities to granular cell tumour, with epithelioid cells and eosinophilic or granular cell cytoplasm, but unlike granular cell tumours, alveolar soft part sarcomas usually show nuclear pleomorphism and frequently have large and prominent nucleoli. Granular cell tumour may also be confused with epithelioid sarcoma of the classic, distal subtype, because both lesions are often located in the superficial soft tissues of the extremities. Epithelioid sarcoma has epithelioid or polygonal cells, but it usually has dense cytoplasm instead of the finely granular cytoplasm of granular cell tumour. Histiocytes also show some similarities to the cells of granular cell tumour, with abundant cytoplasm and an overall pale or eosinophilic appearance, but the nuclei of histiocytes are small, often irregular, and indented, and they lack nucleoli. Granular cells may also be confused with oncocytes, leading to difficulty in the interpretation of thyroid-based tumours {22508678}.

Ancillary testing

The vast majority of granular cell tumours show S100 positivity within the granular cytoplasm. They are also immunoreactive for SOX10, inhibin, calretinin, and CD68, and they display positive nuclear staining for MITF. Nuclear expression of TFE3 has been demonstrated in a subset of cases, a finding that may lead to confusion with alveolar soft part sarcoma. TFE3-positive granular cell tumour does not demonstrate a translocation of the corresponding TFE3 gene {15214825; 31788112}.
