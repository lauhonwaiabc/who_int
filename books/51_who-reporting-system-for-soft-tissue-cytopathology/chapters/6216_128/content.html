6.2.1.6 Kaposi sarcoma

Definition

Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is a locally aggressive endothelial proliferation that usually presents with cutaneous lesions in the form of multiple patches, plaques, or nodules, and can involve mucosal sites, lymph nodes, and visceral organs. It is associated with KS-associated herpesvirus / human herpesvirus 8 (KSHV/HHV8) infection.

Clinical features and imaging

KS lesions predominantly affect skin and mucosal sites as patches or macules in the “patch stage”, plaques in the “plaque stage”, or nodules in the “nodular stage”. Nodules can coalesce and ulcerate. KS has also been described in the lymph nodes, eye, musculoskeletal system, heart, spleen, nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, and other unusual locations {11074626; 18605999; 30066758; 25599055}.

The aggressiveness and extension are related to host immune status and to four clinical-epidemiological forms: classic KS, endemic or African KS, epidemic AIDS-associated KS, and iatrogenic transplant-associated KS. Classic KS is indolent and affects skin of the lower extremities, mainly in older men. The lesions, which can be solitary or multiple, are violaceous/brownish macules, plaques, or nodules. Endemic KS affects skin as multiple lesions in the lower extremities and regional lymph nodes. When KS involves lymph nodes in children and other young people, it is more aggressive than when it occurs in adults. AIDS-associated KS is more aggressive and manifests with disseminated mucocutaneous and visceral involvement. The palate, lymph nodes (including the epitrochlear node), gastrointestinal tract, and lungs are frequently involved. Although KS presents at any stage of HIV infection, it usually occurs in patients with HIV with a low CD4+ T-cell count. Cases in patients receiving HAART exhibit a less aggressive course, and lesions may initially flare but eventually regress. Iatrogenic transplant-associated KS occurs after solid organ transplantation or drug immunosuppression; the presentation can be localized in skin and mucosal membranes or disseminated with visceral lesions {23368874; 25599055; 23885287; 35267506}. The clinical course is variable, but it is usually aggressive in patients with visceral involvement.

Histopathology

The histopathology is similar in all clinical and epidemiological forms. The patch stage is characterized by a subtle proliferation of abnormal vessels lined by flattened endothelial cells, dissecting the upper dermis. Pre-existent vessels can protrude into the lumina of new proliferating vessels, a feature called the “promontory sign”. Vessels are surrounded by chronic inflammatory cells (mainly lymphocytes and plasma cells), extravasated erythrocytes, and haemosiderin pigment. The plaque stage involves most of the dermis and is characterized by a proliferation of vessels and spindle cells, both of which dissect dermal collagen, along with features observed in the patch stage. The nodular stage is characterized by a nodular proliferation of spindle cells arranged in fascicles, with slit-like vascular spaces containing erythrocytes, as well as an admixed variable lymphoplasmacytic inflammatory infiltrate, hyaline globules, and haemosiderin-laden macrophages. Except in anaplastic KS lesions seen in AIDS, atypia is minimal, the mitotic index is low, and necrosis is absent {23368874; 21845069; 19638066}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes reactive spindle cell lesions such as granulation tissue and nodular fasciitis, as well as other mesenchymal spindle cell neoplasms, including leiomyoma, fibrohistiocytic neoplasms, and angiosarcoma {11074626}. In the setting of HIV infection, granulomatous lesions can also be present concomitantly, making the diagnosis difficult {23885287}. The anaplastic variant of KS must be distinguished from other high-grade sarcomas. Sarcomatoid carcinoma and spindle cell melanoma should be excluded.

Ancillary testing

ICC is useful to distinguish KS from other spindle cell lesions {32298511}. KS spindle cells express endothelial markers such as CD31, CD34, and ERG, as well as lymphatic-specific markers such as D2-40 or PROX1. Nuclear expression of KSHV/HHV8 (LANA1) is present in almost all cases {32298511; 18605999; 7997879}.
