8.6.9.1 Ewing sarcoma

Definition

Ewing sarcoma is a small round cell sarcoma showing gene fusions involving a member of the FET family of genes and a member of the ETS transcription factor family, with the most common fusion being EWSR1::FLI1.

Clinical features and imaging

Most patients are young, with peak incidence during the second decade of life; about 80% of all patients are aged < 20 years. There is a slight male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.4:1). Ewing sarcoma is the second most common primary bone malignancy in children and young adults after osteosarcoma, arising most frequently in the long bones, pelvis, and ribs (typically in the diaphysis or diametaphysial regions). Most cases that arise in older patients (i.e. ≥ 30 years of age) are in soft tissue sites, with a wide anatomical distribution {29977059}. Patients with extraskeletal Ewing sarcoma present with pain and a palpable mass.

The current multimodal therapy for Ewing sarcoma has improved prognosis, with a cure rate for localized disease as high as 70%. Poorer prognosis is associated with metastatic disease, pelvic location, incomplete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and early-relapsing tumours.

Histopathology

Ewing sarcoma is the prototypical round cell sarcoma. At low power, tumours are lobulated, with sheet-like growth of monotonous small round cells. Tumour cells have round nuclei with evenly distributed fine chromatin and smooth nuclear contours, inconspicuous nucleoli, and varying clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm with indistinct membranes {16006796; 19841938}. There is often a small proportion of cells with darker nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Some cases designated as “atypical Ewing sarcoma” have larger nuclei with more prominent nucleoli and irregular nuclear membranes, but the presence of these features has no prognostic significance {7361993}. Rarely, tumour cells can show vague rosette-like structures.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of small round cell tumours requires ancillary testing, and in most laboratories it is more efficient to use ICC panels before undertaking molecular testing. Ewing sarcoma has a broad and varied list of cytomorphological mimics. Chief considerations among other sarcomas are alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma, and desmoplastic small round cell tumour, as well as molecularly defined subsets of undifferentiated round cell sarcomas, such as CIC-rearranged sarcoma. Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma usually shows larger, more atypical nuclei and multinucleated wreath-like giant cells. Poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma can show a predominant round cell morphology, but foci of conventional spindle morphology and large tissue fragments may be present. Most cases of desmoplastic small round cell tumour arise in the abdominal cavity of young men, and smears show more cohesive tissue fragments of small round cells and a frequent desmoplastic stroma.

Ancillary testing

ICC is useful and efficient for the diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma and to exclude cytomorphological mimics, facilitating the identification of cases for molecular confirmation. The vast majority of Ewing sarcomas show strong, diffuse membranous staining for CD99 {29661713; 16006796}. NKX2-2 has high sensitivity for Ewing sarcoma and higher specificity than CD99, although staining may also be seen in subsets of other tumour types, including small cell carcinoma, synovial sarcoma, melanoma, and mesenchymal chondrosarcoma {26847175; 22446943; 28616785}. Up to one third of cases can show staining for keratin {16006796} and the neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD56, and INSM1 {33610950; 32203089}.

Many cases previously considered to be atypical Ewing sarcoma are now known to represent molecularly distinct groups of undifferentiated round cell sarcomas (see #37294Table 8.01), the most notable being round cell sarcomas with EWSR1 fusions to non-ETS partners, which have been renamed “sarcomas with EWSR1::NFATC2 fusions”, as well as CIC-rearranged sarcomas and sarcomas with BCOR genetic alterations {24215322}. The distinction depends on some cytopathological nuances and clinical features, but ancillary tests are required in most cases. These tumours often show less consistent CD99 staining than Ewing sarcoma, often with only focal or even negative staining. Most round cell sarcomas with ESWR1::NFATC2 fusions cannot be distinguished from Ewing sarcoma on cytomorphological grounds and can only be identified by molecular sequencing, although some cases show pseudoacinar structures, fibrous or myxoid stroma, and spindle cell morphology. PATZ1-rearranged sarcomas, previously included in the “EWSR1 fusions to non-ETS partners” group, are now grouped with the tumours of uncertain differentiation, in light of the variable immunohistochemical expression of myogenic and neurogenic markers by the tumours cells {30379650}.

CIC-rearranged sarcoma and round cell sarcoma with BCOR alterations frequently show increased nuclear size and pleomorphism, more prominent nucleoli, spindle cells, myxoid or fibrotic stroma, and necrosis. Most cases of CIC-rearranged sarcoma are positive for WT1 and ETV4; the diagnosis should be confirmed by molecular testing using either FISH for CIC rearrangement or sequencing. Sarcomas with BCOR alterations include tumours with BCOR fusions (most commonly BCOR::CCNB3) or internal tandem duplication (ITD) of the BCOR gene. ICC for BCOR is positive, and cyclin B3 (CCNB3) is positive in cases harbouring BCOR::CCNB3, which show more varied and nonspecific ICC, with frequent staining for SATB2, TLE1, and cyclin D1.

Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma is positive for desmin and MYOD1, showing a characteristic diffuse nuclear staining pattern, and FOXO1 rearrangement defines this tumour. Poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma is positive in ICC for SS18::SSX and SSX, and SS18 rearrangement can be confirmed by FISH. Desmoplastic small round cell tumour has a distinctive immunophenotype of staining for keratin, EMA, and desmin, as well as nuclear staining for antibodies against the C-terminus of WT1; it is also characterized by an EWSR1 rearrangement, but the defining fusion is EWSR1::WT1.

Non-mesenchymal tumours frequently enter the differential diagnosis and include lymphoblastic lymphoma, which also shows diffuse CD99 staining, but lymphoid markers, including TdT, make the diagnosis. Malignant melanoma can be excluded by S100 or SOX10, as well as HMB45 and melan-A (MART1). Given that Ewing sarcomas can show staining for keratin and neuroendocrine markers, exclusion of neuroendocrine tumours is often necessary, such as in the lung, where Ewing sarcoma may be mistaken for carcinoid or atypical carcinoid tumour {25441687}.

FISH is commonly employed to detect EWSR1 rearrangement, and it is considered diagnostic in the setting of appropriate clinical, cytomorphological, and ICC features. However, some cases have variant fusions involving FUS in lieu of EWSR1, which can be identified by FISH for FUS. Given that EWSR1 fusions are common in many neoplasms, including other round cell sarcomas, sequencing-specific studies may be necessary to detect the specific EWSR1::FLI1 fusion or the rare EWSR1::ERG variant {31995669}.
