10.2.6 Category: Malignant

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Malignant” demonstrates unequivocal cytopathological features of malignancy.

The “Malignant” category is used when unequivocal cytomorphological features of malignancy are present. No single cytopathological feature is diagnostic of malignancy, but the presence of round cells, prominent nuclear atypia, marked abnormalities of N:C ratio, atypical mitotic figures, marked variability in cell size and shape, necrosis, and hypercellularity are highly suggestive of malignancy.

After confirmation of malignancy, cytopathological examination should establish the type of malignancy by differentiating between sarcomas, metastatic carcinomas, melanoma, and lymphomas. Whenever possible, further subtyping into a specific sarcoma type or at least designation of the sarcoma as low-grade or high-grade should be performed, and ICC, FISH, and molecular analysis can aid in the specific identification of many sarcoma types. Some soft tissue malignancies have characteristic molecular features; for example, MDM2 amplification is particularly helpful in establishing malignancy in fat tumours. Other sarcomas are associated with specific translocations, such as ASPSCR1::TFE3 in clear cell sarcoma, FUS::CREB3L2 in low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, and FUS::DD1T3 in myxoid liposarcoma {24411016; 24138725}. Correlation with histopathological features in CNB is almost always helpful in achieving a precise diagnosis.

The “Malignant” category must be used in conjunction with clinical history, physical examination, and imaging findings. The report should almost always state that “correlation with clinical and imaging findings is required”. The diagnosis of malignancy is dependent in part on the quality of the material provided for assessment and in part on the cytopathologist’s experience and confidence.

This category includes both primary low- or high-grade sarcomas and secondary tumours occurring in the soft tissues, and distinguishing metastases has significant impact on further clinical management. Clinical data and information about previous malignancies, together with the cytopathological features of the FNAB smears, are usually sufficient to render a correct diagnosis in many cases of secondary malignancies {32298511; 9800130; 19763717}. However, when a mass in soft tissue is the first manifestation of a carcinoma, melanoma, or lymphoma, ancillary tests are often necessary to determine the site of the primary malignant lesion and to assist with potential treatment targets.

If the tumour is a primary malignant soft tissue neoplasm but a specific tumour cannot be diagnosed, it is categorized as “Malignant”, and a differential diagnosis is provided. In some cases, a diagnosis of high-grade or low-grade sarcoma can allow for planning surgical treatment and further management. However, ideally as specific a diagnosis as possible should be made in all cases, and this is required in those types of sarcomas where optimal treatment includes neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy and a precise diagnosis is required prior to excision biopsy. Ancillary testing of FNAB material and CNB is often essential to achieve this precise histopathological diagnosis {31868988; 32303388; 32298511; 30156774; 32654990; 34515032; 35417916}; for example, “small round cell neoplasms” can be categorized as “Malignant” routinely on FNAB, but the required specific diagnosis is difficult on the basis of cytomorphology alone {35417916; 34515032}, and ancillary tests are often essential to reach a specific histopathological diagnosis. Similarly, the myxoid soft tissue neoplasms have overlapping cytopathological features {37231794; 33041221}, and although the sensitivity and specificity for detecting malignancy in this group of neoplasms is high {37231794}, additional ICC, FISH, or molecular tests are usually required to make definitive diagnoses {33168472; 35878421}.

The ROM for the “Malignant” category ranges from 91% to 100% based on several retrospective studies {32926591; 12451566; 28739192; 19763717; 11190808; 37231794}. A more recent large retrospective study reported a ROM of 97.69%, noting that the ROM varies depending on both the cytomorphological pattern and the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) categorization {39212584}.

Clinical and imaging correlation is always necessary in evaluating neoplasms within the “Malignant” category {18325355; 16411083}, and management decisions as to any further diagnostic workup and definitive management should be made by a multidisciplinary team. If FNAB does not provide enough material for diagnosis, or if the cytopathological findings are inconsistent with clinical and imaging assessments, the imaging findings should be carefully reviewed and correlated. If suspicion remains high, repeat FNAB should be conducted – preferably with ROSE and triaging of material for appropriate ancillary tests, or CNB, or simple excision biopsy – before proceeding with any major surgical or oncological interventions.
