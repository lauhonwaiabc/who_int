4.5.1.4 Schwannoma

Definition

Schwannoma is a nerve sheath tumour composed entirely or nearly entirely of differentiated neoplastic Schwann cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Schwannomas arise most commonly in the 20–50 years age range, but they can occur at any age, with no sex predilection. They most commonly present as a slow-growing, superficial soft mass within the head and neck region and upper and lower extremities, often associated with a peripheral nerve. Pain may be elicited by FNAB. They can also arise within the paravertebral area, retroperitoneum, posterior mediastinum, visceral regions (including gastrointestinal tract), and bone {22137423}. Schwannomas may be clinically confused with a lymph node, particularly within the head and neck region.

Most cases are sporadic, but multiple schwannomas are a feature of neurofibromatosis type 2 and schwannomatosis. Bilateral cerebellopontine angle tumours arising from the vestibular division of the eighth cranial nerve are a definitional criterion for neurofibromatosis type 2 {19545378}. Among sporadic cases, there is an established increased incidence associated with prior irradiation.

Schwannomas are usually isointense to hyperintense when compared to skeletal muscle on T1-weighted MRI. They are hyperintense on fluid-sensitive images and diffusely enhancing on contrast-enhanced imaging {29114437}.

Schwannomas follow a benign course {25595633}. Malignant transformation is extremely rare, and reported examples include epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) and epithelioid angiosarcoma {11145248}.

Histopathology

Conventional schwannomas are a well-circumscribed mass surrounded by a perineural capsule. They are characteristically composed of uniform spindle cells arranged in variable proportions of cellular fascicular arrays, often with nuclear palisading (Antoni A), and hypocellular, less organized areas (Antoni B). Compact rows of palisaded nuclei separated by fibrillary processes known as Verocay bodies are often present in the Antoni A areas. Oedematous, myxoid stromal, and cystic change are seen in Antoni B areas. The tumour cell nuclei can be spindle, tapered, elongated, buckled, or ovoid. Nuclear inclusions, nuclear atypia, and mitotic figures may be present. Vessels with hyalinized walls and foamy macrophages are common in Antoni B areas. Lymphoid aggregates are often present in a subcapsular distribution, or at the periphery in unencapsulated tumours, which is especially common in visceral sites.

Subtypes include ancient schwannoma, cellular schwannoma, plexiform schwannoma, epithelioid schwannoma, and microcystic/reticular schwannoma. The ancient subtype shows marked, degenerative nuclear atypia. Cystic or ischaemic change, extensive hyalinization, haemorrhage, and microcalcifications are often present.

Cellular schwannomas are exclusively or predominantly composed of Antoni A areas without Verocay bodies {7337161}. They show hypercellularity, as well as hyperchromatic nuclei, mitotic activity, and microscopic necrosis suggesting MPNST; however, the presence of typical features, including encapsulation, hyalinized blood vessels, and whorls of Schwann cells, makes the diagnosis {7337161; 14508393}.

Plexiform schwannomas involve multiple nerve fascicles and show a prominent multinodular or plexiform architecture, appearing as conventional or cellular schwannoma {18439936}. Tumours often arise at younger ages and are common in the head and neck region. Traditional well-formed capsules and hyalinized vessels are absent.

Epithelioid schwannomas are rare; most cases are sporadic but some may be associated with schwannomatosis {26752543; 28368924}. Tumours are multilobular and predominantly epithelioid, with tumour cells in nests or linear arrays within a myxoid or hyalinized stroma. The tumour cells have pale cytoplasm and round uniform nuclei with small or inconspicuous nucleoli and occasional pseudoinclusions. Cytopathological atypia is not present. Areas of conventional schwannoma may be present. Loss of SMARCB1 is common, seen in approximately 40% of cases, and is associated with SMARCB1 inactivation {28368924; 30864974}. Rarely, epithelioid schwannoma undergoes malignant transformation to epithelioid MPNST.

Microcystic or reticular schwannomas are extremely rare and occur most commonly in the visceral organs, most frequently in the gastrointestinal tract {18520439}). These tumours show a reticular arrangement of spindle cells in a myxoid or hyalinized background, with frequent microcyst formation. The presence of Antoni A tissue is helpful in making the diagnosis.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Although schwannomas usually provide diagnostic material, they may be difficult to distinguish from both benign and malignant spindle cell neoplasms on cytomorphological grounds, but most differential diagnoses can be resolved by ICC.

Schwannoma may overlap with spindle cell lipoma with large syncytial tissue fragments of bland spindle cells; however, the spindle cell lipoma characteristically shows extensive myxoid stroma, mature adipose tissue, and collagen fibres.

Neurofibroma may be confused with schwannoma; however, neurofibromas show an extensive myxoid background, with Schwann cells admixed with perineural cells, fibroblasts, and axons.

Focal “ancient” degenerative nuclear atypia in conventional or ancient schwannoma should not be misinterpreted as evidence of malignancy, especially when mitotic activity and necrosis are absent {10319234; 16680779}.

An important differential diagnosis to consider is a low-grade MPNST, especially with cellular schwannoma {10319235}. The distinction between these two entities can be difficult. Cellular schwannomas are often devoid of Antoni B areas, and FNAB shows tissue fragments of tumour cells with greater cellularity, scattered single cells, and stripped nuclei. Some cases demonstrate more marked degrees of nuclear atypia, including pleomorphic cells, than conventional schwannomas, and mitotic figures can be present. Similar features can be present in MPNST, but cellular schwannomas often maintain a fish-net architecture containing uniform spindle nuclei, whereas MPNSTs generally show a greater degree of disorganization, single cells, stripped nuclei, and more plentiful mitoses (including atypical forms). ICC is helpful.

At gastrointestinal sites, schwannoma should be distinguished from gastrointestinal stromal tumour and leiomyoma, because all three entities show overlapping features of uniform spindle cells and fascicular and/or syncytial tissue fragments. ICC makes this possible on FNAB material

Ancillary testing

Schwannomas, including cellular schwannomas and the epithelioid and reticular subtypes, demonstrate strong diffuse positivity for S100 and SOX10 {18636017; 22495377}, and perineural cells in the capsule are positive for EMA. Some tumours in the mediastinum may show staining for keratin {16357842}. Spindle cell lipoma is positive for CD34 and characterized by loss of RB1 expression. S100 is positive in neurofibroma, but the extent of staining is much less than that seen in schwannoma, because in neurofibroma the Schwann cells make up a smaller proportion of the neoplastic cells. MPNSTs frequently show lack of or only focal weak staining for S100 and SOX10 {25189642}. Loss of H3 p.K28me3 (K27me3) occurs in MPNST, although it is more frequent in high-grade tumours, whereas all schwannomas show retained expression of H3 p.K28me3 (K27me3) {28898989}.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumour is consistently positive for DOG1 and KIT (CD117), and leiomyoma can be identified by the smooth muscle markers SMA, desmin, and caldesmon.
