5.1.1.3 Discussion and background

There is no consistent definition of atypia in relation to soft tissue lesions, and there are no standardized criteria for an “Atypical” categorization. The term “Indeterminate” has been used for this category in the literature, but the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) favours the term “Atypical” to allow consistency with other WHO Reporting Systems and to separate the more likely benign “Atypical” category from the more likely malignant category “Suspicious for malignancy”, thereby maintaining a moderate to high negative predictive value for the “Atypical” category, a high positive predictive value for the “Suspicious for malignancy” category, and a hierarchical increased ROM within the WHO System categories.

Categorization of a soft tissue FNAB as “Atypical” may be based on the cellular pattern, high cellularity, nuclear features such as variable nuclear enlargement or degenerative or symplastic features as can be seen in ancient change in a schwannoma or granular cell tumour, and the stromal component. Potential problems arise due to cytomorphological overlap between different tumour types, in particular low-grade spindle cell tumours {32926591}. Lipomatous tumours may show atypical cells in an otherwise adipocyte-rich smear in atypical lipomatous tumours {32926591}.

Soft tissue inflammatory lesions may have nuclear pleomorphism and atypia, such as ganglion-like cells in nodular fasciitis, or fibroblastic proliferation after infarction or reparative changes after biopsy, traumatic haematoma, or radiotherapy, and the features may overlap with those of a neoplastic process. Other cases categorized as “Atypical” are those in which there is insufficient material present to confidently categorize the lesion subtype.

A specimen may also be rendered “Atypical” for technical reasons related to the expertise of the operator performing the FNAB, the quality of smear preparation and staining, the nature and accessibility of the site of the lesion, and the experience of the cytopathologist interpreting the specimen.

In the report, the atypical features should always be specified in the microscopic description and the differential diagnosis discussed, with a favoured diagnosis provided if possible.

This category should be used judiciously and not as a “wastebasket” for unknown diagnoses. In a large review of 1114 bone and soft tissue lesions, 3% showed atypical cells, of which 17 of the 22 with follow-up (77%) were confirmed as malignant {20091838}. One text [[Cibas ES, Ducatman BS. Cytology: diagnostic principles and clinical correlates. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 2009.]] suggests using atypical and suspicious categories for FNAB, but most studies do not provide ROM for atypical/suspicious FNAB and only indicate overall sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant. Several other studies have reported the proportion of cases in the atypical/suspicious category as 1–2%, usually representing only 1 or 2 cases {24876987; 11038426}.
