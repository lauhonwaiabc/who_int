1.1.1.2 The role of soft tissue cytopathology

The range of possible origins of a soft tissue swelling is broad, including tumours of mesenchymal origin. The objective of the diagnostic workup of soft tissue lesions is to provide information that will enable optimal treatment planning. As a best practice, all patients presenting with an unexplained deep soft tissue mass or with a superficial soft tissue lesion with a diameter > 50 mm should be referred to a specialized orthopaedic oncology centre in which the management of sarcoma cases is common practice {34303806}. The diagnostic procedure involves an interdisciplinary joint decision made by an oncologist, a surgeon, a radiologist, a nuclear medicine physician, and a pathologist, who correlate their findings to define an optimal diagnostic strategy {18325355; 16411083}.

Clinical data and appropriate imaging are essential for the evaluation of soft tissue lesions. In most cases, a biopsy is required for further management, and a biopsy is mandatory for all solid deep-seated tumours, to exclude or establish malignancy and assess the histopathological type and grade of the tumour in cases of sarcoma [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]].

Over the past decades, CNB and FNAB, or a combination of both, along with an intraprocedural evaluation of FNAB smears or touch preparation of CNB, have largely replaced surgical biopsies in the examination of soft tissue lesions {24411016; 15918176; 35791462; 20872955; 30992649; 27011434}. CNB through a single tract is currently recommended for obtaining specimens for the diagnosis of most limb and superficial trunk soft tissue tumours [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. However, the role of cytopathology in the evaluation of soft tissue lesions has been steadily increasing due to advances in imaging techniques and the growing availability of ancillary tests using small biopsies {11933269; 21536238; 23288831; 18162764; 25487166; 26780171; 30190145; 32303388; 35427924; 30156774; 30600320; 31868988; 29318761}.

The advantages of soft tissue FNAB are good sensitivity and specificity, low morbidity, a reduced time to diagnosis, and a low cost–benefit ratio. FNAB is less invasive and less expensive than either CNB or open biopsy, and it can sample a lesion more extensively.

FNAB is well established and widely used as a first-line approach or as a complementary diagnostic procedure with CNB for the confirmation of metastatic tumours to soft tissue, for the evaluation and documentation of local recurrences and metastases of previously diagnosed sarcomas, and for the initial diagnosis of malignant soft tissue neoplasms {9800130; 19763717; 20091838; 11038426; 8520003; 32773338; 15918176; 30992649; 32926591; 17973999; 12451566; 3754426; 11190808; 10439797; 11335959; 10230353; 14676638}. FNAB in conjunction with rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) and specimen triage for cost-effective ancillary testing enables adequacy assessment and guidance for concurrent CNB. FNAB offers a fast, precise, and low-risk diagnostic modality, avoiding invasive and expensive diagnostic procedures and unnecessary excisions {19763717; 32298511; 32926591}. The use of ROSE makes confirmation of the malignancy possible in the space of a few minutes in most cases, and consequently can be used to indicate or contraindicate the use of CNB. ROSE is also invaluable for the triaging of samples for ancillary techniques such as molecular and ICC tests, cell block, flow cytometry (in the case of malignant lymphoma), and tissue banking. In the case of distinguishing certain blastemal tumours such as neuroblastoma versus nephroblastoma, ROSE allows confirmation of the need for additional samples of the bone marrow and cerebrospinal fluid, which can then be performed at the same time, particularly in children and adolescents, under the same broad anaesthetic cover.

The majority of palpable soft tissue masses are benign, and the frequency of benign tumours is estimated to be approximately 100 times that of sarcomas [[Rosai J. Soft tissues. In: Goldblum JR, Lamps LW, McKenney JK. Rosai and Ackerman’s surgical pathology. 11th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Elsevier; 2017.]]. FNAB of non-neoplastic and clinically non-aggressive soft tissue tumours with benign imaging features has been shown to be a rapid diagnostic procedure, leading to optimal treatment including either local excision or simply watchful waiting {32926591; 8520003; 18008345; 18521248; 4072661}. Soft tissue masses presenting as tumours in children, adolescents, and adults frequently require consultation to distinguish between benign and malignant lesions, and they can pose a diagnostic dilemma. In superficial or deep lesions where the clinical and imaging hypothesis that the lesion is benign is confirmed by a simple FNAB, the tumour can be observed or excised {25435271; 16411083}.

In many centres, FNAB has been an integral part of the care for patients with soft tissue lesions, and the initial diagnosis (and in some cases the final diagnosis) is based on cytopathology alone integrated with clinical and imaging findings and where necessary ancillary testing [[Klijanienko J, Lagacé R, editors. Soft tissue tumors: a multidisciplinary, decisional diagnostic approach. Hoboken (NJ): Wiley-Blackwell; 2011.]] [[Domanski HA, Walther CS. FNA cytology of soft tissue and bone tumors. Basel (Switzerland): Karger; 2017. (Monographs in clinical cytology series; vol. 22).]]. This multidisciplinary approach allows the establishment of the diagnosis, the initiation of appropriate treatment and further management, and the avoidance of expensive surgical excision and histopathology – which in low- and middle-income countries may not be available {26090002; 10667013; 21760830; 12768643; 29492099}.

When used as the initial diagnostic test, FNAB has been shown to be highly accurate, with 89–100% sensitivity and 90–100% specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant soft tissue tumours and in documenting recurrences or metastases of previously treated sarcomas {20383617; 24099641; 11038426; 12451566; 14748789; 17941014; 14676638}.

However, there is a limitation to using FNAB for the precise subtyping and grading of soft tissue sarcomas compared to CNB and open biopsy. The overall accuracy of FNAB in making the specific histopathological diagnosis of soft tissue lesions has been shown to be somewhat inferior to that of CNB and open biopsy {10439797; 11335959; 17941014}. The diagnosis of rare soft tissue lesions can be difficult, especially with limited material that lacks architectural features, and without ancillary testing. However, ICC and molecular findings can support a specific diagnosis in many cases {11038426; 19763717; 32303388; 32298511}. When adequate sampling is available, FNAB can accurately define tumour type in 77–97% of cases {19763717; 28739192}. In the paediatric age group, FNAB combined with molecular testing and ICC is generally more accurate for subtyping malignant musculoskeletal neoplasms than in adults, because many childhood neoplasms harbour a detectable genetic abnormality {32654990; 30848523; 34703088; 35417916; 34515032; 26800124}.

The grading of soft tissue sarcomas has been acknowledged as a common limitation in the cytopathological evaluation of soft tissue tumours. The US National Cancer Institute (NCI) and the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) systems, based on tumour differentiation, mitotic rate, and necrosis, are the most widely used histopathological grading systems for sarcomas {17090186}. Assessing these parameters may not always be feasible in FNAB samples {14676638; 19688763; 12889044}. Nevertheless, most sarcomas can be accurately graded in smears when segregated into high and low grades, which is usually sufficient information to plan initial treatment or further management. Weir et al. (1999) {10587701} and Ng et al. (2010) {19763717} reported 92% and 95% accuracy, respectively, in cytopathological sarcoma grading, and Palmer et al. (2001) {11335959} reported 90% correctly graded sarcomas. In a subset of sarcomas, a specific histopathological diagnosis from FNAB smears can determine the corresponding malignancy grade {10439797; 32298511}.

One important limitation of cytopathology is the limited experience of most practising pathologists in the wide array of soft tissue neoplasms and their morphological heterogeneity, making the cytopathological diagnosis of soft tissue tumours challenging {18008345; 32298511}. Successful FNAB evaluation requires an experienced cytopathologist and close collaboration between the orthopaedic surgeon, oncologist, and cytopathologist. Complications of a correctly performed FNAB in examination of soft tissue lesions are minor and include small haematomas and localized tenderness. The risk of tumour cell spread in needle tracts is strongly related to the needle size and the number of passes performed; with regular 22–25 gauge needles, the incidence of this complication is exceedingly low {20461770; 27859013; 36255472}.
