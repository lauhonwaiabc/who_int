8.6.5.5 Myositis ossificans

Definition

Myositis ossificans is a benign, self-limited neoplasm of soft tissue composed of spindle cells and osteoblasts, with ossification.

Clinical features and imaging

Myositis ossificans is typically seen in athletic young adults, with an average patient age of 36 years (range: 8–84 years) and an M:F ratio of 1.5:1 {33816108}. Often occurring at a site of known blunt trauma or repetitive minor trauma, myositis ossificans classically presents as a rapidly growing, painful mass in a large skeletal muscle group {13539055; 33816108}. The most frequently affected muscles include the quadriceps femoris muscle and flexor muscles of the arm, although some cases arise in the subcutis or soft tissue without muscle involvement {13539055; 33816108}. As the process matures, the pain resolves, and late-stage myositis ossificans may be painless. CT and MRI features evolve as the lesion progresses.

Histopathology

The histopathological findings evolve as myositis ossificans matures, with a variable time course but predictable pattern (#32057Table 4.01) {13539055; 12894350}. During the initial 3–6 weeks, spindle cells proliferate in association with oedematous and vascular stroma, often with a component of organizing haemorrhage. Mitotic figures may be frequent but are not atypical. As the process matures, the spindle cell proliferation becomes surrounded by an osteoblastic proliferation that produces osteoid, mineralizing into woven bone at the periphery in a distinctive zonal pattern with a circumscribed border. Over time, the woven bone develops into lamellar bone, occasionally with a cartilaginous component that undergoes enchondral ossification. The zonal pattern is retained, with dense lamellar bone at the periphery and woven bone with or without a residual spindle cell component in the centre.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

For cases with a classic clinical history and imaging findings, biopsy is often not required. For cases with atypical imaging findings, biopsy can help to distinguish a benign process from sarcoma, although a definitive diagnosis of myositis ossificans is not always possible.

In the early phase, when spindle cells predominate, the differential diagnosis includes nodular fasciitis and proliferative myositis. Nodular fasciitis typically yields more cellular smears with a more prominent inflammatory infiltrate and delicate, branching blood vessels in myxoid stroma {11135465; 8005041}. Proliferative myositis demonstrates ganglion-like cells with one or more enlarged rounded nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and basophilic cytoplasm, which are not seen in myositis ossificans {20890998}. Multinucleated giant cells may raise the possibility of tenosynovial giant cell tumour when abundant {31991033}.

In the intermediate to mature phases, when bone is present, the differential diagnosis includes benign and malignant bone-forming tumours, particularly extraskeletal osteosarcoma and other high-grade sarcomas with heterologous osteosarcomatous differentiation {23075901}. In cases with prominent enchondral ossification, chondrosarcoma may also enter the differential {23075901}. The lack of cytopathological atypia or abnormal mitotic figures in myositis ossificans argues against sarcoma, and the finding of degenerating muscle fibres is helpful when present. Correlation with imaging findings showing peripheral ossification with a circumscribed border is essential.

Ancillary testing

ICC is of limited value. SMA is expressed in the spindle cells and SATB2 in the osteoblasts, but this immunoprofile is not specific for myositis ossificans {20890998; 8159649}. USP6 rearrangements are present in the majority of myositis ossificans, as well as in nodular fasciitis and primary aneurysmal bone cyst {18265974; 29661729; 33000481}.
