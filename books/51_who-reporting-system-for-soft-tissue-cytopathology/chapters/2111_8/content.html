2.1.1.1 FNAB techniques and specimen management

High-quality FNAB material is fundamental to ensuring an accurate diagnosis that can lead to effective management. The quality depends on the technique of sampling, the tumour size, the consistency of the lesion, the location of the lesion, and the proper management of cytopathological material. How and by whom a lesion is biopsied is preferably decided by a multidisciplinary team composed of an oncologist, surgeon, radiologist, and cytopathologist {20432021}.

Anaesthesia

For sampling superficial lesions in adults, local anaesthesia is optional. Patients usually experience minimal discomfort during the FNAB procedure, but the use of local anaesthesia can reduce patient anxiety as well as any pain. Injection of 1% lidocaine, with or without epinephrine, can be used. For FNAB in children, local anaesthesia with skin cream is recommended, which can be followed with injection of 1% lidocaine if needed. Sedation by inhalation is also an option. Sampling deep lesions may require general anaesthesia.

Needles

Needles of between 22 and 27 gauge are used for FNAB, and as large as 18 gauge for small CNB. The choice of gauge may vary depending on the characteristics of the lesion. Fibrotic lesions are more easily biopsied with a 25–27 gauge needle, while lesions with an abundant myxoid or cellular component are best biopsied with a 22 or 23 gauge needle.

Use of suction

The FNAB technique can be performed with or without suction. In the absence of suction, capillary action is used, and this is generally recognized as comparable to FNAB with suction {3815294}. Capillary action is achieved by using the needle by itself and agitating the needle into and back through the target repeatedly, or by attaching the needle to a syringe and withdrawing the syringe plunger to about 2 mL prior to puncture and moving the needle up and down within the target lesion. This technique is most successful with cellular, less fibrotic lesions, as well as vascular lesions that bleed easily. FNAB using suction is useful for cystic lesions and more fibrotic lesions.

FNAB guidance

Superficial and deeper, palpable lesions can be sampled with or without imaging guidance, but ideally there should be initial assessment with ultrasound or other imaging modalities. All deeply located, non-palpable lesions or lesions with complicated anatomical relationships should be sampled using imaging guidance. In this situation, the guidance technique (e.g. ultrasound versus CT) and the point of needle penetration are chosen by the interventional radiologist. Optimally, the needle and its contents should be entrusted to the pathology team immediately after each FNAB pass, so that the team can expertly smear the material, stain, perform ROSE, judge the quality of the material, and provide cost-effective and efficient triage {12873307}.

Complications

Dissemination of tumour cells in the puncture path has not been reported with the use of a fine needle (22–27 gauge). Dissemination is associated with the use of larger CNB. However, the puncture site and path can be marked with ink, photographed, and excised at the time of surgical resection.

Specimen management

The quality of the FNAB material should be evaluated by the pathologist during the procedure and ROSE provided. The cellularity of a given FNAB sample from soft tissue tumours is extremely variable. Tumours that are fibrotic and/or rich in vascularity may yield hypocellular and/or haemodiluted material, respectively. In some instances, low cellularity is characteristic and may be a diagnostic consideration in the differential diagnosis, such as in desmoid tumours or fibromatoses. Inversely, samples from round cell / pleomorphic / blastemal tumours, melanoma, or lymphoma are usually extremely rich in cells {21774092}.

Fixation and staining

Air-dried, Giemsa-stained smears can be quickly prepared at ROSE. If Pap or H&E staining is planned, smears must be fixed in alcohol immediately after smearing, to avoid air-drying artefact.

Rapid onsite evaluation

ROSE is beneficial for ensuring the adequate quantity and quality of the sample, and it is advised for all soft tissue FNAB. ROSE makes it possible for a cytopathologist to perform the initial provisional cytomorphological diagnosis and determine the quality and quantity of material for both cytopathological assessment and triage for ancillary studies; for example, when a round cell sarcoma is encountered, a portion of the fresh cell suspension may be dedicated for molecular analysis {24411016}. In addition, ROSE can provide the information that immediate CNB is required. Overall, the use of ROSE increases both the efficiency of small-volume biopsy samples and the accuracy of soft tissue tumour diagnosis on FNAB samples {23429365}. If ROSE cannot be performed, cytopathologists should liaise closely with the interventional radiologist to establish a protocol for soft tissue FNAB procedures and the use of material.

Ancillary techniques

The diagnosis of soft tissue tumours frequently requires the use of ancillary techniques. Although ICC can be performed on direct smears or LBC slides, cell block preparations are preferred because most antibodies are validated on formalin-fixed histopathological sections.
