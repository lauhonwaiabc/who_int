4.1.1.4 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

The ROM for the “Benign” category of soft tissue neoplasms is very low. Overall accuracy in FNAB of soft tissue is affected by individual cytopathologists’ experience, which can be limited other than in specialist orthopaedic and oncological institutions where relatively rare soft tissue neoplasms tend to be seen more frequently, and by the availability and use of a multidisciplinary approach {10230353}.

The sensitivity and specificity of FNAB for separating benign from malignant soft tissue lesions ranges depending on various study parameters, with an overall sensitivity of 97.4% and specificity of 98.2% in a large meta-analysis {32622858}. The negative predictive value for a benign lesion ranges from 94% to 98% {17474257; 20091838; 4072661; 11038426; 20512437; 19763717; 32926591}, and the ROM for the “Benign” category can be conservatively estimated to be 5% or less. In a recently published single-institution retrospective review, the ROM was 2.49% {39212584}, although this same review showed that the overall ROM across the categories varied with the cytomorphological pattern, from 3% for lipomatous tumours to 62.78% for spindle cell, 78.15% for epithelioid, 84.58% for myxoid, 94.26% for pleomorphic, and 100% for round cell tumours.

One of the difficulties with diagnostic accuracy in the “Benign” category is the subset of lesions that show significant cytopathological atypia or pleomorphism despite their benign behaviour; for example, benign peripheral nerve sheath tumours often show extensive nuclear pleomorphism and atypia, so-called “ancient change”, despite their benign nature, potentially leading to a misdiagnosis of malignancy {14748789}. The other cytomorphological features of malignancy, such as mitotic activity, necrosis, and hypercellularity, should be present and commensurate with the degree of atypia before categorization as an indeterminate or “Malignant” lesion. Likewise, the cytopathological appearances of the so-called “pseudosarcomas”, including nodular fasciitis and myositis ossificans, can result in false positive diagnoses {11135465}.

A “Benign” soft tissue FNAB should be correlated with the clinical and imaging findings and with ancillary testing, such as ICC for MDM2 in fat neoplasms. The management of soft tissue lesions categorized as “Benign” depends on many factors, including lesion size, depth, and location, as well as patient comfort and preference. Some benign lesions are removed for cosmetic purposes only, and some can be monitored clinically for signs of growth or change. Surgical removal is best accomplished with thoughtful preoperative planning and assessment. With some benign or locally aggressive lesions, such as desmoid fibromatosis, active surveillance is the current preferred approach in most clinical scenarios {28439797}.

Evaluation of any soft tissue lesion, including those that are suspected to be benign, should begin with a detailed patient history and physical examination. In superficial locations, the lesion size, location, depth, mobility, and consistency often provide invaluable information that can direct additional study. Patients should be asked about the duration and growth of the lesion, as well as factors such as pain or sensitivity to touch and pressure. Radiating pain, a phenomenon known as Tinel’s sign, is a characteristic feature of peripheral nerve sheath tumour. Likewise, detection of pulsation is typical of a vascular lesion {25070236}.

Further evaluation of a suspected soft tissue tumour often includes imaging studies. Although MRI is the modality of choice, plain radiographs and ultrasound evaluation are often useful and can be a cost-effective means of evaluating lesions that do not need further imaging evaluation, and ultrasound is often very useful to direct the FNAB {18566812; 25782339}. Further evaluation of a lesion with MRI can be particularly useful in the evaluation of tumour size, borders, and relationships to adjacent structures, as well as the presence or absence of tumour heterogeneity or homogeneity. Cystic change and necrosis are other features that can influence the tumour subtype differential diagnosis.
