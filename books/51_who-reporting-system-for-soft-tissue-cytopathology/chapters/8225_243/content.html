8.2.2.5 Myxoinflammatory fibroblastic sarcoma

Definition

Myxoinflammatory fibroblastic sarcoma (MIFS) is an infiltrative, locally aggressive fibroblastic neoplasm that typically arises in the distal extremities, characterized by pleomorphic fibroblastic cells with macronucleoli in a myxohyaline background with a variably prominent inflammatory cell infiltrate.

Clinical features and imaging

Young to middle-aged adults (mean age: 40 years; range: 4–91 years) frequently present with a slowly growing, painless mass. MIFS occurs commonly in the subcutaneous tissue of the fingers, hands, wrists, ankles, and feet {9578090; 9706971; 9779486; 29072951}. MIFS recurs locally but rarely metastasizes. Unusual locations include the upper arms, thighs, shoulders, and inguinal area {12376919; 24121178}. Imaging findings are nonspecific, showing an infiltrative mass located along the tendon sheath. Like other myxoid tumours, MIFS is T1-hypointense and T2-hyperintense, with homogeneous or heterogeneous enhancement on MRI {17449774; 26563559}.

Histopathology

MIFS is characterized by a lobular architecture; an infiltrative border; and a combination of bland spindle cells, scattered large Reed–Sternberg–like or lipoblast-like cells, and abundant inflammatory cell-rich myxoid stroma {24121178; 32514165}. MIFS overlaps morphologically and genetically with two closely related entities: haemosiderotic fibrolipomatous tumour and pleomorphic hyalinizing angiectatic tumour {20871391; 19199331; 29979612}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Variable cellularity Prominent myxoid matrix in tissue fragments Mixed population of bland spindle cells and mononuclear epithelioid cells Scattered large, atypical Reed–Sternberg–like cells with macronucleoli Mixed inflammatory cells with haemosiderin and macrophages Pseudolipoblasts

Reference(s): {17511780; 21548124; 12713479; 28544823; 23406831; 33431307}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of MIFS is extremely broad and depends on the tumour location and the relative amounts of each component present on the FNAB smears. The differential diagnosis ranges from benign lesions such as ganglion cyst or tenosynovial giant cell tumour to malignant neoplasms such as myxofibrosarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, and Hodgkin lymphoma.

Ancillary testing

The translocation t(1;10) resulting in TGFBR3::OGA (formerly known as MGEA5) and BRAF rearrangement have been identified in MIFS {21717526; 22569967; 26980036}. The detection of these gene alterations is neither sensitive nor cost-effective, and there are no distinctive ICC or molecular markers that can be used in diagnosis {32514165}.
