10.2.3 Category: Atypical

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Atypical” demonstrates features predominantly seen in benign lesions and minimal features that may raise the possibility of a malignant lesion, but with features insufficient, in either number or quality, to diagnose a benign or malignant process or lesion.

The “Atypical” category is used when there are cytopathological features present that are essentially benign, but with occasional features uncommon in benign lesions that raise the remote possibility of a malignancy and thus cannot be ignored. This indeterminate category is included to increase the negative predictive value of a “Benign” categorization. Whenever atypical features are present, the FNAB is categorized as “Atypical”, even if the overall material shows technical faults and would otherwise be categorized as “Insufficient’, or when suboptimal preparation has resulted in a degree of artefactual or degenerative change but sufficient atypical cytomorphological features are present and a malignancy cannot be entirely excluded. Most of the specimens in this category are likely to be from reactive, inflammatory, or reparative processes, but specific features sufficient for a definitive benign diagnosis are lacking.

The WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) uses the term “Atypical”, consistent with the other WHO Reporting Systems, to separate FNAB samples that are considered more likely to be benign from those that are more likely to be malignant and categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy”. The ROM increases from the “Atypical” category to the “Suspicious for malignancy” category, assisting clinicians in the further diagnostic management of their patients.

Categorization of a soft tissue FNAB as “Atypical” may be based on the cellular pattern, high cellularity, the stromal component, and nuclear features such as variable nuclear enlargement and pleomorphism. Nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, and pleomorphism can also be seen as part of the spectrum of degeneration, for example in ancient change in a schwannoma. Potential problems arise due to cytomorphological overlap between different tumour types, in particular low-grade spindle cell tumours {32926591}.

Soft tissue inflammatory lesions may have nuclear pleomorphism and atypia, such as ganglion-like cells in nodular fasciitis, or fibroblastic proliferation after infarction or reparative changes after biopsy, traumatic haematoma, or radiotherapy, and the features may overlap with those of a neoplastic process. A specimen may also be rendered “Atypical” for technical reasons related to the expertise of the operator performing the FNAB, the quality of smear preparation and staining, the nature and accessibility of the site of the lesion, and the experience of the cytopathologist interpreting the specimen.

This category is not a “wastebasket” for unknown diagnoses, and the atypical features should always be specified in the microscopic description and the differential diagnosis discussed, with a favoured diagnosis provided if possible. Several studies have reported the proportion of cases in the atypical/suspicious category as 1–2% {24876987; 11038426}.

The ROM has been reported as 46% in one study of soft tissue and bone lesions {32926591}. A recent single-institution retrospective review of more than 2200 cases categorized using the WHO System reported a ROM of 39.62%, and it was observed that the ROM varied across all categories when the pattern was taken into account, from 3% for lipomatous tumours to 62.78% for spindle cell, 78.15% for epithelioid, 84.58% for myxoid, 94.26% for pleomorphic, and 100% for round cell tumours {39212584}.

Correlation with clinical findings, imaging, and any available ancillary tests on FNAB material are essential once a soft tissue FNAB is categorized as “Atypical”, and discussion by a multidisciplinary team is recommended. If the findings indicate that the lesion is highly likely to be a benign neoplasm or a reactive or inflammatory process, then observation and clinical follow-up may be considered, but if the findings are discrepant or clinical and imaging findings suggest malignancy, a repeat FNAB should be recommended, ideally with ROSE and where available CNB, and incisional biopsy with or without frozen section or excisional biopsy could be considered.
