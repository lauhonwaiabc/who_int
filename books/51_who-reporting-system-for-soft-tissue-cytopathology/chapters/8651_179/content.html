8.6.5.1 Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma

Definition

Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS) is a malignant soft tissue tumour with morphological and immunophenotypic features of embryonic skeletal muscle.

Clinical features and imaging

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common paediatric soft tissue sarcoma, with 4.5 cases per 1 million people aged 0–20 years {30617281}. The most common subtype is ERMS, with one third of cases presenting in children aged < 5 years. Tumours most often arise in the head and neck (including the orbit) and the genitourinary system {25267746; 30091945}, and less frequently in the biliary tract, retroperitoneum, and abdomen. They rarely involve the extremities. The clinical presentation depends on location. Although most ERMSs are sporadic, some are associated with genetic susceptibility syndromes such as Costello syndrome (HRAS mutations), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1 mutations), Noonan syndrome (mutations in several genes), Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (dysregulation of imprinting in the 11p15.5 region), DICER1-related tumour predisposition syndrome, and Li–Fraumeni syndrome (TP53 mutations) {30617281}.

Histopathology

ERMS is composed of primitive mesenchymal cells in various stages of myogenesis. Typical ERMS is made up of primitive stellate to short spindle cells in a loose myxoid matrix, with a pattern of alternating dense and loose cellularity. Differentiating rhabdomyoblasts may be absent or numerous and are characterized by eosinophilic and elongated cytoplasm, or differentiated cells with cross-striations. Differentiation is often more evident after therapy {17006261}. A subset of ERMS cases consist predominantly of primitive round cells, simulating alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (ARMS) in the so-called “dense pattern” {23765537}. Botryoid ERMS is characterized by polypoid architecture with linear condensations of tumour cells in the “cambium layer” abutting an epithelial surface with underlying hypocellular areas. A subset of ERMS cases exhibit anaplasia, defined as the presence of cells with markedly enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei, often with bizarre, multipolar mitotic figures {18985676}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The principal differential diagnostic considerations include ARMS and other round cell mesenchymal tumours. With the exception of ARMS, these tumours lack cells with rhabdomyoblastic differentiation. ARMS is composed predominantly of singly dispersed or loose tissue fragments of round cells with enlarged, round nuclei. Cells with a wreath-like arrangement of their multiple nuclei may be present, and spindle cells and large tissue fragments of densely packed cells are usually absent {9861531; 10934943}. Ewing sarcoma shows monomorphic round cells with scant cytoplasm and round to ovoid pale nuclei with fine chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli. Pseudorosettes may be present, and myxoid matrix is usually absent {10425044; 10934943; 17636492}. The primitive round and spindle cells in CIC-rearranged and BCOR family tumours are larger and have a greater degree of anisonucleosis and pleomorphism than those in ERMS. BCOR family tumours also have delicate vascular fragments that are absent in ERMS {29300189; 31679010; 34265123}. The cells in poorly differentiated synovial sarcoma are relatively uniform and larger, and they have irregular hyperchromatic nuclei with one or more nucleoli {10888758; 26800124}. Rhabdoid cells in malignant rhabdoid tumour have distinct cytoplasmic inclusions and round nuclei with prominent nucleoli and perinucleolar chromatin clearing (“vesicular” in the Pap stain), and they are often larger than round differentiating rhabdomyoblasts. Given the overlap in cytomorphological features between these tumours, definitive diagnosis requires ICC and/or molecular studies {10425044; 10934943; 17636492; 23834194; 26800124; 28718513; 33399252}.

Ancillary testing

By ICC, ERMSs are positive for desmin, myogenin (MYF4), and MYOD1, although the extent of immunoreactivity is variable {16861966}. ERMS usually shows patchy variable staining for myogenin in < 50% of nuclei, which helps to distinguish it from ARMS, in which nuclear staining for myogenin is usually strong and diffuse. ERMS is often positive for MSA and SMA, and aberrant expression of keratins, S100, and NFP may also be seen {2455782}. FOXO1 gene fusion is absent in ERMS, whereas it is present in the majority of ARMS tumours {19147825}. Assessment of fusion status using FISH or other molecular techniques is critical, because cytopathological and histopathological distinction between these tumours is sometimes difficult, and current treatment is in part determined by fusion status {31456361; 34107813}.
