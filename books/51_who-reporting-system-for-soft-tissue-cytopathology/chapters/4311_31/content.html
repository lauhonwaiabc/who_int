4.3.1.1 Acute inflammation

Definition

Acute inflammation yields a purulent material consisting of neutrophils and variable numbers of histiocytes with cellular debris in a fibrinous background.

Clinical features and imaging

Soft tissue infections include a range of clinical lesions, from mild infections such as impetigo and uncomplicated cellulitis to life-threatening conditions, including necrotizing fasciitis. Clinically, soft tissue infections present as tender, oedematous, warm and erythematous swellings. Systemic signs and symptoms that can be seen in more severe infections include fever, hypothermia, tachycardia, and hypotension. The most common causes are bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus spp. Other etiological agents, especially in immunosuppressed individuals, can also cause soft tissue infections. These include infections by bacteria such as Bartonella spp., Vibrio vulnificus, non-tuberculous mycobacteria, and the bacteria that cause leprosy; fungal infections such as candidiasis, aspergillosis, and zygomycosis; viral infections such as herpes zoster and herpes simplex infection; and parasitic infections including leishmaniasis {29404597; 35115188; 27726817; 30564282; 31990815; 26143432; 28378613; 33303335}. Non-infectious causes of inflammation include a foreign body such as a splinter. MRI is the most sensitive diagnostic imaging modality, although CT is more widely available and can detect many necrotizing infections and foreign bodies. Ultrasound is useful for diagnosing soft tissue collections and can be used to direct the FNAB for diagnostic purposes. Plain X-rays are rarely helpful {31964497; 27726741}.

Histopathology

An abscess is a cavity filled with acute inflammatory cells and necrotic debris. Granulation tissue and later fibrosis surround the abscess cavity. Marked acute inflammation, involving subcutaneous fat with extensive necrosis, is seen in necrotizing fasciitis, with ulceration of overlying tissue, thrombosis, vasculitis, and extension of inflammation into skeletal muscle. The causative organism may be seen microscopically, although in many cases ancillary tests are required.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Large numbers of neutrophils Necrotic debris and fibrin in background In the later stages, chronic inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes and histiocytes, as well as granulation tissue Reparative changes in non-inflammatory cells Specific infectious organisms may be seen on routine and special stains

Discussion and differential diagnosis

FNAB is a rapid, virtually pain-free, and simple procedure to procure a sample for cytopathology and microbiological investigation, especially because contamination is minimized {32352648; 30156776}.

Benign entities in the differential diagnosis include epidermoid inclusion cyst, where numerous anucleate squamous cells and a small number of nucleated squamous cells, keratinous debris, and multinucleated histiocytes are seen, along with pus. Acute inflammatory cells may be seen in inflamed branchial cysts, where squamous cells make the diagnosis, and in thyroglossal duct cysts, where variable often scant numbers of squamous cells and respiratory type epithelium are seen with the possible inclusion of thyroid follicular cells and colloid. Clinical and imaging correlation is required [[Chandanwale SS, Buch AC, Chawla KR, et al. Fine needle aspiration cytology of thyroglossal duct cyst: diagnostic pitfalls and a study of 14 cases. Thyroid Res Pract. 2013;10(3):104–7. doi:10.4103/0973-0354.116136.]]. In an immunosuppressed host, mycobacterial infection may present as acute suppurative necrosis.

Acute inflammatory cells may be seen in Hodgkin lymphoma, myeloid leukaemias, and non-Hodgkin lymphomas, especially T-cell lymphoma {19730363; 21965120}. Marked, mainly acute inflammation is also a feature of metastatic squamous cell carcinoma; in a soft tissue FNAB showing acute inflammation, the slides should be carefully screened to ensure that underlying malignant cells are not missed.

Ancillary testing

Microbiological investigations on FNAB purulent material should include Gram staining, an acid-fast stain such as Ziehl–Neelsen or similar, and fungal stains such as a methenamine silver stain, in addition to culture and sensitivity. Microbiological culture and sensitivity are virtually mandatory, and ICC, in situ hybridization, and PCR can be added when appropriate and available. Blood tests include full blood counts, inflammatory markers, and serology. Ancillary tests may also be used to exclude an underlying neoplastic process {27726817; 25699977; 35094696; 28129707; 30156776}.
