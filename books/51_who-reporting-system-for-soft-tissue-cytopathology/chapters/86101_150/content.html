8.6.10.1 Soft tissue metastases

Definition

Metastases in the soft tissue are malignant neoplasms originating from another organ, metastatic to the soft tissues.

Clinical features and imaging

Soft tissue metastases occur mostly in known and advanced aggressive malignancies and are reported in 1–10% of such cases {29708358; 10947022; 19651839; 18040999; 35310441}. The primary tumour is clinically known in most cases; however, soft tissue metastasis may present as a primary manifestation of a metastatic disease {33664218; 22514766; 35520031; 16295788; 19651839}. Only 1% of soft tissue masses turn out to be a metastasis of a primary from another site: lung, melanoma, kidney, breast, gastrointestinal, and bladder metastases are most common {19651839}. Distant soft tissue metastases from primary sarcomas are rare and usually a late event in aggressive sarcoma types {9808512}. Umbilical metastases, the so-called “Sister Mary Joseph’s nodule”, are signs of intra-abdominal or pelvic malignancy, mostly from the female genital tract, including ovary and fallopian tube, or from the stomach {16698532; 24765279; 2375224}.

In most cases, the clinical presentation and the imaging are similar to those of a primary soft tissue tumour. It is clinically important to differentiate between a metastasis and a second primary tumour, and the confirmation of a metastasis and exclusion of a second primary in the soft tissue is the main goal of the FNAB. The acquisition of an adequate tumour sample for ancillary testing of therapeutic targets is another important objective.

Histopathology

The histopathological features of the soft tissue metastasis correspond to the appearance of the primary tumour, including histopathological subtype, and the degree of differentiation.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

High cellularity Variable number of cohesive tissue fragments and single intact cells Necrotic debris may be observed in the background Abundant cytoplasm and nuclei with irregular, coarse chromatin and prominent nucleoli and perinucleolar clearing are seen in cases of metastatic carcinoma or melanoma Adenocarcinoma cells may show intracellular or extracellular mucin Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma shows variable keratinization Melanin in pigmented malignant melanoma Spindle cells can be observed in metastatic sarcomatoid carcinomas, such as primary kidney or lung carcinomas, or in malignant melanomas

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Close correlation with the patient history of known malignant diseases and imaging is a necessary requirement for the efficient evaluation of an FNAB of a potential metastasis. The microscopic features of carcinomas and melanomas are in most cases easy to differentiate from those of most mesenchymal neoplasms, especially those showing spindle or myxoid features. Difficulties may be encountered in cases of soft tissue metastases of sarcomatoid carcinomas, where both microscopy and ICC show features overlapping those of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma. Metastases of sarcomatoid carcinomas may contain heterologous elements such as chondrosarcomatous or osteosarcomatous areas and must be differentiated from rare cases of much less common extraskeletal osteosarcoma. On the other hand, more typical carcinomas must be differentiated from mesenchymal tumours showing epithelioid features, such as granular cell tumour, epithelioid sarcoma, epithelioid angiosarcoma, alveolar soft part sarcoma, and epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour. Soft tissue involvement by haematological malignancy is rarely observed in myeloid sarcoma, plasmacytoma, or non-Hodgkin lymphoma, requiring the differential diagnosis with round cell sarcomas.

Ancillary testing

ICC, preferably on cell blocks and combined with microscopic appearance and clinical history, is the most important ancillary method to establish the diagnosis of a metastasis in the soft tissues {18040999}. Strong cytokeratin expression in the proper microscopic context supports the diagnosis of a metastatic carcinoma, and the additional expression of organ-specific markers helps to identify the primary site of origin: for example, TTF1 for lung carcinoma, GATA3 and hormone receptors for breast carcinoma, GATA3 and p63 for urothelial carcinoma, PAX8 for kidney or gynaecological primaries, and CDX2 for gastrointestinal tract primaries. Metastases of sarcomatoid carcinoma may totally lack or show only very limited cytokeratin expression. On the other hand, several sarcoma types, including synovial sarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and epithelioid angiosarcoma, may be positive for cytokeratin. The expression of melanocytic markers suggests the diagnosis of metastatic melanoma in the vast majority of cases, but the immunoprofile of a clear cell sarcoma is indistinguishable, and the application of molecular testing (FISH, PCR, NGS) to demonstrate the presence of the rearrangement of the EWSR1 gene is necessary to diagnose clear cell sarcoma. Unlike round cell sarcomas, plasma cell neoplasms express CD138, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma express lymphatic markers.

FNAB can provide a cell block for targetable predictive markers {29575423; 30877381}, such as ER or PR; for FISH for ERBB2 (HER2); and for NGS panels including ALK, ROS1, NTRK1, NTRK2, NTRK3, BRAF, and other genes.
