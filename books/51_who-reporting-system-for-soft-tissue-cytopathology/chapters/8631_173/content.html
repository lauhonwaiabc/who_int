8.6.3.1 Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma

Definition

Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma (EHE) is a malignant vascular neoplasm composed of epithelioid endothelial cells with a distinctive myxohyaline stroma. Most cases are characterized by a WWTR1::CAMTA1 gene fusion, but a small subset show distinct morphology, with well-formed vessels lined by epithelioid endothelial cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and are characterized by a YAP1::TFE3 fusion.

Clinical features and imaging

In the forthcoming sixth-edition WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumours, YAP1::TFE3 haemangioendotheliomas have been separated from EHE {34411462}, but at this stage, this entity is presented here to compare with EHE.

EHE can arise at any site but most commonly involves deep soft tissues, lung, and liver. Skin and soft tissue lesions are usually solitary {7093931; 9855348; 9130982}, whereas visceral and bone lesions are often multifocal and may be metastatic at presentation {6088383; 10091730; 6282146; 2430475; 8898834}.

EHE usually arises in adults and is rare in children {18551749}. Cases have a wide age distribution among adults, but patients with YAP1::TFE3 fusion tumours tend to be younger than those with a WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion {23737213}. Visceral lesions have a slight female predominance {18551749; 9130982; 3303234}.

In soft tissues, EHEs usually present as a painful mass. Those arising from large vessels can result in other symptoms of vascular occlusion, such as oedema and thrombophlebitis. EHE of the liver is usually an incidental finding, but it may cause abdominal pain, weight loss, ascites, hemoperitoneum, or portal hypertension {10091730; 29609064}. Thoracic tumours are more likely to be symptomatic, causing pain, cough, dyspnoea, or haemoptysis {21546438}. Approximately 20% of patients with thoracic EHE are asymptomatic, with incidentally detected disease.

EHE of soft tissues can be indolent, but approximately 21% of cases metastasize and pursue an aggressive clinical course, leading to death in approximately 17% of patients {7093931; 9130982; 18551749; 21584898; 24986479}. EHE arising in lung or bone has a worse prognosis than soft tissue tumours, and patients often present with metastatic disease {30340559; 25353289; 10091730; 21546438}. In contrast, cutaneous EHE has an excellent prognosis {9130982; 9855348}.

Histopathology

EHEs form cords and nests of cells in a myxohyaline stroma. The cells are epithelioid, with a moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm. The cytoplasm sometimes contains intracytoplasmic vacuoles, which may be empty or contain red blood cells. The cells have round nuclei and inconspicuous nucleoli {9130982; 8067508}. EHEs with YAP1::TFE3 fusion are more likely to have a solid growth pattern, show brightly eosinophilic cytoplasm, and form vascular spaces {23737213}. A small subset (< 10%) of EHEs have atypical features, such as nuclear pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity, solid sheet-like growth, and necrosis, and these cases may resemble epithelioid angiosarcomas {18551749; 9130982}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Familiarity with the clinical, imaging, and cytomorphological features of EHE helps avoid missing this entity when developing a differential diagnosis. The differential diagnosis varies depending on the anatomical location of the neoplasm, but it generally includes other epithelioid tumours, such as adenocarcinoma, epithelioid mesothelioma, melanoma, epithelioid angiosarcoma, and epithelioid sarcoma. When a vascular neoplasm is suspected, Kaposi sarcoma may be included in the differential diagnosis. The WWTR1::CAMTA1 gene fusion is specific for EHE and thus can help confirm the diagnosis when it is suspected. In the absence of such testing, a definitive diagnosis of EHE is likely to be challenging, although ICC studies can help to greatly narrow the differential diagnosis.

Adenocarcinoma shows cytomorphological overlap and has a higher incidence in visceral sites {21994192}. In addition, EHE may stain positively, albeit weakly and focally, for epithelial markers, suggesting a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma early in the work-up. EHEs do not form the 3D glandular tissue fragments seen in most adenocarcinomas. Adenocarcinomas may contain mucinous vacuoles that mimic the intracytoplasmic vacuoles seen in EHE, but the latter are mucin-negative. EHEs usually lack the pleomorphism, necrosis, and mitotic activity that may be seen in many adenocarcinomas.

Epithelioid angiosarcomas are difficult to distinguish from EHE, but they tend to be more cellular and may contain higher mitotic activity and necrosis {9305386; 10941336}. Kaposi sarcoma tends to yield spindle rather than epithelioid cells.

Melanoma may be considered in the differential diagnosis given the shared characteristics of moderate to abundant often eccentric cytoplasm, cytoplasmic granularity, dispersed cell pattern, and presence of intranuclear pseudoinclusions. However, EHE lacks melanin pigment, and the intracytoplasmic lumina are not seen in melanoma {17276948}. Similarly, epithelioid sarcoma and mesothelioma can be excluded due to ICC differences and their lack of intracytoplasmic lumina.

Ancillary testing

EHEs express the endothelial markers CD34, CD31, podoplanin (D2-40), FLI1, and ERG, but there may be substantial variability in the degree of staining for a given marker {11474291; 15001993; 21317715}. The epithelial antigens CK7, CK8, CK18, and pankeratin are expressed in as many as 40% of cases, but expression of EMA is unusual {9130982; 11014572}. SMA is expressed in approximately 50% of cases {9130982}. Tumours with the WWTR1::CAMTA1 gene fusion typically show diffuse nuclear expression of CAMTA1, whereas tumours with the YAP1::TFE3 fusion demonstrate diffuse nuclear TFE3 expression {23737213}. Expression of TFE3 can sometimes be seen in tumours with the WWTR1::CAMTA1 fusion {26414223; 24986479}.

Epithelioid angiosarcoma lacks the WWTR1::CAMTA1 gene fusion seen in most EHEs. KSHV/HHV8 can be detected in Kaposi sarcoma but not EHE. Melanoma is negative for epithelial and vascular markers, whereas EHE lacks markers of melanocytic differentiation such as melan-A (MART1), SOX10, and HMB45. Epithelioid sarcoma and mesothelioma lack vascular marker expression {7534682; 17351942}, and EHE does not express markers of mesothelial differentiation such as calretinin {17351942}. Adenocarcinomas are negative for the vascular markers ERG and FLI1.
