1.1.1.1 Background

Soft tissue cytopathology is accepted as the first-line approach for following up on previously diagnosed and treated sarcomas, as well as for confirming soft tissue metastases from carcinomas, myeloid tumours, and other sarcomas {9800130; 19763717; 20091838; 11038426}. In many institutions, fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) has been successfully used as an initial diagnostic procedure or as a complement to core needle biopsy (CNB) for the diagnosis of soft tissue masses in the context of a multidisciplinary approach {8520003; 19763717; 11038426; 32773338; 15918176; 30992649; 32926591; 17973999; 12451566; 3754426; 11190808; 10439797; 11335959; 10230353}. In low-resource settings, FNAB has been used as a readily available, less expensive, and less infrastructure-demanding initial diagnostic test for examination of soft tissue lesions {26090002}. Progress in minimally invasive diagnostic procedures, advances in imaging techniques, and the continuously growing array of ancillary tests applied to cytopathological specimens over the past decades have brought widespread acceptance of the use of FNAB in the primary diagnosis of soft tissue lesions globally {32303388; 32298511; 25487166}. Important limitations of cytopathology stem from the limited experience of most pathologists in the cytopathological diagnosis of soft tissue tumours {14676638; 32773338; 18008345} and from the lack of a uniform cytopathological nomenclature with a standardized system for reporting soft tissue cytopathology {32298511; 32926591} similar to those used in other organs systems.

In 2019, an international group of cytopathologists experienced in soft tissue cytopathology was brought together by the International Academy of Cytology (IAC) and began the process of developing such a system. The objectives were to produce a reporting system that would reflect the fifth-edition WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumours (STB5) [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]], which is currently undergoing revision to produce the sixth edition (STB6), and to create a basis for a minimally invasive approach to the cytomorphological diagnosis of soft tissue lesions. The development of a standardized reporting system with clearly defined diagnostic categories that can be applied in everyday practice is an essential step to increase and support the use of FNAB in the diagnosis of soft tissue tumours and maximize its synergies with other diagnostic modalities. The WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) has continued the development of a reporting system, and the members of the expert editorial board acknowledge the work of the members of the IAC group.

In 2020, the IAC signed a memorandum of understanding with the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), which is the WHO agency responsible for developing and promulgating the WHO Classification of Tumours series (“the WHO Blue Books”), to develop a series of WHO Reporting Systems for Cytopathology {37702127}. Three other WHO Reporting Systems have been published to date, for cytopathology of the following sites: lung; pancreaticobiliary; and lymph nodes, spleen, and thymus [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.fr/620.]] [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.fr/621.]] [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Lymph Node, Spleen, and Thymus Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2024. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/644.]]. The primary aim of this collaboration is to establish universal standardized cytopathology reporting systems for each site, with standardized terminology, as well as to establish by international consensus the key diagnostic cytopathological criteria for entities, to ensure robust cytopathological diagnoses globally, including accommodating and supporting laboratories with limited resources in low- and middle-income countries {37702127}.

This WHO System is intended to assist cytopathologists in their daily work of interpreting and reporting FNAB samples and to help optimize the appropriate handling and preparation of FNAB material for ancillary studies. The WHO System provides a structured reporting system to facilitate communication between cytopathologists and clinicians, with the fundamental aim of improving patient care {37702127}. The WHO System is based on a consensus of expert cytopathologists and the published literature of the established key diagnostic cytopathological criteria and the most cost-effective and accurate ancillary tests. Six diagnostic categories are clearly defined and form the reporting structure:

Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic Benign Atypical Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential (STNUMP) Suspicious for malignancy Malignant

Individual entities are presented with brief clinical, imaging, and histopathological descriptions to assist the cytopathologist, and then the focus is on the cytopathology and its differential diagnosis, followed by a discussion of ancillary tests. The Definition, Clinical features and imaging, and Histopathology subsections correspond closely to STB5 and are following by subsections on Key diagnostic cytopathological features, Discussion and differential diagnosis, and finally Ancillary testing. The differential diagnoses of specific entities focus on their cytopathological criteria so that the WHO System can be used globally. A detailed review of the most recent ancillary testing is presented for each entity.

FNAB is based on high-yield specimens and well-prepared direct smears and cell blocks that rely on the expertise of the FNAB operator. The WHO System includes detailed guides on sampling methods, specimen preparation, and the use of rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE). ROSE increases the cost-effectiveness and efficiency of the triage of FNAB material to optimize the diagnosis based on cytopathology and ancillary tests supporting the multidisciplinary approach.

Specific entities and neoplasms are presented in the “Benign”, “STNUMP”, and “Malignant” chapters, organized by the pattern and cell-type presentation encountered by the reporting cytopathologist, which is similar to the lineage presentation in STB5. Entities in the WHO System are not the complete equivalent of those presented in the WHO Blue Books, because there is a lack of cytopathological criteria and references for several rare or recently defined soft tissue neoplasms, and because not all soft tissue lesions are amenable to cytopathological examination. Most of these entities are discussed in the introductory sections of the various chapters and in the differential diagnoses of neoplasms with clearly defined cytopathological diagnostic criteria.

The WHO System integrates cytomorphology and the six defined diagnostic categories with lineage-based molecular classifications, in alignment with the soft tissue sections of the forthcoming STB6, which is currently being written and has some changes from the fifth edition. These changes include the renaming of “undifferentiated small round cell sarcomas” as “undifferentiated spindle and small round cell sarcomas”, which include Ewing sarcoma as the prototype and other sarcomas defined by specific gene fusions. This forms a bridge between morphology and molecular-based classifications based on specific gene alterations, especially fusions. But the sarcomas other than Ewing sarcoma in this section are not only composed of round cells. Sarcomas with BCOR genetic alterations contain an admixture of round and spindle cells, and sarcomas with EWSR1::NFATC2 fusions (now a discrete entity rather than a subtype of what was previously called “round cell sarcoma with EWSR1–non-ETS fusions”) or with CIC rearrangement contain a significant admixture of epithelioid cells {34265123; 34515032; 26800124; 33399252}. PATZ1-rearranged sarcomas are now grouped with the tumours of uncertain differentiation, in light of the variable immunohistochemical expression of myogenic and neurogenic markers by the tumours cells {30379650}.

There are also a number of new entities in STB6, most of which have been described in only a handful (or in some cases no) cytopathological reports {34411462; 36151940; 36856555; 39670651; 35514197} and will require the awareness of cytopathologists so as to correlate the diagnosis of these tumours by histopathology and molecular testing with any subsequent cytopathology in the future. The new entities in STB6 include lipoblastoma-like tumour, which enters the differential diagnosis of lipoma and has a PLAG1 rearrangement {11549588; 37307874}; calcifying fibrous tumour; PRRX1-rearranged fibroblastic tumour; nodular necrotizing fibroblastic sarcoma {35546636}; chondroid synoviocytic neoplasm {39181450}; spindle cell haemangioma with HMGA2::NCOR fusion; SRF-rearranged myoid neoplasm; OGT-rearranged acral mesenchymal neoplasm {31567281}; acral fibrochondromyxoid tumour {32047233}; GLI1-altered mesenchymal tumour {35514197}; superficial FET::ETS neurocristic tumour, which harbours the same fusion event as Ewing sarcoma but with location in skin and superficial soft tissues and an indolent course {38866368}; kinase-altered spindle cell neoplasms; NUTM1-rearranged sarcoma {38946048}; and FET::CREB round to epithelioid mesenchymal tumours {34272753; 39864664; 32243019; 31305268; 39031200; 39249507}.

In the forthcoming STB6, the terminology and status of atypical spindle cell / pleomorphic lipomatous tumour, with its RB1 gene deletion, and atypical lipomatous tumour / well-differentiated liposarcoma, with its distinctive MDM2/CDK4 amplification, are confirmed, and the cytopathology has been further described {36856555}. However, it is recognized that atypical spindle cell / pleomorphic lipomatous tumour can undergo sarcomatous transformation. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma has been moved to “fibroblastic and myofibroblastic tumours” from “tumours of uncertain differentiation”. YAP1::TFE3 haemangioendothelioma has been separated from epithelioid haemangioendothelioma {34411462}. “NTRK-rearranged spindle cell neoplasm” has been replaced by “kinase-altered spindle cell tumour, including NTRK-rearranged mesenchymal tumour”, which includes other tumours showing gene rearrangements such as NTRK rearrangements and EGFR internal tandem duplication (ITD), as well as RET, RAF1, BRAF, ALK, ROS1, and MET rearrangements {39670651}. “Superficial CD34-positive pleomorphic fibrous tumour” has replaced “superficial CD34-positive fibroblastic tumour”, as a better descriptor of this tumour with its PRDM10 rearrangement {36151940}. “SMAD3-rearranged fibroblastic tumour” has replaced “EWSR1::SMAD3 tumour” {29957732}. “Fusion-associated spindle cell rhabdomyosarcoma”, including infantile cases characterized by a recurrent VGLL2-related fusion {26501226} and “MYOD1-mutant spindle cell sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma” {35929754}, have replaced the single group “spindle cell / sclerosing rhabdomyosarcoma”, based on morphology, genetic changes, and importantly clinical outcomes {27562493; 31383960}. Another molecular subset of fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcomas, containing epithelioid and spindle cells, with proclivity for bones followed by soft tissues (mostly in the head and neck region), is also included {31383960}. “Inflammatory leiomyosarcoma” has been renamed “inflammatory rhabdomyoblastic tumour” and grouped with the skeletal muscle tumours {33318583}. The diagnostic criteria of keratin-positive giant cell–rich tumour with HMGA2::NCOR2 fusion have been refined {33742141}. A number of entities have been deleted, including deep fibrous histiocytoma and ectomesenchymoma.

These newly defined entities demonstrate the need to further expand the spectrum of soft tissue tumours diagnosable by FNAB cytopathology, assisted in most cases by molecular testing, while at the same time it is crucial to be aware that all molecular test results must be interpreted in a clinical-cytopathological context, particularly when remembering the non-specificity of certain rearrangements (e.g. EWSR1 rearrangements) and gene fusions (e.g. FET::CREB fusions) {24320889; 22510762}.
