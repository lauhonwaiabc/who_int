4.3.1.2 Granulomatous and suppurative granulomatous inflammation

Definition

Granulomatous inflammation is characterized by epithelioid granulomas with or without necrosis and multinucleated giant cells. Suppurative granulomatous inflammation additionally includes a suppurative component of neutrophils and cellular debris.

Clinical features and imaging

Granulomatous lesions in soft tissue usually present as a mass or swelling and can mimic a soft tissue tumour. Heat and erythema are usually less pronounced than in purely acute inflammation, and ulcers can be present in the skin overlying the site of infection, along with sinus formation with serosanguinous or suppurative discharge. Depending on the etiological agent, the lesions can be solitary, multifocal, or disseminated. Systemic signs can be present, including weight loss, fever, and night sweats. Patient history, such as contact with pulmonary tuberculosis, HIV status, migration, travel, and country of residence can be useful for diagnosis {25144462; 28892913; 19386847}.

Histopathology

Granulomas are composed of epithelioid histiocytes, along with multinucleated histiocytes as giant cells. Central necrosis, caseous or non-caseous, can be present or absent, as can suppuration. The etiological agent can sometimes be identified with routine and/or special stains and other investigations.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Epithelioid granulomas, which can include multinucleated giant cells, in a background that can include necrosis and/or suppuration Epithelioid histiocytes with elongated, “footprint” or oval nuclei, with fine chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli The cytoplasm of histiocytes is eccentric and abundant, sometimes syncytial and pale Depending on the etiology, inflammatory cells (mainly lymphocytes, histiocytes, multinucleated histiocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils) can be present in the background, mixed with a variable amount of necrosis Infectious microorganisms can be identified with special stains for mycobacteria, including acid-fast stains (e.g. Ziehl–Neelsen, rhodamine–auramine, and autofluorescence of Pap stains) and fungal stains (e.g. methenamine silver and DPAS stains)

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Granulomatous and suppurative granulomatous lesions can involve soft tissues in the setting of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, atypical mycobacterial infections, other non-tuberculous mycobacterial diseases (e.g. Buruli ulcers, leprosy), and actinomycosis. Granulomas associated with caseous necrosis in the background or suppuration with neutrophils can be found in tuberculosis and are associated with variable numbers of mycobacteria as observed in the Ziehl–Neelsen stain and its variants {26800030}. Atypical mycobacterioses tend to have a greater number of mycobacteria in the foamy cytoplasm of histiocytes and negative curved bacilli in the background serum in Giemsa stains.

Fungal infections include cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, aspergillosis, candidiasis, blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, sporotrichosis, and mycetomas. Granulomatous and suppurative granulomatous patterns with or without non-caseous necrosis admixed with eosinophils can be found in fungal infections. In some cases, identification of the etiological agent is possible in the routine stain on the basic morphological features, but this must be complemented with specific special stains if available {9564567}.

Parasitic infections including filariasis and cysticercosis can also elicit granulomatous and granulomatous suppurative inflammation {34876043; 29957160; 25144462; 25605666; 28892913; 19386847; 11335960; 29538642}.

The causes of non-infectious granulomatous conditions are multiple and include foreign-body reactions (e.g. to cosmetic soft tissue fillers), sarcoidosis, autoimmune and allergic conditions, drug reactions, and neoplasia. In these conditions, granulomas present without necrosis. Giant multinucleated cells can be numerous in the response to foreign bodies {32725258; 9564567}.

Some tumours are granulomatous, include a reactive granulomatous component, or resemble granulomas and should be considered in the differential diagnoses of epithelioid granulomas. These include Kaposi sarcoma, epithelioid sarcoma (usual type), Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphomas, metastatic germ cell tumour, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma {19679122; 11507697; 28195109; 12508177; 11241266; 10204106; 35612406}. Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma is the most notable to elicit a granulomatous response. Careful examination of smears for scattered malignant cells and the use of appropriate ancillary testing are required.

Ancillary testing

Special stains such as the Ziehl–Neelsen are useful for mycobacterial infections, and methenamine silver and DPAS are useful for fungal infections. Fluorescence microscopy is also used in mycobacterial infections. Polarization helps to identify foreign bodies in foreign-body granulomas. Mucicarmine stains the cryptococcal capsule, which is also seen as a negative image between the positive yeast body and the surrounding serum in the Giemsa stain, highlighting the diagnostic narrow neck budding. Correlation with culture and PCR is helpful when available. ICC with appropriate markers is useful to distinguish tumours presenting with granulomas {9564567; 26800030; 33202553; 28195109; 12508177; 11241266; 10204106; 35612406; 27145123}.
