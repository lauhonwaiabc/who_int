2.2.1.5 Molecular testing

With the rapidly evolving technology that enables the determination of disease-related genetic aberrations, molecular testing of cytopathological material, which is a minimally invasive tumour sampling technique, is likely to increase to provide cytopathologists and clinicians with accurate diagnostic, prognostic, and treatment predictive information {33119213; 28475299; 33792981; 30600320; 30877381; 31135103; 26399861}.

Technical requirements

Successful molecular testing of FNAB samples of soft tissue tumours is directly dependent on specimen adequacy. Sequencing-based molecular testing has two basic input requirements: the amount of nucleic acid yield (which is based on the overall cellularity of the specimen) and the ratio of tumour to neoplastic cells or neoplastic nuclear fraction {27333361; 28739858}.

Management of types of cytopathological specimens

DNA and RNA can be extracted from different types of routine cytopathological specimens. Different approaches are used for processing the specimens depending on individual laboratory preferences. Any of the resulting cytopathology specimen preparations and stains are suitable for molecular testing {29575423; 29547001}.

Fixation

Among the preanalytical factors affecting the quality of molecular assays, fixation is key. As a general rule, non–cross-linking fixation using alcoholic reagents (e.g. wet or spray fixatives) or air-drying provides higher-quality nucleic acids than fixation in formalin. In fact, a prolonged fixation time in formalin leads to C>T sequence artefact, a potential pitfall of cell block–based molecular assays {16452569}. In general, air-dried and ethanol-fixed slides provide high-quality nucleic acids and similar results in terms of DNA quality {28739858; 27333361}. Assessment of nucleic acid quantity and quality to assure successful sequencing-based molecular testing requires several techniques to be used in a complementary manner, including spectrophotometer analysis, fluorescence assays, capillary electrophoresis, and quantitative PCR assays {28739858}.

Controls

The use of control material is an integral component of any type of molecular testing, including NGS. Control samples are a requirement for clinical testing and are used to readily detect sources of error and monitor the overall performance of the assays. For this purpose, positive, negative, and no-template controls are generally used. Each run should also include a sensitivity control to ensure the detection of the target of interest at the lowest limit of detection established for the assay {28341590}. Clinical laboratories typically derive a set of controls needed to match the specifics of the methods used with respect to the intended clinical use. In this context, the incorporation of controls that reflect the quality of cytological samples, depending on differences in preparation, is also an important consideration. For example, for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cell blocks, controls should be prepared using similar procedures from a well-characterized sample with variants near the limit of detection of the assay.

Procedures

A variety of molecular testing methodologies can be used for assessment of soft tissue tumour cytopathology specimens. These include methods ranging from single-gene–based mutation assays (e.g. Sanger sequencing, pyrosequencing, and real-time PCR), to multiplexed assays, to broad expanded molecular profiling approaches such as NGS. Testing using an expanded NGS panel approach offers the advantage of simultaneously screening multiple genes for a variety of alterations, including single-nucleotide variations, insertion-deletions, copy-number changes, gene fusions, tumour mutation burden, and microsatellite instability, depending on the design of the assay. In the diagnosis of soft tissue tumours, RNA-based gene fusion NGS testing is of particular interest because the procedure combines the simultaneous testing for numerous aberrations, in contrast to single-gene FISH or the single-breakpoint search of Sanger sequencing PCR.

Diagnostic applications

Molecular testing of soft tissue tumour samples is primarily used for diagnostic purposes. Specific gene fusions, point mutations, or amplifications/deletions can be detected either by a dedicated search for a particular aberration with FISH or PCR or as part of a broader panel analysis with NGS {35441831; 33608696}. The development of mutation antibodies, such as KIT (CD117) and β-catenin, and fusion-specific antibodies, as used for identifying synovial sarcoma and myxoid liposarcoma, has improved differential diagnosis and facilitated the choice of the proper molecular study. The detection of any particular mutation must be correlated with the cytopathology and ICC findings, because many mutations are not specific for just one entity, and some, such as ALK or EWSR1 gene fusions, occur in both mesenchymal and non-mesenchymal tumours.

The detection of mutations in one of the involved genes of gastrointestinal stromal tumours, such as KIT, PDGFRA, or an SDH gene, is not only of diagnostic importance but also of prognostic and predictive value, because these mutations determine the appropriate therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors. The complete diagnostic workup of gastrointestinal stromal tumour can be performed on EUS-guided FNAB samples, including the molecular analysis if adequate tumour material is available {17145623; 24997326; 35301817}.
