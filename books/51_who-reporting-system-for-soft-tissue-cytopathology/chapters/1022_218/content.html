10.2.2 Category: Benign

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Benign” demonstrates unequivocal benign cytopathological features, which may or may not be diagnostic of a specific process or benign neoplasm.

A “Benign” categorization is applied when a sample is adequate for interpretation and shows unequivocal features of a benign lesion. This may represent a specific benign neoplasm or process that is subclassified based on its distinctive cytomorphological pattern, such as a specific type of stroma or matrix constituting adipocytic, myxoid, and chondro-osseous patterns; a prominent cellular feature such as inflammation or multinucleate giant cells; or a specific cell shape, as in spindle or epithelioid patterns.

In this WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System), the “intermediate” lesions of the WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumours are divided and categorized into two distinct categories: “locally aggressive” lesions such as fibromatosis are categorized into the “Benign” category; and “rarely metastasizing” tumours such as angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma are categorized into the “Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential” (“STNUMP”) category. The “STNUMP” category includes neoplasms whose cytopathological and often histopathological and ICC features are not always a reliable predictor of the behaviour of the neoplasm, as well as some unpredictable neoplasms that most commonly behave in a benign fashion but have a small potential for malignant behaviour.

Benign soft tissue lesions greatly outnumber aggressive or malignant tumours, including sarcomas, by an estimated factor of 100 to 1 {6670522}. Many of the lesions included in this FNAB category are not neoplastic, such as focal fat necrosis, mass-forming inflammatory processes, and ganglion cysts, but they are included because they fall into the differential diagnosis of neoplastic lesions clinically and on imaging and are frequently sampled by FNAB.

Benign lesions can cause significant morbidity by obstruction, impingement, or other physical mechanisms, and they may be difficult to remove; for example, vascular neoplasms and malformations can be large and infiltrative, necessitating difficult and often multistage removal; fibromatosis, a locally aggressive tumour, is also difficult to excise {28439797}. In addition, some benign lesions may be transient or self-limited, such as nodular fasciitis {21826056}.

Overall accuracy in FNAB of soft tissue is affected by individual cytopathologists’ experience, which can be limited other than in specialist orthopaedic and oncological institutions where relatively rare soft tissue neoplasms tend to be seen more frequently, and by the use of a multidisciplinary approach, which is highly recommended {10230353}.

The sensitivity and specificity of FNAB for separating benign from malignant soft tissue lesions ranges depending on various study parameters, with an overall sensitivity of 97.4% and specificity of 98.2% in a large meta-analysis {32622858}. Studies vary in requiring surgical pathology follow-up or accepting that clinical and imaging follow-up suffices to confirm the “Benign” FNAB. The negative predictive value for a benign lesion ranges from 94% to 98% {17474257; 20091838; 4072661; 11038426; 20512437; 19763717; 32926591}, and the ROM for the “Benign” category can be conservatively estimated to be 5% or less. In a recently published single-institution retrospective review, the ROM was 2.49% {39212584}, although this same review showed that the overall ROM across the categories varied with the cytomorphological pattern, from 3% for lipomatous tumours reported on FNAB to 62.78% for spindle cell, 78.15% for epithelioid, 84.58% for myxoid, 94.26% for pleomorphic, and 100% for round cell tumours.

A “Benign” soft tissue FNAB should be correlated with the clinical and imaging findings and with ancillary testing, such as ICC for MDM2 in fat neoplasms. Any discrepancy in this triple approach should lead to consideration of repeat FNAB with ROSE and cell block, and potentially CNB if available. CT scans, ultrasound, and particularly MRI are all useful in assessing soft tissue neoplasms {18566812; 25782339}, and ultrasound is recommended in assessing superficial lesions and for FNAB guidance. The management of soft tissue lesions categorized as “Benign” depends on many factors, including lesion size, depth, and location, as well as patient comfort and preference. Some benign lesions are removed for cosmetic purposes only, and some can be monitored clinically for signs of growth or change. Surgical removal is best accomplished with thoughtful preoperative planning and assessment. With some benign or locally aggressive lesions, such as desmoid fibromatosis, active surveillance is the current preferred approach in most clinical scenarios {28439797}.
