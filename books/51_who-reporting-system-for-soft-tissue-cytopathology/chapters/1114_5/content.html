1.1.1.4 Diagnostic categories and report structure

Diagnostic categories

Each of the categories of the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) is associated with a ROM that is directly linked to a recommendation for further diagnostic testing to achieve a specific diagnosis or to refine a differential diagnosis and suggest further management. Specific processes or tumours that have a similar ROM when diagnosed on cytopathology are placed in the same category; for example, inflammatory and benign neoplastic entities are placed in the “Benign” category. The WHO System uses categories to assist communication of the ROM to the clinician, but a specific diagnosis or a differential diagnosis that can be further refined by ancillary testing is the aim of all cytopathology reporting and paramount in the report.

There are six WHO System categories that can be used for direct FNAB smears, LBC, and touch preparations of soft tissue CNB:

Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic Benign Atypical Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential (STNUMP) Suspicious for malignancy Malignant

Currently there are limited publications defining the ROM for these categories in soft tissue cytopathology, and there is a need for further research using the WHO System to more clearly define the ROM associated with each category.

Individual cytopathologists, as well as departments with more than one cytopathologist, should choose to use either “Insufficient” or “Inadequate” as the term for cases where there is no material for assessment or when there are technical problems that prevent assessment of material on the slides. If there is adequate benign soft tissue material on the smears, then this is described and reported as “Benign”. Correlation with the clinical and imaging findings should be carried out wherever possible, and if the material on the slides shows benign material that does not correlate with or explain the imaging or clinical findings, then it is mandatory to include a caveat in the report conclusion, that the material “may not represent the lesion seen on imaging and further diagnostic workup is recommended”. This discrepancy may relate to sampling error, particularly with small lesions seen on imaging, or it may reflect the limitations of imaging in accurately distinguishing some soft tissue lesions.

It is recognized that in some cytopathology practices in some parts of the world the term “Non-diagnostic” has been applied to encompass not only cases where there is insufficient or technically obscured material, but also cases where there is benign cytopathological material that does not correlate with or explain the clinical and/or imaging findings. This is the first WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology, and the expert editorial board accepts that in line with current usage, these cases may in some regions of the world be categorized as “Non-diagnostic” and placed in the “Insufficient/Inadequate” category. However, the term “Non-diagnostic” is not encouraged in soft tissue FNAB. Research into the impact of this approach on the “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” and “Benign” ROM is required.

If any atypical features are seen in a case showing otherwise insufficient material, the case is regarded as “Atypical” and not “Insufficient”.

The “Benign” category includes specific neoplasms and inflammatory processes including suppurative, granulomatous, and specific infectious or inflammatory diseases. If the features are benign but a precise diagnosis cannot be made, the features should be described and a differential diagnosis provided. If the features raise a differential diagnosis that includes a low-grade or high-grade lesion, then the case is placed in the “Atypical” category. As with all categories, correlation with clinical features and wherever possible imaging is mandatory.

The “Atypical” category includes cases where there is scant or poorly prepared cellular material, as well as, importantly, cases with considerable material showing mainly benign cytopathological features but with some features that may raise the possibility of malignancy, for example a spindle cell pattern with low cellularity but moderate nuclear atypia. The ability of individual cytopathologists and their teams to confidently diagnose soft tissue lesions on cytopathology alone varies with local practice and expertise, as well as the expectations and support of their clinical colleagues.

The “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low to intermediate ROM and allows for a high negative predictive value for a “Benign” diagnosis, while the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to have a higher ROM and provide for a high positive predictive value, approaching 100% for a “Malignant” categorization. The categories stratify the ROM and allow for the development of recommendations for the further diagnostic workup of the case to achieve as specific a diagnosis as possible, which is the aim of all cytopathology.

The “Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential” (“STNUMP”) category encompasses two distinct groups: firstly, cases where the diagnosis of a specific neoplasm can be made and the neoplasm’s behaviour (in terms of recurrence, invasiveness, or ability to metastasize) is uncertain, for example solitary fibrous tumour; and secondly, the “STNUMP” category includes tumours with intermediate biological potential and tumours requiring the application of histopathological criteria to determine risk stratification as to their risk of metastasis.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cases where there is scant, poorly smeared, or poorly prepared and stained material suggestive of malignancy, as well as cases where there is considerable material, some of which appears malignant, but not all features of a particular malignancy are present or there may be discrepant features. The report should specifically describe the features that are suspicious and provide a differential diagnosis of the lesions suspected.

The “Malignant” category is reserved for those cases in which there are unequivocal features of malignancy without any discrepant findings. All cytopathology specimen types are obtained by a sampling technique, and it is not always possible to make a precise diagnosis of a specific soft tissue tumour even with ancillary testing. The precise diagnosis of a primary soft tissue malignancy or metastatic carcinoma or lymphoma involving soft tissue often requires ancillary testing.

The presentation of tumours in this WHO System loosely follows the order found in the sixth-edition WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumours, but the grouping of tumours is by smear pattern, cell type, and the architectural features of the tissue fragments, reflecting a cytopathologist’s diagnostic approach [[Field AS, Zarka MA. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2017.]]. Tumours that have not been described in the cytopathology literature are not included, and rare tumours with few publications are presented in the differential diagnosis of more common tumours.

Structured reporting

Each cytopathology report on a soft tissue FNAB should have a structured format, with a heading of a specific diagnostic category using the WHO System’s standardized terminology and a diagnosis. Structured reporting improves the quality, clarity, and reproducibility of reports within individual pathology departments and between countries, and it will improve patient care and facilitate research and quality assurance measures. Standard guidelines for cell block preparation, ICC, FISH, and flow cytometry in cases of lymphoid lesions, as well as molecular pathology for tests of diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive markers, will improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce cost, and improve patient care. Structured reports are based on key diagnostic cytopathological findings for each lesion, which act as a checklist for the reporting cytopathologist and will also improve reporting.

The essential or core requirements in a cytopathology report are presented here. This is not intended to make any particular format mandatory, but some components are required: the precise location of the lesion; the number of direct smears and amount and type of fluids received; the diagnostic category using the defined terminology of the reporting system; a microscopic description of the key diagnostic features; and a diagnosis, which may be specific or descriptive with a differential diagnosis. The aim of all FNAB cytopathology is to provide as accurate and specific a diagnosis as possible, and if not possible, a differential diagnosis that weights the most likely diagnoses. Supplementary reports of any ancillary tests and their results are also required, wherever possible in an integrated final report and diagnosis. Ancillary tests may change the final category, for example when ICC confirms a malignancy suspected on the cytopathology alone. The diagnostic summary, including site of biopsy, category, and diagnosis, must be clearly delineated and stand out in the report so that it can be easily seen by the patient care team, and it is best placed early on the first page rather than at the end of multiple pages of report.

It is not recommended to use a number in defining a category at any stage. A category number can be included in the body of the report for audit and research purposes but not in the diagnostic summary. Reducing a cytopathology report to a number reduces the information that can be given to the clinician and management team and increases the risk of error.

The diagnostic category states whether there is inadequate material and an “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” categorization is applicable, or whether there is adequate material and a “Benign”, “Atypical”, “STNUMP”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant” category can be applied. But it is recognized that some cytopathologists prefer to include a statement in the report that “there is adequate material for assessment”. Other cytopathologists require a statement to be included regarding the degree of cellularity of the material from the target lesion; this assessment of cellularity does not include inflammatory cells except in cases of an inflammatory lesion.

The microscopic description should provide a clear, concise cytopathological description, which usually includes the degree of cellularity and focuses on the presence or absence of key cytopathological diagnostic features. Some cytopathologists prefer to provide a full description of these features; others may prefer, in cases where there is a specific diagnosis, to provide minimal or no description.

The aim of cytopathology is to provide as specific a diagnosis as possible in the vast majority of cases, and this remains the priority. Categories allow the grouping of lesions, including those that are “Atypical”, “STNUMP”, or “Suspicious for malignancy”, and the establishment of a ROM and subsequent management recommendations as part of an algorithm to aid communication with the clinician and further diagnostic management of the patient.

The ancillary tests should always be part of the final cytopathology report, and if ICC, flow cytometry, in situ hybridization, or molecular pathology reports are issued by a different laboratory, then it is recommended that they should be added as a supplementary report to the original cytopathology report, in the interest of having a final all-inclusive integrated report for optimal patient care. The WHO Reporting Systems for cytopathology are based on the cytomorphology findings for the initial categorization and reporting of the lesion, which can be applied in any well-resourced or low- and middle-income country (LMIC) setting, but they also incorporate the application of appropriate ancillary testing as current best practice. Subsequent ancillary testing may well change the final diagnostic category and provide a specific diagnosis of a particular case.
