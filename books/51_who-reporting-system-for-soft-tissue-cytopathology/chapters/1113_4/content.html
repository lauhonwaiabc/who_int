1.1.1.3 Integration of clinical, imaging, and key FNAB cytopathological features with ancillary testing in a diagnostic approach

Soft tissue tumours comprise a heterogeneous group of benign, intermediate, and malignant lesions with mesenchymal differentiation. Sarcomas are rare, constituting < 1% of all malignant neoplasms. The frequency of benign soft tissue tumours is estimated to be at least 100 times that of sarcomas. Soft tissue tumours occur in various planes and locations, and 99% of benign soft tissue neoplasms are superficial, whereas two thirds of the extremity and trunk-wall sarcomas are deep-seated. Sarcomas often present complex clinical and morphological features. The optimal diagnostic management of soft tissue lesions is a multimodal evaluation with synthesis of clinical data, imaging, and cytomorphology, supported by ancillary studies when necessary {34303806}. Patients presenting with a soft tissue lesion or a suspicion of a sarcoma should preferably be referred to specialized orthopaedic oncology centres with access to multidisciplinary expertise {6581679; 22810399; 24692232; 30554196; 36376458; 34303806}. In such centres, oncological, imaging, histopathological and cytopathological, and molecular pathology expertise can be combined in a multidisciplinary team to facilitate the adequate evaluation, therapy, and follow-up of patients with soft tissue neoplasms. This multidisciplinary management favourably influences outcomes for patients with both benign and malignant soft tissue lesions {25733913; 23983648; 31073021; 28748443; 29661168; 19525085}.

Clinical history and clinical examination are important to guide the initial evaluation of soft tissue lesions, including the decision as to whether there is a need for imaging and biopsy, and if so, what type of imaging and biopsy will provide the information necessary to inform further patient care. Relevant clinical characteristics should include the patient’s age, sex, clinical history, and family history of cancers and genetic tumour syndromes, as well as the clinical findings, such as tumour site, presentation, duration and type of symptoms, and laboratory test results.

Imaging is an important part of the workup of soft tissue lesions; it helps to determine whether a lesion is benign or malignant, homogeneous or heterogeneous, as well as to identify any notable structures, such as origin from a large nerve trunk {34567971; 36105415; 30663160}. MRI is the main imaging modality in the examination of soft tissue lesions in the extremities, pelvis, and trunk; CT has a role in the evaluation of calcified lesions and pleuropulmonary and retroperitoneal sarcomas {34303806}. Ultrasound and MRI both provide diagnostic information that assists in distinguishing between benign and malignant soft tissue masses {26029024}, and combining these two modalities increases diagnostic performance, particularly the specificity {35574339}. Nuclear medicine and hybrid imaging such as PET-CT are valuable to detect multiple lesions and evaluate tumour activity.

Most soft tissue tumours require a biopsy for diagnosis, particularly when malignancy cannot be ruled out by clinical or imaging means. A biopsy also provides information regarding the histopathological type of soft tissue tumour and grade of malignancy in cases of sarcoma {34303806}.

Ideally, an FNAB should be the first biopsy method in the examination of a suspected recurrence or metastasis of a previously treated sarcoma or other malignancies presenting as soft tissue tumours {9800130; 19763717; 20091838}, followed by CNB and excision biopsy when necessary. Depending on local expertise and the site of the lesion, FNAB can be used for the evaluation of primary soft tissue lesions alone in the clinical setting of a multidisciplinary diagnostic team {11038426; 11190808; 3754426; 14676638; 8520003; 12451566; 17973999}, or it can be followed immediately by CNB {30992649; 15918176; 32926591; 30709446}. ICC and molecular analysis performed on cytopathological specimens enhance the role of FNAB {25487166; 23288831; 18162764; 30190145; 11933269; 30156774}, but their use and availability vary between institutions and socioeconomic settings.

The quality of the FNAB procedure and the adequacy of the cytopathological material and its suitability for ancillary tests have a great impact on the efficacy of FNAB of soft tissue neoplasms {32303388; 26780171}. ROSE offers significant benefits in terms of controlling the quality and quantity of FNAB samples and for immediately triaging material for ancillary tests {32298511; 32654990}; it is recommended by the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) as best practice for the FNAB and the triaging of cytopathological material.

The nomenclature and classification of soft tissue neoplasms used in daily clinical practice are based on the fifth-edition WHO classification of soft tissue and bone neoplasms [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. In cytopathology practice, soft tissue lesions can be classified according to their presumed histogenesis or according to their differentiation towards a predominant cell type based on low-power smear patterns, the cell and tissue fragment architectural features, and the background of the FNAB smears {30156774; 14676638; 14518076} [[Klijanienko J, Lagacé R, editors. Soft tissue tumors: a multidisciplinary, decisional diagnostic approach. Hoboken (NJ): Wiley-Blackwell; 2011.]] [[Field AS, Zarka MA. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2017.]].

In this WHO System, each entity is placed into a cytopathological pattern and cell type group, such as adipocytic, myxoid, spindle cell, epithelioid, chondro-osseous, pleomorphic, and round cell. Each of these groups presents certain differential diagnoses to the cytopathologist and affects the approach to diagnosis, while the cytopathology of each entity mirrors its histopathology. ICC and molecular genetic tests on FNAB material can then be used to confirm or rule out a specific diagnosis.

Surgery remains the treatment of choice for most high-grade soft tissue sarcomas in adults, with additional radiotherapy or chemotherapy for cases with risk of local recurrence or metastasis. Low-grade sarcomas and many benign soft tissue neoplasms may be treated by surgery alone. In adequate FNAB specimens, it is usually possible to categorize a sarcoma cytomorphologically as low-grade or high-grade {10439797; 11335959; 10587701}. The treatment of some paediatric sarcomas (e.g. small round cell sarcomas) may include neoadjuvant therapy with potentially curative histogenetic-specific chemotherapy protocols; with the use of ancillary tests based on FNAB material, a specific diagnosis can be established in almost all cases {32654990; 34515032; 35417916; 11933269}.

However, assessing the parameters for sarcoma grading in histopathological sections and establishing the histopathological diagnosis is not always feasible using FNAB samples {11954025}. In any case in which FNAB has failed to yield material sufficient for diagnosis or the cytopathological diagnosis does not match the suspected clinical and imaging diagnosis, the imaging findings should be reviewed and correlated, and if suspicion remains high, CNB or simple excision biopsy should be performed before any major surgical or oncological procedure is undertaken.
