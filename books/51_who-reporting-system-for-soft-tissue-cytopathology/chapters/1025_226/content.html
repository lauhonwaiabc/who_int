10.2.5 Category: Suspicious for malignancy

Definition: A specimen categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” demonstrates some cytopathological features suggestive of malignancy, but with features insufficient, in either number or quality, for an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy.

There is likely to be wide interobserver variability in this category, dependent on the expertise of the FNAB operator, the quality of material provided, the quality of the smear preparation, the availability of material for cell block and ancillary testing, the inherent nature of the lesion, and the cytopathologist’s experience and confidence in rendering a malignant diagnosis.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category should have a high positive predictive value for malignancy, because the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) includes a separate “Atypical” category for atypical FNAB samples, which are most likely benign, with a lower ROM. The “Suspicious for malignancy” category also functions to avoid false positive diagnoses and ensure a positive predictive value, close to 100%, in the “Malignant” category.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cases producing cellular smears in which there is significant nuclear enlargement, anisonucleosis, irregularity of nuclear membranes, and coarse chromatin, possibly with prominent nucleoli and mitotic figures, in particular because soft tissue neoplasms may show considerable intratumoural cytomorphological heterogeneity and overlap between lesions, and none of these features is diagnostic of malignancy. It also includes cases that produce low cellularity, such as lipomatous tumours, where it may be difficult to obtain sufficient atypical cells in a cytopathology preparation to allow for a diagnosis of malignancy, as well as cases where there are atypical cells showing features suggestive of malignancy but a confident diagnosis of malignancy cannot be made due to suboptimal preparation, preservation, or staining. Such specimens cannot be regarded as “Inadequate/Insufficient/Non-diagnostic”. The background features may include necrosis, myxoid material, and inflammatory cells.

Ancillary techniques, such as ICC on cell block material, can assist in confirming a specific tumour type, but the inherent features of some soft tissue tumours produce low cellularity, insufficient for definitive assessment, or the ICC may suggest a cell lineage but not a malignant diagnosis. Molecular studies in an increasing number of specific tumours, such as MDM2 FISH analysis for liposarcoma, may allow a definitive diagnosis where the cytomorphological categorization was “Suspicious for malignancy”; if one of these tumours is suspected on the FNAB, such ancillary tests can be requested. In all cases, the tumour type suspected, or a differential diagnosis, should be stated in the cytopathology report.

The rate of “Suspicious for malignancy” cases varies. In a review of 1114 bone and soft tissue lesions, 1% showed cells suspicious for malignancy, of which 5 of the 7 with follow-up (71%) were confirmed as malignant on histopathology {20091838}. One text [[Cibas ES, Ducatman BS. Cytology: diagnostic principles and clinical correlates. Philadelphia (PA): Saunders; 2009.]] suggests using atypical and suspicious categories for FNAB, but most studies do not provide ROM for atypical/suspicious FNAB and only indicate overall sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant. Several other studies reported the proportion of cases in the atypical/suspicious category as 1–2%, usually representing only 1 or 2 cases {24876987; 11038426}.

The category has a high positive predictive value for malignancy, with a reported ROM of 71–80% {20091838; 32926591}, and in one recent retrospective single-institution study of more than 2200 cases, the ROM was 68.42% based on using the WHO System’s six categories {39212584}. The authors of the study made the interesting observation that the ROM varies not only with categorization into the six categories but also with the FNAB pattern seen, from 3% for lipomatous tumours, which include one of the most common FNAB targets (superficial lipoma), to 62.78% for spindle cell, 78.15% for epithelioid, 84.58% for myxoid, 94.26% for pleomorphic, and 100% for round cell tumours. The WHO System approach to diagnosis is based on these cytopathological patterns, which have been previously detailed [[Field AS, Zarka MA. Practical cytopathology: a diagnostic approach to fine needle aspiration biopsy. St. Louis (MO): Elsevier; 2017.]]. Further research around this finding will be worthwhile to guide cytopathologists and clinicians.

It is essential that clinical, imaging, cytopathological, and any ancillary findings available should be correlated, ideally in a multidisciplinary meeting. If there are discrepancies, repeat FNAB should be recommended to confirm the malignancy, preferably with ROSE to effectively triage material for cell block, ICC, FISH, or NGS, along with CNB whenever available. In some situations, incisional biopsy with or without frozen section or a surgical excision biopsy may be required.
