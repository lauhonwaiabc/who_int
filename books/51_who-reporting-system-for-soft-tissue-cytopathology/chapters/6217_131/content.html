6.2.1.7 Gastrointestinal stromal tumour

Definition

Gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) is a mesenchymal neoplasm with variable behaviour, characterized by differentiation towards the interstitial cells of Cajal.

Clinical features and imaging

GISTs arise in the stomach (60%), small bowel (30%), rectum (4%), colon (1–2%), and oesophagus (<1%) {17193820; 21997685; 10680889; 11684962}, and rarely in the mesentery, omentum, and retroperitoneum {10824931}. Most GISTs are sporadic, but 5–10% of cases occur in association with cancer predisposition syndromes. Most syndromic GISTs are succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-deficient, including those associated with the non-hereditary Carney triad, which includes GIST, pulmonary chondroma, and paraganglioma {10377927}, as well as those associated with the autosomal dominant Carney–Stratakis syndrome, which includes GIST and paraganglioma in the context of SDH germline mutations {17667967; 19522824} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. GISTs that are < 20 mm are usually asymptomatic and incidental findings {16136600; 15648083}. The most common presentations are vague abdominal symptoms, acute and chronic bleeding, an abdominal mass, and pain {16136600; 25031785; 34887632; 25178335}. Advanced GISTs spread into the peritoneal cavity and often metastasize to the liver {10636102; 21997685; 30498699}. CT and MRI, as well as FDG PET, are useful in the detection and functional characterization of GIST {12835984; 26022432; 20457867}. EUS-guided FNAB is often used to obtain diagnostic tissue material of submucosal GIST {12297748; 19482280}.

Histopathology

GISTs display spindle (70%), epithelioid (20%), or mixed (10%) cytomorphology {15365079}. SDH-deficient GISTs are characterized by striking multilobular or plexiform architecture and epithelioid or mixed morphology {21997692} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. Most gastric GISTs are spindle cell tumours, with epithelioid morphology seen in approximately 20–25% of cases. Small intestinal and colonic GISTs are usually spindle cell tumours with diffuse sheets or vague storiform architecture. Risk stratification in GIST is performed on resection specimens.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Because the cytopathological features of GIST vary according to the histopathological subtype, the differential diagnosis of GIST includes a range of mesenchymal, neural, and neuroendocrine neoplasms, such as leiomyoma of the gastrointestinal tract, which usually involves the oesophagus and colorectum, as well as leiomyosarcoma, schwannoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour, desmoid fibromatosis, inflammatory fibroid polyp, neuroendocrine tumour, and sarcomatoid carcinoma. Leiomyoma and schwannoma are among the most common differential diagnoses and consist of relatively uniform spindle cells similar to those found in GIST. The anatomical site and cytopathological features, such as the presence of thin-walled blood vessels, can support a diagnosis of GIST, but ICC is usually required.

Ancillary testing

GISTs are positive in ICC for KIT (CD117) in 95% of cases, DOG1 in 98%, PDGFRA in 80%, and CD34 in 70–80%, and they usually retain expression of SDHB and SDHA {15365079}. SDH-deficient GISTs are characterized by loss of SDHB expression {21997692; 22804613}. Additional loss of SDHA expression is observed in SDHA-mutant GIST {22804613}. In the differential diagnosis from schwannomas and leiomyomas, positivity for KIT (CD117) and DOG1 and negativity for desmin and S100 are required {18208801; 21350101}.

Molecular testing is rarely needed for establishing the diagnosis of GIST, but it can inform therapeutic decisions. Approximately 85% of GISTs are initiated by gain-of-function mutations in the KIT {9438854} or PDGFRA {12522257} receptor tyrosine kinase gene, whereas genomic inactivation or epigenetic silencing of NF1 {27390349} and SDH subunits, including SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD {27011036}, can be observed in many KIT/PDGFRA-wildtype GISTs. The most common GIST chromosomal aberrations are deletions of 14q inactivating MAX {28270683}, deletions of 22q inactivating DEPDC5 {31636198}, and deletions of 1p and 15q, followed by cell-cycle regulator mutations {10919666; 17171690; 17226762} and dystrophin inactivation {24793134}. SDH-deficient GISTs have occasional 1q deletion, presumably targeting SDHC {17535989; 19522824}.
