4.3.1.3 Fat necrosis

Definition

Fat necrosis is a non-neoplastic inflammatory lesion characterized by necrotic adipose tissue often accompanied by acute and chronic inflammatory cells and foamy vacuolated histiocytes.

Clinical features and imaging

Fat necrosis is commonly secondary to trauma, operative procedures, or acute pancreatitis. Fat necrosis presents as a painless, solitary mass, which when superficial can be relatively firm and circumscribed on palpation. When involving the breast, it is most often seen in perimenopausal women after trauma, surgery, irradiation, or CNB, or it can occur without apparent cause {16198567; 31185981}. Similarly, in extramammary sites, there is often a history of trauma or an operative procedure, although the etiology may be unknown. Fat necrosis can occur in both normal and neoplastic fat and can clinically simulate malignancy. An uncommon disorder of infants is subcutaneous fat necrosis of the newborn, which is a transient panniculitis.

With mammography, fat necrosis can have varying appearances, including discrete, circumscribed, or oval radiolucent cysts; focal mass-like structures; and occasionally an ill-defined spiculated mass, which can simulate a carcinoma. Ultrasound shows a well-circumscribed to ill-defined solid or cystic nodule, often associated with distortion of the breast parenchyma. The findings can vary depending on the stage of evolution of the process, including the degree of fibrosis that causes the architectural distortion simulating malignancy {25861475}.

Histopathology

The features of fat necrosis vary depending on this stage of the lesion. Early fat necrosis is characterized by necrosis of adipose tissue with fine vacuolization of adipocytes and nuclear dropout, with or without haemorrhage and cystic degeneration. In the intermediate stage, a mixed inflammatory cell infiltrate of histiocytes, lymphocytes, plasma cells, and occasional eosinophils can be seen, along with early fibrosis and haemosiderin deposition. Histiocytes surrounding fat vacuoles can be present, and occasional multinucleated histiocytes are noted. Late-stage fat necrosis demonstrates increased amounts of fibrosis, with or without calcification. Loculated foci of necrotic adipose tissue can be surrounded by fibrosis, forming an oil cyst, which can also contain calcification in its wall. Occasionally, a cellular proliferation of histiocytes can occur in mammary, omental, and mesenteric fat necrosis. The histiocytes can become spindle, arranged in a fascicular pattern admixed with chronic inflammatory cells and multinucleated giant cells. This cellular spindle cell histiocytic proliferation can form a mass, which can simulate a malignancy.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Fat necrosis, when occurring in the breast, can simulate malignancy, especially when skin retraction and thickened skin are present and imaging demonstrates a stellate lesion. Helpful features for a definitive diagnosis of fat necrosis are the bland appearance of the histiocytes and the lack of definite epithelial cells showing any nuclear atypia. Fat necrosis can occur in a lipoma, and it lacks the atypical nuclei, lipoblasts, and confirmatory molecular abnormalities of liposarcoma.

Ancillary testing

The histiocytes in fat necrosis are positive for CD68 and CD163, and CK7 is negative in cases where FNAB has been performed to assess recurrent breast carcinoma in the postsurgical setting. Fat necrosis in the omentum and other deep sites raises a differential diagnosis of liposarcoma, and MDM2 by ICC is negative in fat necrosis.
