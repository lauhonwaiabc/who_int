2.1.1.2 Imaging guidance of soft tissue FNAB

Imaging guidance in soft tissue FNAB may be helpful or in some cases mandatory, depending on the skill and preferences of the cytopathologist and interventional radiologist {25609446; 11038426; 32773338}. Small localized subcutaneous lesions may be sampled using manual palpation as guidance. Soft tissue lesions that are deep-seated or adjacent to a neurovascular bundle; that are intra-abdominal, pelvic, or intrathoracic; or that for some other reason are difficult to reliably biopsy using manual guidance should be biopsied using imaging guidance.

During the FNAB procedure, the imaging appearance before and after the sampling should be documented, as well as the needle position when the biopsy is actually obtained. If the procedure is performed by a radiologist or clinician, biopsy results are improved if ROSE is used {21570872; 8191828; 32654990}.

Ultrasound is a low-cost and readily available modality for biopsy guidance {9401998; 28583640; 11127010}. Ultrasound of soft tissue lesions can determine their echogenicity, size, vascularity, and relationship with adjacent structures, as well as provide a working differential diagnosis. If guiding fixtures on the transducer are used, lesions at a comparatively large distance from the skin may be biopsied reliably. With ultrasound, it is possible to guide the biopsy needle to parts of a heterogeneous tumour that are not necrotic, and vascularized parts of the tumour may be identified and avoided by using colour or power Doppler. Ultrasound guidance may be used by the cytopathologist or by a radiologist in the radiology department, depending on local expertise and equipment.

CT may be used for all biopsies of soft tissue lesions, but it uses ionizing radiation for imaging {8571862}. To reduce the radiation dose to the patient and the operator, CT guidance should be reserved for deep-seated lesions and lesions surrounded by complex anatomy. Usually, image guidance with CT is done without intravenous contrast enhancement. However, if the lesion is cystic or heterogeneous or shows other suspicious signs for necrosis, intravenous contrast may be used to delineate the viable parts of the tumour for biopsy.

Biopsies requiring image guidance are usually complex procedures, which may be associated with more risks due to more difficult access routes. With CT, the biopsy tract can be planned beforehand, and the approach can be discussed with the operating surgeon before biopsy if needed. In some cases, lesions detected on MRI may not be visible on CT, which makes CT guidance difficult, with the risk of having a false negative biopsy result. In such cases, hybrid imaging, where previously obtained MRI images are superimposed on the CT image, may be used. In addition, ROSE plays an important role: the immediate feedback helps the operator to decide the best needle path for additional passes or for acquiring a concurrent CNB.

Biopsies in the musculoskeletal system are occasionally done as both an FNAB and a CNB during the same procedure {15918176; 32654990}. If repeated FNAB passes are planned, especially in combination with CNB, the patient should receive local anaesthetic at the puncture site and towards the periosteum if a bone biopsy is planned. Some patients may also benefit from a sedative administered some time before the procedure. If a trocar is used for the CNB {21629408}, a hybrid biopsy may be done, where the trocar is also used to guide the FNAB. This reduces the numbers of both skin passes and biopsy tracts, and it reduces the radiation dose if CT is used for guidance. Such a coaxial biopsy may also be performed using a thicker needle as a guide, through which thin 22 gauge or 25 gauge needles can be inserted, making it easier to perform repeated biopsies of deep-seated lesions.
