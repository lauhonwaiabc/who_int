1.1.1.5 Risk of malignancy and diagnostic management recommendations

The goal of establishing a ROM for each category in the WHO Reporting System for Soft Tissue Cytopathology (WHO System) is to supply clinically relevant information by linking each diagnostic category with recommendations for the further diagnostic management and triage of individual cases (see #39038Table 1.01). The ROM for each category is based on the published literature reporting the experience of centres actively involved in the diagnosis and management of a significant number of soft tissue cases. In this first edition of the WHO System, the ROM is extracted from a limited number of studies, because most publications regarding musculoskeletal FNAB are based on a single diagnostic entity or series of both bone and soft tissue tumours with significant heterogeneity regarding primary versus recurrent or metastatic lesions, FNAB technique, use of imaging guidance, and use of ROSE and ancillary testing. The data in these studies are often not sufficient for the calculation of ROM as applied to the WHO System. In addition, there has been no established reporting system for soft tissue FNAB cytopathology, although a seven-category system was proposed in 2021 {32926591}. The ROM will be the subject of further research and subsequent modification in future editions of the WHO System.

The management of all diagnostic categories requires correlation with clinical and imaging findings, and a multidisciplinary approach is essential for the workup of patients with soft tissue tumours. Intraprocedural evaluation of FNAB smears by ROSE is strongly recommended during the sampling procedure.

The “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” category includes cases where there is insufficient cytopathological material for assessment due to technical reasons, such as an insufficient number of cells on the smears, poor quality of smears, air-drying artefact in alcohol-fixed Pap-stained or H&E-stained smears, and slow air-drying artefact in air-dried Giemsa-stained smears. Several factors may affect the quality and quantity of FNAB samples: the size, consistency, and location of the mass; its imaging characteristics; and its proximity to the vital structures all have an impact on the quality of the FNAB yield and subsequent biopsy results, as does the inherent nature of the lesion. The expertise and technical skills of the physician who performs the FNAB procedure are a crucial factor, as is the expertise of the cytopathologist, laboratory staff, or other staff who prepare the direct smears for routine examination and triage for ancillary studies {32298511; 18521248; 10230353}.

In a study of FNAB of soft tissue of 828 patients, the inadequate rate was 3% and the ROM 42%, and this included a CNB with FNAB in all but 2 patients {32926591}. A retrospective study of FNAB of 301 soft tissue lesions {12451566} showed a 7% inadequate rate (with haemangiomas accounting for almost half of the inadequate specimens), with a ROM of 9%. In a study of 1114 soft tissue and bone FNAB specimens, most of which had ROSE, 3% of cases were inadequate, and within that group, 70% were soft tissue lesions {20091838}. In a retrospective study of 94 bone and 36 soft tissue FNAB specimens, the insufficient rate for soft tissue lesions was 8%, with an estimated ROM of 27.3% {28739192}. In a recent retrospective study of 2214 cases at one institution, the ROM of an “Insufficient” categorization was 29.87% {39212584}.

Diagnostic management recommendations for the “Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic” category include correlation with the clinical and imaging findings, and in most cases repeat FNAB with ROSE and imaging guidance, especially in deep-seated, cystic, or haemorrhagic or necrotic lesions. In cases where the imaging is uncertain or a suspicious mass remains unresolved by further FNAB, CNB or a surgical biopsy should be recommended.

The majority of palpable soft tissue lesions are benign. The “Benign” category includes cytopathological features of mass-forming inflammatory lesions that can explain the clinical context, as well as benign neoplasms of various types, such as lipomatous, myxoid, spindle cell, epithelioid, and chondro-osseous neoplasms.

In general, a benign diagnosis is based on evidence-based, established cytopathological criteria and ancillary techniques where required. Wherever possible, the cytopathological categorization of “Benign” should be accompanied by a specific diagnosis, such as lipomatous tumour, nodular fasciitis, ischaemic fasciitis, or myositis ossificans, but precise diagnosis may not always be possible {11135465; 14748789}.

For superficial or deep soft tissue lesions where the clinical and imaging impression is that a benign lesion or neoplasm is considered most likely, and this is confirmed by FNAB, treatment options depend on the clinical symptoms, size, and anatomical location of the lesion or neoplasm, as well as the precise diagnosis. For asymptomatic benign tumours, such as lipoma or nodular fasciitis, the management options may be to wait and watch, whereas other lesions categorized as “Benign” may require limited or wide excision if causing symptoms {25435271; 16411083}. The ROM for the “Benign” category is very low, estimated at 0–3.6% {32926591; 12451566; 28739192; 19763717; 11190808}; it was found to be 2.49% in a large recent study using the WHO System reporting categories {39212584}. FNAB is highly accurate when used as an initial diagnostic test, with 89–100% sensitivity and 90–100% specificity in distinguishing benign from malignant soft tissue tumours {20383617; 24099641; 11038426; 12451566; 14748789; 17941014; 14676638}; it showed 97.4% sensitivity and 98.2% specificity, depending on various study parameters, in a recent large meta-analysis {32622858}.

The “Atypical” category includes samples where a specific diagnosis of a benign process or neoplasm cannot be made on the FNAB smears although the cytopathological features appear most likely benign, but some features raise the possibility of an indeterminate or perhaps malignant neoplasm, which cannot be excluded. The cytopathological features that make the smear atypical should always be stated, and (if possible) a differential diagnosis should be provided to enhance correlation with other findings. Most cases represent poorly sampled neoplasms and include smears from myxoid lesions or smears where small numbers of spindle cells show no or low-grade atypia. In a report of 828 patients, the ROM in this category was 46% {32926591}, and in a recent large retrospective study the ROM was 39.62% {39212584}.

Diagnostic management recommendations for the “Atypical” category include mandatory correlation with the clinical history and imaging findings. This category requires a cell block and usually ancillary testing, and it most commonly requires repeat FNAB with cell block or CNB whenever this is available and can be used at the site of the neoplasm. Incisional or excisional surgical biopsy may be considered to confirm a benign or malignant lesion. Alternatively, if clinical and imaging findings indicate that the process is highly likely to be a benign reactive or inflammatory lesion, a “watch and wait” approach may be considered.

There is a lack of published data to estimate the ROM for the diagnostic category “Soft tissue neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential” (“STNUMP”). One recent large retrospective study that recategorized reports based on the WHO System categories found a ROM of 51.43% {39212584}. In the management of cases in this category, every effort should be made to establish a precise diagnosis according to the WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumours, and this usually requires ancillary tests and correlation between cytopathology and clinical and imaging findings. Further workup can include performing a repeat FNAB (to provide additional smears for cytopathological assessment and particularly to obtain material for cell blocks that can be used for H&E stains, ICC, FISH, and NGS), proceeding to CNB for histopathological material and ancillary testing, or proceeding to incisional biopsy or resection. In some cases, depending on the surgical accessibility of the neoplasm or considerations of patient age and status, observation and clinical follow-up may be used. Ideally, the clinical, imaging, and FNAB findings are assessed and treatment decisions are made by a multidisciplinary team. Risk assessment in some of these tumour types, for example solitary fibrous tumour and gastrointestinal stromal tumour, requires histopathological assessment {28731041; 18774375}.

The report of an FNAB categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” should always state what features make the material suspicious and what lesion is suspected, and a differential diagnosis should be provided. In almost all cases where sufficient material is available, ancillary testing is required and may make it possible to render a specific malignant or benign diagnosis. This category supports a high positive predictive value for the “Malignant” category, but it should not be used as the sole basis for initiating therapy. The ROM for the “Suspicious” group was found to be 72% in a study proposing a seven-category system {32926591} and 80% in another retrospective study of 301 patients {12451566}. A recent large retrospective study using the WHO System’s six categories found a ROM of 68.42% {39212584}. The authors of that study made the interesting observation that the ROM varied not only with categorization into the six categories, but also with the FNAB pattern seen, from 3% for lipomatous tumours, which include one of the most common FNAB targets (superficial lipoma), to 62.78% for spindle cell, 78.15% for epithelioid, 84.58% for myxoid, 94.26% for pleomorphic, and 100% for round cell tumours. Further research around this finding will be worthwhile to guide cytopathologists and clinicians.

Close communication with the members of the clinical team and a review of all relevant clinical and imaging findings is mandatory to avoid unnecessary risks. The further workup of the patient to establish as specific a diagnosis as possible in a management plan should be determined within the multidisciplinary team. This may include repeating the FNAB, preferably with ROSE and triaging of material for appropriate ancillary tests; in most cases, CNB, to assess histopathological features, or surgical biopsy, for a definitive histopathological diagnosis, is also required.

The “Malignant” category includes all cases where the material is unequivocally malignant with the key cytopathological features diagnostic of a specific tumour or group of tumours, for example metastatic carcinomas, high-grade lymphomas, or low- or high-grade sarcomas. Ideally, a specific diagnosis should be made, or the case should be categorized as “Malignant” and a differential diagnosis provided. Ancillary testing is usually required to confirm or make a specific diagnosis, for example the use of ICC to diagnose the type and origin of metastatic carcinoma; the use of ICC, FISH, or molecular tests to diagnose the specific type of soft tissue sarcoma (e.g. round cell sarcoma, including Ewing sarcoma; rhabdomyosarcoma; or a spindle cell sarcoma, including a synovial sarcoma); or on uncommon occasions the use of flow cytometry to diagnose a lymphoma involving soft tissues. The estimated ROM for the “Malignant” category is 91–100% based on several retrospective studies {32926591; 12451566; 28739192; 19763717; 11190808}, and it was 97.69% in a recent large retrospective study, which also found that the ROM varied with the cytomorphological pattern as well as with the WHO System categorization {39212584}. The plan for further management should be determined by the multidisciplinary team and includes workup for distant metastasis. The type of surgical intervention depends on whether the neoplasm is subcutaneous or deep, accessible for excision, small or large, and/or adjacent or even involving vital structures, but wide radical excision is envisaged for the majority of soft tissue sarcomas sited in the extremities, the chest wall, and (in most cases) the intra-abdominal region. The optimal neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemo and/or radiotherapy is tailored to the individual patient and type of malignancy.
