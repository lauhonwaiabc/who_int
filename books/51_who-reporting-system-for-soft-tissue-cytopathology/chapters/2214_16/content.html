2.2.1.4 In situ hybridization

FISH is a well-established in situ hybridization (ISH) method to visualize and diagnose numerical and structural abnormalities in chromosomal DNA. It is widely used in clinical laboratories as a gold standard for identifying deletions, amplifications, and rearrangements of selected genes {27479335; 33489824}. The advantage of FISH lies in the low number of tumour cells needed and the fact that alterations can be detected specifically within the neoplastic cells of interest.

Technical requirements

FISH is a robust method that is applicable to almost all types of cytopathological specimens. Targeted evaluation of cells of interest is a prerequisite for reliable FISH results. FISH requires as few as 50–100 tumour cells and permits direct correlation with cytomorphology {27479335}. Internal validation of FISH assays is necessary, and external quality assurance is recommended {29396253; 25170637; 29755669}. All steps of the procedure should be carried out according to the manufacturer’s specifications (with some adjustments appropriate to smaller biopsy specimens and preparations), using probes approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or other national regulatory body {28743163; 32401054}. Any deviation requires separate validation. The use of adhesive slides with positive electrostatic charge is recommended to prevent cell loss during pretreatment and DNA denaturation {29494220}. FISH analysis requires a fluorescent microscope with appropriate filters, ideally equipped with an automated stage for precise relocalization of cells of interest. Scoring of FISH signals can be done at the fluorescent microscope or based on high-quality compressed 2D images from z-stacks at the computer screen {23689429}. Probes for chromogenic ISH for MDM2 gene amplification are helpful in the diagnostics of lipomatous neoplasias {25132285}, whereas this method is less suitable for the detection of gene fusions.

Management of types of cytopathological specimens

A wide range of cytopathological preparations have been used for FISH, including unstained, Pap-stained, Giemsa-stained, and H&E-stained smears, cytospins, and LBC preparations, as well as unstained formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cell blocks {27479335; 28743163; 30600323; 23536384}. Cell blocks are usually preferred for FISH analysis, because they can be handled like surgical pathology specimens. However, the quality of DNA is compromised by formaldehyde fixation due to cross-linking and chemical modifications of nucleotides {30600323; 29494220; 23536384}. The wide variety of cell block processing protocols introduces an additional factor of variability {32401054; 30600323}. Besides the lack of formalin fixation, the main advantage of performing FISH on non–cell block cytopathology specimens is that the tumour cells are complete rather than sectioned, and they therefore have intact, non-truncated nuclei containing the true number of signals {27479335; 29494220; 23536384; 24648382}. FISH is also feasible on previously immunostained ethanol-fixed cytopathological slides, for example TTF1-stained slides, as long as autofluorescent diaminobenzidine is avoided as a chromogen {16778285}.

Validation is needed in each laboratory to control for potentially interfering factors due to variability in staining and fixation protocols. At least 50 tumour cells should be evaluated, avoiding 3D tissue fragments with overlapping nuclei and areas with necrosis. The best-suited area for FISH analysis should be selected on the stained smears, allowing for efficient use of the probe {27479335; 29494220; 23536384; 30731462}. When using the only diagnostic slide in a particular case, capturing images of selected areas or digitally scanning the whole slide prior to the procedure is recommended. When testing for rearrangements, a result is considered positive when > 15% of tumour cells either have isolated 3′ signals in the deletion pattern or have break-apart patterns. However, results within the borderline range (15–20% of cells) should be interpreted with caution because of possible false positive results due to technical factors {23207439}.

Fixation

Cytospins and direct smears are particularly well suited for FISH techniques, because the retained 3D nature of the intact nuclei allows the evaluation of the ISH signals, devoid of the truncation artefact typical for cut slides of cell blocks or biopsy samples, and they provide evenly spread samples with isolated, non-overlapping nuclei, which are easily accessible for scoring {16645199}. Alcohol-based fixation or air-drying offers better preservation of nucleic acids for hybridization than formalin fixation and paraffin embedding. While formalin fixation is standard for surgical pathology specimens, alcohol fixatives are mostly used for non–cell block cytopathological preparations. The quality of DNA in air-dried or alcohol-fixed cytopathological samples is actually better than that following formaldehyde fixation {27479335; 28743163; 29494220; 23536384; 24648382}. Fixatives containing acidic components, such as Bouin solution or acidic decalcification solutions, have been shown to yield suboptimal FISH results {27562496}.

Controls

Running external or on-slide control specimens is not required for FISH. The presence of signals in all cell nuclei in a specimen serves as the internal control for good hybridization efficiency.

Procedures

The most widely used method of ISH is the application of fluorescent labels for the nucleic acid probes (i.e. FISH), and this is particularly well suited for the detection of gene translocations using break-apart and fusion probes, as well as for enumeration of gene signals, including gene loss, deletion, gain, and amplification. Chromogenic or silver ISH may also be applied to cytopathological specimens in selected situations {20661016}, including for soft tissue tumours, especially in the context of the detection of gene amplification rather than gene rearrangement {25132285; 34019299}.

The steps involved in FISH include slide pretreatment, denaturation of the probe and the slide to obtain single-stranded DNA, hybridization, post-hybridization washes, nuclear DNA counter-staining, and examination and scoring {27479335; 31236139}. There are different protocols for FISH analysis in different types of cytopathological specimens. If smears or cytospins are used, adhesive-coated slides are recommended, to avoid tumour cells floating off during the procedure. In cytopathological specimens, the target cells for FISH evaluation may be sparse and strongly intermixed with benign cells; therefore, in some cases it may be difficult to differentiate benign from malignant cells on the DAPI-counterstained slide after hybridization. This can be resolved by using a relocation software coupled with an automated stage and photo documentation of the cells before uncovering and hybridization of the specimen. This approach improves the sensitivity and specificity of FISH analyses {27479335; 28743163}. There are also fully automated FISH systems for scanning and computer-assisted interpretation of FISH images {25248157; 28838404}.

Diagnostic applications

Depending on the genetic aberration, ISH detects translocations or gene fusions with the application of break-apart or fusion probes, respectively. ISH can also detect gene amplifications, such as MDM2 amplification in atypical lipomatous tumours and well-differentiated and dedifferentiated liposarcomas, as well as duplications, deletions, insertions, and aneusomies. ISH may also demonstrate the presence of genetic material of a pathogen, for example in EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours.

The appropriate choice of FISH probe is suggested by the differential diagnosis based on the cytopathology of the sample, as well as the ICC and the clinical data {18162764; 19851129; 29806089; 22192800; 30600320} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Soft tissue and bone tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 3). https://publications.iarc.who.int/588.]]. The successful application of this approach to FNAB of translocation-associated soft tissue tumours is documented in the literature for several entities, such as Ewing sarcoma {27766786; 32369242}, synovial sarcoma {27774755; 33792887}, alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma {32342940}, myxoid liposarcoma {31042519}, extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma {32828707}, and CIC::DUX-rearranged sarcoma {30353686}.
