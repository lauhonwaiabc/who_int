8.6.3.2 Angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is a malignant vascular neoplasm that variably recapitulates the morphological and immunocytochemical features of endothelial cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Approximately half of all angiosarcomas are cutaneous, and soft tissue angiosarcoma can occur in the lower extremities, trunk, head and neck, breast, bone, retroperitoneum, and viscera {9630175}. A large age range is observed, but these tumours are very rare in children, and peak incidence is during the seventh decade of life {18987547; 1409148}. There is a male predominance.

Patients typically present with a rapidly growing and often painful mass. Some tumours are associated with acute haemorrhage, and a subset of patients present with symptoms such as anaemia, coagulopathy, and haematoma {9630175}.

Imaging features are usually nonspecific, and tumours are often large and show infiltrative growth and frequent necrosis. Tumours show intermediate signal on T1-weighted MRI, with high signal intensity occurring with haemorrhage, and they appear bright on T2-weighted imaging {28471264; 34607656}.

A subset of angiosarcomas arise after radiation exposure or chronic lymphoedema; these cases are associated with high-level MYC amplification {20949568; 20008140}, although MYC amplification also occurs in a small subset of primary angiosarcomas. Rarely, angiosarcoma may arise in association with implanted foreign material, arteriovenous fistulas, pre-existing haemangioma or vascular malformation, or prior trauma or surgery, as well as in certain syndromes, such as neurofibromatosis and Maffucci syndrome {10592050; 3052791; 21659738; 10460655; 12360047}.

Angiosarcomas are highly aggressive, with high rates of local recurrence (~20%) and distant metastases (~50%); more than half of all patients die of disease within 1 year {9630175}.

Histopathology

Angiosarcomas are characteristically infiltrative tumours. Their appearance can range widely in the degree of differentiation and the degree of atypia, but histopathological grade does not appear to predict prognosis. Some angiosarcomas are composed of well-formed, anastomosing vessels lined by enlarged nuclei. The presence of endothelial multilayering, “hobnailing” (where individual angiosarcoma cells bulge into the lumen), and complex dissecting growth aids in recognition, especially for subtle low-grade cases. Many tumours frequently show high-grade features with a sheet-like or solid growth of epithelioid or spindle cells, and there are often areas of anastomosing or ramifying vascular channels. Tumour cell nuclei show atypia, although marked pleomorphism is rare. Nuclei are typically large, and the chromatin is cleared or “vesicular”, with prominent nucleoli and often abundant cytoplasm that is eosinophilic or pale. Mitotic activity and necrosis are common, especially in high-grade tumours. Some cases show extensive haemorrhage, organizing haematoma, or blood lakes. Epithelioid angiosarcoma is composed of diffuse sheets of epithelioid or polygonal cells, usually with only rare foci of vasoformation {1718176}.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis is broad and depends on the features. Tumours with spindle and epithelioid cytomorphology may resemble malignant melanoma, and haemosiderin pigment may be mistaken for melanin. Angiosarcoma may also resemble carcinoma, especially epithelioid angiosarcoma, in which keratin staining is frequent. Clinical correlation and careful examination for any vascular architecture and vasoformative features are helpful.

It may be challenging to distinguish angiosarcoma from epithelioid haemangioendothelioma (EHE), because these tumour types share many cytomorphological features, including spindle and epithelioid morphology, round nuclei, eccentric cytoplasm, and intracytoplasmic vacuoles. EHE is more likely to show myxohyaline stroma and a more uniform cell population, with mild to moderate nuclear atypia.

Ancillary testing

Angiosarcomas are positive for the endothelial markers CD31, CD34, and ERG {21317715}; however, these markers do not distinguish angiosarcoma from other vascular neoplasms. Angiosarcoma may stain for keratin, and this is most frequent in epithelioid angiosarcoma (50%) {11014572}. Radiation-associated angiosarcomas and lymphoedema-associated angiosarcomas show nuclear MYC staining secondary to MYC amplification {20949568; 20008140}. The differential diagnosis includes melanomas, which are positive for S100, SOX10, HMB45, and melan-A (MART1), and epithelioid sarcoma, which like angiosarcoma shows positivity for CD34, ERG, and keratin, but is characterized by loss of SMARCB1 (INI1). EHE also expresses endothelial markers, but it is defined by the CAMTA1::WWTR1 fusion, resulting in ICC nuclear staining for CAMTA1, which is highly sensitive and specific for EHE {26414223}.
