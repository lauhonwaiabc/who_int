4.5.2.1 Lipoma

Definition

Lipomas are benign tumours composed of mature adipocytes.

Clinical features and imaging

Lipomas are the most common mesenchymal tumours. They present as painless lesions of variable size in the superficial soft tissue, within skeletal muscle, or rarely within visceral organs and bone. In large lesions or those in the deep retroperitoneum, an atypical lipomatous tumour / well-differentiated liposarcoma (ALT/WDLPS) must be excluded {29493968; 34958506}. Lipomas occur most commonly in adults aged 40–60 years and rarely in children, in whom lipomas and lipoblastoma represent < 10% of soft tissue tumours {34958506}. Some lipoma subtypes are associated with characteristic findings or locations, such as spindle cell / pleomorphic lipomas, with a predilection for the posterior shoulder and neck {15637670}, and angiolipomas, which are commonly painful and arise on the extremities {22935891}. Rare familial cases or cases in genetic syndromes associated with lipomas or lipomatosis have been described, such as in Gardner syndrome and Madelung disease, in addition to cases associated with medications such as HAART for the treatment of HIV infection {9422314; 18087023}. Patients with Dercum disease or adiposis dolorosa typically have multiple painful lipomas. On imaging, lipomas typically show a well-circumscribed uniform lesion outlined by a greater density of the soft tissue around the lesion. On CT, hypodense areas are typically indicative of a lipomatous tumour.

Histopathology

Lipomas are well-circumscribed lesions with uniform mature adipocytes displaying univacuolated clear cytoplasm and bland nuclei without appreciable nucleoli. Small thin- and thick-walled blood vessels can be identified, in addition to relatively thin fibrous septa. Various histopathological subtypes have been described, including spindle cell / pleomorphic, chondroid, angiolipoma, myolipoma, angiomyolipoma, myelolipoma, fibrolipoma, adenolipoma, ossifying, and myxolipoma. Lipoma subtype can also be defined by anatomical location, such as intramuscular lipoma.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Although the diagnosis of a benign lipoma can be made on cytopathology, there should be careful investigation for atypical features, in addition to correlation with clinical and imaging features, before rendering such a diagnosis {12920746}.

The main differential diagnosis is fibroadipose tissue near another lesion of interest that has not been sampled, and clinical and imaging correlation is mandatory. Fat necrosis, lipoblastoma, lipogranuloma, and hibernoma are other benign fat lesions to exclude. Fat necrosis shows tissue fragments of dead adipocytes lacking nuclei, with punctate debris and occasional histiocytes. Lipoblastoma often contains mature adipocytes and lipoblasts, and it is associated with myxoid stroma. Lipogranulomas consist of epithelioid histiocytes and vacuolated histiocytes containing fat, some multinucleated, with occasional lymphocytes. Histiocytic lesions such as xanthoma and xanthelasma and neoplasms and disorders such as Erdheim–Chester disease can also mimic lipomas, given they may exhibit only mild nuclear atypia and contain vacuolated cells {22279491}. The distinction between lipomas and ALT/WDLPS relies on the site and imaging findings, although ALT/WDLPS may show a more prominent fibrovascular pattern in tissue fragments and potentially multivacuolated and univacuolated lipoblasts with variably hyperchromatic nuclei. Spindle cell lipomas overlap with other spindle cell lesions of the soft tissue (e.g. myofibroblastoma and desmoid-type fibromatosis {30850230; 34958506; 28152270}) and fat-forming or “lipomatous” solitary fibrous tumour {11014579}. Less common entities included in the differential diagnosis are silicon granuloma and neoplasms with clear cell features, such as signet-ring cell carcinoma, balloon cell melanoma, and perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa).

Ancillary testing

By ICC, lipomas are positive for S100. CD34 can be positive in spindle cell / pleomorphic lipomas, and it may highlight the thin-walled vasculature in routine lipomas. Most lipomas show abnormalities of chromosome 12, including trisomy 12, or structural rearrangements of HMGA1 (chromosome 6p21-p23) or HMGA2 (chromosome 12q13-q15). Less common alterations of chromosome 13 have been reported, including rearrangements in angiolipomas and deletions of chromosome 13q, which is more common in spindle cell lipomas. ICC showing nuclear PLAG1 positivity or PLAG1 FISH studies can be helpful in the diagnosis of lipoblastomas, seen more commonly in children {32692992}. MDM2 amplification is a feature of atypical lipomatous lesions, such as ALT/WDLPS and dedifferentiated liposarcoma, and it should be negative in lipomas {27508976}. Rare examples of MDM2 positivity in hibernomas have been reported {32815035}. Specific markers such as STAT6 for solitary fibrous tumour can help exclude other tumours with lipoma-like features.
