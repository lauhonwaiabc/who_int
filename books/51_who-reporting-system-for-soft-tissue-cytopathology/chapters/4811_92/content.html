4.8.1.1 Soft tissue chondroma

Definition

Soft tissue chondroma is a benign neoplasm composed of chondrocytes that produce hyaline or myxoid cartilage, arising in the extraosseous and extrasynovial soft tissues.

Clinical features and imaging

Soft tissue chondroma most commonly arises in the distal extremities, with the hands and feet being the most common sites {17375540; 3957252}. There are extremely rare examples reported in unusual sites such as the head and neck region {19349148}, dura {25250651}, and oral cavity {19476101}. Chondromas of the digit are typically centred in the periarticular tissues. Affected individuals tend to be adults (middle-aged or older), and there is a slight male predominance, with an M:F ratio of 1.5:1. The tumours present clinically as slow-growing, painless masses and tend to remain small, typically < 30 mm. Depending on the extent of intralesional calcification, chondromas may or may not be visible on conventional X-ray. On MRI, they appear as circumscribed nodules that are bright on T2-weighted sequences {16377482}.

Histopathology

Soft tissue chondromas are typically lobulated or multilobulated, with sharp borders, and they are surrounded by a fibrous pseudocapsule. Individual chondrocytes reside in lacunae and are plump with a small, dark nucleus. Mild to moderate nuclear atypia and pleomorphism may be present, but mitotic activity is usually very limited, and atypical mitoses are absent {76505; 3957252}. There may be focal calcification of the matrix. In some instances, there may be a pattern of calcification surrounding individual chondrocytes, imparting a chondroblastoma-like appearance {11342780}. Degenerative changes such as myxoid or cystic degeneration are common. Individual chondrocytes may show subtle atypia and bi-nucleation, but multinucleated cells are typically not identified.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Due to the frequent proximity of chondroma to bones and joints of the phalanges, the differential diagnosis includes a number of periosteal-based lesions that frequently arise in the digits, including florid reactive periostitis and bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (Nora lesion). Both of these lesions occasionally show chondroid or chondromyxoid matrix, but this is seldom a predominant feature. Overall cytopathological and histopathological features are shared with a number of benign cartilage-forming tumours, including enchondroma, periosteal chondroma, and synovial chondromatosis. Enchondroma of the digits in particular is relatively common. Therefore, correlation with imaging studies is crucial to confirm that the lesion is located within the soft tissues and exclude tumours based in the medullary cavity, attached to the surface of the bone, or within the synovium. Chondrosarcoma of soft tissue displays more pronounced cellularity, mitotic activity, and cytopathological atypia and pleomorphism.

Ancillary testing

Soft tissue chondroma stains positively for S100. A gene fusion involving FN1 and FGFR1 or FGFR2 has been identified by both FISH and RNA sequencing in a subset of soft tissue chondromas {31273315}. Usually the diagnosis is apparent, and ancillary studies are unnecessary.
