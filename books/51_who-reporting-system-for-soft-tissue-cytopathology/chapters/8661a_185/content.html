8.6.6.1A Extraskeletal osteosarcoma

Definition

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma (EO) is a primary soft tissue malignant neoplasm characterized by the production of bone or osteoid matrix by neoplastic cells and arises without connection to the skeletal system.

Clinical features and imaging

EO accounts for < 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas and occurs in late adulthood, with a slight male predominance. Clinically, EO appears as a progressively enlarging, deep-seated soft tissue mass that may be associated with pain. The lower extremity is the most common site, followed by the shoulder, trunk, and retroperitoneum. About 10% of EOs may be associated with prior radiotherapy {3475157; 2160317; 8635029; 9591729; 6571801; 18521290; 24382730; 346199; 28167373; 25294959; 27386494; 19154266; 27561385; 30232644; 24401667}. Conventional imaging, CT, and MRI reveal a deep-seated soft tissue mass with variable mineralization, which in some cases prevents effective FNAB {18242029}.

Histopathology

The osteoblastic type is composed of osteoblasts that are spindle or polygonal, with variable mitotic activity and possible necrosis {3475157; 8635029; 9591729}. Well-differentiated EO shows abundant bone formation surrounded by spindle cells {1929787; 15892008}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

High cellularity Variably round, epithelioid, or spindle atypical osteoblasts with eccentric cytoplasm Benign or malignant-looking giant cells of osteoclastic type Eosinophilic (Pap) or orange osteoid (Giemsa) Magenta chondroid matrix (Giemsa) Frequent vessels Bone-producing myxoid matrix may be present Necrosis may be present

Discussion and differential diagnosis

An accurate diagnosis relies on the presence of the characteristic osteoblasts and osteoid or bone-producing matrix {17427216; 9678733; 12203867}. Bone formation and polymorphous sarcomatous cells, including osteoblasts and osteoclasts, are indicative of EO. EO should be differentiated from skeletal osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, myositis ossificans, and dedifferentiated liposarcoma. The distinction between EO and bone osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and myositis ossificans may be difficult. Chondrosarcomas may mimic EO, especially the chondroblastic subtype. Myositis ossificans is characterized by a zonal phenomenon, whereas EO displays an inverted zonal phenomenon, hence the importance of architectural characterization to differentiate both entities. Imaging in this context is of special value, because plain films and CT may help in the diagnosis, with a typical pattern of irregular peripheral calcification. Dedifferentiated liposarcoma is composed of pleomorphic spindle or multinucleated cells with cytoplasmic vacuolization and rare lipoblasts, but osteoblasts and bone formation are absent. A small cell EO should be differentiated from Ewing sarcoma {22180234; 24711902}.

Ancillary testing

No specific diagnostic molecular pathology tests are available. SATB2 immunoreactivity may be used to confirm osteoblastic differentiation {23701429; 28235629}.
