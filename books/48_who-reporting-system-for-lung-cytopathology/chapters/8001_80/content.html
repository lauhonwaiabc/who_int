8.0.0.1 Introduction

Most carcinomas of the lung present at an advanced stage, and the majority of advanced lung cancers are diagnosed utilizing limited tissue samples, including cytopathology and forceps or core needle biopsy {21252716}. The cytopathology specimens employed for the diagnosis of lung malignancies include FNAB and exfoliative specimens, including sputum samples, bronchial washing, bronchial brushing, and (rarely) bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). The FNAB of mass lesions can be guided by endobronchial ultrasound or CT. Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) allows for ensuring specimen adequacy during the procedure, helps to avoid the need for repeat tissue sampling, and allows for best practice and cost-effective triaging of specimens {23429365; 22071327; 21266922; 25931443}.

Precise characterization of malignancies is essential because patient management with targeted therapy is based on the WHO classification tumour type and molecular biomarker profile. This has been driven by major advances in the personalized treatment of advanced non-small cell lung cancer with various tyrosine kinase inhibitors for tumours with sensitizing genetic alterations of EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK, BRAF, and MET, as well as the recent addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors {26291008; 30285222; 33671873; 27718847}. Attaining this goal of a precise diagnosis may be challenging in limited tissue samples of primary or metastatic lung tumours because of the small amount of tumour or the low neoplastic cell fraction in small samples and/or poor tumour differentiation. Therefore, it is important to employ a multidisciplinary strategy to obtain adequate tumour cellularity for pathological diagnosis, to confirm histopathological type by a limited panel of ICC markers and then perform molecular biomarker testing {28659503; 30572031}. Molecular testing including next-generation sequencing can be successfully performed on cytopathology samples {21266922; 29656758; 31021538}. Obtaining multiple biopsy and cytopathology samples during the same procedure helps in prioritizing and optimizing the use of different tissue samples for ancillary testing.

A classification for lung cancer diagnosis in non-resection specimens, which are mostly cytopathology and core needle biopsy specimens, was recommended in the 2015 WHO classification and updated in the 2021 edition. This has provided a workflow for managing small biopsy and cytopathology samples to make a specific diagnosis of primary lung adenocarcinoma by ICC and molecular testing {21252716}. It is recommended to limit the use of ICC and/or mucin stains to diagnose and subtype non-small cell lung cancer, so as to preserve tumours cells for subsequent molecular analysis.
