3.1.0.1 FNAB techniques and specimen management

FNAB is a frequently used and indispensable diagnostic technique in the current practice of pulmonary medicine (see #24197Table 2.01). Broadly, there are two approaches: the percutaneous transthoracic biopsy and the transbronchial (or transtracheal) biopsy performed at bronchoscopy. The former is done under ultrasound guidance in case of subpleural lesions or usually under CT guidance if the lesion is deeply situated {14586042; 16138366}. The transbronchial or endobronchial FNAB are performed through a flexible bronchoscope using fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance, the latter known as endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB) {23649436; 26113224; 19124238}. In addition to endobronchial and peribronchial lesions, EBUS-TBFNAB can also access enlarged mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes or mass lesions {28511234; 26034060}. Furthermore, electromagnetic navigation, ultrathin bronchoscopy, and radial probe endobronchial ultrasound are other technologies that assist in the sampling of small and peripherally placed lung lesions {25849298; 17360724; 33385003}. To further increase the diagnostic yield of sampling, the most recent technological advancement is robotic bronchoscopy, which enables better acquisition of the target and hence a potentially improved diagnostic yield of the procedure {32822675; 32103596; 31072355}. FNAB samples a broader area of the lesion, because the needle can be directed in multiple directions into the lesion, in contrast to the unidirectional sampling of core needle biopsy procedures.

Processing of FNAB samples

All pulmonary FNABs are carried out by either a radiologist or an interventional physician, under imaging guidance. It is preferable if a cytopathology biomedical scientist or a cytopathologist is also present on site to provide rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) and liaise with the physician, including viewing imaging. After FNAB, the material is expelled onto a glass slide and smeared. The material can be split between several slides. Air-dried smears can be used for ROSE using Giemsa staining, while other smears for Pap staining should be immediately and rapidly immersed in 95% ethyl alcohol as soon as they are prepared, to avoid air-drying artefact. A rapid Pap or H&E can also be used for ROSE. The needles and syringes, if they contain material or blood, should be immediately rinsed (along with any repeat FNAB) into an appropriate collecting medium for cell blocks. The cell blocks can be made as per individual laboratory-based standardized protocols. Additional FNAB passes can be requested if a satisfactory specimen has not been obtained or to provide more material for ancillary studies based on the ROSE {28639854; 8157740; 30153355; 25113785}.

Complications

The most common complication of percutaneous FNAB is pneumothorax, which has been reported to occur in 0–61% of cases (varying with the study definition of pneumothorax and other factors), and this has required intervention in 3–15% of cases. Other potential complications include pulmonary haemorrhage (in ~5–15% of cases) and haemoptysis (in ~1–5%) {14586042}. The use of a large-diameter needle (i.e. > 22 gauge) rather than a finer-gauge needle that defines FNAB, as well as obtaining FNABs of small and deeply located lesions, increases the risk of postprocedure complications {27108299} including bleeding and the small risk of neoplastic cell implantation {16305838}. Complications related to endobronchial ultrasound procedures are very low. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 1299 patients, major complications due to EBUS-TBFNAB occurred in 0.07% patients in the form of pneumothorax and hypoxaemia {19124238}.
