5.2.0.9 Spindle cell tumours

Definition

Benign spindle cell tumours and related spindle cell tumours that may have a low risk of recurrence or metastasis include a broad range of tumours in which bland spindle cells predominate or are the sole cell type.

Clinical features and imaging

Most spindle cell tumours arising in the lung do not have distinctive clinical or imaging features. In the thoracic cavity, most schwannomas arise in the posterior mediastinum in the paravertebral region. Schwannomas can also occur in other mediastinal compartments and the pleura {25595633}. Schwannomas typically occur as slow-growing solitary tumours. Most are asymptomatic and are detected on imaging or at autopsy.

Histopathology

Conventional schwannomas are spindle cell neoplasms showing alternating areas of the compact Antoni A pattern and the hypocellular, more open Antoni B pattern. Areas of nuclear palisading, also known as Verocay bodies, may be seen. The spindle cells have pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and ovoid or buckled nuclei with tapered ends. Some tumours may show cytoplasmic nuclear pseudoinclusions, mild pleomorphism, and mitotic activity. Ancient schwannomas feature scattered atypical or bizarre nuclei with dark, smudgy chromatin, considered a degenerative change.

Pulmonary hamartomas have fibromyxoid stroma along with chondroid stroma and entrapped bronchial epithelium.

Fibromatoses, also known as desmoid tumours, have long, sweeping fascicles of bland fibroblasts and myofibroblasts without cytopathological atypia.

Leiomyomas consist of cells closely resembling normal smooth muscle cells, with eosinophilic cytoplasm and uniform blunt-ended, cigar-shaped nuclei, arranged in orderly intersecting fascicles.

Solitary fibrous tumours consist of haphazardly arranged spindle to ovoid cells with pale eosinophilic, indistinct cytoplasm, within a variably collagenous stroma that may be myxoid, admixed with branching and hyalinizing staghorn-shaped or haemangiopericytomatous blood vessels, and in some cases they may have a high mitotic count, with or without increased cellularity, atypia, necrosis, and an infiltrative growth pattern.

Inflammatory fibroblastic tumours have variable amounts of three patterns: a myxoid pattern of plump or spindle myofibroblasts in an oedematous background with plasma cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophils, mimicking granulation tissue; a hypercellular pattern with a fascicular spindle cell proliferation; and a collagenized stromal hypocellular pattern.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Benign spindle cell tumours usually show low cellularity, with spindle cells predominantly in small tissue fragments that may show a fascicular architecture or myxoid change or a variable amount of collagenous material separating the spindle cells, which contain bland elongated nuclei. Dispersed single spindle cells are less prominent. In FNAB cytopathology, most of these cases will not have sufficiently distinctive features for a specific diagnosis; they will be reported as “benign spindle cell tumour”, and ICC on cell blocks may allow a more specific diagnosis. Mitoses and necrosis are not seen, except in rare cases of solitary fibrous tumour, and their presence should suggest that the tumour may be a related malignant spindle cell tumour, including spindle cell variants of lung carcinoma and mesothelioma, or a metastasis.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Spindle cell lesions encountered in lung FNAB include a broad spectrum of inflammatory lesions, benign neoplasms, and neoplasms that can recur or rarely metastasize, and they have to be distinguished (if possible) from high-grade spindle cell tumours, which are most commonly metastases of soft tissue neoplasms. The differential diagnosis of spindle cell neoplasms is assisted by correlation with imaging and negative microbiological studies and by finding few or no inflammatory cells.

Some lesions and neoplasms may be specifically diagnosed, particularly with the assistance of ancillary testing, whereas others will simply have inflammatory or spindle cell material that correlates with the imaging and clinical findings and is regarded as benign. In other cases, the FNAB material may show significant atypical cytopathological features, such as high spindle cell cellularity, nuclear atypia, necrosis, and mitoses, and be categorized as “Atypical”. If a spindle cell tumour that can occasionally recur or rarely metastasize (e.g. solitary fibrous tumour) is specifically diagnosed, this is best categorized as “Atypical” or “Suspicious for malignancy”, but the specific diagnosis guides management.

Inflammatory and reactive processes that produce spindle cells include a wide range of infections and organizing fibrotic lesions, and the number of associated acute or chronic inflammatory cells (including plasma cells, eosinophils, and multinucleated giant cells) correlated with the clinical history, imaging, special stains (for specific bacterial, fungal, or mycobacterial infections), and microbiological findings can be diagnostic {11510004; 11391606}. Granulation tissue and fragments of often partially crushed alveolar septa may appear to consist mainly of spindle cells. Fibrosing mediastinitis may also be considered, depending on the FNAB site {9285187}.

Schwannomas show cohesive tissue fragments, with fibrillary, fibrotic, and/or myxoid stroma sometimes forming rough meshworks of intersecting stroma in a clean background, with focal palisading of the spindle, wavy, or fish-hook–like nuclei with pointed ends, fine chromatin, smooth and regular nuclear membranes, and inconspicuous nucleoli and no mitoses, and bipolar spindle cytoplasm. Ancient schwannomas may show scattered hyperchromatic pleomorphic nuclei {16680779; 25641870; 9884818; 6702462; 11135474; 10319235}. Schwannomas may be difficult to distinguish from other spindle cell processes and neoplasms on cytopathological smears alone, and correlation with the clinical history, imaging, and site is required; the use of ICC on cell block and core needle biopsies is a useful adjunct {30457183; 9285187}.

Pulmonary hamartomas may have a spindle cell component along with the fibromyxoid stroma and chondroid stroma and usually bronchial epithelial tissue fragments.

Fibromatoses (also known as desmoid tumours) show low but variable cellularity, spindle cells separated by magenta stroma (Giemsa) arranged in cohesive long fascicular fragments, a small number of single bland spindle fibroblasts/myofibroblasts with tapering elongated cytoplasm, oval to elongated nuclei with evenly dispersed fine chromatin, and rare inflammatory cells {8498134; 17477374}. Mitoses may occasionally be seen, but no atypical mitoses are present.

Leiomyomas in FNAB smears show variable cellularity, irregularly contoured cohesive tissue fragments of variable cellularity, and often a parallel arrangement of spindle cells with moderate amounts of relatively dense cytoplasm with distinct boundaries. The bland ovoid nuclei have blunt ends (cigar-shaped nuclei); fine, even chromatin distribution; and in some cases, occasional mitoses and focal nuclear atypia with enlargement and pleomorphism {11510004; 20967877}. Necrosis or increased mitotic activity are not present {27684974}. Differentiating a leiomyoma from normal muscular tissue may be difficult on cytopathological material, because many leiomyomas are not hypercellular and have low mitotic activity {27684974}.

Solitary fibrous tumour FNAB yields low to moderate cellularity, reflecting the histopathological spectrum of hyalinized to more cellular, classic haemangiopericytoma tumours. The relatively discohesive cellular tissue fragments have irregular fascicles consisting of bland spindle cells with often pale indistinct cytoplasm and a fusiform nucleus, finely dispersed chromatin, and an inconspicuous nucleolus {31016889}. Collagenous stroma is prominent in some tissue fragments. These tumours have been reported to recur or metastasize on rare occasions, and the pathology is not predictive of behaviour {28914981; 20209623}.

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour shows scant cellularity varying to hypercellularity, reflecting the histopathological spectrum, from myxoid or hyaline stroma containing loosely arranged spindle or stellate cells with variable inflammatory cells, through to a compact proliferation of spindle cells arranged in a storiform architecture, and then hypocellular sclerotic fibrotic tumours with scattered spindle and inflammatory cells. The cells vary from bland fibroblast-like cells, to spindle cells, to epithelioid cells with prominent nuclear atypia and prominent nucleoli, and the tumours can be rich in plasma cells and lymphocytes. Necrosis or marked mitotic activity are not present {29673102; 27087029; 20730898}. Very occasionally these tumours have been reported to recur or metastasize {21472870}.

Ancillary testing

All schwannomas show diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining for S100, particularly in Antoni A areas. SOX10 nuclear staining is usually extensive {18636017; 22495377}. GFAP and CD34 staining is variable. Mediastinal schwannomas can show staining for cytokeratin AE1/AE3, often extensively {16357842}.

Leiomyomas are positive for SMA and desmin and negative for S100, KIT (CD117), DOG1, BCL2, β-catenin, and ALK.

Desmoid fibromatoses usually show patchy SMA expression and rarely are reactive for desmin, whereas nuclear β-catenin expression is usually seen secondary to CTNNB1 or APC mutations in as many as 75% of cases, particularly most sporadic cases {17711447; 18832571; 20197769; 11241320; 8150351}. CD34, DOG1, S100, and KIT (CD117) are negative.

Solitary fibrous tumours are typically positive for CD34, CD99, BCL2, EMA, SMA, and nuclear STAT6 immunoreactivity {23313954; 23313952; 28914981}, which may be lost in dedifferentiated tumours. S100, DOG1, and KIT (CD117) are negative.

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours show variable positivity for SMA, calponin, and desmin; positivity for pankeratin in less than one third of cases; and negativity for CD34, S100, and DOG1. Cytoplasmic immunoreactivity for ALK is distinctive and detectable in 50–60% of cases, and it correlates well with the presence of ALK gene rearrangement {17938159; 11684952; 11485898; 25723109}. ALK-negative inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours occur in adult patients and have greater nuclear pleomorphism, atypia, and atypical mitoses {17414097}. Microbiological studies and special stains are negative.
