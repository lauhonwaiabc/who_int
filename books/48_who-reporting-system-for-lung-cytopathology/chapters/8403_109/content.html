8.4.0.3 Primary germ cell tumours of the mediastinum

Definition

Primary germ cell tumours of the mediastinum include seminoma and non-seminomatous carcinomas, including yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma and mixed germ cell tumour, and have the same morphology and ICC as primary gonadal germ cell tumours.

Seminoma is a malignant neoplasm composed of cells resembling primordial germ cells.

Embryonal carcinoma is a malignant neoplasm composed of large primitive cells of epithelial appearance, resembling cells of the embryonic germ disc.

Yolk sac tumours can occur in extragonadal sites, such as the mediastinum, as a primary or metastatic tumour.

Choriocarcinoma is a highly malignant trophoblastic neoplasm composed of cytotrophoblast, syncytiotrophoblast, and variably intermediate trophoblast.

Teratoma is a germ cell tumour composed of somatic tissues derived from two or three of the germ layers; it may be mature and composed exclusively of mature, adult-type tissues, or immature, containing immature embryonic or fetal tissues either exclusively or in addition to mature tissues.

Mixed germ cell tumours are neoplasms composed of two or more types of germ cell tumours.

Histopathology

Each of the germ cell tumours can occur in a pure form or in mixed germ cell tumours.

Seminoma is composed of round to polygonal tumour cells with oval, central, non-overlapping nuclei with one or more large central nucleoli, frequently with a lymphoid infiltrate and a granulomatous reaction.

Embryonal carcinoma is composed of solid sheets or tubules or papillae, consisting of large, pleomorphic tumour cells with pleomorphic nuclei and prominent nucleoli, often in a background of haemorrhage and necrosis.

Yolk sac tumours have a variety of morphological growth patterns, most commonly reticular but also including microcystic, macrocystic, solid, glandular/alveolar, endodermal sinus with characteristic Schiller–Duval bodies, papillary, myxomatous, polyvesicular vitelline, hepatoid, and enteric.

Choriocarcinoma consists of cytotrophoblasts and syncytiotrophoblasts typically intermingled in a bilaminar plexiform pattern or in disordered sheets.

Mature teratoma consists of two or three of the mature germ layers throughout the tumour in a haphazard distribution, most commonly skin and cutaneous appendages lining cysts, and also bronchial mucosa, gastrointestinal mucosa, brain tissue, smooth muscle, and adipose tissue. Granulomatous inflammation may be found in association with ruptured cysts.

Immature teratomas contain embryonic tissues derived from the various germ layers such as immature glands, fetal lung, primitive cartilage, rhabdomyoblasts and blastema-like stromal cells, and (most commonly) neuroectodermal tissue.

A germ cell tumour can be accompanied by a non–germ cell or somatic-type malignant component of sarcoma or carcinoma. Somatic-type solid carcinomas and sarcomas occur more commonly in association with mature teratomas and less commonly with immature teratomas, choriocarcinomas, yolk sac tumours, and embryonal carcinomas, but not with seminomas. Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most frequent sarcoma, and carcinomas include adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma. A germ cell tumour can be accompanied by haematological malignancies that are clonally related to the underlying germ cell tumours, although these have not been reported in the cytopathology literature.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Seminoma
  • Moderate to high cellularity with a homogeneous cell population
  • Predominantly dispersed malignant cells, some flat sheets
  • Large cells with fragile cytoplasm and plentiful stripped large nuclei with prominent nucleoli
  • Typical tigroid background consisting of crushed cytoplasm full of glycogen (Giemsa)
  • Numerous small mature lymphocytes and small epithelioid histiocytes in a granulomatous background

Reference(s): {12219784; 7544720; 31568647; 22298374; 9034742}

Embryonal carcinoma
  • Often highly cellular smears
  • Malignant cells arranged in flat sheets or 3D tissue fragments or papillary or acinar tissue fragments
  • Large pleomorphic nuclei with coarse chromatin and prominent nucleoli
  • Numerous mitotic figures, may be atypical
  • Necrosis and haemorrhage in the background

Reference(s): {31568647; 1326777; 9034742}

Yolk sac tumour
  • Loosely cohesive tissue fragments with occasional alveolar-type spaces, glands or microcysts, and stripped nuclei
  • Cytoplasm shows microvacuolization and is fragile
  • Round to irregular nuclei, less uniform than seminomas (except for the hepatoid variant), with plentiful stripped nuclei
  • Cells lining the spaces tend to have more compressed nuclei or endometrioid features
  • Background shows string-like metachromatic material, hyaline globules, and basophilic proteinaceous fluid (Giemsa)
  • Syncytiotrophoblast, granulomas, and prominent lymphocytes have only rarely been described

Reference(s): {12203871; 1281767; 21938171; 17718136}

Choriocarcinoma
  • Background of abundant necrosis and inflammatory cells including neutrophils and lymphocytes
  • Cytotrophoblast and/or intermediate trophoblast is seen as tissue fragments or isolated medium-sized, mononucleated cells with indistinct cell borders, vesicular (Pap) nuclei showing perinucleolar clearing, prominent nucleoli, and moderate pale cytoplasm
  • Syncytiotrophoblast consists of large giant cells with bizarre shapes, hyperchromatic pleomorphic mononucleated or multinucleated nuclei, irregular nuclear membranes, indistinct cell borders, and abundant cytoplasm

Reference(s): {30262237; 28397369; 26712464}

Mature teratoma
  • Usually low cellularity
  • Mature squamous epithelium, mature columnar epithelium, cells of choroid and glial tissue, stromal fragments, and adipose tissue
  • Background may be mucoid owing to mature mucin-producing glands, or thick and proteinaceous with keratinous debris

Reference(s): {18980289; 31568647; 9034742}

Immature teratoma
  • High cellularity, small blue cell tumour
  • Tissue fragments and rosettes
  • Small, round to oval tumour cells
  • Bland nuclei and high N:C ratio
  • Focal neuropil matrix

Reference(s): {18618728; 11747240; 9034742; 9100787}

Mixed germ cell tumours
  • Various types of germ cell tumours in any combination
  • Cytomorphology of each single component is identical to that of the pure form

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Seminoma is the second most common mediastinal germ cell tumour, and a pure seminoma has an excellent prognosis. In FNAB, sampling error may pose a problem as other components of a mixed germ cell tumour may be missed. Elevated levels of serum AFP or β-hCG suggest a diagnosis of mixed germ cell tumour. In hypocellular smears the typical tigroid background can be difficult to identify. The differential diagnosis includes melanoma, poorly differentiated large cell carcinoma, and malignant lymphoma. Metastasis of a testicular primary should always be considered {1326777; 9264352}.

The differential diagnosis of embryonal carcinoma includes poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, thymic carcinoma, and other malignant germ cell tumours. Distinction from metastatic carcinomas requires clinical, imaging, and serological correlation. Elevated levels of AFP and β-hCG suggest an embryonal carcinoma {9034742; 9264353}.

The differential diagnosis of yolk sac tumours includes other subtypes of germ cell tumours, such as yolk sac tumours with a hepatoid pattern or a lymphoid background mimicking seminomas {15767805; 29129333; 17718136; 22261704}. The hyaline globules and ICC help to identify yolk sac tumours {15767805}. Rare forms of yolk sac tumours with a sarcomatoid pattern can occur after prior treatment {29129333}. A glandular pattern in yolk sac tumours can closely resemble the endometrioid, intestinal/enteric, or alveolar morphology seen in other tumours, but adenocarcinomas tend to be more mucinous {24921638}. The ropy metachromatic (Giemsa) material in yolk sac tumours mimics that seen in mesenchymal tumours, renal cell carcinomas, and salivary gland–type tumours {21591276}.

Choriocarcinoma can mimic sarcomatoid carcinomas with giant cell features, but giant cell carcinomas show a continuum between smaller or medium-sized tumour cells and the larger giant cells rather than the dual presence of cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast.

Teratoma is the most common mediastinal germ cell tumour. Mature teratomas are easily diagnosed in cases with squamous cells, columnar cells, stromal fragments, and adipose tissue. If a cystic component of a mature teratoma is sampled, the cyst fluid may be hypocellular and the diagnosis may be missed. A ruptured cyst in a mature teratoma containing squamous epithelium may lead to a granulomatous reaction. Important differential diagnoses are other cystic lesions of the mediastinum. If the diagnosis of a mature teratoma is considered, the slides should be searched carefully for any immature component. The cytopathological findings should be correlated with clinical, imaging, and serological findings. Immature teratoma has a differential diagnosis of a small blue cell tumour, and in the paediatric age group, neuroblastoma, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, Wilms tumour, Ewing sarcoma, and malignant lymphoma have to be excluded {18980289; 11747240}.

Mixed germ cell tumours may be missed in cytopathology if only one of the components is sampled, and thorough sampling of different areas of the tumour, if possible, is required. If two or more subtypes of germ cell tumour are found in FNAB or there are elevated serum markers that are discordant from the cytomorphological findings, a mixed germ cell tumour should be strongly considered. For example, elevated serum AFP in a patient with a diagnosis of seminoma should lead to a review of all slides and correlation to clinical and imaging findings {9100787; 9292737; 9034742}.

Somatic-type solid malignancies found combined with germ cell tumours may not be distinguishable in FNAB cytopathology from metastatic carcinoma or sarcoma from a different primary site {3693570}.

Ancillary testing

See #28332Table 7.10 .

With limited material, a panel including pancytokeratin, OCT3/4, glypican-3 (GPC3), and AFP is recommended. A seminomatous component is positive for OCT3/4 and negative for AFP and GPC3, whereas a yolk sac tumour component is positive for AFP and GPC3 and negative for OCT3/4. KIT (CD117) can stain yolk sac tumours and is not a reliable marker to distinguish yolk sac tumours from seminoma.
