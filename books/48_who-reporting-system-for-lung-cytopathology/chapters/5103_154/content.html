5.1.0.3 Granulomatous disorders

Definition

Granulomatous disorders are entities or lesions containing characteristic granulomas consisting of epithelioid cells and histiocytic multinucleated giant cells with a variable admixture of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils. Necrosis may or may not be present.

Clinical features and imaging

Presenting symptoms vary depending on the primary disorder and are often mild and vague, with low-level fevers, but progress to weight loss and debilitation compounded by cough, haemoptysis, and deteriorating lung function. Imaging also varies with the cause, but commonly CXR and CT show enlarged lymph nodes and variable pulmonary infiltrates, raising the differential diagnosis of the primary tuberculosis primary complex and sarcoidosis. Postprimary tuberculosis shows variable hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, pulmonary infiltrates, and cavities, with or without progressive fibrosis. Sarcoidosis presents in most cases with bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy with or without bilateral perihilar or other pulmonary infiltrates {26288514; 17330195}.

Histopathology

A granuloma is a focal aggregation of epithelioid histiocytes, lymphocytes, and histiocytic giant cells. Their size may vary. Depending on the underlying entity or disease, the granulomas may be discrete or coalescing, have a peripheral lymphoid cuff and/or a peripheral fibrotic zone, have central necrosis, be either caseous or non-caseous in type, and be associated with suppuration. Histiocytic giant cells may also vary in morphology, depending on the arrangement of nuclei within the cell. At times, etiological agents may be identified on routine/special stained sections within or outside the histiocytic giant cells and granulomas {28794143}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Epithelioid histiocytes form distinct granulomatous tissue fragments with a variable number of histiocytic multinucleated giant cells that show grouped uniform nuclei, usually in abundant cytoplasm, and lymphocytes
  • Epithelioid histiocytes have elongated, often indented (so-called barefoot) nuclei; fine chromatin; and inconspicuous nucleoli
  • Cytoplasm is vaguely demarcated and often syncytial in the granulomas
  • If necrosis is present it may be granular and caseous or non-caseous and is usually central
  • There is a variable amount of hyaline sclerosis around the granulomas
  • A variable number of small lymphocytes are present – they may be predominant, whereas eosinophils or neutrophils may be predominant in other cases

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Granulomas occur in a variety of diseases. Infectious agents are an important cause of granulomatous disorders, especially Mycobacterium tuberculosis, atypical mycobacteria, and other bacteria including Francisella tularensis; and fungal infections including Aspergillus spp., Blastomyces, Coccidioides immitis, Cryptococcus, histoplasmosis, mucormycosis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii. Several bacteria and fungi can be identified in routine stains, but the assessment of granulomatous cytopathology material requires appropriate special stains (see #32162Table 4.03) {30156776; 30889574; 27555237; 26800030; 24975566; 25318988}. Correlation with microbiological studies, including cultures and PCR studies, is mandatory where available.

If no infectious cause is found, further workup in correlation with the medical history and imaging is required. Important non-infectious causes include autoimmune diseases, such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (previously known as Wegener granulomatosis) and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (previously known as Churg–Strauss syndrome), foreign body reactions, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and drug-related reactions. A common differential diagnosis in respiratory cytopathology is sarcoidosis, in which clearly demarcated epithelioid granulomas show no necrosis and few multinucleated giant cells. Granulomas with suppuration are seen in granulomatosis with polyangiitis {28794143; 17250597; 30404114; 22679012; 29698718; 24500810}.
