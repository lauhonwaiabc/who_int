5.2.0.11 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a tumour showing neuroectodermal differentiation and is believed to be of Schwann cell origin.

Clinical features and imaging

Granular cell tumours are typically small (< 50 mm). They present mainly within the 30- to 60-year-old age group but can arise at any age, and they show a slight female predominance {9323154; 6330328}. Although they most commonly arise in the tongue, hypopharynx, oesophagus, colon, skin, and breast {28160713; 20066540}, granular cell tumours can arise anywhere in the body, including the lung. Pulmonary granular cell tumours are usually associated with the airways at bifurcation sites, with an endobronchial component in approximately 90% of cases, although rare peripherally located parenchymal tumours have also been described {7755149; 11294991}. Granular cell tumours are almost always benign neoplasms, but malignant granular cell tumours can occur, representing only 1–2% of all granular cell tumours {24300348; 14613555}.

Granular cell tumours are often incidentally detected by CXR or CT, but larger tumours can cause bronchial obstruction and symptoms such as wheezing, cough, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, chest pain, or pneumonia. Imaging may show a postobstructive opacity that may have irregular borders or enhancement on MRI, thus yielding a differential diagnosis of atelectasis, infection, or neoplasia {28160713}. PET is negative for metabolic activity.

Histopathology

The histopathology of granular cell tumours is the same regardless of the site of origin. Tumours are composed of sheets, nests, cords, or trabeculae of distinctive polygonal tumour cells with round central nuclei and abundant granular cytoplasm.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Moderately cellular
  • Loosely cohesive sheets and some single cells
  • Medium-sized to large, monomorphic round to oval polygonal cells
  • Characteristic abundant eccentric eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, which is fragile, leading to spilled granules in the background
  • Granules are seen better on H&E- or Pap-stained smears than on Giemsa-stained smears
  • Liquid-based cytopathology demonstrates more cohesive, smooth-margined, small tissue fragments with indistinct individual cell borders
  • Nuclei are round and are small to medium in size, with occasional small nucleoli
  • Mitotic activity and necrosis are absent, and if necrosis is present the rare malignant granular cell tumour should be considered

Reference(s): {7755149; 29516941; 30582298}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of granular cell tumours in the lung is different to that at other sites and includes carcinoid tumours with oncocytic morphology and pulmonary macrophages, which show more vacuolated cytoplasm, often containing carbon pigment, and do not demonstrate the distinct cytoplasmic coarse granularity that is so characteristic of granular cell tumours. Metastases to the lung of tumours with granular cytoplasm, such as acinic cell carcinoma of the salivary gland or acinar cell carcinoma of the pancreas, warrant consideration {1927169; 1927170}.

Ancillary testing

Granular cell tumours are positive for PAS with and without diastase, and positive by ICC for S100, myelin basic protein (MBP), calretinin, α-inhibin, and CD68. Granular cell tumours are negative for all cytokeratins, desmin, thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), and the endocrine markers chromogranin and synaptophysin. Pulmonary carcinoid tumours are positive for pancytokeratin, endocrine markers, and TTF1. Acinic cell carcinomas also stain with PAS and PASD and may show focal staining for endocrine markers, but they are distinguished by showing complete membranous staining for SOX10 and DOG1. Pancreatic acinar cell carcinomas are positive for pancytokeratin, trypsin, and BCL10. Macrophages stain for CD68 {15214825; 24746209}.
