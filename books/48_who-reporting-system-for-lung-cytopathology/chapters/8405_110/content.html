8.4.0.5 Primary angiosarcoma of the lung

Definition

Primary angiosarcoma of the lung is a very rare high-grade malignant soft tissue tumour with vasoformative features or endothelial differentiation.

Clinical features and imaging

Almost all primary and metastatic angiosarcomas occur in adults. Manifestations include haemoptysis, cough, shortness of breath, and chest pain. In the thorax, angiosarcomas may occur in association with chronic lymphoedema or after radiation exposure, particularly in the setting of prior breast carcinoma {12951681}. Rare cases of angiosarcoma involving the pleura or pericardium have been described {10941336; 2323298}. Most angiosarcomas in the lung represent metastases from previously diagnosed tumours.

The most common imaging finding is that of multiple nodules, often bilateral, which may include calcifications or cavitation. Less often, a solitary nodule or mass is identified. In either case, extension to the pleura is common. Very infrequently, a dominant pattern of air space infiltration is recognized. On MRI, these tumours may appear bright on T1- and T2-weighted images, depending on the amount of haemorrhage.

Histopathology

Angiosarcomas are high-grade neoplasms that need to be distinguished from lower-grade epithelioid haemangioendothelioma. At low magnification, a spectrum of vasoformative features is usually present, from obvious small-calibre vessels to slit-like spaces to large blood-containing spaces, and papillary and micropapillary growth may also be present. But in some instances, a solid architecture is present. The cells may be predominantly epithelioid or spindle or an admixture of the two, and cytoplasmic lumina are characteristic. Nuclei tend to be elongated and show marked hyperchromasia and pleomorphism.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Variable cellularity; may be low if blood lakes or necrotic foci are sampled
  • Single cells, loosely cohesive tissue fragments with irregular outlines, and tissue fragments with irregular vascular spaces
  • Spindle, elongated tumour cells with oval nuclei and indistinct borders can be seen arranged in very poorly formed fascicles in solid areas
  • Epithelioid polygonal tumour cells with moderate to plentiful cytoplasm and more distinct borders; may appear plasmacytoid and have cytoplasmic lumina
  • Nuclei are round, elongated to pleomorphic, with coarse chromatin
  • Characteristically, irregular nucleoli are oval or bar-shaped and possess delicate chromatin extensions connecting to the nuclear membrane, imparting a spider-like nuclear appearance
  • Cytoplasmic vacuoles may contain erythrocytes or segmented leukocytes
  • Haemosiderophages

Reference(s): {10941336; 25195738; 27088896}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes both carcinomas and other sarcomas, especially those with a spindle cell component. The epithelioid forms need to be distinguished from non-small cell carcinomas, especially those with slit-like luminal spaces seen in tissue fragments. Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma has lower-grade nuclei and lacks large irregular nucleoli, whereas abnormal mitotic figures favour angiosarcoma. Melanoma and plasmablastic lymphomas enter the differential diagnosis especially with angiosarcomas that show prominent discohesion and single cells, although these entities show prominent nucleoli that tend to be round and centrally placed.

Ancillary testing

Angiosarcoma expresses endothelial markers, including CD31, CD34, ERG, and FLI1, but may be positive for pankeratin, which is a potential pitfall in the differential diagnosis from carcinoma. Angiosarcoma is negative for CAMTA1, which is typically positive in epithelioid haemangioendothelioma, due to the CAMTA1::WWTR1 translocation.
