9.0.0.1 Introduction

Cytopathology plays a crucial role in the management of patients with symptomatology or imaging findings that suggest lung infection or carcinoma, particularly because lung infections do not require surgery and most patients with lung cancer present with advanced disease where surgical management is not appropriate {30620402}. Cytopathological samples and, where necessary, core needle biopsies or forceps biopsies are often the only material available for diagnosis, ICC, and predictive marker molecular studies. Cytopathology offers a variety of complementary sampling techniques that are often used in combination for the evaluation of patients with lung symptomatology and lesions seen on imaging {21266922; 21841504}. In addition, cytopathological evaluation of intrathoracic lymph nodes utilizing endobronchial ultrasound guidance during bronchoscopy at the same time as FNAB of the mass lesion in the lung is an efficient and safe method for the staging of lung carcinoma, which in turn determines clinical management and prognosis. Evaluation of pleural effusion is also used for determining stage IV in lung cancer.

Appropriate management of cell material, methods, and procedures in patients who are suspected on clinical or imaging findings to have lung cancer is an important task for cytotechnologists and cytopathologists. The aims are the precise cytopathology and ICC diagnosis and accurate assessment of predictive biomarkers, to be achieved in the least invasive and most cost-effective manner, usually in a situation of a limited amount of material {28659503; 32401054; 32439185}. Notably, lung cytopathology does not only aim to diagnose or exclude lung cancer but can also serve as an important method for the rapid and inexpensive diagnosis of infectious diseases in all countries, especially in countries with low levels of medical infrastructure {29412881}. Recently published guidelines on the collection and handling of thoracic small biopsy and cytopathology specimens serve as a useful resource for the management of cytopathological specimens {32401054}.

Effective management of the patient depends not only on categorizing the findings and providing a precise cytopathological diagnosis, but also on the material type, the cellularity of individual specimens, and clinical factors such as general health status and correlation with imaging or microbiological findings {32439185}. The ability of the cytopathologist to communicate with clinicians and proceduralists in the real-time setting of rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE), or by phone or at the multidisciplinary conference, is the key to optimizing management. The multidisciplinary team meeting to discuss the clinical, imaging, and cytopathological findings (including ICC and next-generation sequencing results) of individual patients is a crucial requirement in the best-practice management of patients with lung tumours. Direct access to the clinical and imaging database can also greatly facilitate interpretation and deciding on the next diagnostic steps.

It is important to realize that in many instances, cytopathology, core needle biopsy, or forceps biopsy specimens are sampled during the same procedure, and this requires systematic correlation between these sample types. This necessitates a tight collaboration between cytopathologists and histopathologists. Ideally, one single team of (cyto-)pathologists oversees all respiratory specimens from the same patient. Uncertainty in a cytopathology specimen can often be resolved by clear findings in a concurrent small biopsy, or vice versa.

Once there is an established categorization of “Malignant”, further management aims at ensuring the selection and reporting of the appropriate predictive biomarkers, which will depend on the histopathological lung cancer subtype and tumour stage. In the other categories, the risk of malignancy is the key determinant for defining the further diagnostic strategy and the diagnostic workup for each individual patient.

The WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology includes recommendations for the diagnostic workup of patients with particular categories of cytopathology reports and specific diagnoses. It is recognized that the availability of local expertise and laboratories, as well as radiological and other imaging modalities, will be major determinants in the actual diagnostic workup of individual patients. Local circumstances and resources may well modify these recommendations, and this is most obviously the case in low- and middle-income countries. Therefore, it is not intended that these recommendations are mandatory in any way. It is clear that local practice and cytopathologists may be challenged by the recommendations, and it is expected that this reporting system will generate considerable debate and further research which will improve the overall management of lung disease.

The intention of this chapter is to bring all the recommendations for diagnostic management of each category into one place, so as to assist clinicians and their use of the WHO Reporting System.
