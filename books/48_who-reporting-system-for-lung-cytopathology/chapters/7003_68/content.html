7.0.0.3 Discussion and background

A high degree of accuracy for the diagnosis of malignancy is important in the cytopathology of all body sites, particularly in respiratory cytopathology, where obtaining samples (apart from sputum samples) by bronchoscopy or via the transthoracic approach is relatively invasive, and subsequent unnecessary therapeutic interventions to the chest can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Historically, the categories of “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” have been used to indicate, respectively, a low and a high degree of uncertainty for a diagnosis of malignancy, and to stratify the risk of malignancy (ROM). The category of “Suspicious for malignancy” is used when the cytopathologist encounters an adequate preparation that contains cytopathological features that are inconclusive for an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy but wants to convey a high degree of concern for the presence of a malignant tumour to the clinical team so that further diagnostic management can be planned. The use of this diagnostic category has been cemented further by studies that applied risk stratification for the final diagnosis of a malignant tumour. Several studies have estimated the ROM for the “Suspicious for malignancy” category to be 75–100% for exfoliative samples and 75–88% for FNAB {26990836; 30051969; 32222718; 34319004; 31051765}, whereas the ROM for the “Atypical” category is 62–100% for exfoliative samples and 46–55% for FNAB {30051969; 26331778}. Although the ROM is high, the cytopathological categorization should not be used alone as a substitute for a malignant diagnosis, and further biopsy is recommended. However, when this is correlated with suspicious clinical and imaging findings in a multidisciplinary setting, there may be situations where pulmonary multidisciplinary teams proceed to specific treatment.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category is applicable to a suspicion of any malignant tumour type, including non-small cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), small cell carcinoma, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), lymphoma, sarcoma, and metastatic carcinomas, and the type of tumour or a differential diagnosis of the suspected tumour types should always be stated in the cytopathology report. The category is equally applicable to any cytopathological study, i.e. sputum sampling, bronchial wash, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), bronchial brush, CT-guided transthoracic FNAB, and endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category is usually applied in cases where there is significant cytopathological atypia (including nuclear enlargement; anisonucleosis; irregular nuclear membrane; coarse or hyperchromatic chromatin; and prominent, often large or irregular nucleoli) along with loss of architectural orientation or polarity, nuclear crowding and moulding, and variability in cell size and shape, but where there are only a small number of cells showing these features or the qualitative features are not definitive for a diagnosis of malignancy. Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) has been shown to reduce the rate of “Suspicious for malignancy”, but limiting factors related to the intrinsic cytopathological characteristics of the tumour and technical problems remain {26656954; 28639854; 30195266}.

Necrotic tumours that yield limited intact cell numbers, dense obscuring inflammatory backgrounds, obscuring granulomatous reactions, and tumours with a prominent fibrotic component are examples of intrinsic tumour characteristics that may produce highly atypical samples with scant cellularity independent of the number of FNAB passes or ROSE. There may be overlap of cytopathological features between inflamed reactive epithelium and the sheets of cells with low nuclear atypia found in well-differentiated adenocarcinoma of the lung, which may be categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” owing to a lack of overtly malignant nuclear characteristics. Prior radiation and or chemotherapy, particularly for lung carcinomas, may result in significant atypia in a sample with low cellularity, which mimics and cannot be distinguished from previous carcinoma, and the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is appropriate. The size of the tumour (particularly those measuring < 20 mm), the location of the targeted nodule, and the experience of the interventionalist procuring the biopsy may result in low cellularity and an inability to give an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy {26367186; 12527571; 28066604; 17873165}.

Ancillary techniques, particularly ICC, can assist in making a malignant diagnosis, for example where ICC can confirm metastatic and neuroendocrine carcinomas. But in many cases the quality and quantity of the suspicious cells on direct smears and in cell blocks may prevent further definition of the diagnosis, and a “Suspicious for malignancy” categorization and a statement regarding the type of carcinoma suspected should be included in the report. For example, positive ICC for neuroendocrine markers on a small amount of extensively crushed material in a cell block from a lung FNAB may favour a NET and exclude lymphoma, but there may be insufficient tumour for the proliferation marker Ki-67 to define the tumour grade {30572031; 27998793}. Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) as a marker of lung adenocarcinoma and p40 as a marker of squamous differentiation are often used for further classification of non-small cell carcinomas. However, these markers are also expressed in reactive cells and do not separate benign from malignant.
