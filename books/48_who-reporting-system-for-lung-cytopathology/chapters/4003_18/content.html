4.0.0.3 Discussion and background

The causes of "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic" cytopathology include a paucity of cellular material; extensive necrosis; poor preparation, fixation, or staining; and obscuring of potentially diagnostic material by blood, inflammatory cells, mucus, or other material. The reasons for an "Insufficient" specimen should be documented in the report. If there is any atypical material, however scant, in a cytopathology specimen, even if the slides are otherwise insufficient, the case is immediately categorized as “Atypical” rather than “Insufficient”.

Adequacy assessment in lung cytopathology is critical to rendering an accurate diagnosis and allowing triage for the selection of cost-effective ancillary testing if needed for timely management. However, such assessments are challenging, primarily because of poorly defined adequacy criteria for both exfoliative and FNAB samples. In this context, integration with clinical and imaging findings is required in a triple-test approach, wherever possible. It is recognized that in some low- and middle-income country settings there may not be any imaging available with which to correlate a sputum sample.

Any specimen can be regarded as adequate if the material demonstrates a pathological process consistent with the clinical and imaging findings and can be appropriately categorized as “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant”. However, if nonspecific benign cytopathological material is present on the slides and does not correlate with a suspicious radiological mass or lesion, a malignant process cannot be excluded and the implication is that further biopsy, repeat sampling of the lesion, or other investigation will be required. Cytopathologists can decide to report these cases as “Benign” with a clear description of the material found on the cytopathological preparations, and add a mandatory caveat statement in the report conclusion that “the material may not represent the target lesion on imaging and further biopsy is recommended”, or they can report the material as "Non-diagnostic". Each cytopathologist and institution will determine how they report these cases, depending on local historical usage, the availability of imaging to allow for correlation, and billing concerns related to the terms “Insufficient” and “Inadequate”, and they should be consistent and clearly communicate the result to their clinicians.

The term “Non-diagnostic” is not recommended for use in cases where there is good benign cytopathological material that does not correlate with a pulmonary nodule or other suspicious imaging, and at the same time, “Insufficient” or “Inadequate” is used to categorize cases where there is a lack of cellular material, poor smearing, poor fixation with artefact, and poor staining, or where there is inflammation or other material obscuring the cytopathological material.

The diversity of cell types and tumour types in the respiratory system and the criteria for specimen adequacy will vary according to the specimen type and sampling technique being used and on the availability of rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE). Currently, there are no universal numerical cellular adequacy criteria. In the case of non-small cell lung cancer diagnosis, the specimen also must be adequate for ancillary testing including ICC and next-generation sequencing for predictive biomarker testing.

The semi-quantitative criteria for sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimens have remained the same over the past few decades, but adequacy criteria are difficult to define. The presence of respiratory epithelial cells, alveolar macrophages, or inflammatory cells may not provide a useful assessment of adequacy when the cytopathological material is discordant with the imaging findings, such as a distinct, well-defined lung lesion on CXR {9316777; 26990836}. Therefore, in these settings, a multidisciplinary evaluation of clinical, imaging, and cytopathological features plays a crucial role in assessing sample adequacy.

To recognize and define an "Inadequate" cytopathological specimen, the features of an adequate specimen should be recognized. Specific features to determine adequacy in each of the following specimen types are listed, and it is noted that none require a specific number of cells or cell types.

Sputum

Sputum sample adequacy depends on several factors, including the number of alveolar macrophages, the volume of material processed, the modality of sputum sampling (i.e. whether a spontaneous, induced, or deep-cough specimen is being assessed), and the location and size of the radiologically suspected lesion. An indicator of sputum sample adequacy is a “high number of alveolar macrophages” (although no numerical cut-off point has been defined), along with ciliated columnar bronchial cells {6352461; 18232006} and a recommended sufficient volume to produce at least two smears {9316777; 26990836}. Samples should include at least a few alveolar macrophages and ciliated columnar cells on several slides or in a liquid-based cytopathology (LBC) preparation, and if these are absent or obscured the material is categorized as “Inadequate” {26990836}. A large meta-analysis of classic sputum sample series showed "Insufficient" rates ranging from 0.8% to 29.7%, with an average of 13.8% {1546511}, but this variability is influenced by the lack of agreement in the definition of an adequate sample. In a recent series, statistically significant differences in adequacy rates were observed between spontaneous and induced sputa, with the latter showing a higher number of adequate samples (91.8%) {19852034}. In addition, several studies showed a decreased sensitivity of sputum sampling in the detection of diagnostic cells for patients affected by peripherally located lung masses {12527571}.

Bronchial washing and bronchoalveolar lavage

Bronchial washing (BW) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) have been deemed adequate when they contain > 10 alveolar macrophages per 2 mm2 using a high-power field diameter of 0.5 mm, which is equivalent to the previously reported value of 20 alveolar macrophages per 10 HPF for most modern microscopes {34079071}. BB and BAL specimens are considered "Inadequate" when containing numerous bronchial epithelial cells, the number of which should not exceed the number of alveolar macrophages, or when there is obscuring mucopurulent exudate or blood contamination {3673466; 10405813; 25210231}. BW is preferentially employed for the cytopathological sampling of centrally located lesions, whereas BAL allows sampling of the peripheral lung parenchyma. BAL is usually employed in combination with other cytopathological and histopathological sampling techniques during bronchoscopies, but its diagnostic yield is limited: in a series of 260 bronchoscopies, only 1% of cancer diagnoses would have been missed in the absence of BAL sampling {26831835}. Recent evidence suggests that routine BW cytopathology may not be justified during bronchoscopy procedures {28007409}.

Bronchial brushing

Bronchial brushing (BB) specimens, either as direct smears or in LBC preparations, should include abundant bronchial epithelial cells {10405813}, and macrophages may be present. A lack of bronchial epithelial cells, or poor smearing or fixation of the epithelial component, which is most commonly air-drying artefact of alcohol-fixed Pap-stained smears, is regarded as “Inadequate”. BB cytopathology is often performed at the same time as or after a forceps biopsy (with no difference in the diagnostic yield based on the sequence of sampling), or at times after FNAB {16002962}. Although BB provides the highest diagnostic yield for peripheral lesions beyond the segmental bronchus, the sensitivity of endobronchial FNAB for the sampling of central lesions is reported to be higher than for both BB and BW {12527571}.

Transthoracic FNAB

A transthoracic FNAB should include some alveolar macrophages, which generally contain carbon with or without haemosiderin, and may include bronchial epithelial cells, reactive type 2 pneumocytes, and tissue fragments of collapsed alveolar septa.

Endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB

An endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB) should include reactive bronchial ciliated columnar cells or > 40 lymphocytes in the area of highest cellularity, in greater numbers than a CT-guided transbronchial approach {20015257; 18701418}. Direct smears or LBC preparations are regarded as "Inadequate" when they are too sparsely cellular and lack alveolar macrophages or bronchial epithelial cells, or when they are too poorly smeared or fixed to allow cytomorphological assessment.

EBUS-TBFNAB is usually employed for sampling central and endobronchial lung lesions suspicious on imaging and for staging patients with lung cancer through the sampling of mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes. The ultrasound-guided technique has largely substituted the earlier blind procedure originally developed by Wang and colleagues {27747023}. The “Insufficient” rate depends on different factors, including the presence of a cytopathologist providing ROSE, the cytopathological preparations employed, and the learning curve and expertise of the interventional radiologist or clinician {18805141}. ROSE is generally recommended at EBUS-TBFNAB to assess specimen adequacy, ensure that the targeted lung lesion is being sampled, and triage the material for ancillary testing, which will minimize the number of repeat procedures required and improve the availability of sufficient material for ancillary testing. ROSE is associated with higher adequate sample rates, with one study showing 7% "Insufficient" FNAB with ROSE versus 25% without ROSE {25926258}, but when ROSE is not available, multiple (≥ 3) passes are recommended {32401054}. LBC combined with cell block preparations has been shown by some authors to offer a higher diagnostic yield than conventional smears alone, because it maximizes the yield from needle rinsings, avoids blood contamination that obscures cellular detail, and improves non-small cell lung cancer subtyping by ICC {21965831; 20299387; 22264305}. However, direct smears (which can be used for directly scraped material for next-generation sequencing) coupled with cell block preparation are preferred by most cytopathologists. It is recommended to use both air-dried Giemsa-stained direct smears, which are commonly used at ROSE, and alcohol-fixed Pap- or H&E-stained smears in all cases, because each stain offers benefits in certain situations. The decision to use direct smears or LBC should be made locally by cytopathologists and their departments, based on historically established local usage and expertise that may favour direct smears, availability of appropriate infrastructure, and cost. If LBC is used for Pap-stained slides, air-dried Giemsa-stained smears will still be required for ROSE, although some cytopathologists use alcohol-fixed rapid Pap or H&E at ROSE. In cases of non-small cell lung cancer diagnosis, for a specimen to be adequate it must provide sufficient material for ancillary testing by ICC {21965831; 20299387; 22264305}.

In a study of > 13 000 transthoracic FNAB cases, the "Insufficient" rate was low (9%) {1580748}. However, a recent meta-analysis of 143 studies showed that the incidence of "Non-diagnostic" results was significantly lower when core needle biopsy was adopted, but it should be noted that "Non-diagnostic" results were more often observed in FNAB from lesions with a final benign diagnosis, indicating a reduction in specificity rather than sensitivity {31455841; 30561276}. The gauge and the needle type can influence the diagnostic yield of FNAB, with the adoption of a thinner-gauge cutting needle resulting in more samples classed as adequate than aspiration alone {30561276}. As with the choice of stains and the use of direct smears or LBC, the choice of needle type will be determined by local expertise and experience with the biopsy technique, and the use of ROSE at FNAB (followed by core needle biopsy where required) should be considered to maximize adequate sampling and reduce costs related to repeat procedures. The recent evidence-based guidelines on the collection and handling of thoracic small biopsy and cytopathology specimens strongly recommend ROSE during transthoracic FNAB {32401054}. In particular, although adequacy rates assessed by cytopathologists, cytopathology biomedical scientists, and cytopathology fellows were comparable, ROSE performed by cytopathologists was more frequently concordant with the final diagnosis {31051697}.
