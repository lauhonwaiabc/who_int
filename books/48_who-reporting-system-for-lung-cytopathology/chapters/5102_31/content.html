5.1.0.2 Histiocytic, lymphocytic, and eosinophilic inflammatory patterns

Definition

A histiocytic inflammatory pattern is an inflammatory reaction with a predominance of histiocytes, whereas lymphocytic and eosinophilic inflammatory patterns are inflammatory reactions with numerous lymphocytes and eosinophils, respectively.

Clinical features and imaging

Clinical presentations vary widely depending on the etiology: patients with acute pneumonia present with fever and dyspnoea, and those with chronic inflammatory processes present with slowly increasing dyspnoea, whereas many disease processes have an indolent course with minimal symptomatology (see #32160Table 4.01) {27134484; 9869825}.

Imaging findings also vary and include local or bilateral pulmonary infiltrates and nodular or reticular-nodular patterns with or without enlarged hilar lymph nodes.

Histopathology

A number of diseases give rise to these three cytopathological patterns, each with a predominant cell type. The histopathology varies with the predominant cell present and includes lipid-laden macrophages with a chronic foreign body reaction in lipoid pneumonia {19901490}; a lymphoid infiltrate of variable cytopathological appearance expanding the marginal zones of reactive lymphoid follicles and lymphoepithelial lesions in MALT lymphomas {26797028}; eosinophilic infiltrates in alveolar spaces and interstitium with eosinophilic abscesses in acute eosinophilic pneumonia {29206477}; allergic granulomas and eosinophilic vasculitis in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Churg–Strauss syndrome) {16088493}; bronchocentric granulomas, mucoid impaction of bronchi, tissue eosinophilia, and fungal elements in allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis {3344888}; acute eosinophilic alveolitis and histiocytic infiltration depending on the stage of the disease in tropical pulmonary eosinophilia {24135173}; and collections of Langerhans cells and other inflammatory cells in small airways, resulting in nodular inflammatory lesions in pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis {22429393}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Histiocytic pattern
  • Numerous histiocytes of variable cytomorphology, including multinucleated giant cells
  • Macrophages may contain lipid, haemosiderin, surfactant protein, dust particles, and mineral fibres
Lymphocytic pattern
  • Numerous lymphocytes and various lymphoid cells of varying size and morphology
  • Admixtures of plasma cells, histiocytes, and eosinophils
Eosinophilic pattern
  • Numerous eosinophils
  • Occasionally Charcot–Leyden crystals

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The commonest proliferation of macrophages is seen with carbon-laden macrophages in cigarette smokers and people living in smoke-polluted settings, whereas in pneumoconiosis the macrophages contain dust particles, which most commonly are carbon and may include birefringent silica and other pollutants (see #32161Table 4.02, for the differential diagnosis). Histiocytes predominate in samples of organizing pneumonia and in atypical mycobacterial infections. In lipoid pneumonia, lipid-laden macrophages can be seen and contain Oil Red O–positive exogenous lipid vacuoles, and foamy histiocytes contain endogenous lipids derived from cholesterol in cell membranes in fine vacuoles that may also be positive for Oil Red O. Foamy histiocytes are also found in e-cigarette or vaping product use–associated lung injury (EVALI), which is becoming more important. In amiodarone-induced lung toxicity, macrophages with very fine, clear, Oil Red O–negative vacuoles are seen. In pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, macrophages are filled with flocculent surfactant protein, and in diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, with haemosiderin. Mineral asbestos fibres may be visible as ferruginous bodies within macrophages {9869825; 28724562; 30112015; 33106908; 33145073; 32363736}.

Benign lymphoid follicles in the bronchial mucosa may be sampled by FNAB or bronchial brush and show predominantly small lymphocytes with a variable number of larger lymphoid cells. Elevated lymphocyte counts in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and an altered distribution of subtypes are seen in interstitial lung diseases, sarcoidosis, Behçet disease, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis. Malignant lymphoma with a low proliferation rate may mimic inflammatory reactions {18773446; 25729724; 22679012; 24261323}.

Eosinophilia may occur in acute and chronic eosinophilic pneumonia, vasculitis, asthmatic inflammatory material, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Churg–Strauss syndrome), and parasitic or fungal infections {29206477; 30364279; 30404114; 33193446}.

In patients infected with COVID-19, BAL is reserved for those with an atypical clinical presentation, to exclude an alternative diagnosis and to detect coinfections. Hospitalized patients with COVID-19 pneumonia are particularly at risk of developing secondary fungal infections, as are immunocompromised patients who can have a prolonged course of COVID-19 pneumonia.

Ancillary testing

Special stains include the Oil Red O stain for neutral fats, the PASD stain for surfactant protein, and the Prussian blue or Perls stain for iron, and these are routinely performed at the same time with the auramine or Ziehl–Neelsen or related stain for mycobacterial infection and the methenamine stain for fungi, to exclude infectious causes. Many bacteria and fungi are visible in routine Giemsa and Pap staining {27555237}. ICC may be used when assessing lymphoid material, but the standard practice is to use flow cytometry on lymphoid samples to exclude a B-cell monoclonality and assist with other lymphoid marker analyses.
