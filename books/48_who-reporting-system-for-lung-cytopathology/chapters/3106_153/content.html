3.1.0.6 Cell preparation methods

The samples should be optimally collected, transported, and kept according to guidelines issued or supervised by the cytopathology laboratory. The preanalytical steps have a critical role in specimen processing. The processing in the laboratory should be carried out according to the standard operating procedure of the cytopathology laboratory, validated and audited for quality assurance and quality control at designated regular intervals to enable the ideal diagnostic workup and interpretation.

Processing of samples
Cell collection, transportation, and storage

Bronchial washing (BW) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimens can be directly fixed in 50% ethanol, and liquid-based cytopathology (LBC) specimens are fixed in their commercial media. LBC offers transportation in a stable collecting medium {25427721}. The volume of fixation fluid should be sufficient and at least the same volume as the sample itself. In an institutional setting, BW specimens may be directly and rapidly transported to the laboratory, centrifuged, and then handled by cytospin or direct smearing using appropriate biohazard cabinets, before actual fixation in alcohol; but since the COVID-19 pandemic, direct fixation of respiratory specimens has been recommended {32259402; 32284276}. Direct smears from FNAB and bronchial brushing (BB) can be either air-dried or fixed by spraying with ethanol or by immersing immediately in 96% ethanol for 15 min. Some institutions process BB immediately into LBC medium. Referral to laboratory should be accompanied by a request form that includes the patient’s demographics, the proceduralist and referring clinician information, the specimen type and site, the sampling technique, indications, disease-related clinical data, and treatment history. The specimens can be transported at room temperature but should be stored at +4 °C. Longer storage at room temperature can cause bacterial or fungal contaminant growth in a poorly preserved specimen.

Adequacy assessment and triage of material

On arrival in the laboratory, visual inspection and the nature of the clinical information guide the triaging of sample material. Haemorrhagic, mucinous, and sparse specimens may need pretreatment with various haemolytic and mucolytic agents. Cell concentration methods may be applicable in sparse specimens. In cases of possible lymphoproliferative lesions, fresh material for flow cytometry should be submitted in Hanks solution, RPMI medium, or sterile buffered saline. If an infectious agent is considered, material should be placed in a sterile container for microbial culture, and this may be routine in some settings, such as the assessment of sputum samples in areas of the world where mycobacterial infections are endemic, or the evaluation of BW or BAL specimens in heart, lung, renal, bone marrow, and other transplant patients.

Preparation methods
Direct smears

Smears can be prepared by spreading material into a thin, even layer on a glass slide. The slides can be rapidly air-dried using a heating plate or hairdryer or immediately fixed by immersing the slides in 96% ethanol for 15 min or using an alcohol spray. Some institutions place FNAB and other smears for a short period into modified Carnoy fixative to lyse red cells in place of ethanol, and then transfer to ethanol.

Cytospins of ethanol-fixed material and fluids

Fluid cytopathological material including BW and FNAB rinses or brushed material fixed in ethanol can be spun down using a centrifuge to make a cytospin preparation. This technique is well established but may be more sensitive to technical variables than LBC. Because of biosafety issues in the COVID-19 pandemic era, fixation is preferable in potentially infected samples {32259402; 32284276}.

Liquid-based preparation

Various commercial systems are widely available. LBC has been reported to reduce the unsatisfactory rate of specimens. It offers certain advantages over conventional methods, such as elimination of obscuring elements, ease of screening, excellent preservation, and easy application of ancillary techniques {25427721}, but it may reduce the diagnostic cytopathological information by eluting the background elements and rounding up all epithelial tissue fragments and components, making assessment more difficult. LBC may be more expensive in many laboratory settings.

Cell blocks

A cell block involves the collection of any type of cytopathological material derived from FNAB, BB, or BW, which is concentrated and processed as a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsy by embedding a centrifuged compacted cell pellet into a paraffin block (see #24151Table 2.03). As with surgical biopsies, the paraffin block material can be stained with H&E, potentially offering diagnostic architectural features, and it can then be used for ancillary techniques including ICC, special stains, FISH, and molecular techniques. Cell blocks greatly enhance the use of cytopathology in the assessment of lung lesions.

Optimum cell block cellularity depends on the intrinsic nature of the lesion, with fibrotic and cystic lesions often providing limited material; the collection and triage of the sample; the expertise of the cytotechnologists; and the type of preparation method. Cell blocks can be prepared by a variety of techniques, which include simple clot and scrape, plasma thrombin, agar, colloidal bag, and automated methods. The methods also vary as to whether formalin or alcohol-based fixatives are used, and as to the method of concentration, which can include centrifugation, filtration, or scraping. Different adjuvants, such as thrombin clot, agar, or albumin, are used to bind cells into the compact button. The majority of the methods are modified in-house techniques (which depend on the availability of resources, funding, laboratory infrastructure, and the historical preference of cytopathologists) and are not standardized across laboratories {29909418; 30153355; 25113785; 31367220} [[Russell DK, Jain D. Cytopreparatory techniques. In: Chandra A, Crothers BA, Kurtycz DFI, et al., editors. The International System for Serous Fluid Cytopathology. Cham (Switzerland): Springer 2020. pp. 239–65.]]. In general, the methods using simple sedimentation with formalin fixation of the cell pellet and the use of plasma thrombin are the most popular and widely used {24799951}.

Cell culture

FNAB material and malignant pleural effusions have been used to produce different types of cancer cell cultures {10578968; 26928703; 21789168}. Subsequently to determining the adequacy of the specimen and tumour typing, samples are used to establish cell cultures. In brief, density gradient centrifugation and hypotonic lysis are used to purify tumour cells before plating them in culture Petri dishes or flasks. Finally, cultures are checked by in vitro growth kinetic and in vivo xenograft experiments.

Alternatively, commercially available cell lines of different tumours can be prepared as cytospins after fixation for their use as ICC positive controls {9218910}.

Recently, cultured tumour cells have been used to study chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic drug responses, enabling better and personalized treatment approaches by testing for individual drug sensitivity and resistance. Technical limitations due to patient sample acquisition, cellular variability, and culture conditions are the main reasons for the limited use of these techniques in clinical practice, as well as cost {32276643; 25253633; 26928703; 27548442}.

Staining methods

A wide variety of Romanowsky (Giemsa) stains, including the May–Grünwald–Giemsa, Wright–Giemsa, and others, can be used on air-dried slides. They are quick and easy to perform, cost-effective, and useful in rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE). The stain enhances cytoplasmic features, the background elements, and the stroma including the extracellular matrix. Pap and H&E require immediate fixation in ethanol and offer excellent nuclear detail and enhanced evaluation of squamous lesions. LBC primarily uses commercial variants of ethanol fixation. Ideally for FNAB, both Giemsa and Pap are used routinely to optimize the diagnostic cytopathological features. Pap staining is routinely used for sputum, BW, BB, and BAL specimens. Special stains for bacteria (e.g. Gram and Warthin–Starry stains), mycobacteria (including the Ziehl–Neelsen and related stains), and fungi (e.g. the methenamine silver stain) can be applied to direct smears and cytospin preparations. Various mucin stains such as the PASD can assist in the diagnosis of adenocarcinomas and adenosquamous carcinomas.

Quality assurance

The laboratory process should be validated and audited internally and externally on a regular basis. The preanalytical component of processing should be included in any quality assurance programme.
