3.1.0.5 Rapid onsite evaluation

Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) refers to quick onsite evaluation of the FNAB sample obtained by a radiologist, interventional pulmonologist, or thoracic surgeon to check for adequacy of the specimen, ensure sufficient material has been obtained for cytopathological diagnosis and ancillary testing, triage the material for cost-effective and efficient ancillary testing, and provide a preliminary diagnosis {28639854}. It requires some basic facilities (see #23903Box 2.01).

ROSE can be performed either by a cytopathologist or by a trained cytotechnologist, whereas provisional diagnoses at ROSE can only be provided by the cytopathologist. However, pulmonologists, once trained, can be competent in assessing the adequacy of the specimen if onsite cytopathologists are not available {31479551; 29874653}. Alternatively, to minimize the time taken by cytopathology staff to reach the bronchoscopy suite and the downtime between passes, telecytopathology has been adopted by some centres to improve the efficiency of ROSE as the cytopathologist remains in the laboratory and interprets images remotely on slides prepared by a cytotechnologist at the ROSE suite {29546032}. The advantages and limitations of ROSE are given in #24152Box 2.02 .

Processing of samples

ROSE is widely used in interventional pulmonology during thoracoscopy for pleural lesions, and transthoracic or transbronchial FNAB of lung, mediastinal, or hilar lesions {32702873; 32401054; 26656954}, and it can also be used on imprints from forceps and core needle biopsies, bronchial brush material {26119885}, transbronchial catheter aspirations {26156311}, and intraoperative imprints and FNAB {32226490; 32226502}.

ROSE includes four steps: (1) preparation of smears, (2) staining, (3) interpretation, and (4) triaging the sample.

  1. The material is quickly expressed over labelled glass slides for direct smear preparation.
  2. The prepared smears are stained by rapid stains, usually with an air-dried Giemsa but some institutions use toluidine blue or a Leishman stain on air-dried slides or a rapid Pap or H&E on alcohol-fixed smears. The air-drying should be rapid for the Giemsa using a warm plate or hairdryer, and, similarly, alcohol-fixed stains should be immediately placed in alcohol, although some may use an alcohol spray fixative. The choice of stain depends on laboratory infrastructure and set-up, available resources, and trained personnel for staining {30585436; 21322124}.
  3. The material is evaluated under a light microscope, usually without coverslipping. A sample is considered adequate if it contains diagnostic material – such as granulomas, an infectious agent, or tumour cells – that correlates with the clinical and imaging findings. If the sample contains only normal components of lung, then correlation with the clinical and imaging findings is required, and in most cases at ROSE, repeat FNAB is recommended to gain further material. An adequate transbronchial FNAB sample from a hilar lymph node should possess lymphoid cells and/or anthracotic pigment-laden macrophages in the absence of any specific diagnosis {26431922}.
  4. Subsequent to acquisition of adequate diagnostic material, the procedure can either be terminated or additional passes can be requested for ancillary testing on the basis of the preliminary diagnosis on ROSE. ROSE triaging allows for a timely and efficient decision to be made as to how to handle the material. Needle and syringe rinses and any clots from the prior passes can be collected for making cell blocks. If the material is lymphoid, it can be collected into RPMI medium or Hanks solution or sterile buffered saline for flow cytometry, clonality assays, or gene rearrangement analysis and cell block; or, if the material suggests an infectious or granulomatous process, it can be collected into sterile saline and sent for microbiological examination. If ROSE suggests carcinoma or a metastasis such as melanoma, extra samples should be obtained for cell block and molecular testing, and placement directly into formalin is generally recommended, although some institutions place the material into RPMI medium or saline at ROSE and then in the laboratory prepare the cell block before placement into formalin.

1.          Sufficient space in the procedure room for the microscope, spreading out slides, and staining; there must be clear access for the proceduralist to rapidly deliver the material expelled from the FNAB on to slides

2.          Light microscope

3.          95% ethyl alcohol jars for wet fixation of slides

4.          Rack for air-dried slides

5.          Jars containing reagents for rapid staining

6.          10% formalin jars for cell blocks or obvious tissue fragments/clots

7.          Other tubes containing media, saline, etc. for ancillary testing

8.          Glass slides

9.          Pencil for labelling slides

10.      Personnel for ROSE

Advantages of ROSE:

·         Adequacy assessment with real-time guidance to the physician performing the FNAB procedure

·         Increased diagnostic yield

·         Appropriate guided termination of the procedure after obtaining the appropriate material

·         Reduction in number of unnecessary passes and slides

·         Triage of sample and ancillary testing possible

·         Careful control of preanalytical factors with a possibility of sampling/testing at different sites

·         Possible benefits to sample and patient management

Limitations of ROSE:

·         Staff requirement – either a cytopathologist or a trained cytotechnologist must be in attendance

·         Time-consuming process for which cost reimbursement is an issue in some countries {29546032}
