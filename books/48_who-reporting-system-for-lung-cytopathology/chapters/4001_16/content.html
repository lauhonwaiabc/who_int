4.0.0.1 Introduction

In respiratory cytopathology, specimen adequacy plays a pivotal role in the interpretation and rendering of a diagnosis, the application of ancillary studies, and clinical decision-making. Specimen adequacy is defined in relation to the specimen type, medical history, clinical symptoms, and imaging findings using the triple-test approach {26990836; 10334135}. Terms such as “Insufficient”, “Inadequate”, and “Non-diagnostic” have been used interchangeably to describe this category of specimens. A specimen is categorized as "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic" when the cytopathology material does not contain any diagnostic material that could explain the imaging findings or clinical symptoms. A cytopathologist or institution should select one term and use it consistently. The report should always state that “correlation with clinical and imaging findings is required”.

Until recently, sputum specimens have been the only respiratory specimen with a relatively well-defined adequacy criterion {6352461; 18232006}. More recently, there have been several published definitions of an inadequate sample, by the Papanicolaou Society of Cytopathology (PSC) in 2016 {26990836; 10405813}, and by the Japan Lung Cancer Society (JLCS) and the Japanese Society of Clinical Cytology (JSCC) in 2020 in a revised cytology reporting system for lung cancer {32222718}. These reflect the different modalities available for the cytopathological diagnosis of pulmonary lesions, each of which strictly requires a different definition of what is “Satisfactory” or “Adequate”. The PSC guidelines include the category “Non-diagnostic” for a “specimen […] which provides no useful diagnostic information about the pulmonary nodule, cyst, or mass lesion identified by imaging findings”. The Japanese system uses a stepwise approach, with an initial assessment of “Adequate”, which is defined as adequate for cytopathological evaluation and clarified as the presence of abundant cellular material, or “Inadequate”, where there is no abundant cellular material or where obscuring artefact is present, but both of these can have limitations in interpretation and may not diagnose the lesion identified on imaging {32259828}.
