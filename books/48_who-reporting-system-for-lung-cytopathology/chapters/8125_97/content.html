8.1.2.5 Carcinosarcoma

Definition

Carcinosarcoma is a biphasic tumour usually composed of poorly differentiated non-small cell carcinomas as adenocarcinoma and/or squamous cell carcinoma and/or large cell carcinoma, and a malignant mesenchymal component, which must include heterologous differentiation.

Clinical features and imaging

As is characteristic of many poorly differentiated non-small cell carcinomas, carcinosarcoma typically arises in older adults, with a predominant male bias. The majority of patients have a strong history of cigarette smoking. The most frequent symptoms include cough and chest pain. On imaging, carcinosarcoma typically occurs in the upper lobes as a large, solitary, peripheral, well-delineated mass. The central lymph nodes may appear enlarged.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of carcinosarcoma requires both malignant components to be present: carcinomatous elements may show glandular and/or squamous differentiation or represent a large cell carcinoma, and the sarcomatous component may largely comprise nonspecific spindle tumour cells or a variable number of pleomorphic malignant giant cells, but it must also manifest chondrosarcomatous, osteosarcomatous, or rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation by light microscopy. In both components, nuclei are large, irregular, and hyperchromatic. Nucleoli may be multiple and irregular, and macronucleoli occur.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • High cellularity with obvious biphasic malignant components
  • Adenocarcinoma, squamous, or undifferentiated carcinoma cells in loose to tightly cohesive tissue fragments and as single cells
  • Malignant mesenchymal cells with spindle and/or giant cell morphology, with one or more nuclei
  • Stromal heterologous differentiation consisting of primitive skeletal muscle differentiation, either as single isolated cells or in small, loosely cohesive tissue fragments, and/or malignant bone or cartilage in tissue fragments
  • Large, hyperchromatic, highly pleomorphic nuclei, often with highly irregular large nucleoli
  • Necrotic debris in background

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Carcinosarcoma is one of the five types of sarcomatoid carcinoma. The diagnosis of a malignancy in FNAB should be straightforward even in small samples of this tumour, but a precise diagnosis will require considerable material on direct smears and in the cell block. Only very rare isolated reports of the FNAB cytopathological findings of pulmonary carcinosarcoma have been published, and on review, most do not include descriptions of heterologous sarcomatous elements and therefore would be more accurately classified as pleomorphic carcinomas.

The differential diagnosis from a pleomorphic carcinoma, which is also in the sarcomatoid carcinoma group, is based on the identification of heterologous elements in the carcinosarcoma. The differential diagnosis from a spindle cell carcinoma is based on the recognition of a malignant glandular or squamous epithelial component in carcinosarcoma. The diagnosis of a spindle cell carcinoma should not be made in limited specimens because of the potential for sampling error where the epithelial carcinomatous elements are not present. The differential diagnosis from another biphasic neoplasm, pulmonary blastoma, can be made because its mesenchymal component is composed of primitive cells with no obvious differentiation and very high N:C ratios, and because its carcinomatous element resembles low-grade fetal adenocarcinoma. The differential diagnosis from most metastases, including those from the female genital tract, should be made on cytomorphology and the essential review of patient history, and even the great morphological mimic, melanoma, does not manifest heterologous elements.

Ancillary testing

Epithelial differentiation can be confirmed by pankeratins and CK7, with the glandular components thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and napsin A being positive, and with the squamous components being positive for p40 {12604887; 30572031}. The sarcomatous component is positive for vimentin. Desmin or myogenin may confirm muscle differentiation, and S100 supports cartilaginous differentiation.
