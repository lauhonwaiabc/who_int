2.0.0.2 The role of lung cytopathology

Sputum samples

Sputum sample cytopathology is a simple, non-invasive, and economical technique for the detection of malignancy and infectious processes in the respiratory tract. Sputum sample cytopathology has been used for many years as an efficient diagnostic tool for lung carcinomas {4810100} and, more recently, in association with lung cancer screening programmes {20526284; 25645403; 27729459; 23868001}. However, as lung cancer screening shifted to the evaluation of small peripheral lesions, other diagnostic technologies became more prevalent {27130532}. In geographical regions with limited resources, sputum sampling is still a cost-effective diagnostic modality.

Sputum samples can be obtained spontaneously or induced. Spontaneous sputum samples can be produced by instructing the patient to cough deeply and to expel the contents into a sterile container. Induced sputum samples can be produced by the same process but after inhalation of a bronchodilator and/or saline. The latter has been reported to produce more adequate and better-quality samples for cytopathological evaluation in comparison to spontaneous sputum samples, especially for the evaluation of small peripheral nodules {11601749}.

There is a wide range of reported sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of lung carcinoma in sputum samples (see #31989Table 1.03), mostly due to variations in specimen processing, tumour location, histopathological type, and pathological stage {30195266; 9100773; 25645403}. On average, the sensitivity for the diagnosis of malignancy has been reported to be 66%, ranging from 34% for peripheral lesions to 97% for centrally located lesions, with a specificity of 99% (range: 68–100%) {30195266; 23649436}. The diagnostic rate increases with the use of sequential specimens, with three to five consecutive daily collections being reported as the most effective {1057739}. Whether the sputum sample is collected fresh on demand or in the mornings does not appear to greatly influence the diagnostic rate {28535501}, although one study did show a higher yield of malignant cells with morning samples {28535501}. Prompt specimen fixation after collection has been shown to increase diagnostic yields, as well as facilitating cell block preparations and the use of molecular diagnostics tests {25881088}. Cell block preparation has been reported to increase the sensitivity and specificity of sputum sample cytopathology {14674072}. The use of molecular techniques such as DNA promoter methylation {23868001; 27729459} and microRNA analysis {23868001; 20526284} have been used with sputum sample cytopathology for the early detection of lung cancer.

Bronchoalveolar lavage

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is a commonly used procedure for the evaluation of both non-neoplastic and neoplastic diseases of the lower respiratory tract. The procedure is performed by the insertion of a bronchoscope into the bronchial tree and the instillation of 100–300 mL of sterile room-temperature saline into a segmental bronchus. At least 30% of the instilled saline should be recovered for cytopathological examination {30195266}. The sensitivity of BAL for the diagnosis of lung cancer varies from 15% to 65% {28007409; 28066604; 12527571; 33145073}. The sensitivity tends to be lower in peripheral lesions smaller than 20 mm {12527571}. Because of its low sensitivity, BAL is rarely used as a sole diagnostic procedure for the diagnosis of lung cancer. It is often used in association with other cytopathological sampling techniques or small forceps biopsies {28007409; 28066604}. BAL is more commonly used in the evaluation of inflammatory cell counts and composition in interstitial lung diseases and infections {33145073}. There are suggestions, however, that BAL could be used in the surveillance of lung cancer as an indicator of local recurrence and response to therapy, local immune response, and tumour microenvironment {31865801}.

Bronchial washing and brushing

Bronchial washings and bronchial brushings are obtained during bronchoscopy in a similar manner to BAL. For these procedures, the lesion is sampled under direct visualization. In bronchial brushings, the entrapped cells on the bristles can be used for direct smears or collected in saline or other carrier liquids such as cell culture medium or fixative, according to the laboratory protocol {30195266}. In bronchial washings, 5–20 mL of fluid is instilled directly onto the lesion and the recovered fluid is then processed for diagnosis. The sensitivity for these modalities varies from 33% to 60% for bronchial brushings and is slightly lower for bronchial washings {12527571}. The combination with endobronchial biopsy increases the sensitivity to 88% {12527571}. This technique is mostly used when a central visible lesion is targeted, as opposed to submucosal or small peripheral lesions.

FNAB

Endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB) and transthoracic FNAB are minimally invasive procedures used in the diagnosis and staging of lung cancer {32401054; 30195266}. EBUS-TBFNAB is the diagnostic procedure of choice for the evaluation of hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes to achieve tumour staging, and for centrally located lesions to diagnose the tumour. More peripheral lesions can also be sampled by this technique if associated with an airway that can accommodate the endoscope. EBUS-TBFNAB is often used for the evaluation of recurrence and/or disease metastatic to the mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes {32401054}.

Transthoracic FNAB involves the introduction of a needle through the skin and chest wall into the pulmonary lesion under CT guidance and is preferentially used for the diagnosis of peripheral nodules.

Both FNAB procedures are usually performed under moderate or general sedation. Pneumothorax and intraparenchymal bleeding are rare complications {32401054; 26367186}. The diagnostic yield for both procedures is high, with an average sensitivity of 89% (range: 76–95%) and a specificity of 100% {32401054; 28054531; 24760496; 26367186; 17873165}. The best diagnostic rates for EBUS-TBFNAB are seen in centrally located tumours measuring > 20 mm {17873165; 26367186}. A minimum of two and as many as five passes should be performed for each procedure to ensure adequate diagnostic material {32401054; 30195266}. Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) is often recommended for specimen adequacy and triage {32401054}. Material obtained by these procedures can be prepared as direct smears for adequacy assessment and diagnosis at ROSE, and part or all of the specimen can be placed in transport medium for further cytopathological evaluation including cell block preparation. There is very good correlation of adequacy for molecular testing of samples procured by EBUS-TBFNAB and FNAB {32134701; 21266922; 29547001}.

Transbronchial core needle biopsy

Transbronchial core needle biopsy is obtained through a bronchoscope under endobronchial ultrasound guidance using a large-bore needle (usually 19–22 gauge) {28054531; 32451285}. These core needles were created in response to the increased demand for core biopsy material in patients with advanced inoperable stages of lung cancer, for the determination of biomarkers for targeted therapy and inclusion in clinical trial protocols for lung cancer. The procedure produces a delicate core of tissue embedded in a blood clot. Touch preparations of the core can be used for ROSE but there is a risk of compromising the tissue for histopathology. Squash preparations can also be used for ROSE, but this sacrifices the core and the ability to perform subsequent ancillary studies {32451285}. The core should be placed directly into formalin and not saline for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded processing. The sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of lung cancer is high, ranging from 87% to 97%, which is similar to that of EBUS-TBFNAB {28054531; 32451285; 32894657}. There is good utilization for ancillary tests, including molecular analysis {28210144}. Transbronchial core needle biopsy is also used to supplement FNAB for the diagnosis of non-neoplastic lesions such as those in sarcoidosis {27261933; 30141992; 32451285} and lymphoproliferative disorders {27261933}.
