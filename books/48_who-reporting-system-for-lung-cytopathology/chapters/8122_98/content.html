8.1.2.2 Adenosquamous carcinoma

Definition

Adenosquamous carcinoma is a carcinoma consisting of adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma components.

Clinical features and imaging

The relative frequency of pulmonary adenosquamous carcinoma (PASC) is reported to range from 0.4% to 5% of all lung carcinomas {30147334; 30040455; 26746368; 25002356; 30172267}. Most tumours occur in patients ranging in age from 50 to 80 years (predominantly men with a strong smoking history), and < 3% of adenosquamous tumours occur in younger individuals {25002356; 30040455; 22054879}. There is a strong association with smoking history. Adenosquamous carcinoma behaves aggressively compared with other histopathological types of bronchogenic carcinoma. They usually present as large tumours at a higher clinical stage, with a frequent rate of metastases {32965978; 29850145}.

Imaging studies have shown that adenosquamous carcinoma can present in radiotherapy-related fibrosis and pneumoconiosis, and it usually occurs in the intermediate or peripheral portion of the affected lobe, but when it does occur centrally it can cause obstructive pneumonia. Adenosquamous carcinoma can show a poorly defined tumour–lung interface, pleural indentation, lobulation, spiculation, and peripheral ground-glass opacities {24206962; 24417920}.

Histopathology

In surgical resection specimens, the tumours will show discrete or intermingled components of both adenocarcinoma (which tends to show acinar or solid growth with or without a lepidic pattern) and squamous cell carcinoma (either as keratinizing well-differentiated or solid poorly differentiated carcinoma). ICC for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and p40 and a mucicarmine stain are used to highlight the adenocarcinoma component. The diagnosis of adenosquamous carcinoma can be challenging in small biopsies because it may show only one of the components, and ICC is essential to prevent the solid and non-keratinizing squamous carcinoma component being mistaken for poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma {30147334; 30040455; 26746368; 25002356; 32965978; 31392181; 30678417; 22054879; 21500121}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Loosely cohesive tissue fragments and single cells showing features of adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma in a background of extensive necrosis
  • Occasional keratinized cells can be seen, along with cells showing perinucleolar clearing of chromatin (Pap), prominent nucleoli, and intracytoplasmic mucin, suggesting adenocarcinoma

Reference(s): {21500121; 33269250; 31080486; 21416646; 23312886; 21319328; 21258246; 16200036; 1665154}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

As in transbronchial core biopsies, the diagnosis of PASC can be challenging in FNAB samples, because of a lack of readily identifiable adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma components. It is advisable to confirm the dual differentiation by ICC before rendering a diagnosis of PASC, and most cases of PASC on FNAB have been diagnosed as poorly differentiated carcinoma with focal differentiation and a differential suggesting the possibility of PASC. The diagnosis of PASC is important in FNAB and limited tissue samples, because this tumour has the same response to treatment targeting EGFR mutations as adenocarcinoma, when compared with squamous cell carcinoma {30147334; 30040455; 26746368; 25002356; 32965978; 31392181; 30678417}. The differential diagnosis of PASC includes all primary and secondary pulmonary tumours showing varying degrees of squamous differentiation, including primary and metastatic squamous cell carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and metastatic endometrioid carcinoma {25002356}. Reactive atypia in lung parenchyma surrounding other primary tumours including squamous cell carcinoma can be mistaken for an adenocarcinoma component, leading to a misdiagnosis of adenosquamous carcinoma.

Ancillary testing

ICC with TTF1 and p40 is essential for rendering a diagnosis of PASC, especially for cases that fail to show distinct cytomorphological evidence of adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, particularly in non-keratinizing cases. The p40 should show strong and diffuse staining to confirm the squamous component. ICC also helps in differentiating between other primary and secondary lung tumours showing squamous differentiation {30147334; 30040455; 26746368; 25002356; 32965978; 31392181; 30678417; 24126803; 23312886}. A stain for mucicarmine or PASD can be used to highlight intracytoplasmic mucin {25002356}. PASC may harbour gene rearrangement in ALK, ROS1, or RET, or gene mutation in EGFR, KRAS, AKT1, ERBB2 (HER2), PIK3CA, or STK11  {33269250; 33679868}.
