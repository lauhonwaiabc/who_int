2.0.0.3 Diagnostic categories and report structure

Categories

Each of the categories generates a risk of malignancy (ROM) that is directly linked to a recommendation for further diagnostic testing aimed at achieving a specific diagnosis or refining a differential diagnosis. Specific processes or tumours, which have a similar ROM when diagnosed on cytopathology, are placed in the same category; for example, inflammatory and benign neoplastic entities are placed in the “Benign” category. The WHO Reporting Systems use categories to assist communication of ROM, but a specific diagnosis or differential diagnosis further refined by ancillary testing is the aim of all cytopathology reporting and is paramount in the report.

The WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology applies the same five categories for all lung cytopathological specimen types:

  • "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic"
  • "Benign"
  • "Atypical"
  • "Suspicious for malignancy"
  • "Malignant"

Currently there are limited publications defining the ROM for these categories, and there is a need for further research using the WHO Reporting System to more clearly define the ROM.

Individual cytopathologists and departments with more than one cytopathologist should choose one term from “Insufficient”, “Inadequate”, and “Non-diagnostic” for cases where there is no material for assessment or there are technical problems that prevent the assessment and diagnosis of the material on the slides. If sputum, bronchial washing (BW), and bronchial brushing (BB), and FNAB specimens have adequate benign material then this is described and reported as “Benign”. Correlation with imaging should be carried out wherever possible with lung cytopathology specimens, and if the material on the slides shows benign material that does not correlate with or explain the imaging or clinical findings, then it is mandatory to place a caveat in the report conclusion, that the material “may not represent the lesion seen on imaging and further diagnostic workup is recommended”. However, it is recognized that some cytopathologists prefer the term “Non-diagnostic” to encompass not only cases where there is insufficient or technically obscured material, but also cases where there is benign cytopathological material that does not correlate with or explain the clinical and/or imaging findings; for example, a bronchial brush may show only bronchial epithelium or an FNAB may show only benign components of lung in a patient with a solid lesion on imaging. This is the first WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology, and it accepts that some cytopathologists prefer to categorize these cases as "Non-diagnostic" and place them in the "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic" category. Research into the impact of this approach on the ROM is required.

The “Benign” category includes normal lung components, inflammatory processes, and benign tumours such as pulmonary hamartomas. If the features are benign but a precise diagnosis cannot be made, the features should be described and a differential diagnosis provided of the findings if possible. As with all categories, correlation with clinical and (wherever possible) imaging findings is mandatory.

The “Atypical” category is expected to have a relatively low to intermediate ROM and allows for a high negative predictive value for a “Benign” categorization, while the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is expected to have a high ROM and provide for a high positive predictive value approaching 100% for a “Malignant” categorization. The categories stratify the ROM and allow for the development of management recommendations. The “Atypical” category will include cases where there is scant or poorly prepared but atypical material, and cases where mainly benign cytopathological features are present, but there are some features, such as nuclear atypia or limited necrosis, which may raise the possibility of malignancy. If any atypical features are seen in a case showing otherwise insufficient material, then the case is regarded as “Atypical” and not “Insufficient”.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category includes cases where there is scant, poorly smeared, or poorly prepared and stained material suggestive of malignancy, as well as cases where there is considerable material, some of which appears malignant, but not all features of a particular malignancy are present or there may be discrepant features.

The “Malignant” category is reserved for those cases in which there are unequivocal features of malignancy without any discrepant findings. All cytopathology specimen types are obtained by sampling techniques, and it is not always possible to make a precise diagnosis of a specific carcinoma or other malignancy, even with ancillary testing. The precise diagnosis of carcinoma subtypes usually requires assessment of the entire resected tumour, most typically for mixed tumours such as carcinosarcoma.

The presentation of tumours in this WHO Reporting System follows the order found in the fifth-edition WHO classification of thoracic tumours, but not all tumours are included in this volume, because they have not all been described in the cytopathology literature.

Spindle cell lesions with a low risk of recurrence or rare metastasis, such as inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour, are presented in section Chapter 4: Diagnostic category: Benign, with a clear discussion of the cytopathological features and ancillary studies that often allow a specific diagnosis.

Structured reporting

Each cytopathology report on a sputum sample, BW, BB, and transthoracic or endobronchial ultrasound–directed FNAB should have a structured format, with a heading of a specific diagnostic category using the WHO Reporting System’s standardized descriptive terminology and a diagnosis. Structured reporting improves the quality, clarity, and reproducibility of reports within individual pathology departments and between countries, and it will improve patient management and facilitate research and quality assurance measures. Standard guidelines for cell block preparation, ICC, in situ hybridization, and other molecular tests of prognostic and predictive markers improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce cost, and improve patient care. The establishment of key diagnostic cytopathological findings for each lesion, which will act as a checklist for the reporting cytopathologist, will also improve reporting.

There are essential or core components in a cytopathology report and these are presented here. This is not intended to make any particular format mandatory, but some components are a general requirement, namely the diagnostic category using the defined terminology of the reporting system, and a diagnosis or conclusion, which may be specific or descriptive with a differential diagnosis. Frequently, a microscopic description of the key diagnostic features can be added, particularly in cases where the diagnostic conclusion is a differential diagnosis. An addendum outlining any ancillary tests and their results is also required wherever possible. The diagnostic summary must be clearly delineated and stand out in the report so that it can be easily seen by the patient care team.

A number should not be used in place of the diagnostic category terminology or a specific diagnosis. A category number can be included in the body of the report for audit and research purposes, but it should not appear in the conclusion. Reducing a cytopathology report to a number reduces the information that can be given to the clinician and management team and increases the risk of error.

The diagnostic category states whether there is inadequate material and an "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic" categorization is applicable, or whether there is adequate material and a “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant” category can be applied. But it is recognized that some cytopathologists prefer to include a statement on specimen quality in the microscopic description of the report that “there is adequate material for assessment”. Other cytopathologists require a statement to be included regarding the degree of cellularity of the material, referring to the cellularity of the material from the target lesion rather than inflammatory cells.

The microscopic description should provide a clear, concise cytopathological description, which includes the degree of cellularity and focuses on the presence or absence of key cytopathological diagnostic features. Some cytopathologists prefer a full description of these features; others may prefer only a brief comment or, in cases where there is a specific diagnosis, no description at all.

The aim of cytopathology is to provide as specific a diagnosis as possible in the vast majority of cases. The indeterminate categories of “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” establish a ROM and subsequent management recommendations as part of an algorithm to aid communication with the clinician about a particular biopsy and the further diagnostic management of the patient.

The ancillary tests should always be part of the final cytopathology report, and if flow cytometry, in situ hybridization, or molecular pathology reports are issued by a different laboratory, then it is recommended that they should be referred to in the report. Wherever possible, the ancillary results should be added as a supplementary report or addendum to the original cytopathology report, with the aim of having a final all-inclusive report for optimal patient care.

The WHO cytopathology reporting systems are based on cytomorphology for the initial categorization and reporting of the lesion, which can be applied in any well-resourced or low- and middle-income country setting, but which also incorporate the application of appropriate ancillary testing as current best practice. Subsequent ancillary testing may well change the final diagnostic category and provide a specific diagnosis of a particular case.

The standardized cytopathology report
Demographic information
  • Patient’s name, date of birth, address, patient identifiers / patient episode, date of request, and laboratory accession number
  • Referring doctor and contact details
Type of specimen
  • Sputum sample, BW, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), BB, FNAB (endobronchial ultrasound, transthoracic)
Clinical and imaging information
  • Site, size (mm), imaging (ultrasound, CXR, tomogram, CT, MRI) features
  • Previous cytopathology procedures and results and previous other biopsy results when available
Diagnostic summary
  • Totally in bold, on the first page (if the laboratory information system supports this)
  • Each institution should select a term for the main heading of the diagnostic summary, such as "Diagnostic summary" (as used throughout this volume), “Diagnosis”, or “Final diagnosis”, and use it consistently

Category

  • Reporting system diagnostic category: using terminology not a number

Diagnosis

  • Specific diagnosis or differential diagnosis
  • Optional but recommended to include in the diagnostic summary: the specimen type and exact site of a BW/BB or FNAB (verbatim from the request form), e.g. “FNAB of right upper lobe of lung: adenocarcinoma”; this should correlate with the labelling of any specimen container, and if it does not, the clinician should be contacted
Other information included in the report

Macroscopic description

  • Number of air-dried slides, number of alcohol-fixed slides
  • Type (saline, RPMI medium, formalin, only specimen without diluent, liquid-based cytology) and volume of fluid received, with description of fluid (colour, presence of blood, viscosity, presence of particulate matter), including any other specimens received and specifically designated for flow cytometry, microbiology, cell block

Details of rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) (if conducted)

  • Number of passes, gauge of needle used, imaging modality used
  • Names of those performing the clinical and diagnostic procedures, including the cytology biomedical scientist, pathology trainee, or cytopathologist attending ROSE
  • Number of slides processed and stained at ROSE, air-dried and alcohol-fixed, and other specimen types prepared
  • The specific ROSE report provided verbatim and documented, and the name of the cytopathologist or cytology biomedical scientist issuing ROSE, with further noting of subsequent ROSE cytopathological procedures

Microscopic description

  • Optional but recommended to include a statement of the degree of cellularity (e.g. scant, mildly cellular, moderately cellular, highly cellular) or the specimen quality (e.g. “specimen satisfactory for assessment”)
  • Optional to include the key diagnostic cytopathological features whether present or absent, but if the final diagnosis is not specific and is based on a differential diagnosis, then a microscopic description is required
  • Include a specific diagnosis or, if this is not possible, a discussion of the differential diagnosis, emphasizing the most likely diagnosis; cytopathologists may prefer a subheading at the end of the microscopic description to convey the diagnosis
  • If the lesion is "Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic", "Atypical", or "Suspicious for malignancy", then the report should include a clear description of what material is present on the slides, and the reason why the material is insufficient, atypical, or suspicious for malignancy should be stated; for the “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” categories, this may include a discussion of the differential diagnosis
Ancillary testing
  • Depending on local practice, the cytopathology report (including its conclusion) can be issued as a final or provisional report, with supplementary reports or addenda on ancillary studies to follow and to be added to the final report, or the cytopathology report can be withheld until ancillary reports are available
  • Includes all cell block H&E and special stains, ICC, in situ hybridization, molecular techniques performed on cell block or direct smear slide scrapings, and further stains; the report should state which type of specimen was used for each ancillary test to facilitate continuous quality assurance (e.g. cell blocks or direct smears)
  • If flow cytometry results are issued by the same laboratory, then these results should be entered here; if flow cytometry results are issued by a different laboratory, their results should be added as a supplementary report, when available, under ancillary testing in the original report
  • If a core needle biopsy or other surgical biopsy was performed at the same time as the FNAB, the supplementary report should recommend correlation with the surgical report and its accession number
