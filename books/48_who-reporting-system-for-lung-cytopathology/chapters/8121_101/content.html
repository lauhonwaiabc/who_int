8.1.2.1 Salivary gland-type carcinomas

Definition

Pulmonary salivary gland–type carcinomas are neoplasms that are believed to originate from the seromucinous glands of the central airways and that closely resemble their counterparts in the salivary glands.

Clinical features and imaging

Primary pulmonary salivary gland–type tumours (PSGTs) account for 0.1–0.2% of all pulmonary neoplasms (see #32482Table 7.01) {7638445; 17918258; 30527184; 33457200; 30829245; 29486180; 24389442; 23789697}, and the great majority are malignant {31065298; 30527184}. Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC) of either low or high grade is the most common, followed by adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC), with fewer reports of epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC), acinic cell carcinoma, and other rare neoplasms. EMC is a low-grade malignant tumour {7638445; 17918258; 29868475}.

The clinical symptomatology associated with PSGT includes cough, dyspnoea, wheeze, chest pain, hoarseness, pneumonia, and haemoptysis {31065298; 30527184}.

Both CT and MRI are useful. Pulmonary MEC on CT appears as one or more well-defined tumour nodules involving the segmental or lobar bronchi. Usually AdCCs are infiltrative and occur in the inferior portion of the trachea or mainstem bronchi, leading to circumferential thickening of the airway wall with or without intraluminal or extraluminal thickening. The pulmonary EMC presents as an endobronchial mass on imaging {31065298; 33407915; 29196470}.

Lymph node metastases are rare; however, tumour recurrence and distant metastasis have been reported with AdCC {7638445; 30527184}.

Histopathology

MEC can be low-grade and show cystic architecture with minimal or no invasive growth into the lung parenchyma, and it consists of a varying proportion of mucin-containing cells, squamous (epidermoid) cells, and intermediate cells. High-grade carcinomas show a solid growth pattern with infiltration into the surrounding lung parenchyma and consist of a varying number of mucus-secreting squamous and intermediate cells, with frequent necrosis and mitoses.

Adenoid cystic carcinoma can show (most commonly) cribriform and (on occasion) tubular or solid architecture, with infiltrative growth at the edges of the tumour and without associated tumour necrosis. The cribriform architecture shows a readily identifiable mucinous-type matrix filling the luminal spaces. The cells are basaloid with minimal nuclear pleomorphism and scant mitoses.

EMC is a biphasic tumour consisting of cuboidal epithelial cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm in the inner luminal layer of tubules, and myoepithelial cells (which are round or spindle with eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm) at the outer margin of the epithelial cells and in solid nests {7638445; 29868475; 26645458; 29338644}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
  • Cellularity depends on the grade of tumour, with low-grade tumours producing abundant background mucin and macrophages due to cystic change, often with few epithelial tissue fragments
  • Mucinous cells have goblet cell–type single large vacuoles or foamy oval regions of mucin (pink by Pap, magenta by Giemsa) and are scattered among polygonal intermediate cells in tissue fragments, as well as cohesive sheets of squamous (epidermoid) cells with denser cytoplasm and well-defined borders but lacking keratinization
  • High-grade carcinomas are readily identifiable as malignant due to their atypical epidermoid cells with a lesser number of interspersed goblet-type mucinous cells, more prominent nuclear pleomorphism, larger nucleoli, necrosis, and (in some cases) mitoses
Adenoid cystic carcinoma
  • Variably cellular
  • Sheets with microcysts or cribriform, tubular, or complex tissue fragments and a lesser number of single cells
  • Monotonous population of basaloid cells with a high N:C ratio and bland, oval to angulated nuclei with evenly dispersed dark chromatin and indistinct nucleoli
  • The tumour matrix is usually sharply defined, balled up or branching, thick, and pale eosinophilic to virtually non-staining (Pap) and magenta (Giemsa); in some cases it forms a rim surrounding basaloid tumour cells
Epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma
  • Usually highly cellular
  • Two cell types, with tightly cohesive tubules or balls of basaloid epithelial cells with a high N:C ratio surrounded by larger myoepithelial cells with clear cytoplasm and oval nuclei
  • Loosely cohesive tissue fragments of myoepithelial cells and stripped oval nuclei in the background
  • Variable (but usually small) amount of eosinophilic (Pap) and magenta (Giemsa) matrix including stromal balls

Discussion and differential diagnosis

PSGTs are rare tumours and can mimic primary lung neoplasms owing to their growth patterns and cellular composition. They are being increasingly recognized partly because of an increasing familiarity with their counterparts in the head and neck region, and it is important to exclude the possibility of metastatic head and neck salivary gland tumours to lung before making a diagnosis of PSGT {7638445; 29868475}. However, imaging features of a single mass involving the airways should favour the diagnosis of a primary lung neoplasm {32097509; 25043143}.

The differential diagnosis includes primary adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma and bronchial mucous cell adenoma, other pulmonary salivary gland tumours, metastatic clear cell renal carcinoma, and perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa, clear cell tumour) {29868475}.

The relatively bland appearance of tumour cells with a mix of different cell types and the presence of extracellular matrix material assist in the diagnosis of PSGT.

In MEC, a mixture of mucous cells (with their eccentric goblet cell–type single vacuoles or foamy or punctate magenta [Giemsa] mucin), intermediate cells, and squamous cells is typical for this tumour, along with a lack of any keratinization {31343850}. In AdCC, diagnosis can be difficult in the solid-type tumour lacking cribriform foci and lacking extracellular basement membrane material. In these cases, the densely packed basaloid cells may be mistaken for small cell lung carcinoma, basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, carcinoid tumour, or even basal cell hyperplasia {28484699; 14696146; 32329967; 33776449}.

In EMC, a dual population of small basaloid epithelial cells and larger myoepithelial cells is found, and acellular basement membrane material may by present. The differential diagnosis includes AdCC and basaloid squamous cell carcinoma {32329967}.

Because most PSGTs are exophytic endobronchial tumours covered by intact mucosa, these tumours may be missed by bronchial brushing or washing {31343850}.

Ancillary testing

An ICC panel can help to differentiate PSGT from other lung primaries such as adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma {26645458}. In MEC, mucous cells are positive for MUC5AC; intermediate and squamous cells are positive for CK5/6, p40, and p63; and all cells are positive for CK7 and negative for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) {24743219; 28484699; 26575266}. In AdCC, the myoepithelial cells are positive for p63, S100, SMA, calponin, and vimentin, whereas the luminal cells are positive for CK7, EMA, CK5/6, and KIT (CD117) {33776449; 31343850; 28484699}. In EMC, the epithelial cells are positive for AE1/AE3, CK7, MNF116, SOX10, and EMA, and the myoepithelial cells are positive for p63, p40, CK5/6, S100, SOX10, and SMA {27583848; 11353064}.

FISH studies on cell blocks and direct smears can confirm PSGT. The CRTC1::MAML2 fusion gene is specific for MEC and useful in diagnostic workups, where the FISH positive MAML2 rearrangement should be validated by MAML2 polymerase chain reaction or next-generation sequencing. Demonstration of MYB or MYBL1 rearrangements or gene fusions by FISH or other methods can help establish a diagnosis of AdCC, and assessment of HRAS mutations is useful for diagnosing EMC {30994537; 33526226}.
