2.0.0.1 Background

Lung cytopathology has experienced tremendous progress since 1860 when Dr L.S. Beale published the first case report describing detection of malignant cells in a sputum sample from a patient with cancer of the pharynx, followed by Dr Papanicolaou’s publications on the detection of cancer cells from the lung {18114650; 14801781; 20983696}. A pilot study for the early detection of lung cancer using sputum samples was initiated in 1945 by the American Cancer Society {14902140}.

The introduction of flexible bronchoscopy with fluoroscopy in the 1970s was a significant leap forward in medical practice. The technique offered the ability to reliably and safely visualize the airways down to the segmental level and to sample peripheral lesions, and specimens could be obtained with multiple minimally invasive tools including bronchial brushings, bronchial washings, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), transbronchial FNAB, and forceps biopsies. These techniques contributed greatly to the diagnosis of both benign and malignant lung diseases. Transthoracic CT-guided FNAB was also frequently performed, especially for the diagnosis of peripheral lung nodules. Endobronchial ultrasound, first described in 2006, uses ultrasound technology to visualize structures in the airway wall, peribronchial lung, mediastinum, and hila. Later, radial endobronchial ultrasound and transbronchial FNAB resulted in another major step forward in the practice of lung cytopathology, allowing both the diagnosis and staging of the disease to be done in one setting.

The utility of transbronchial FNAB is further maximized when rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) of the sample can be offered by a cytopathologist or cytotechnologist, thus increasing accuracy, reducing the “Inadequate” rate of the technique, and facilitating efficient and cost-effective triage for ancillary testing {30195266}. In addition, the concept of the clinical cytopathology laboratory emerged, where the interventionalist can be trained to smear and assess the sample, offering a cost-effective alternative to ROSE that is practised in many countries and is especially well suited to low-resource settings. The integration of molecular testing for targeted therapy further enhanced the utility of lung cytopathology. Currently, approximately 70% of lung cancers are diagnosed by cytopathology or small biopsies {21252716}.

Cytopathology plays a crucial role in the management of patients with pulmonary nodules. This is particularly important considering that the majority of patients with lung cancer present with advanced disease and surgery is not a therapeutic option {30620402}. Thus, cytopathological samples and, where necessary, core needle biopsies or forceps biopsies are frequently the only source of tissue for diagnosis and for the determination of predictive markers. Cytopathology offers a variety of sampling techniques that are complementary to each other and that are often used in combination for the evaluation of lung nodules {21266922; 21841504}. In addition, cytopathological evaluation of intrathoracic lymph nodes utilizing endobronchial ultrasound guidance during bronchoscopy at the same time as FNAB and diagnosis of the mass lesion in the lung is an efficient and safe method for staging of the disease, which in turn determines clinical management and prognosis. Evaluation of pleural effusions is also used for determining stage IV in lung cancer.

The emergence of prognostic markers and targeted therapies required more specific classification of lung carcinomas and a multidisciplinary approach to patient management. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop a standardized diagnostic terminology that allows clear communication of the diagnosis and critical information relevant to patient management and to enrolment in trial studies.

Small biopsies and FNAB are sampling techniques and may not demonstrate the architectural pattern of a tumour (particularly FNAB). The diagnosis by FNAB and small biopsy, which in most cases is a core needle biopsy, and the terminology used by cytopathologists and those reporting core needle biopsy and resection specimens, will vary to a minor extent (see #36991Table 1.01, and #36992Table 1.02). Classification and subclassification of cancers are possible in many cases by integrating the histopathology, cytopathology, and cell block findings with immunohistochemistry, but the subclassification into adenocarcinoma subtypes may not be possible and requires an intact and good-quality small biopsy or excision. In some cases where good FNAB smears are available with or without cell block material, lepidic, papillary, micropapillary, or solid subtypes can be described and mentioned as a component of the tumour (see #36993Box 1.01) [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Thoracic tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2021. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 5). https://publications.iarc.fr/595.]].

Practical considerations

In practical terms, decisions in this volume were made to use commonly used abbreviations, which are listed at the front of the book. Of note, immunochemistry of cytopathological specimens including cell blocks, direct scrapes of direct smears, and liquid-based cytopathology, is designated “immunocytochemistry” (ICC). The term “non-small cell lung carcinoma” (NSCLC) is used, although it is recognized that the term “non-small cell carcinoma” (NSCC) should be used for NSCCs diagnosed in cytopathology and small biopsy specimens in which the type of carcinoma cannot be determined by morphology or ICC or in which even a metastasis cannot be excluded.

Wherever cytopathology features are listed that may show different features in the alcohol-fixed Papanicolaou (Pap) or haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and the air-dried Romanowsky–Giemsa stain (Giemsa), then the stain is noted; for example, keratinized squamous cells are eosinophilic in the Pap and light-blue in the Giemsa. Similarly, described nuclear features may differ in the air-dried and alcohol-fixed stains; for example, vesicular nuclei, which usually represent prominent perinucleolar chromatin clearing, are seen in Pap, but not in Giemsa. The term “Giemsa” has been used throughout this volume for the many variants of it (e.g. Romanowsky, Wright–Giemsa, May–Grünwald–Giemsa, and commercial products) as a shortened form, because practice varies widely. All figure descriptions include the specific stain type and, unless otherwise stated, represent FNAB rather than bronchial brushing, bronchial washing, or sputum sample.

This cytopathology reporting system volume is cross-referenced to the fifth-edition Thoracic tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series (also known as the WHO Blue Books) on the website, and it is not intended to repeat the information presented in that publication. The codes, epidemiology, etiology, pathogenesis, and staging sections of the thoracic WHO Blue Book are not included in this cytopathology volume, and readers are referred to the tumour classification volume. However, the Definition, Clinical features and imaging, and Histopathology subsections are designed to assist the cytopathologist or cytotechnologist in their diagnostic approach; for example, the clinical history and the site of the lesion in some cases is a determinant of the differential diagnosis. In the near future, the electronic versions of the cytopathology text will be directly linked section by section to the WHO Blue Book electronic version, and vice versa. On the website for each text, the individual references can be accessed by direct links using the PubMed reference numbers (PMIDs) in the text. The text aims at being clear, succinct, and able to cover the essential facts and differential diagnosis.

Each lesion or tumour will be presented using the following subheadings:
Definition

This is fundamentally the same as in the corresponding tumour classification WHO Blue Book, with the provision that it can be modified to suit any cytopathological nuance that is required. It is a short, succinct statement.

Clinical features and imaging

These are brief and emphasize any factors that assist in the diagnosis.

Histopathology

These subsections are brief and aimed at assisting cytopathologists in understanding features seen in cytopathology, and readers are referred to the tumour classification. Correlation between histopathology and cytopathology is a core element of teaching and reporting cytopathology.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

These are generally presented as bullet points rather than free text, to maximize the clarity of the criteria, and features are described in both air-dried Giemsa-stained and alcohol-fixed Pap-stained direct smears and, where appropriate, in cytospin and/or liquid-based cytology. Pattern or overall low-power appearance, architecture of individual tissue fragments if present, cell features including size and cytoplasmic features, nuclear features, background components, and other features are presented.

Discussion and differential diagnosis

This subsection discusses and expands on the features and presents any variants or nuances. If a group of rare processes or tumours in the lung, such as soft tissue tumours, is grouped under one heading, different subtypes of tumours can be discussed here. Then the differential diagnosis is presented, focusing on the cytopathological details of the tumour as compared with other processes and tumours. This is a crucial part of the text for all cytopathologists and cytotechnologists, and this is particularly the case in low- and middle-income countries where there is greater reliance on just the cytomorphology. The findings of ancillary testing are constantly checked against the cytomorphology.

Ancillary testing

This subsection is for the presentation of the flow cytometry, ICC, in situ hybridization, and next-generation sequencing that help diagnose the lesion and distinguish the lesion or tumour from other processes.

Specific ancillary testing is presented for each lesion, or potentially each group of lesions, in the diagnostic category chapters, and there is also an overview chapter of diagnostic ancillary testing. The WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology aims to present current best practice in the selection of ancillary testing and recommends that when the ancillary testing results are available, these should be added to the cytopathology report as supplementary reports, producing one final integrated report. The ancillary tests will often augment and refine the cytopathology report, and they may change the category and enable a specific diagnosis.

Sample reports

At the end of each diagnostic category chapter there are sample reports to assist cytopathologists in writing standardized reports. There will be regional historical or information technology–related issues influencing the style and formatting of reports, and the recommendations relate to the components of the report rather than any rigid style.

The WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology

After this first iteration, this reporting system will evolve with usage, research, and the testing of its hierarchical category and related risk-of-malignancy structure. It is a system for categorizing cytopathology cases, so that we can provide information to clinicians that is uniform nationally and internationally, assisting them in making management decisions to achieve a full diagnosis. This is a fundamental difference to the tumour classification WHO Blue Books, which define and classify tumour types. The second aim of the cytopathology series is to standardize the diagnostic cytopathological criteria of each lesion, which will improve the quality and reproducibility of cytopathology reporting. Of course ancillary testing greatly assists this diagnostic process, and best practice in the selection and interpretation of ancillary testing will be provided for all lesions. This is a more similar role to that of the WHO Blue Books.

In preparation for this initiative, an international survey of 82 questions was offered. Participants included pathologists, non-pathologists, and cytotechnologists. The survey demonstrated a diverse practice among various laboratories and countries using a wide spectrum of techniques for specimen processing and handling and various reporting systems {26990836; 32222718}, and it informed the discussions of the International Academy of Cytology (IAC) – International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) – World Health Organization (WHO) Joint Editorial Board.

1.          For small biopsies and cytopathology, non-small cell carcinoma should be further classified into a more specific type, such as adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, whenever possible.

2.          The term “non-small cell lung carcinoma NOS” should be used as little as possible, and only when a more specific diagnosis is not possible.

3.          When a diagnosis is made in a small biopsy or cytopathology specimen in conjunction with special studies, it should be clarified whether the diagnosis was established on the basis of light microscopy (including mucin stains) alone or whether immunohistochemistry was diagnostic.

4.          The term “non-squamous cell carcinoma” should not be used by pathologists in diagnostic reports.

5.          The above classification of adenocarcinoma vs other histopathology and the terminology in <<#36991>>Table 1.01 (p. XXX) and <<#36992>>Table 1.02 (p. XXX) should be used in routine diagnosis, future research, and clinical trials, to ensure a uniform classification of disease cohorts in relation to tumour subtypes, stratified according to diagnoses made by light microscopy alone vs diagnoses requiring immunohistochemistry.

6.          When paired cytopathology and biopsy specimens exist, they should be reviewed together to achieve the most specific and concordant diagnosis.

7.          The terms “adenocarcinoma in situ” and “minimally invasive adenocarcinoma” should not be used for diagnosis of small biopsies or cytopathology specimens. If a non-invasive pattern is present in a small biopsy, it should be referred to as a lepidic growth pattern. Similarly, if a cytopathology specimen has the attributes of adenocarcinoma in situ, the tumour should be diagnosed as an adenocarcinoma, possibly with a comment that this may represent, at least in part, adenocarcinoma in situ with a lepidic growth pattern.

8.          The term “large cell carcinoma” should not be used for diagnosis in small biopsy or cytopathology specimens and should be restricted to resection specimens where the tumour is thoroughly sampled to exclude a differentiated component.

9.          Tumour biopsies that show sarcomatoid features (e.g. marked nuclear pleomorphism, malignant giant cells, or spindle cell morphology) should be initially classified as above in relation to adenocarcinoma; non-small cell carcinoma, favour adenocarcinoma; squamous cell carcinoma; or non-small cell carcinoma, favour squamous cell carcinoma. This diagnosis may influence management. An additional statement can be made that giant and/or spindle cell features are present. If such features are not present, the term “non-small cell carcinoma NOS” should be used, again with a comment on the sarcomatoid features.

10.      Staining for neuroendocrine immunohistochemical markers should be performed only in cases where there is suspected neuroendocrine morphology.
