9.1.0.4 Category: Suspicious for malignancy

The category “Suspicious for malignancy” is used in respiratory cytopathology for cases with adequate material, which the cytopathologist assesses as most likely malignant but where there is a degree of uncertainty as to a definitive diagnosis of malignancy {26990836; 32222718; 27221749}. The majority of cases categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” will have core needle biopsy or surgical excision follow-up showing malignant lesions; the risk of malignancy of the “Suspicious for malignancy” category is 75–100% for exfoliative specimens and 75–88% for FNAB {26990836; 30051969; 32222718; 34319004}.

This category is used when there is insufficient quality or quantity of cellular material in an adequate smear for a definite diagnosis of malignancy, and it has a high rate of interobserver variability; therefore, consultation with more experienced cytopathologists is recommended when assessing cases with a limited cellularity, obscuring or technical problems, or with cytopathological features that may be shared by inflammatory reactive epithelium and carcinoma {30294354}. It is mandatory to review the clinical presentation and any imaging that is available, ideally in a multidisciplinary setting, before proceeding to further diagnostic steps or definitive management.

The high rate of interobserver disagreement varies because the threshold for the diagnosis of malignancy depends on the cytopathologist’s experience, the type of cytopathological preparation, and the degree of cellular atypia. The category is used particularly to avoid a false positive diagnosis of malignancy (which may lead to unnecessary intervention) and so maintain the high positive predictive value of a “Malignant” categorization. Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) has been shown to reduce the rate of “Suspicious for malignancy” {26656954; 28639854; 30195266}, but limiting factors related to the intrinsic cytopathological characteristics of the tumour and technical problems remain. Necrosis, obscuring inflammatory changes, fibrosis, tumour cytopathology that may overlap with benign reactive processes, and prior radiation and/or chemotherapy (particularly for lung carcinomas) may all contribute to a “Suspicious for malignancy” categorization. Tumour size < 20 mm, the location of the targeted nodule, and the experience of the interventionalist procuring the biopsy may result in low cellularity and an inability to give an unequivocal diagnosis of malignancy {26367186; 12527571; 28066604; 17873165}.

The categorization of a case as “Suspicious for malignancy” should not be used as the sole basis for therapy and is not equivalent to a final diagnosis of malignancy {27221749; 32359109; 32222718}. It is imperative to have good communication with the members of the clinical team (with review of all relevant clinical and imaging information) for the determination of a management plan, in order to avoid unnecessary additional risks and costs.

The “Suspicious for malignancy” category can be applied to any malignant tumour type, including non-small cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine (carcinoid) tumours, small cell carcinoma, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), lymphoma, sarcoma, and metastatic carcinomas, and the type of tumour or a differential diagnosis of the suspected tumour types should always be stated in the cytopathology report. The category is equally applicable to any cytopathological study, including sputum sampling, bronchial washing, bronchial brushing, CT-guided transthoracic FNAB, and endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB.

If a sputum sample is “Suspicious for malignancy”, it can be repeated or the patient can proceed to bronchoscopy with bronchial washing and bronchial brushing or endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB with or without core needle biopsy (see #29152Table 1.04, and #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). Transthoracic FNAB for peripheral lesions or where bronchoscopy is not available in low- and middle-income countries is an option. If the bronchial washing/brushing and/or FNAB are benign and correlate with benign clinical and imaging findings, routine follow-up with imaging at 3–6 months can be recommended.

If the cytopathology remains “Suspicious for malignancy” and the clinical and imaging findings are suspicious, repeat FNAB (preferably with ROSE) is recommended and core needle biopsy can be added, although proceeding to surgical biopsy (with or without frozen sectioning) should be considered on the basis of the overall findings (see #29152Table 1.04, and #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).

Ancillary techniques, particularly ICC, may make a specific malignant diagnosis possible, but in many cases the quality and quantity of the suspicious cells may prevent further definition of the diagnosis. Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) as a marker of lung adenocarcinoma and p40 as a marker of squamous differentiation are often used for further classification of non-small cell carcinomas, but these markers are also expressed in reactive cells and do not separate benign from malignant.
