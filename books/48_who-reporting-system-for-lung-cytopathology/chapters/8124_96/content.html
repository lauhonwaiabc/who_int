8.1.2.4 Pulmonary blastoma

Definition

Pulmonary blastoma is a biphasic tumour composed of both a glandular carcinoma, which mirrors low-grade fetal adenocarcinoma, and a malignant stromal blastema component largely composed of primitive undifferentiated mesenchymal cells.

Clinical features and imaging

Pulmonary blastomas occur mostly in middle-aged adults with a smoking history and are only rarely seen in paediatric patients. Presenting symptoms of cough, chest pain, dyspnoea, and haemoptysis, as well as imaging of a solitary circumscribed mass in the lung periphery (frequently with a unilateral pleural effusion), are similar to those of most other non-small cell carcinomas of lung {1849449; 12125652; 18827617}.

Histopathology

Pulmonary blastoma must have both malignant epithelial and blastemal components recognizable microscopically {1849449; 15223963; 31824199}. The epithelial component consists of a well-differentiated fetal adenocarcinoma with simple to complex glands composed of uniform epithelial cells with single, relatively bland nuclei with only small nucleoli. Within the glands, nuclear palisading is well developed, and subnuclear vacuoles are a very characteristic feature. Within some of the glandular lumina are sheets of slightly larger cells with squamoid differentiation, forming morules. Adjacent to or surrounding the glands is the blastemal sarcomatous component, comprising small, uniform malignant cells with short spindle or ovoid contours, a solitary hyperchromatic nucleus, and scant cytoplasm. Very rarely, heterologous differentiation is present.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Moderate to highly cellular smears with two or more distinct cell populations
  • Adenocarcinoma cells have relatively uniform solitary nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and subnuclear vacuoles
  • Squamoid cells form small sheets or morules and are larger with well-defined, denser cytoplasm, but no keratinization is seen
  • Sarcomatous cells are small and homogeneous, with hyperchromatic nuclei and high N:C ratios

Discussion and differential diagnosis

If both components are recognizable, the diagnosis should be straightforward. If only the fetal carcinoma component is present, features including bland palisaded nuclei, subnuclear vacuoles, and squamoid morular elements help distinguish the tumour from adenocarcinoma. If only the blastomatous component is present, the differential diagnosis is from small cell carcinoma, which shows greater nuclear pleomorphism, nuclear moulding, and prominent crush artefact and apoptotic debris.

Ancillary testing

Both components show nuclear and cytoplasmic staining for β-catenin. The glandular component is positive for cytokeratin and often for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1). Neuroendocrine markers including synaptophysin and chromogranin may be seen in the glandular and squamous and morular cells. The sarcomatous cells are positive for vimentin and SMA.
