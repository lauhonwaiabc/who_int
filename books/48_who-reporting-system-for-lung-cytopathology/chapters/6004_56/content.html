6.0.0.4 Risk of malignancy and management recommendations

The reported risk of malignancy for the “Atypical” category for all respiratory cytopathology varies from 22% to 62% [[Layfield LJ, Baloch Z, editors. The Papanicolaou Society of Cytopathology System for Reporting Respiratory Cytology: definitions, criteria, explanatory notes, and recommendations for ancillary testing. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2019.]]. This wide variation is partly because not all studies distinguish between the categories of “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy”. The risk of malignancy for the “Atypical” category also varies with the procurement method. In recent studies that have included an “Atypical” category, the risk of malignancy was 50–60% for the “Atypical” category and 54–90% for the “Auspicious for malignancy” category {26331778; 30051969} (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).

The initial management of cases assigned an “Atypical” category, for all respiratory samples, is correlation with the clinical and imaging findings. If the imaging features are atypical or concerning for malignancy, further investigation is required, either by a repeat cytopathological study or by the use of a more invasive procedure, when available. An “Atypical” sputum sample could be repeated or the patient could progress to bronchial washing/brushing or FNAB (depending on the site of the lesion and presentation), and an “Atypical” bronchial washing/brushing could proceed to endobronchial ultrasound or transthoracic FNAB, with or without transbronchial core needle biopsy if endobronchial ultrasound is used (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). An “Atypical” FNAB is usually followed by a repeat FNAB or core needle biopsy via endobronchial ultrasound or a transthoracic approach (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). Additional procedures may also procure adequate tissue for ancillary studies {27221749}. However, if the atypical cytopathological features correlate with benign imaging and clinical features, then clinical observation for a period of time may be an appropriate option.

Monitoring of “Atypical” case numbers within a pathology department or as reported by individual cytopathologists is essential to assess the quality of a cytopathology service. This may include regular audits of the proceduralists providing the specimens.
