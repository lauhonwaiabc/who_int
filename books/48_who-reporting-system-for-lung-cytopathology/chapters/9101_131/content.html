9.1.0.1 Category: Insufficient/Inadequate/Non-diagnostic

The causes of insufficient/inadequate/non-diagnostic cytopathology specimens include a paucity of cellular material; extensive necrosis; poor preparation, fixation, or staining; and obscuring of potentially diagnostic material by blood, inflammatory cells, mucus, or other material. The reasons for an inadequate specimen should be documented in the report. If there is any atypical material, however scant, in a cytopathology specimen – even if the slides are otherwise insufficient – the case is immediately categorized as “Atypical” rather than “Insufficient”.

Integration into a multidisciplinary evaluation of clinical, imaging, and cytopathological features plays a crucial role in assessing sample adequacy {9316777; 26990836}. It is recognized that in some low- and middle-income country settings there may not be any imaging available with which to correlate the cytological sample.

Any specimen can be regarded as adequate if the material demonstrates a pathological process consistent with the clinical and imaging findings and can be appropriately categorized as “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant”. However, if nonspecific benign cytopathological material is present on the slides and does not correlate with the suspicious lesion seen on imaging, a malignant process cannot be excluded and the implication is that further biopsy or repeat sampling of the lesion or other investigation will be required. Cytopathologists can decide to report these cases as “Benign”, with a clear description of the material found on the cytopathological preparations, and add a caveat statement in the report conclusion that “the material may not represent the target lesion on imaging and further biopsy is recommended”, or they can report the material as “Non-diagnostic”. Each cytopathologist and institution will determine how they report these cases, depending on local historical usage, the availability of imaging to allow for correlation, and billing concerns related to the terms “Insufficient” and “Inadequate”, and they should be consistent and clearly communicate with their clinicians.

The term “Non-diagnostic” is not recommended to be used for cases where there is good benign cytopathological material that does not correlate with a pulmonary nodule or other suspicious imaging, and at the same time, “Insufficient” or “Inadequate” is used to categorize cases where there is a lack of cellular material, poor smearing, poor fixation with artefact and poor staining, or inflammation or other material obscuring the cytopathological material.

Reporting the reasons for inadequacy in a specimen can contribute to improving the diagnostic yield of a repeat examination; for example, to perform an adequate deep cough to produce a sputum sample after a previous “Insufficient” report due to a predominantly buccal sampling, or to control or cease anticoagulant therapy in the case of a pure bloody specimen {32222718}. In the case of an endobronchial brush or FNAB of an endobronchial mass that yields only normal bronchial cells, which is most likely due to a submucosal location or a fibrotic tumour, the proceduralist can change the approach and perform an FNAB under ultrasound assistance or add a core needle biopsy.

After unsuccessful non-invasive or minimally invasive procedures such as sputum sampling or bronchial washing (BW) and bronchial brushing (BB), FNAB can be used under EUS direction, known as endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB (EBUS-TBFNAB), or using CT guidance and a transthoracic approach or an exploratory mediastinoscopy may be appropriate.

In principle, a lung mass on imaging that is > 30 mm in diameter is considered malignant {18195376; 28240562}. If an “Insufficient” result is obtained after several attempts to get a cytopathological and core needle biopsy diagnosis, then a final clinical and imaging correlation is required, and the management decision must be based on other diagnostic modalities, such as open surgical biopsy where intraoperative frozen sectioning can guide the extent of surgery.

It is recommended that cytopathology services regularly audit their “Insufficient” rates to highlight problems with the procurement of the specimens and the reporting of these cases. The risk of malignancy varies with the mode of cytopathological sampling and the imaging characteristics of the lung lesion; it is reported as 40% for EBUS-TBFNAB, as high as 60% for transthoracic FNAB, and > 60% for exfoliative samples {26990836; 18195376; 28240562; 26331778; 31455841; 30051969} (see #29152Table 1.04, and #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).

If a sputum sample is “Insufficient”, the clinical and imaging findings can be reviewed and the sputum sample repeated. This applies both in low- and middle-income countries and in better-resourced countries. If the imaging is suspicious and further biopsy is required, BW/BB, EBUS-TBFNAB, or transthoracic FNAB or mediastinoscopy can be used to obtain material, depending on the site of the lesion (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). If these procedures are not available in a low- or middle-income country, then there may be a need to proceed to biopsy of another site, such as a left supraclavicular lymph node if enlarged, or an open wedge surgical biopsy.

If a BW/BB is “Insufficient”, correlation with clinical and imaging findings is recommended, but in almost all cases, repeat BW/BB or EBUS-TBFNAB or transthoracic FNAB with or without a core needle biopsy will be required (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). Some clinical teams will have mediastinoscopy available.

If an EBUS-TBFNAB or transthoracic FNAB is “Insufficient” at rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE), as many as four or five repeat passes are recommended {29320774}. If the specimen is still “Insufficient”, or if the diagnosis of inadequacy is reported without ROSE at a later date, repeat FNAB with or without core needle biopsy is recommended and again correlation with the imaging findings is required. Mediastinoscopy may be available. Ancillary testing on the cell block may provide diagnostic material, which can allow the initial cytopathology report to be changed to a “Benign”, “Atypical”, “Suspicious for malignancy”, or “Malignant” category. The multidisciplinary clinical team may decide to progress to lung wedge biopsy and/or lobectomy, with frozen section providing information to determine the extent of surgery (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).
