8.1.1.2 Squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma is a malignant epithelial tumour characterized by the presence of keratinization and intercellular bridges or, if non-keratinizing, by immunocytochemical markers of squamous cell differentiation.

Clinical features and imaging

The signs and symptoms of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) are similar to those of other non-small cell lung carcinomas but are generally related to the tumour location, which is more commonly central, causing endobronchial obstruction, leading to haemoptysis, cough, and recurrent pneumonitis. SCC tends to be locally aggressive, with progressive involvement of peribronchial structures through direct invasion, but distant metastases and related symptoms occur similarly to other non-small cell lung carcinomas. With central tumours, the proximity to the carina is a crucial factor for planning surgical treatment {26291008}. Peripherally located SCC may show necrosis and cavitation with superimposed fungal and bacterial infections {29313654}.

Histopathology

Keratinizing, non-keratinizing, and basaloid SCC subtypes are recognized, with the same histopathological features as SCC in other anatomical regions. All histopathological subtypes of SCC can show a variety of unusual patterns, including spindle and clear cell changes, as well as papillary, pseudovascular, or alveolar-filling patterns. Defining histopathological features include the presence of keratinization and/or intercellular bridges, but other tumours may be non-keratinizing or poorly differentiated. Keratinizing SCC is often associated with a granulomatous reaction.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Well-differentiated keratinizing SCC
  • Usually high cellularity
  • Single keratinized cells and relatively cohesive small to larger tissue fragments, including flat sheets
  • Dense, sharply defined cytoplasm with well-demarcated margins
  • Orangeophilic or eosinophilic (Pap) or light sky-blue (Giemsa) cytoplasm
  • Centrally located nuclei, usually large, which may show angular contours and hyperchromatic, irregularly distributed clumped chromatin
  • Polygonal, rounded, elongated, or bizarre cells that can be tadpole-, spindle-, or fibre-shaped or show pseudopodia
  • Foci of keratinization (keratin pearls) in cohesive tissue fragments may be present
  • Keratinized cells frequently show degenerative pyknotic dense nuclei, making it very difficult to examine nuclear details, or they may be anucleate
  • Inconspicuous nucleoli except in basal or non-keratinizing cells
  • Dirty background with necrotic debris, particularly in FNAB samples, due to central necrosis and cavitation
  • Acute inflammation with abundant neutrophils or a granulomatous reaction with multinucleated giant cells and epithelioid histiocytes can be associated with necrosis and keratinous debris
Poorly differentiated non-keratinizing SCC, including basaloid SCC
  • Usually high cellularity with necrotic debris in the background, particularly in FNAB material, and less cellularity in sputum samples
  • Tissue fragments are cohesive, often flat sheets or more 3D, with a variable number of dispersed single cells
  • Cells have well-defined, often dense cytoplasm, which is frequently elongated or spindle, but lacks keratinization and/or intercellular bridges; occasional keratinized cells may be present
  • Nuclei are usually centrally placed, elongated, or pleomorphic, with irregular dense chromatin and prominent nucleoli
  • Basaloid subtype shows predominantly cohesive sheets or irregular tissue fragments with variable palisading at their margins, consisting of small to medium-sized cells with a high N:C ratio, round to oval to variably angulated nuclei with dense and coarsely granular chromatin, usually inconspicuous nucleoli, and a scant rim of cytoplasm; focal nuclear moulding may be seen
  • Basaloid subtype may show pseudoglandular spaces, palisades of nuclei or pseudorosettes, necrosis, nuclear crushing, and stromal hyaline basement membrane–like material wrapping the epithelial tissue fragments

Discussion and differential diagnosis

SCC of the lung can be diagnosed in cytopathological samples when keratinization is present, or when the less common intercellular bridges are visible. Poorly differentiated or non-keratinizing SCC usually requires additional stains to confirm squamous differentiation {22619130}.

Distinguishing well-differentiated metastatic SCC from primary pulmonary SCC is not possible either in cytopathology or in core needle biopsy. HPV positivity in head and neck primaries and clinical and imaging correlation offer the most significant assistance, including p16 ICC and HPV probes {22173119; 31157548; 28949800}.

Squamous in situ lesions sampled in sputa and in bronchial brush and wash preparations are difficult to distinguish from invasive SCC, although invasive tumours usually exfoliate a large number of neoplastic cells with a greater degree of nuclear atypia. Similarly, reactive, metaplastic, and dysplastic squamous cells may show overlapping nuclear features, hence clinical and imaging correlation is mandatory to avoid overcalling these cases as malignant {20490328; 19856422}.

In regard to distinguishing less differentiated SCC from poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas, adenocarcinomas have delicate, finely or coarsely vacuolated, and often eccentric cytoplasm; more oval nuclei with less marked nuclear irregularities; prominent, often single large nucleoli with perinucleolar clearing (vesicular in Pap-stained preparations); and intracytoplasmic and/or extracellular mucin. On the other hand, the squamous phenotype is characterized by dense cytoplasm, well-defined cytoplasmic margins, and coarser nuclear chromatin. In many cases, the two carcinomas cannot be differentiated and the report will be that of non-small cell carcinoma, where ICC is required {35331963}.

Basaloid SCC closely resembles small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) but lacks the predominantly discohesive cell pattern, extensive nuclear moulding, dark and light (salt-and-pepper when Pap-stained) finely granular chromatin, marked nuclear irregularity, chromatinic smearing, and apoptotic debris of SCNEC {21319315}. The distinction is particularly difficult in sputum samples. Owing to peripheral palisading and large cohesive tumour cell tissue fragments, SCC and its basaloid subtype can also be difficult to distinguish from large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC).

NUT carcinomas show remarkable similarity to basaloid and poorly differentiated SCC, but show predominantly discohesive, monotonous, primitive, basaloid tumour cells with a high N:C ratio and prominent nucleoli {32877005}. ICC is required.

The presence of thick basement membrane material wrapping around tissue fragments of basaloid carcinoma can be mistaken for hyaline globules of adenoid cystic carcinoma, and ICC is required.

Ancillary testing

Diffuse positive staining with a marker for SCC and negative staining for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) is used to confirm the diagnosis of SCC, although some markedly keratinized tumours may show a lack of staining with squamous cell markers. In SCC, the percentage of tumour cells staining positive for p40 should be > 50% {30572031}. p40 is considered the most specific marker for SCC, whereas high-molecular-weight cytokeratins (CK5/6, CK14, 34βE12), desmocollin, desmoglein, and p63 are less specific {23740564; 23486764; 30572031}. Diffuse p40 expression and largely negative neuroendocrine markers favour a diagnosis of basaloid carcinoma and exclude SCNEC and LCNEC, which are positive for TTF1. NUTM1 ICC is required for the diagnosis of NUT carcinomas {32877005}. Adenoid cystic carcinoma can be differentiated from basaloid SCC by the dual staining of epithelial and myoepithelial markers, i.e. CK7 and p63, and by the more specific NFIB or MYBL1::NFIB fusions detected by FISH in a subset of cases.

Predictive PDL1 testing, if available, should be done for all advanced SCCs {30718843}. EGFR mutation or ALK rearrangement can occur in lung SCC. Therefore, testing should be considered in young, never-smoking patients {29396253; 27330767; 22228640; 33565277}. EGFR mutations are also common in adenosquamous carcinomas that can appear as SCC in small biopsy and cytopathology specimens due to limited sampling {33679868; 33137577}.
