9.1.0.3 Category: Atypical

The risk of malignancy of the “Atypical” category in the literature is highly variable, ranging from 62% to 100% for exfoliative specimens and from 46% to 55% for FNAB {30051969; 32222718; 34319004}, and not all studies distinguish between the categories of “Atypical” and “Suspicious for malignancy” (see #29152Table 1.04, and #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). The value of an “Atypical” category in a hierarchical system is to maintain a high negative predictive value of a “Benign” categorization and a high positive predictive value of a “Malignant” categorization. A lesion categorized as “Atypical” is more likely than not to be benign, whereas one categorized as “Suspicious for malignancy” is more likely than not to be malignant, and the risk of malignancy increases in a stepwise fashion from “Benign” through “Atypical” to “Suspicious for malignancy” and “Malignant”. The use of the “Atypical” category should be kept to a minimum, and the features of the atypical material should always be stated in the report.

The cytopathologist’s approach to atypical cytopathology is often a two-step process: the first step being the identification of the atypical cells and the second an attempt to resolve the diagnosis by repeating the sample or preparing a cell block and performing appropriate ancillary tests. However, performing ancillary tests on FNAB cases with scant atypical cells is not recommended {29575423; 28639854; 30917368}.

If additional laboratory studies resolve the dilemma, the “Atypical” case may be downgraded to “Benign” or upgraded to “Suspicious for malignancy” or “Malignant”. If the additional workup is inconclusive because of a paucity of available material, inherent ambiguous cytopathological features, or equivocal ancillary test results, the case remains categorized as “Atypical”. Adequately cellular specimens consisting of atypical lymphoid cells should be submitted for flow cytometric analysis to further characterize the lymphoid population. The availability of such ancillary tests in low- and middle-income countries will impact this process.

The initial management of cases categorized as “Atypical” is similar for all respiratory cytopathology samples: correlation with the clinical and imaging findings. If the clinical and imaging features are atypical or suspicious for malignancy, further investigation by the same or a different cytopathological modality, with core needle biopsy as required, is recommended. However, if the imaging and clinical features are consistent with or explain the atypia, either a repeat sample may be obtained or the patient may be clinically observed for a period of time. An “Atypical” sputum sample can be repeated or bronchial brushing/washing or FNAB can be performed, depending on the lesion’s nature and site. If a bronchial brushing/washing sample is “Atypical”, then it could be repeated or FNAB performed (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). An “Atypical” FNAB after correlation with imaging could be repeated, or the patient could be monitored (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).
