8.2.1.1 Carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumours of the lung

Definition

Carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumours are well-differentiated neuroendocrine malignancies showing pure neuroendocrine differentiation.

Clinical features and imaging

Pulmonary neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) are also known as carcinoid tumours and account for approximately 2% of all lung malignancies; they can be subtyped as typical carcinoid tumours (TCs), accounting for 80–90% of cases, and atypical carcinoid tumours (ACs), accounting for only a minority of cases {21043816; 25646366; 25065926}. NETs are not related to smoking, have a median patient age of 55 years {31782060; 21043816; 2981373}, and can arise in adolescents {20620301; 30422774}.

Approximately 80% of lung NETs are central and present on imaging as a well-defined, round or ovoid endobronchial or peribronchial nodule, whereas 20% are peripheral and present as sharply circumscribed intraparenchymal nodules {11896225; 22021498; 2014296}. Clinically, central NETs may present with symptoms related to bronchial obstruction, whereas peripheral NETs are generally asymptomatic. Symptoms related to hormone secretion are rare {28238592; 2981373}. NETs typically show milder FDG uptake on PET scans {17116005; 2014296}, although FDG avidity is increased in ACs.

Regional lymph node metastases occur in < 5% of TCs and 30–50% of ACs. Distant metastases are rare for TCs but occur in 20–30% of ACs {21043816; 23915584; 2981373}. Metastasis can occur to a variety of sites but most commonly involves the liver, bone, and brain {25218177; 30923345; 33227805}.

Histopathology

Neuroendocrine architecture is present, characterized by an organoid, nested, or nodular arrangement; trabecular patterns; cell cords; and frequent rosettes. Cells are polygonal or plasmacytoid, with round nuclei showing uniform smooth nuclear membranes and granular salt-and-pepper chromatin. Histopathological variants include the spindle or fusiform cell pattern (which is most commonly peripherally located {29770932}), as well as glandular, oncocytic, papillary, and pigmented NET {21043816; 25065926}. NETs are subtyped as TC (> 2 mitoses/2 mm2 and lacking necrosis) or AC (2–10 mitoses/2 mm2 and/or featuring necrosis, which is usually focal, punctate, and comedo-like). NETs with mitotic counts exceeding 10 mitoses/2 mm2 are increasingly recognized {30923345; 26960398; 27879513; 32916569; 33171403}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • High cellularity
  • Small, discohesive sheets; larger tissue fragments consisting of plexiform, branching, thin fibrovascular strands surrounding and attached to sheets of neoplastic cells; trabeculae, cords, and single intact cells; a papillary or acinar-like architecture can be seen
  • Pseudorosettes are observed in half of the cases
  • Stripped branching and anastomosing fibrovascular strands without attached neoplastic cells may be present
  • Cells are uniform, round to oval, and occasionally spindle, with well-defined margins
  • Eccentric cytoplasm produces a plasmacytoid appearance, or the nuclei can be central (Giemsa)
  • Nuclei are monotonous, are round or ovoid with smooth contours, and show salt-and-pepper finely granular chromatin (Pap)
  • Nucleoli are small or inconspicuous
  • Binucleation, intranuclear pseudoinclusions, and nuclear grooves may be seen
  • Cytoplasm is moderate in amount and finely granular, with eosinophilic (H&E), greyish-blue (Pap), or red to magenta granules (Giemsa)
  • Numerous naked nuclei are present in a finely granular background related to the release of granules from fragmented cytoplasm
  • Oncocytic variant shows more granularity and deep eosinophilia in the cytoplasm
  • Pigmented variant shows small melanin pigment granules

Discussion and differential diagnosis

The diagnosis can be established by FNAB or by exfoliative samples if the tumour erodes the overlying mucosa {230682}. NET is rarely seen in sputum samples, where neoplastic cells are usually discohesive, although expectorated tissue fragments and large tissue fragments have been reported in patients with haemoptysis {17017452}. In cell blocks, solid or organoid fragments of uniform cells with no crush artefact suggest NET. In the spindle cell variant, the cells present as loosely cohesive and haphazardly arranged curved spindle cells with pulled-out slender cytoplasm {7483991}.

Because of the lower cellularity of cytopathology samples compared with resections, mitotic counts may be difficult to assess accurately {30140036; 9706973; 8551926; 32145220}. Although the presence of mitotic figures, disorganized architecture, cellular atypia, and single cell necrosis are features that were reported to favour AC in cytopathology, distinction between TC and AC is not possible in the majority of cases and is best deferred to surgical resection {23166111; 7483991}. In cytopathology, well-differentiated NET should be reported as “well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumour NOS” because these tumours cannot be graded {30923345}.

Technically adequate sample processing, good cellular preservation, and ICC are necessary to distinguish NET from its potential mimics {15859631; 21240996}. The differential diagnosis includes small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) (see #23219Table 7.02), low-grade adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, and other rare malignancies including adenoid cystic carcinoma and paraganglioma {21240996; 32329967; 29575826; 14696146; 26457802}. NET, especially the spindle pattern, may be confused with bronchial cells on bronchial brush {23166111; 2375219}. Other differential diagnoses include metastatic breast lobular carcinoma and plasmacytoma {31672294}, melanoma for the pigmented variant {20196165}, metastatic oncocytic tumours for the oncocytic variant, and low-grade mesenchymal tumours for the spindle cell pattern {10740615; 18077984; 2992204; 11391606} (see #23221Table 7.03).

Metastatic extrapulmonary NETs are morphologically and immunocytochemically similar to primary lung NET and may require organ-specific ICC and clinical context for diagnosis {31672294}.

Ancillary testing

Between 90% and 100% of NETs diffusely express neuroendocrine markers including INSM1, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and CD56 {30572031; 31345782; 29360191; 29316326; 31913660}. Thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) expression in pulmonary NET varies from 0% to 69%, with higher rates in spindle cell NET and AC {29316326; 25125621; 29770932; 22034892}. The Ki-67 proliferation index is low in TC (1–5%) and higher in AC (3–10%) {33474631; 15644774}. Although this is not included in the diagnostic criteria, it may assist in differentiating NET from high-grade pulmonary neuroendocrine neoplasms in small specimens {30572031; 33474631; 15644774; 12931551; 21043816; 31398281}, but some NETs, particularly metastatic tumours, may have an elevated Ki-67 index {30923345} and definitive classification should be deferred to a surgical specimen. The Ki-67 proliferation index may predict response to therapy in advanced-stage NET {31041430; 25646366}, but it is not widely used for clinical management. There is currently no clinical role for cytogenetic or molecular testing in pulmonary NETs, because they have not been shown to harbour actionable targets of therapy {30923345; 31431620}. PDL1 is expressed in a subset of pulmonary NETs, and immunotherapy may improve outcomes in advanced disease but is not currently the standard of care {32320048; 32532787}.
