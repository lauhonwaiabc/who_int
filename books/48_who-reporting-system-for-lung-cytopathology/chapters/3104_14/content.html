3.1.0.4 Sputum sampling techniques and specimen management

Sputum sample cytopathology is a simple, non-invasive, and inexpensive tool for sampling the airways for the analysis of cellular components and infectious agents and for diagnosing malignant lung diseases. However, with the development of less invasive flexible bronchoscopic procedures, bronchoscopic diagnostic methods including bronchial washings and brushings, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), forceps biopsy, and transbronchial FNAB when available, are more effective and preferred to sputum sampling.

Sputum samples can be collected spontaneously or can be aerosol-induced. Early morning samples on three consecutive days of spontaneous sputum samples produced by deep cough reportedly give better diagnostic yields than isolated samples. After thoroughly rinsing the mouth with water, the patient must be encouraged to cough from the deep airways {3915250}. In patients who are unable to spontaneously expectorate an adequate sputum sample, sputum production is induced by inhalation of nebulized sterile isotonic or hypertonic saline solution. An adequate amount of sample is usually obtained after 15–20 min, during which the patient is asked to cough and expectorate at 5 min intervals. Induced specimens, especially with hypertonic saline, reportedly produce better-quality specimens and better diagnostic yield for peripheral smaller lesions than spontaneous sputum samples {1681343; 11601749}.

Sputum sample cytopathology is used in diagnosing malignant lung tumours in individuals suspected of having lung cancer. It is used in resource-limited settings where bronchoscopy or FNAB are not easily available, or if there are any contraindications for the use of invasive bronchoscopy-related methods. The efficiency of the technique depends on the location, size, and exfoliative tendency of the tumour, as well as the number of sputum samples, with 3–5 being preferable {1546511}. The diagnostic yield is highest for large, centrally placed, and airway-communicating lesions, with better sensitivity for 3-day pooled sputum samples or an increased number of sputum samples. The sensitivity ranges from 27% to > 80%, and specificity is about 99%. The diagnostic accuracy is > 80% with ≥ 3 samples {6308932}. The sensitivity is higher in central tumours (71%) than in peripheral lesions (49%) {12527571}. Because a sputum sample result does not localize the lesion, bronchoscopy is required after a positive sputum sample. Although molecular biomarker-based studies have been performed to detect early lung cancer from sputum samples, it has no role in lung cancer screening programmes because of its low sensitivity {27130532}.

Sputum sample cytopathology can be also used for the assessment of airway inflammation in obstructive pulmonary diseases, the diagnosis of tuberculosis and opportunistic infections in immunocompromised hosts, and in some lung transplant protocols {10619799; 18550615; 14583873}.

Processing of samples

All samples should be regarded as potentially infectious and processed with standard precautions for aerosol-generating procedures. The sputum specimens should be collected in a clean, shallow, wide-mouthed, sterile glass bottle or plastic container and sent to the laboratory unfixed, if no significant delay is expected. In case of delay in transportation, Saccomanno fixative (50% ethyl alcohol and 2% carbowax) has been recommended for collection, transport, and fixation {14063649}. Alternatively, sputum samples can also be collected in 70% ethyl alcohol.

The sample is decanted into a Petri dish and checked grossly against a dark background for tissue fragments and blood-tinged areas using a pick-and-smear method. Smears are prepared from these areas and fixed in 95% alcohol for Pap staining {2923357}.

In the Saccomanno technique, the specimen is blended with the fixative in a high-speed laboratory blender or homogenizer for effective fixation, mechanical liquefaction, and mixing of the specimen; this is followed by centrifugation and smear formation from the cell button {14063649}. Mucolytic agents such as dithiothreitol (DTT) and acetylcysteine (also called N-acetylcysteine [NAC]) can also be used for chemical liquefaction {25881088}. Liquid-based cytopathology, with or without mucolytic agents, reduces the unsatisfactory rate and increases the diagnostic yield of sputum sample cytopathology significantly by eliminating mucus and blood from the background, but it is more costly {25427721}.

Pap staining is recommended for sputum specimens to better delineate nuclear characteristics in the mucoid background. Squamous differentiation and keratinization are well demonstrated, and squamous cell carcinoma is the most commonly detected malignant neoplasm in sputum sample cytopathology. Giemsa staining can also be used, preferably in tandem with the Pap.

In general, sputum is composed of a variable number of inflammatory cells and squamous epithelial cells. Changes in inflammatory cell composition, pigmentation in alveolar macrophages, Curschmann spirals and cytomorphological atypia in epithelial cells signify an underlying pathological process. An adequate sputum sample must contain alveolar macrophages, although no numerical cut-off value has been established {10405813}.

Complications

Induction of sputum production can cause bronchoconstriction {9027440}.
