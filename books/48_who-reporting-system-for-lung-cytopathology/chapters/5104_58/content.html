5.1.0.4 Inflammatory and reactive changes in glandular cells and squamous cells

Definition

Inflammatory and reactive changes in glandular or squamous cells are benign alterations of cytomorphology that may mimic neoplastic lesions and occur in inflammatory conditions of various etiologies.

Clinical features and imaging

Inflammatory, reactive, and regenerative changes may be very marked in a variety of clinical settings, including pneumonia, pulmonary fibrosis, bronchiectasis, pulmonary infarction, and cavitation related to abscesses and granulomatous infections, as well as after instrumentation and tracheal intubation, after radiation therapy and chemotherapy, and in cases related to fungal and viral infections {8384547; 20490328; 19856422; 9407200; 8834320}.

Histopathology

The histopathology varies with the etiology. Papillary hyperplasia shows folding of mucosa to form papillary projections of the bronchial epithelial surface consisting of normal ciliated and goblet cells, with smaller intermediate cells in the deeper layers. Reactive bronchial epithelium may show enlarged nuclei within an obviously benign tissue fragment {8384547}. Basal cell hyperplasia is an abnormal multiplication of basal cells forming several layers, and it frequently coexists with squamous metaplasia. Type 2 pneumocyte hyperplasia shows large cells with cuboidal cytoplasm and large nuclei, each with a prominent macronucleolus, lining the lumina of alveoli, and it may mimic atypical alveolar hyperplasia. Squamous metaplasia can replace respiratory mucosa with non-keratinizing squamous epithelium, while some scattered residual respiratory cells or cells with partial differentiation are present; the metaplastic cells are seen amid columnar cells without cilia or a terminal bar and with intracytoplasmic mucin. Squamous metaplasia frequently coexists with basal cell hyperplasia.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Bronchial cell hyperplasia
  • Large papillary tissue fragments of columnar cells
  • Palisading of peripheral cells
  • Terminal bars and/or cilia present
  • Nuclei are oval and may contain single nucleoli but are relatively uniform within any tissue fragment
Basal cell hyperplasia
  • Cohesive sheets
  • Small uniform cells, scant cytoplasm, and a high N:C ratio
  • Round to oval nuclei, even chromatin, inconspicuous or absent nucleoli
  • Occasional cells show maturation towards columnar cells
  • Clean background
Reactive bronchial epithelium, repair and regeneration
  • Papillary tissue fragments that may be 3D or cohesive sheets
  • Large polygonal cells with abundant cytoplasm
  • Cilia and terminal bars may be lost
  • Nuclei are enlarged and oval, with a regular smooth nuclear membrane and a central macronucleolus
  • Cells with abnormal nuclei can occur within a benign tissue fragment with cilia
Type 2 pneumocyte hyperplasia
  • Large cells
  • Single cells or small round complexes with knobbly borders
  • Some large nuclei with large nucleoli
  • Cytoplasm may be vacuolated
Squamous metaplasia
  • Sheets and single cells
  • Round to slightly irregular nuclei
  • Variable to high N:C ratio
  • Variable chromatin, occasional nucleoli
  • Dense eosinophilic cytoplasm with some keratinization
Cytopathic changes in viral infection
  • CMV: large cells with large round to oval nuclei showing a single large intranuclear viral inclusion with chromatinic rimming, giving an owl-eye appearance; some cells will show multiple small basal cytoplasmic inclusions
  • Herpes simplex virus: virion forms a nuclear inclusion filling the entire nucleus with a frayed nuclear envelope, dark degenerative nuclear inclusion bodies, and often multinucleated giant cells
  • Adenovirus: smudge cells, with mild cellular enlargement and smudged nonpunctate chromatin, often associated with detached ciliary tufts (ciliocytophthoria), which is a helpful finding but not pathognomonic
Chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-related changes
  • Both squamous epithelium and respiratory epithelium are affected
  • Enlarged cells with vacuolated cytoplasm, which may be relatively dense
  • One or several enlarged nuclei, smudged chromatin
  • Large nucleoli occur frequently
  • Squamous metaplasia
  • Enlargement of type 2 pneumocytes
  • Chemotherapy-related changes: appear within a few weeks, resolve within a month or two after finishing the chemotherapy
  • Radiation-induced changes: acute changes appear within 6 months; chronic changes may persist for years or for life

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Reactive changes in the respiratory tract may sometimes exhibit features that make it difficult to exclude malignancy. A spectrum of reactive changes, from mild to marked, favours a benign diagnosis. Bronchial cell hyperplasia and repair may mimic adenocarcinoma. The presence of cilia and/or a terminal bar strongly suggests that inflammatory atypia is present.

Basal cell hyperplasia may mimic small cell carcinoma. The regular shape of the nuclei in basal cell hyperplasia and the even chromatin pattern are similar to those of the ciliated cells in the neighbourhood, whereas cells of a small cell carcinoma often show irregular nuclear contours and a punctate dark and light chromatin with nuclear moulding and chromatinic smearing, with apoptotic debris in the background. Basal cells usually are arranged in flat sheets with a regular pattern.

Reactive bronchial epithelium may mimic adenocarcinoma or non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma. The nuclear changes can be severe in reactive bronchial epithelium and may be misleading. Sometimes cells with severe nuclear changes are found within an intact tissue fragment of ciliated cells, and the changes can be assumed to be reactive and not malignant. A spectrum of changes from normal to atypical, instead of two distinct populations of normal cells and malignant cells, is an important feature in inflammatory changes.

Type 2 pneumocyte hyperplasia may also mimic adenocarcinoma, and a certain diagnosis is not possible in every case. In the context of longstanding inflammation, oxygen therapy, or regeneration after lung injury, type 2 pneumocyte hyperplasia is more probable than malignancy. Usually the number of atypical type 2 pneumocytes will decline over time if the cause of the inflammation is removed. In the case of atypia, repeat studies should be recommended.

Squamous metaplasia may be difficult to distinguish from squamous cell carcinoma. In inflammation, especially in fungal infection and in cavitary lesions, squamous metaplasia including keratinization may show marked nuclear atypia. In squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, keratinized and non-keratinizing tumour cells are usually found. These non-keratinizing tumour cells, which are often arranged in crowded 3D tissue fragments, are rare in squamous metaplasia.

In chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-related changes, the therapy-induced changes can be severe and mimic residual or recurrent carcinoma. Correlation with the history and bronchoscopy findings is important to avoid a false positive diagnosis.

Ancillary testing

In selected cases, ICC may be helpful, and it may be diagnostic of CMV, herpes simplex virus, and adenovirus on cell blocks of suspected viral infection cases.
