8.4.0.2 Diffuse pleural mesothelioma

Definition

Diffuse pleural mesothelioma is a tumour of malignant mesothelial cells showing diffuse involvement of the pleura.

Clinical features and imaging

Pleural mesothelioma is a disease primarily caused by asbestos and/or erionite fibre exposure, with male predominance, a median age of 65–69 years, and a latency period of > 30 years between fibre exposure and disease presentation {29068368; 8460968; 10412176; 9227715}. Mesothelioma has a slight predominance in the right pleura and less commonly involves the peritoneum and (rarely) the pericardium {3059081; 8460968}. Patients present initially with the insidious onset of unilateral recurrent pleural effusions and persistent, slowly increasing non-pleuritic pain, followed by dyspnoea and cough (see #23625Table 7.07). However, a small proportion of patients present with an acute onset of severe pain and dyspnoea, usually secondary to either haemothorax or spontaneous pneumothorax {16989167}. A definitive diagnosis is usually established 2 months to > 6 months after the onset of symptoms {3335886}. Assuming a high performance status, < 30% of patients are treated surgically {20975379}. Tumour seeding may occur in sites of previous invasive procedures {6202538; 7790544; 7656629}. The 6-month, 1-year, and 5-year overall survival rates are 55%, 33%, and 5%, respectively {20975379}.

Initial CXR demonstrates a large unilateral pleural effusion, which may obscure the pleural thickening. CT with contrast is more sensitive and often shows the pleural effusion and pleural thickening (which tends to form a rind that encases the lung and often extends into the fissures and along the mediastinal pleura and diaphragm), as well as the size of the lymph nodes. MRI with contrast is more sensitive in identifying invasion of the chest wall and diaphragm. PET is more reliable in detecting contralateral chest involvement and extrathoracic spread, thus facilitating staging, especially for patients who are considered for potential curative surgery {17075562}.

Histopathology

Diffuse pleural mesotheliomas are divided into epithelioid, biphasic, and sarcomatoid subtypes. Epithelioid mesothelioma is mainly composed of polygonal, rounded cells that infiltrate the pleura most frequently in a tubulopapillary pattern; however a broad range of architectural patterns, including trabecular, solid, micropapillary, and (rarely) adenomatoid and microcystic, may be observed. Cells range from the classic bland cuboidal to deciduoid, rhabdoid, signet ring, small cell, and pleomorphic. Some architectural patterns and cellular features in epithelioid pleural mesothelioma have been shown to have prognostic significance; for example, micropapillary and solid architecture and pleomorphic and rhabdoid cytopathological features in epithelioid pleural mesothelioma are associated with an adverse prognosis {21358344; 22388762; 29327706; 16400322; 31546041}. A nuclear grading system that merges nuclear atypia with mitotic count has been developed for epithelioid mesothelioma and has prognostic significance. Subsequently, a two-tiered grading system incorporating low and high grades and utilizing nuclear grade and the presence of necrosis has been suggested on the basis of data from current multi-institutional studies {21983936; 29327706; 31546041; 32045387}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Often extremely highly cellular FNAB smears with innumerable, often large tissue fragments and or single cells
  • Numerous morular (berry-like), papillary, or tubular tissue fragments showing a wide variation in size; papillary fragments may have green (Pap) or magenta (Giemsa) collagen cores
  • Enlarged pleomorphic cells with wide variation in size, varying from that of benign mesothelial cells to gigantic cells the size of small morular tissue fragments, and numerous multinucleated cells with more than three nuclei and abundant cytoplasm
  • Dense cytoplasm, which can be concentric or eccentric, is green with an outer pale region with intercellular windows (Pap) or shows an inner lighter and outer blue staining (Giemsa); hyaluronan may appear as magenta material in the cytoplasm (Giemsa)
  • Enlarged nuclei (although atypia may be subtle) and prominent nucleoli of varying shape and size, including macronucleoli
  • Atypical mitoses
  • Pseudokeratotic cells with pyknotic nuclei and eosinophilic (Pap) cytoplasm
  • Numerous perinuclear lipid vacuoles detected on Giemsa staining and confirmed by Oil Red O staining on a fresh smear
  • Abundant hyaluronan is seen as a pink or red granular background (Giemsa) in cytospin preparations, and as mucin-like extracellular matrix in cell blocks and direct smears (H&E, Pap, and Giemsa)

Discussion and differential diagnosis

This section discusses diffuse pleural mesothelioma, and not localized pleural mesothelioma, well-differentiated papillary mesothelial tumour of the pleura, adenomatoid tumour, or mesothelioma in situ, where FNAB cannot make a definitive diagnosis at present. Given that as many as 90% of patients with diffuse pleural mesothelioma present with a pleural effusion, the fluid is usually the first sample acquired in the diagnostic workup. Mesothelioma may also be diagnosed by FNAB of pleural-based masses or metastatic sites. Rare examples have been reported in bronchial samples and sputum samples. For sputum samples, the largest published series contained 5 cases where papillary tissue fragments of malignant cells were seen. In 2 of these patients, infiltration of the mucosa of large bronchi by mesothelioma was found at autopsy {3720479}. Only rare, mainly single case reports of bronchial washing, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), or bronchial brushing have described diffuse pleural mesothelioma seen as single atypical cells and small tissue fragments from pulmonary invasion by mesothelioma {14674071; 33133607; 18232009}.

Two studies from the 1980s reported 58 cases diagnosed by transthoracic FNAB {2656144; 3469852}, and a more recent study reported the diagnosis of 4 pleural mesotheliomas by endobronchial ultrasound–directed FNAB {26315262}. FNAB shows a spectrum of features including cohesive papillary tissue fragments, plentiful dispersed single cells, and biphasic or mesenchymal features {3469852}. In any given case of pleural mesothelioma, the cytomorphology in effusion and that in FNAB usually show some resemblance.

In effusions, only the epithelioid type or epithelioid component of a biphasic type may exfoliate adequate cells for diagnosis {26052757}. Sarcomatoid mesotheliomas usually present as a bloody effusion but rarely exfoliate any tumour cells. The reported sensitivity ranges from 30% to 75%, with a specificity approaching 99–100% {23814259; 23222247}. Both the lack of exfoliation in some cases and the subtle cytopathological presentation contribute to the low sensitivity. Grossly, these fluids are very thick in consistency (tar-like), secondary to the hyaluronan secreted by the cells, and they are frequently serosanguineous or bloody. The diagnosis is based on the recognition of the malignant nature of the cytopathology, and this can be supported by molecular surrogate markers {26052757; 30040460; 30259910}.

Mesothelioma usually presents with marked cellularity that far exceeds any reactive effusion; however, not all cases exfoliate with high cellularity and may overlap with that of reactive processes. Although atypia may be subtle in mesothelioma and significant in reactive lesions, the presence of variably sized and complex tissue fragments and often markedly enlarged mesothelial cells should always raise a concern for mesothelioma. On the other hand, the presence of significant cellular pleomorphism is a feature that favours carcinoma over mesothelioma.

The differential diagnosis of diffuse pleural mesothelioma, whether in effusions or FNAB, includes reactive mesothelial proliferations and metastatic carcinomas (see #23626Table 7.08). Carcinomas can show diffuse pleural or pseudomesotheliomatous spread and mimic mesothelioma clinically and on imaging. In rare cases, mesothelioma can present as a predominantly discohesive single cell population, for which melanoma and high-grade lymphoma should also be considered in the differential diagnosis. Metastatic carcinomas often will show a distinct population of malignant cells, usually in tissue fragments in a background of distinctly different mesothelial cells and chronic inflammatory cells.

The differential diagnosis of reactive mesothelial proliferations and mesothelioma can be challenging because reactive mesothelial proliferations can demonstrate striking cytopathological atypia and it is not possible to confirm the presence of invasion in cytopathology. Ancillary testing becomes essential. The Oil Red O stain on fresh fluids highlights the perinuclear fat droplets, which are present in abundance in mesothelioma but of which only few, if any, are seen in reactive mesothelial cells or rare carcinomas.

Mesothelioma in situ (MIS) is an extremely rare entity. It is considered a precursor of pleural mesothelioma, with a published median time to progression of 60 months, occurring in patients with known asbestos exposure who develop unresolved pleural effusions, yet no mass lesions are seen either by imaging studies or by thoracoscopy {31375770}. Instead, normal, smooth pleural thickening or tiny nodules may be seen. Histologically, MIS shows a layer of flat or cuboidal cells with or without cytopathological atypia and/or small papillary projections, or small nodules that may show moderate to severe atypia and demonstrate loss of BAP1 and/or MTAP and/or show CDKN2A homozygous deletion {32307274; 29350783}. The role of cytopathology in diagnosing MIS is controversial. Pleural effusion specimens that are cytopathologically concerning for mesothelial malignancy would raise the possibility of MIS, but it is not possible to exclude MIS from early stages of invasive diffuse mesothelioma on cytopathology even with imaging correlation {33775406}. Although the diagnosis of MIS is not currently recommended on cytopathology, a cytopathology specimen may represent the first clue towards the histopathological diagnosis of MIS. The current literature is scarce regarding the cytopathological features of MIS, with only rare cases reported as retrospectively demonstrating loss of BAP1 on pleural fluids originally diagnosed as reactive, but cytopathology will play a role in this diagnosis in the future as more cases of this extremely rare entity are recognized and reported {29085180; 32814330}.

Ancillary testing

Mesothelioma can show malignant nuclear features and be categorized as “Malignant”, but ICC is usually needed to establish a mesothelial origin. However, if the atypia is subtle, or if the cellularity is not striking or only some of the supportive features are noted, ancillary testing is required to establish both the “Malignant” categorization and precise diagnosis. In rare cases where the cytopathology is either diagnostic or very suspicious for pleural mesothelioma with confirmatory surrogate findings (e.g. loss of BAP1 and/or MTAP expression) but the imaging presentation of a typical diffuse pleural mesothelioma is absent, the possibility of early stage mesothelioma should be considered, and rarely, the possibility of a diagnosis of MIS could be raised {32814330}.

The main roles of ancillary testing are to confirm the mesothelial origin, distinguish epithelioid pleural mesothelioma from adenocarcinoma, and (when relevant) confirm malignancy of a mesothelial proliferation. It is recommended that at least two epithelial and two mesothelial ICC markers should be used {26052757} [[Davidson B, Firat P, Michael CW, editors. Serous effusions: etiology, diagnosis, prognosis and therapy. 2nd ed. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2018.]].

The ability of ICC to distinguish mesotheliomas from carcinomas depends substantially on the origin of the carcinoma, and the differential diagnosis from serous carcinoma of female genital or peritoneal origin is the most challenging. The most robust mesothelial marker is calretinin, but it is not specific and can be expressed (with various percentages) in some carcinomas, such as squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma of the lung, serous carcinoma of the ovary, and renal cell carcinoma {11684957; 23075894; 18306350; 16415794; 17056092; 16056246}. WT1 is another sensitive mesothelial marker, with high specificity in some differentials (e.g. against lung adenocarcinoma {18064689}), but it cannot be used for differentiating mesothelioma from serous carcinoma of female genital origin, which is also positive for WT1. Similarly, D2-40 is a robust mesothelial marker, but it is expressed in the majority of serous carcinomas {16819331}. CK5/6 is expressed in the majority of mesotheliomas, although it is also present in some carcinomas {18064689; 29802871; 26226841}. Tenascin X is another marker whose expression has been reported to be specific for mesothelioma {19738457}. HEG1 has been recently reported as a highly sensitive marker for mesothelial cells and was only expressed in about 30% of ovarian serous carcinomas {32441895}.

BerEP4 and MOC31, targeting the surface molecule EpCAM, are among the most widely used carcinoma markers. However, they stain, at least focally, a significant number of mesotheliomas {18064689}. Claudin-4, CEA, and B72.3 are more specific in this differential diagnosis, with claudin-4 being more sensitive {32184064; 24421209; 27015745}. Less commonly used markers include CD15 (LeuM1) and BG8. Other potential candidate carcinoma markers that have been reported to be absent in mesothelioma include claudin-1 and claudin-3 {17509990}, CD24 {27589896}, and MMP7 {26520416}.

A summary of selected site-specific markers used to establish a primary origin of malignancy and markers that may be coexpressed with mesothelioma is presented in #23627Table 7.09 .

Loss of nuclear expression of BAP1 by ICC is 55–60% sensitive in distinguishing mesothelioma from benign proliferations {31713897; 29802871; 26226841; 30059257; 28213009; 29053210; 31231129}. Both MTAP and BAP1 ICC are 100% specific for mesothelioma in the differential diagnosis of benign mesothelial proliferations, but they must only be interpreted in the presence of adequate positive internal controls {30059257; 29053210; 33493384; 31231129}. Combinations or panels of p16 FISH, merlin (NF2) FISH, BAP1 ICC, and MTAP ICC have been reported to yield a greater sensitivity of 84–98%, which is greater than that of any individual assay {30059257; 28213009; 29053210; 33493384; 31231129}.

The soluble markers in effusions that have been reported to be the most useful in supporting the diagnosis of mesothelioma compared with metastatic carcinomas and benign effusions are mesothelin and hyaluronan, both overexpressed in mesothelioma {25037982; 23991032; 23249166; 22729037}, whereas galectin-1 and galectin-3 are overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma {24361865; 22729037}.

Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A (9p21; encoding p16) detected by p16 FISH distinguishes mesothelioma from benign mesothelial proliferations with a high specificity of 100% and sensitivity of 60–78% {20139227; 28213009; 29053210; 33493384; 31231129; 30885343}. But p16 FISH is less sensitive for epithelioid mesothelioma than for biphasic and sarcomatoid types, which are very rare in effusion specimens but may be encountered in FNAB. It is important to emphasize that ICC for p16 is not an acceptable alternative to FISH {10976692}. Recently, hemizygous NF2 loss (chromosome 22 monosomy or hemizygous deletion) by FISH has been reported to demonstrate 52–53% sensitivity and 100% specificity in differentiating mesothelioma from reactive mesothelial proliferations {33493384; 31231129}.

However, FISH can be technically difficult and truncation artefact can be an issue when detecting deletions, and FISH is not widely available worldwide. Cytoplasmic loss of MTAP expression by ICC can serve as a surrogate marker for CDKN2A deletions. Because of the proximity of MTAP and CDKN2A genes on chromosome 9, MTAP is codeleted in approximately 90% of tumours with homozygous CDKN2A deletion {12796375}. Cytoplasmic loss of MTAP expression by ICC has been reported to be 42–70% sensitive in distinguishing mesothelioma from benign mesothelial proliferations {30059257; 28213009; 29053210; 33493384; 31231129}. Rare cases showed a loss of MTAP expression by ICC but no CDKN2A deletion by FISH {30059257}.

Additionally, detection of any of the molecular alterations commonly seen in mesothelioma, including BAP1, CDKN2A, NF2, and SETD2 by next-generation sequencing can confirm a diagnosis of mesothelioma because these alterations are seen only in malignant processes.

For quality assurance, it is essential that proper validation studies of these methods, particularly in cytopathology specimens, are performed in laboratories providing these tests. Use of cell block material can be helpful for ICC but it should be noted that fixation differences may cause differences in results and, in particular, issues with false negative staining {31398281; 26882197}. Cytopathology specimens, including FNAB smears, are well suited for molecular methods {22619126; 30877381; 27788046; 30917368; 32401054}. Formalin fixation is recommended for ICC with BAP1 and MTAP because they are sensitive to fixatives and may be leached with alcohol {10976692}.

It is important to note that any of these ancillary tests can be used to distinguish mesothelioma from benign mesothelial proliferations but none of them are appropriate for distinguishing mesothelioma from other malignant tumours and should only be used to support a diagnosis of mesothelioma once mesothelial lineage has been established through other ancillary tests.
