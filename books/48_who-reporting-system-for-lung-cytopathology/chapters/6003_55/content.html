6.0.0.3 Discussion and background

The value of an “Atypical” category in a hierarchical system is to maintain a high negative predictive value of a “Benign” category” and a high positive predictive value of a “Malignant” category. An “Atypical” categorization is more likely than not to be benign, whereas a “Suspicious for malignancy” categorization is more likely than not to be malignant, and the risk of malignancy increases in a stepwise fashion from “Benign” through “Atypical” to “Suspicious for malignancy” and “Malignant”. In the “Atypical” category the cytomorphological findings are mostly benign, but there are some features that are uncommon in benign lesions and which may suggest a malignant lesion. The use of the “Atypical” category should be kept to a minimum, and an institution should regularly audit their indeterminate categories to monitor the use by individual cytopathologists.

The main causes for an “Atypical” categorization include the following:

  1. The intrinsic cytomorphological characteristics of the targeted lesion; there may be overlap in the cytopathological features of a reactive process and a neoplastic process
  2. The expertise of the operator performing the bronchial wash or bronchial brush procedure or the FNAB, and the level of instruction and assistance given to the patient producing a sputum sample, which impact on the amount and quality of the cellular and background material on the slides
  3. Technical issues related to obtaining and preparing the material, particularly the making of direct bronchial brush or FNAB smears
  4. The experience of the cytopathologist in interpreting the specimen

Reference(s): {20490328; 2678710; 31606956; 31019760} [[Layfield LJ, Baloch Z, editors. The Papanicolaou Society of Cytopathology System for Reporting Respiratory Cytology: definitions, criteria, explanatory notes, and recommendations for ancillary testing. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2019.]]

Certain pathological processes may produce cytopathological changes that cannot confidently be recognized as a benign process, and which overlap with the cytomorphological criteria of malignant lesions (see #32167Box 5.01).

Cytomorphological findings that should lead to an “Atypical” categorization can be qualitative or quantitative in nature and include the following:

  1. Atypical bronchial or other epithelial cells showing extreme reactive and reparative cellular changes, which may mimic malignancy, occurring in a setting of concurrent inflammatory or infective changes, radiation therapy, or chemotherapy
  2. Squamous or other metaplastic changes that cannot be distinguished from a neoplasm; for example, goblet cell hyperplasia, which can mimic mucinous adenocarcinoma
  3. Low cellularity resulting in only scant cells, with some cells showing cytopathological features usually seen in an epithelial malignancy or a lymphoproliferative disorder
  4. Specimens with background elements suggestive of a neoplasm, such as necrotic or keratinous debris, thick mucin, or apoptotic cells
  5. Spindle cell lesions with bland cytopathology that are likely to be initially assessed as “Atypical” until ancillary tests are able to provide a specific diagnosis

Reference(s): {27221749; 30156772; 12820712; 28659503; 20607685}

When reporting atypical features in a pulmonary sample, the salient cytopathological features should be described and the specific atypical features specified, the differential diagnosis discussed, and the most likely diagnosis provided {27221749; 30051969; 32222718}. The extent to which a detailed cytopathological description is included in a report may vary with local practice, but the aim is to avoid overusing the “Atypical” category as a waste basket, without a clear analysis in each individual case. Inflammatory, degenerative, regenerative, therapy-related, and other reactive conditions, when recognized, should be described as “changes” rather than “Atypical”, and the use of such terms as “inflammatory atypia” should be avoided (see section Inflammatory and reactive changes in glandular cells and squamous cells, and #32167Box 5.01). It is important to assess whether the atypical cells represent a distinct cell population, which in general favours a neoplastic lesion, or whether there is a continuum of normal to atypical respiratory epithelial cells, which is likely to be reactive {27221749; 30051969; 32259828}.

The approach to specimens categorized as “Atypical” is often a two-step process, the first step being the identification of the atypical cells and the second an attempt to resolve the diagnosis by additional workup tests, such as repeating a liquid-based preparation or preparing a cell block and performing appropriate ancillary tests. However, performing ancillary tests on FNAB cases with scant atypical cells is not recommended {29575423; 28639854; 30917368}. Adequately cellular specimens consisting of atypical lymphoid cells should be submitted for flow cytometric analysis to further characterize the lymphoid population.

If additional laboratory studies resolve the dilemma, the “Atypical” case may be downgraded to “Benign” or upgraded to “Suspicious for malignancy” or “Malignant”. If the additional workup is inconclusive owing to a paucity of available material, inherent ambiguous cytopathological features, or equivocal ancillary test results, the case remains categorized as “Atypical”. The availability of such ancillary tests in low- and middle-income countries will impact on this process.

1.          Nonspecific cellular changes due to inflammatory conditions:

·         Loss of architectural polarity in epithelial tissue fragments associated with nuclear crowding and disorganization

·         Cytomegaly and anisonucleosis within tissue fragments

·         Reactive bronchial cells showing nuclear enlargement and oval nuclei with few minor nuclear membrane irregularities or indentations, although usually showing evenly distributed chromatin and nucleoli; these may lack cilia (ciliocytophthoria); the nuclei tend to show uniform changes, including nucleolar enlargement, across any particular tissue fragment

·         Inflammatory debris mimicking tumour type necrosis; there is usually a prominent inflammatory cell component, varying from predominantly neutrophils to histiocytes or granulomatous epithelioid histiocytes

2.          Reserve cell hyperplasia due to chronic irritation and inflammation:

·         Usually encountered in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and bronchial brushing specimens

·         Occurs as small cohesive sheets and more 3D tissue fragments of small cells with an increased N:C ratio; hyperchromatic, small, round to mildly pleomorphic nuclei with uniform coarse chromatin and single nucleoli; scant cytoplasm; no cilia are present

·         May mimic neuroendocrine tumours (NETs)

·         Usually cellularity is low, with only a small number of atypical epithelial tissue fragments present in a background of prominent histiocytes

3.          Squamous metaplasia:

·         Occurs due to chronic irritation and/or infections, including abscesses and fungal or viral infections

·         Round to oval squamous cells with hyperchromatic, round to elongated nuclei and a variable but often low N:C ratio

·         Can be encountered in all types of respiratory cytopathology specimens

·         Can mimic well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma: difficult differential diagnosis in the setting of possible residual carcinoma after radiotherapy, when need to assess changes related to therapy

·         Associated keratinous debris can mimic tumour necrosis

4.          Postradiotherapy and postchemotherapy changes:

·         Usually low cellularity with scattered epithelial tissue fragments showing a degree of irregularity in the cell arrangement

·         Readily identifiable cytomegaly with low N:C ratio and vacuolization, nucleomegaly, and nuclear pleomorphism involving all cellular elements of the respiratory tract

·         Hyperchromatic nuclei with varying degrees of blurring of chromatin and vacuoles

·         Foamy histiocytes are seen in amiodarone-associated lung injury

5.          Hyperplasia and reactive changes of type 2 pneumocytes:

·         Encountered in acute respiratory distress syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and viral infections

·         Enlarged nuclei with vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli

·         Crowded tissue fragments (Creola bodies) with a degree of nuclear overlapping, mimicking malignancy

·         Abundant clear cytoplasm, mimicking mucinous adenocarcinoma

6.          Viral infections:

·         Encountered with herpes simplex virus, CMV, and other viral infections

·         Cytomegaly and enlarged round to oval to mildly pleomorphic nuclei

·         Abnormal nuclear chromatin showing margination and clearing of viral inclusions, and (particularly in herpetic infections) multinucleated epithelial cells; CMV shows classic owl-eye nuclei

·         Bronchial cells with reparative changes including nuclear enlargement, prominent nucleoli, and ciliocytophthoria; nuclei tend to be relatively uniform in size and shape
