8.4.0.4 Pulmonary and thoracic metastases

Definition

Pulmonary and thoracic metastases represent spread to the lungs and thorax of malignancies that arise in other sites.

Clinical features and imaging

Although primary pulmonary carcinomas are common lethal neoplasms, metastases to the lungs are much more frequent occurrences and are associated with very high mortality rates. When assessing any carcinoma of the lung, the cytomorphology and ICC findings should always be correlated assiduously with the clinical history of any previous carcinoma or other malignancy, as well as with the imaging. Adenocarcinomas are the most common histopathological type of metastasis to the lung, including breast, colorectal, and renal cell, and along with head and neck carcinomas are the most frequent sites of origin to produce metastatic deposits, but essentially any type of cancer may spread to involve lungs in all age groups. Many patients with pulmonary metastases are asymptomatic and the lung lesions are initially detected via staging or surveillance imaging. However, in other patients the presenting symptoms most commonly include cough, chest pain, and shortness of breath, and less commonly include haemoptysis and pneumothorax, thus mirroring the manifestations of primary tumours. Most metastases evolve through haematogenous spread in variably sized veins of the caval system, but less often lymphatic vessels or arteries are the routes.

The classic and most common imaging findings are peripherally located multiple, bilateral nodules of variable size with rather smooth, rounded contours. However, it must be remembered that primary carcinomas may spread, producing multiple masses, and patients may, rarely, have two synchronously developed lung primaries {31722085; 32633224; 28463856; 32519151}. The lower lung fields are preferentially involved. Imaging may show a solitary mass, which may appear solid with or without foci of necrosis {31964481}, and metastases (most often from a squamous cell carcinomas primary in the head and neck) may appear cavitated {27838234}. The metastatic nodules can be associated with a prominent feeding vessel {31964481}. Typically, metastatic nodules are smaller than a primary mass but grow more rapidly than non-small cell carcinomas. When tumour spread occurs largely or exclusively in small blood vessels or lymphatics, imaging identifies linear densities rather than a mass. In contrast to primary tumours, which classically manifest a spiculated imaging, metastases have smooth contours. Metastases are usually not associated with hilar adenopathy. For some patients, pleural effusions are the dominant imaging feature.

Histopathology

The morphology of pulmonary metastases generally mirrors that of the primary neoplasm.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Breast carcinomas commonly present as poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas, with minute tissue fragments arranged in single file or in small nests, and (frequently) single cell dispersal; the cells often have a high N:C ratio and eccentric cytoplasm, with some showing vacuoles
  • Gastric adenocarcinomas tend to be moderately to poorly differentiated with less well formed glands, and more diffuse types may show signet-ring cells with large single vacuoles in the eccentric cytoplasm impinging on the nucleus
  • Colorectal carcinoma is typically moderately differentiated, with distinct glandular structures consisting of oriented columnar cells with elongated pleomorphic nuclei in a background of necrosis and mucin
  • Pancreatic adenocarcinomas tend to be poorly differentiated, with poorly formed glands and high-grade pleomorphic nuclei, vacuoles, necrosis, and mucin
  • Renal cell carcinoma is most commonly of the clear cell type, with large cells that have abundant eosinophilic or variably vacuolated cytoplasm and large, often central, round to pleomorphic nuclei with prominent single nucleoli
  • Prostate adenocarcinoma usually shows an acinar pattern, with cuboidal cells and peripherally sited, round to mildly pleomorphic nuclei, but nuclear grade can be high with prominent single nucleoli
  • Urothelial carcinoma is usually poorly differentiated, with cells showing prominent eosinophilic cytoplasm that can resemble squamous cells with large pleomorphic nuclei
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma can display a spectrum of cytopathological features often encountered in well-differentiated pulmonary adenocarcinoma; the cells from both can show round to oval nuclei, powdery chromatin, small and eccentrically placed nucleoli, and intranuclear grooves and pseudoinclusions; in addition, psammoma bodies can be seen in both papillary thyroid carcinoma and well-differentiated pulmonary adenocarcinoma; thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) will be expressed in both tumours, but PAX8 and thyroglobulin are positive in the thyroid metastasis
  • Melanoma metastases usually show high cellularity with single dispersed cells that typically are plasmacytoid, with large nuclei that vary from round to highly pleomorphic with plentiful binucleation, parachromatin clearing, and intranuclear cytoplasmic pseudoinclusions; melanin pigment in tumour cells is variably present and must be distinguished from haemosiderin pigment in macrophages; rare cases of metastatic melanoma, especially amelanotic types, can demonstrate a rich capillary framework and papillary formations leading to an expanded differential including primary and metastatic neoplasms of lung with papillary growth architecture

Reference(s): {33088028; 32894657; 26990836; 27890992; 20490328}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

Metastatic adenocarcinomas to the lung originating from various extrapulmonary sites are known to mimic primary lung adenocarcinomas. Metastatic adenocarcinoma from the breast, prostate, gastrointestinal tract, colorectum, and kidney can be easily mistaken for primary lung adenocarcinoma, but there are some cytopathological clues that suggest particular adenocarcinomas.

It is virtually impossible to distinguish between primary and metastatic squamous cell carcinoma of the lung by cytomorphology and ICC. This requires a thorough clinical and imaging correlation.

Adenoid cystic carcinoma, with its classic features of basaloid cells arranged in a cribriform growth pattern with globules of basement membrane–like material, can be easily recognized as a metastatic tumour in a patient with a known history. However, the high-grade forms of this tumour may be challenging to differentiate from primary adenocarcinomas of lung and from the very rare primary adenoid cystic carcinoma of lung.

Primary pulmonary lymphoid neoplasms are rare and account for < 1% of all lung tumours. Lung involvement by lymphomas from the haematopoietic system are far more common and spread via haematogenous dissemination from other sites or through direct invasion from involved hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes. When a lymphoproliferative disorder is suspected, an aliquot of the specimen should be submitted for flow cytometry and FISH {33499258; 24293460}.

In the paediatric population, > 95% of all malignant pulmonary tumours represent metastases from non-pulmonary tumours. The list includes adrenocortical carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, germ cell tumours, neuroblastoma, nephroblastoma, and osteosarcoma. Some of these tumours display small blue cell morphology and can mimic primary neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the lung {18158138}.

Some primary tumours may manifest a histopathological spectrum of differentiation, with only a certain clonal fraction spreading and developing metastases. Thus, whenever prior histopathological, cytopathological, or ICC slides exist, it is prudent to review the preceding slides or digital images when evaluating a new lung lesion, and always to closely check the patient’s history for previous malignancies.

Although rare, some primary tumours are known to frequently metastasize to lungs, including choriocarcinoma, uterine leiomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma, and germ cell tumours.

Ancillary testing

In the majority of cases, the cytomorphological findings and ICC utilizing cell block material will elucidate the type and origin of tumour in the lung {27561242} (see #31897Table 7.11).

Gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma producing copious amounts of mucin and containing mucinous cells can be impossible to differentiate from mucinous well-differentiated adenocarcinoma of lung. In some cases the ICC may be of little value, because TTF1 can be negative in a subset of mucinous adenocarcinomas of lung, which, however, are usually positive for napsin A. Both tumours will show positive CK20, CDX2, and MOC31 ICC.

Metastatic melanoma can be confirmed by positive S100, HMB45, melan-A (MART1), and SOX10.

There may be a role for p16 in distinguishing HPV-related head and neck squamous cell carcinomas from lung primaries, which are negative.

A panel of TTF1, CK7, KIT (CD117), and napsin A often proves helpful in arriving at a definite diagnosis to separate metastatic adenoid cystic carcinoma from a primary lung adenocarcinoma, which will be positive for TTF1 and napsin A and negative for KIT (CD117) and myoepithelial markers such as p63.
