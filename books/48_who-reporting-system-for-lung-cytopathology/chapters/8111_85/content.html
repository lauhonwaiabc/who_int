8.1.1.1 Adenocarcinoma of the lung

Definition

Adenocarcinoma is an epithelial neoplasm with glandular differentiation and is the most common type of primary lung malignancy.

Clinical features and imaging

At presentation, the vast majority of patients are middle-aged and older adults. Common presenting symptoms are not specific and include cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, and haemoptysis. Relatively commonly, patients present with clinical manifestations related to distant metastases. Most patients are current or prior smokers, but a significant minority are non-smokers.

On imaging, a single peripherally located mass is most often detected, but other presentations include multiple unilateral or bilateral nodules or masses, a miliary pattern, or lung consolidation resembling pneumonia. With CT scans, in general, nodules may be classified as pure ground-glass or non-solid, pure solid, and mixed partly solid partly non-solid, with solid appearances commonly corresponding to invasive tumour. Pleural effusions are present in some patients.

Histopathology

Adenocarcinomas show varying degrees of glandular differentiation, from well to poorly differentiated. The glandular differentiation may include acini, papillae, and micropapillae, whereas other growth patterns include solid, lepidic, and cribriform. With solid growth, the neoplastic cells show glandular differentiation via mucin histochemistry or ICC. The vast majority of resected tumours show heterogeneity with a mix of the architectural patterns, and for prognostic purposes grading is based on the predominant pattern. In addition to the histopathological pattern, lung adenocarcinoma may be classified as mucinous, characterized by tumour cells with clear-cut cytoplasmic and (at times) extracellular mucin, or more frequently as non-mucinous, showing no obvious cytoplasmic mucin production. The cells may be columnar or goblet cells, typically with distinct cell borders. The nuclei are often oval and vary in size, and the N:C ratio is often low, owing to the abundant eccentric mucinous cytoplasm. Rare histopathological variants include colloid, fetal, and enteric.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Variable cellularity, largely related to specimen type: low in sputum samples and usually high in brushings and FNAB
  • Variable proportions of tumour cells varying with specimen type: low in sputum samples and high in brushings and FNAB
  • Varying numbers of cohesive tissue fragments and dispersed single cells
  • Tissue fragments include flat sheets; spheres; papillae with true fibrovascular cores, acini, and glands; pseudopapillae; and micropapillae, which present as floret-like tissue fragments
  • Glandular and acinar tissue fragments show peripheral or basal nuclei and more-centrally located cytoplasm; small, less cohesive tumour cells exfoliate from the surface of tissue fragments
  • Sheets show subtle to prominent loss of polarity and may contain acini that resemble rosettes with central cytoplasm or show a true central luminal space
  • Tumour cells are cuboidal to columnar with moderate to abundant cytoplasm, which varies from homogeneous to granular to foamy to clearly vacuolated, with fine vacuoles more frequent than large vacuoles containing mucin droplets
  • Goblet cells are present in mucinous and enteric variants
  • Solitary, large, round to oval nuclei with fine to coarse chromatin, which varies in staining intensity but often is not highly hyperchromatic
  • Nuclear membranes often show minor irregularities, and pseudoinclusions and grooves occur, especially in well-differentiated tumours, whereas poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas may show marked pleomorphism of nuclear shape and chromatin
  • One or more large and prominent nucleoli
  • Background may include necrotic debris, which is seen particularly in high-grade carcinomas, as well as mucin and psammoma bodies
Less common defined types
  • Fetal adenocarcinoma: uniform cells with small nuclei and subnuclear glycogen-rich vacuoles best appreciated by Giemsa, and occasional squamoid morules, seen best in Pap-stained preparations
  • Colloid carcinoma: low cellularity; tumour cells in tissue fragments and as single cells, often resembling histiocytes, suspended in thick extracellular mucin pools
  • Mucinous carcinoma: flat sheets with irregular nuclear arrangement with typical columnar or goblet cells with voluminous vacuolated cytoplasm and bland nuclei with finely granular chromatin pattern

Discussion and differential diagnosis

One limitation of cytopathological specimens is that the tissue fragment architecture seen may not predict the often mixed histopathological patterns seen in resected specimens. However, histopathological grade does correlate with cytopathological nuclear features (which include size, contour, chromatin patterns, and nucleoli), and nuclear grading may have prognostic value {22083932; 29388266}.

The distinction between adenocarcinoma in situ, minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, and invasive adenocarcinoma of lepidic and related types is not highly accurate in small samples from FNAB and core needle biopsy, and it should be restricted to the assessment of resection specimens.

Poorly differentiated carcinomas are challenging to subclassify by cytomorphology alone, and ICC and molecular studies are recommended {35331963}.

The differential diagnosis of mucinous versus non-mucinous adenocarcinoma of the lung rests on the recognition of malignant mucin-containing cells. Intracellular mucin is seen as single vacuoles containing mucin droplets or fine vacuolation or fine magenta granules (Giemsa) in the eccentric cytoplasm, and it must be present, because extracellular mucin in the background alone is insufficient to make this distinction. When these mucinous carcinoma cells are present in sheets, the nuclear arrangement will show irregularities with focal displacement of nuclei representing the drunken-sailor or irregular honeycomb pattern. Mucin must be distinguished from other vacuoles in the cytoplasm that may push the nucleus to an eccentric position and represent degenerative changes. A mucin stain such as mucicarmine or PASD can be helpful if there is a cell block. The background of both mucinous and non-mucinous tumours is usually clean, with the exception of the fine mucin threads that may be present. This is in contrast to metastatic tumours from the gastrointestinal tract, such as colorectal cancer, that often have a necrotic and inflamed background with a prominent mucinous component {21999127}.

Benign inflammatory processes that are in the differential diagnosis of well-differentiated adenocarcinoma include acute inflammation, granulomatous inflammation, diffuse alveolar damage, oxygen toxicity, fibrosing alveolitis, pulmonary infarcts, and particularly chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. Features favouring a benign process include low cellularity with few atypical tissue fragments, preservation of cellular cohesion and nuclear polarity in tissue fragments that are often flat and small, and a low number of atypical cells. Macronucleoli in the absence of hyperchromasia, nuclear membrane irregularities, and denser cytoplasm also support a reactive process. The following favour well-differentiated adenocarcinoma: high cellularity including a large number of atypical tissue fragments showing loss of polarity; a papillary, micropapillary, or distinctly glandular or acinar architecture; and nuclear pleomorphism with indentations, grooves, and pseudoinclusions.

One benign tumour that may closely mimic adenocarcinoma in FNAB smears is the sclerosing pneumocytoma {24518736; 8291347}. Arranged in sheets and papillae, the cuboidal tumour cells have single bland nuclei with grooves, pseudoinclusions, and single nucleoli.

The distinction from squamous cell carcinoma can generally be made on a constellation of features: adenocarcinoma cells have more translucent eccentric cytoplasm with fine or sometimes larger vacuoles, oval and often less pleomorphic nuclei with relatively smooth membranes, finer chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. However, poorly differentiated carcinomas cannot be distinguished and require ICC and molecular studies on cell blocks.

Ancillary testing

It is imperative to preserve as much of a small sample as possible for molecular analyses, so ICC should be minimized on cell block material, although cellular material can be scraped from Pap-stained and Giemsa-stained smears for molecular next-generation sequencing. The majority of adenocarcinomas are positive for thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) and napsin A and negative for p40; TTF1 and p40 are usually sufficient. Regardless of whether TTF1 is positive or not, if neuroendocrine differentiation is suspected cytopathologically, synaptophysin, chromogranin, or INSM1 should be added, although focal positivity with one or more of these markers does not negate the diagnosis of adenocarcinoma {21252716}.

ICC stains may be confusing in primary mucinous adenocarcinoma of the lung, which may not stain for TTF1 (although it usually is positive for napsin A) and instead may have markers suggesting a gastrointestinal primary. In such cases, the diagnostic terminology should include a statement that the tumour is acceptable as a lung primary if other sites of disease have been ruled out. Mucinous adenocarcinoma of lung is almost never positive for EGFR mutations but shows KRAS mutations {26791238; 25029118; 23208128}.

If there is a history of a specific malignancy and a metastasis is considered, such as from a breast or colon primary, then specific ICC should be considered on the cytopathology material.
