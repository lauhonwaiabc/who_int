5.1.0.1 Acute inflammation and suppuration

Definition

Acute inflammation is an inflammatory pattern dominated by neutrophils, with or without necrosis. It may result in suppuration (the formation of pus).

Clinical features and imaging

Patients with acute pulmonary inflammation usually present with an acute febrile illness with cough and dyspnoea, purulent expectoration, and sometimes haemoptysis, and on imaging there are diffuse or lobular infiltrations {21088086; 29956050}.

Histopathology

The lung tissue is infiltrated predominantly by neutrophils, whereas macrophages predominate in the resolution phase. With added suppuration, the tissue architecture is lost owing to necrosis, and the area is filled with intact and degenerating neutrophils as well as necrotic detritus, forming an abscess. Granulation tissue develops around the abscess {26366400}.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

  • Neutrophils showing varying stages of degeneration predominate in a fibrinous, proteinaceous background with or without degenerative changes
  • Acutely inflamed tissue fragments of bronchial epithelial cells and fragments of inflamed alveolar septa (in FNAB smears)
  • Variable numbers of macrophages, eosinophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells, which increase during resolution
  • Detritus and cellular debris in the background
  • Bacteria (which can be of a single type or mixed types) or fungal elements are commonly seen in Pap-stained and Giemsa-stained specimens
  • Aspiration pneumonia has pus and foreign-body (often birefringent) vegetable material and non-birefringent muscle fragments, as well as multinucleated giant cells with squamous metaplasia in the epithelial component in later specimens
  • Pulmonary infarct presents with hyperplastic bronchial cells showing nuclear atypia, haemosiderophages, and a variable neutrophilic and macrophagic component
  • Lipoid pneumonia shows lipid-laden macrophages and a variable number of neutrophils

Discussion and differential diagnosis

An acute inflammatory reaction may occur in a variety of clinical conditions, including acute pneumonia caused by bacterial, fungal, or mycobacterial infection; pulmonary infarction; lung abscess; and aspiration of foreign material. Some infectious agents that induce a granulomatous reaction in immunocompetent patients may result in a suppurative inflammation in immunocompromised patients. This holds true especially for mycobacterial and fungal infections. In aspiration pneumonia, fragments of birefringent plant cells and muscle fibres derived from food may be observed. Acute inflammation can also be seen in various vasculitides. Acute inflammation is uncommon in malignancies unless there is an associated pneumonia related to bronchial obstruction. An acute inflammatory reaction should induce a thorough search for the causative agent. Many bacteria and fungi are visible in routine stains (see #32162Table 4.03, in section Granulomatous disorders).

In pulmonary infarction and in other severe acute inflammatory changes, hyperplasia and atypia of bronchial epithelium may occur and present with marked nuclear and cytoplasmic changes. The affected epithelium shows enlarged nuclei, often with prominent nucleoli, and nucleomegaly can occur. Cytoplasmic changes include clear vacuoles and loss of cilia or the terminal bar. Squamous metaplasia is frequently present, often with nuclear atypia. The differential diagnosis of these changes includes adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. The most important distinguishing feature is the cytomorphological continuum: from normal epithelium, to slight changes, to more severe changes, and finally to reactive atypia in severe inflammation. If cilia are found, this strongly supports a benign inflammatory process. Often, suspicious nuclear findings can also be seen in neighbouring ciliated cells.

Ancillary testing

Many bacteria and fungi can be seen in routine Giemsa and Pap staining, but PASD, Gram, acid-fast stains, and the Grocott methenamine silver stain applied to direct smears, cytospins, and cell blocks are all required to adequately assess suppurative material {27555237}. ICC on cell block material can be used, particularly for viral infections. Cultures for bacteria and fungi, and PCR for viral infections, mycobacteria, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and other fungi, are all indicated {30156776; 30889574; 27555237}.
