9.1.0.2 Category: Benign

The published risk of malignancy for pulmonary cytopathology cases categorized as “Benign” is highly variable and differs somewhat between the different types of cytopathology specimens: 0–42% for sputum, 32–42% for bronchial washing (BW) and bronchial brushing (BB), and 19–64% for FNAB specimens {7716347; 26990836; 25640352; 30051969; 34319004; 32222718}.

The “Benign” cytopathology report should always be correlated with the clinical and imaging features. The aim of the cytopathology study is to exclude malignancy and diagnose infections, various inflammatory processes, and benign neoplasms. If the cytopathology report correlates with the clinical and imaging findings, and confirms an inflammatory process or a benign neoplasm, then recommended routine follow-up may be to review the patient in 3–6 months after treatment of any infection, or to get an immediate surgical assessment if the patient has symptoms related to the neoplasm, such as respiratory obstruction (see #29152Table 1.04, and #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).

If a sputum sample “Benign” report does not correlate with the clinical and/or imaging findings, then BW, BB, and FNAB should be considered to further define the lesion, depending on the site of the lesion and the status of the patient (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). These techniques have a combined sensitivity of 88% {27753244; 8432124}.

Infectious or granulomatous entities require the cytopathologist or cytotechnologist at rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) or in the laboratory to triage the sample for further workup, including cultures, drug sensitivities, and PCR molecular testing, and to possibly recommend serological analysis {30889574}. BW and BB have a low diagnostic yield for detecting granulomatous disease; however, BW when combined with sputum sampling has a sensitivity of 88% for detecting acid-fast bacilli. Postbronchoscopy sputum samples can also be collected, with a sensitivity of 35%. Unless the samples are transferred immediately, they should be kept at −4°C.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is the method of choice for the diagnosis of a wide range of interstitial lung disease, with a sensitivity of 85% alone and 94% when combined with biopsy {3044685; 27753244}. If a BW, BB, or BAL “Benign” report does not correlate with the clinical and/or imaging findings, then endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB or transthoracic FNAB should be considered (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology).

In general, endobronchial ultrasound–guided FNAB is best for central submucosal lesions, and transthoracic FNAB is best for peripheral lesions {27753244; 30195266}. CT-directed sampling can be performed for well-defined nodules or non-infectious tumour-like lesions. Benign lesions account for 30% of transthoracic FNAB specimens {26990836}. If an FNAB does not correlate with the clinical and/or imaging findings, transbronchial core needle biopsy should be considered immediately if ROSE has been performed, or at a later procedure (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). If transbronchial core needle biopsy is not available, open surgical biopsy should be considered. ROSE plays a pivotal role in the workup of both infectious and neoplastic lesions. It allows efficient and cost-effective triage of the sample and has been demonstrated to reduce the need for subsequent procedures {30195266; 32401054; 30889574}.
