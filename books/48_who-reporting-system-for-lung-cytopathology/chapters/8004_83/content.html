8.0.0.4 Risk of malignancy and management recommendations

The reported risk of malignancy for a sputum sample categorized as “Malignant” is 100%; however, the sensitivity of sputum sample cytopathology is as low as 5%, particularly for more peripheral lung cancers {9306936; 12485170} (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). If a sputum sample is categorized as “Malignant”, the next step should be bronchoscopy to identify central lesions, and imaging including CXR and CT for peripheral lesions {1495303}. Lesions detected can then undergo endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB or transthoracic FNAB depending on their site and the available biopsy expertise. Supplementing bronchoscopy, autofluorescence bronchoscopy has improved the detection of preinvasive and malignant endobronchial lesions, and narrow-band imaging is useful for detecting the microvascular network of bronchial mucosa {17291623; 20541831}. If these further procedures are negative, close follow-up of initially undetectable cases with repeat sputum sample studies and imaging is required to localize carcinoma in cases with positive sputum sample cytopathology.

The reported risk of malignancy for exfoliative cytopathology, i.e. bronchial wash and bronchial brush, is 94–100% (see #29152Table 1.04, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). A bronchial brush, bronchial wash, or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is typically performed for the evaluation of peripheral lung lesions, which are usually small in size, solitary, and not visible on bronchoscopy {23649436}. For a peripheral non-small cell lung carcinoma without evidence of distant metastasis, further management is dependent on imaging: a small primary tumour (< 30 mm) without evidence of mediastinal lymph node involvement can directly undergo surgical resection if not contraindicated by poor lung function or comorbidities.

Mediastinal staging by endobronchial ultrasound–guided transbronchial FNAB is indicated in cases where there are enlarged mediastinal or hilar lymph nodes measuring > 10 mm in their short axis on CT or there is uptake on PET, or where the primary tumour is > 30 mm {28881918}. If the FNAB does not yield a diagnosis, transthoracic FNAB and/or core needle biopsy or surgical resection (depending on the site and size of the lesion) are recommended.

The reported risk of malignancy for FNAB specimens categorized as “Malignant” at endobronchial ultrasound is 87–100% {26331778; 32353212; 30051969} (see #29153Table 1.05, in section Chapter 1: Introduction to the WHO Reporting System for Lung Cytopathology). The cytopathology specimen frequently provides material for further specific diagnostic testing using ICC, FISH, and next-generation sequencing performed on cell block and direct smear slide scrapings. If there is correlation between the clinical, imaging, and cytopathology findings, the decision to proceed to definitive treatment may be made. If there is insufficient material for ancillary testing or if there is no correlation that the lesion is malignant, then repeat FNAB with or without core needle biopsy should be considered. Each case should, as far as possible, be reviewed with full correlation and discussion in a multidisciplinary setting to establish a management plan.
