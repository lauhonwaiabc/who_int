3.2.0.3 Molecular testing

Introduction

With recent progress in treatment options for patients with advanced, inoperable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), predictive biomarkers – including morphological subtype, PDL1 status, and the results of targetable oncogenic driver molecular alterations – are a prerequisite for deciding on the best possible therapy {30285222} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [Internet]. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2021 [accessed 2021 Feb 23]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org.]]. In particular, adenocarcinoma represents a group of distinct molecular subtypes defined by oncogenic driver alterations, comprising gene mutations, rearrangements, and amplifications {27013409}. Molecular subtyping with matched targeted therapy has led to unprecedented improvements in outcomes in a significant subset of patients with NSCLC {32786189; 33433946}.

Metastatic non-squamous NSCLC should be tested for targetable oncogenic driver alterations {30285222; 29398453} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [Internet]. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2021 [accessed 2021 Feb 23]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org.]]. The primary tumour or a metastasis can be investigated {29398453}. As treatment options are rapidly evolving, broad molecular profiling is encouraged as the best standard of care, and, when available, this will enable enrolment in clinical trials and access to the off-label use of drugs. Routine molecular testing is not recommended for pure squamous cell carcinoma, except in select patients with a history of never smoking or light smoking, those of a young age, and those whose malignancies have been diagnosed by small biopsy or cytopathology samples, where the glandular component of an underlying adenosquamous carcinoma may not have been sampled {29398453}. In the case of acquired resistance while under targeted therapy, molecular reanalysis of metastases at the time of progression enables optimal adjustment of treatment.

Advances in minimally invasive sampling techniques have made cytopathology specimens highly relevant as a source of material for predictive biomarker testing. Often lung cancer is diagnosed by cytopathology alone. Frequently cytopathology may provide complementary tumour material to small biopsies, which may have a limited number of tumour cells and fall short of adequate material for molecular testing {28084342; 31913583}. Assessment of all available tumour material with optimal triage and processing is the key to obtaining all treatment-relevant information. Current guidelines acknowledge that any cytopathology preparation, including direct smears, cytospins, liquid-based preparations, or cell block, can be used for predictive molecular testing {29398453}. As discussed below, a wide range of molecular techniques, with varying requirements on tumour cell fraction and quantity, are applicable to cytopathology specimens, provided the assays have been validated for cytopathological substrates and a robust and rigorous quality assurance is in place {32401054}.

Technical requirements

Successful molecular testing of lung cancer cytopathology samples is directly dependent on specimen adequacy {30731462; 29494220}. Sequencing-based molecular testing has two basic input requirements: the amount of nucleic acid yield, which is based on the overall cellularity of the specimen; and the tumour to neoplastic cell or neoplastic nuclear fraction {27333361; 28739858}. A minimal amount of amplifiable DNA/RNA is required to obtain a result, and a minimum neoplastic cell fraction corresponding to the analytical sensitivity of the assay is essential to detect a mutation in a background of wildtype signal {27333361; 28739858; 30600323}. When DNA/RNA is insufficient, no result is obtained, and this is regarded as “uninformative”, whereas an insufficient neoplastic cell fraction can lead to false negative results, regarded as “misinformative”. The minimum requirement for the neoplastic cell fraction depends on the molecular assay used and the analytical sensitivity of the assay. Enrichment of neoplastic cells by macrodissection or microdissection may be necessary to avoid false negative results due to contaminating DNA from admixed benign cells.

Rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) by a cytopathologist or trained cytology biomedical scientist can improve the adequacy of cytopathology samples for molecular studies. Cytopathologists are pivotal in the preanalytical evaluation of cytopathological samples for molecular analysis. In this setting, their role is to review the adequacy of the cytopathological material in terms of the neoplastic cell content and the presence of contaminants, which may either lead to false negative results due to non-neoplastic cells or necrosis, or inhibit the amplification process because of mucus.

Besides specific analytical considerations that are dependent on the given molecular technique, standardized protocols for appropriate test request, specimen collection, fixation, processing, staining, storage conditions, tumour fraction enrichment, nucleic acid quality/quantity assessment, and quality assurance / quality control metrics are also crucial {27678269; 33119213; 32726941}. In particular, assessment of the quantity and quality of nucleic acids requires several techniques to be used in a complementary manner {28739858}. Routine lung cancer cytopathological samples may yield only very limited amounts of nucleic acid. Lowering the input DNA concentration below the manufacturer’s recommended threshold may be feasible in cytopathological samples, but the DNA input requirements vary between different sequencing platforms {29494220; 31021538}. Although the exponential increase of target DNA via PCR usually leads to technically adequate results, the total mutation burden may drop below the lower threshold of detection for the assay. Therefore, assay validation with low-input DNA levels is crucial to reliably process cytopathological samples {28739858}. Indeed, a number of factors can affect the yield and quality of DNA. Fully frosted slides yield lower amounts of DNA, partly because of their high cellular adhesion capability, which poses difficulty when dislodging cellular material during tissue extraction {26630358}. Significantly higher DNA yield has been reported from slides using a low-hazard, organic, polymer-based mounting medium when compared with the xylene-based mounting medium {28739858}.

Management of types of cytopathological specimens

DNA and RNA can be extracted from different types of routine cytopathological specimens. Different approaches are used for processing the specimens depending on individual laboratory preferences. Any of the resulting cytopathology specimen preparations and stains are suitable for molecular testing {29575423; 29547001}.

As previously mentioned, cytomorphological assessment of adequacy and careful selection of specimens are crucial for reliable results. A cytopathological slide or an area on a slide with adequate tumour cell fraction and quantity is selected. The coverslip is removed using xylene and the selected cells on the slide are transferred into buffer medium for nucleic acid extraction without destaining the cells. The most common method for transfer of tumour cells is macrodissection by scraping off the selected area of tumour cells with a scalpel blade. Laser capture microdissection has the benefit of enabling the harvesting of tumour cells from specimens with a very low tumour cell fraction {28659503; 29575423; 28739858}. In cases where only a single diagnostic slide is available for molecular testing, whole slide image scanning and digital archiving of the slide for cytomorphological evidence of malignancy, before sacrificing the slide for molecular testing, will avoid medicolegal issues {28739858}. Cell blocks can be processed according to biopsy protocols, using an H&E-stained section for the assessment of adequacy.

In addition to the analysis of intact tumour cells, the analysis of cell-free tumour DNA or liquid biopsy from peripheral blood, residual liquid-based cytopathology fluid, supernatants of FNAB, and effusion or cerebrospinal fluid material has become an option for molecular testing in selected patients. Because the sensitivity of liquid biopsy is still lower than that of tumour samples, a negative liquid biopsy cannot exclude a false negative result and requires molecular testing of a tumour sample for confirmation {31751001; 31212145; 29885479}.

Fixation

Among preanalytical factors affecting the quality of molecular assays, fixation is key. As a general rule, non-cross-linking fixation using alcoholic reagents (such as wet or spray fixatives) or air-drying provides higher-quality nucleic acids than fixation in formalin. In fact, a prolonged fixation time in formalin leads to C>T sequence artefact, a potential pitfall of cell block–based molecular assays {16452569}. In general, air-dried and ethanol-fixed slides provide high-quality nucleic acids and similar results in terms of DNA quality {28739858; 27333361}. Assessment of nucleic acid quantity and quality to assure successful sequencing-based molecular testing requires several techniques to be used in a complementary manner, including spectrophotometer analysis, fluorescence assays, capillary electrophoresis, and quantitative PCR assays {28739858}.

Controls

Several single-institution studies have shown that the quality of next-generation sequencing (NGS) multigene mutation assays on lung cancer cytopathological specimens is high. However, multi-institutional studies are needed to exclude potential sources of inconsistent results due to differences in gene panels, target enrichment strategies, and platforms across institutions. Because the cell material from routine cytopathological samples is limited, the availability of control specimens is an issue. To get around this limitation, genetically engineered cell lines can be used to mimic actual clinical cytopathological samples in routine practice. However, genetically engineered cell lines have traditionally been only available as artificial formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded histopathological sections or scrolls. More recently, to fill this gap, the Molecular Cytopathology Meeting Group has developed molecular reference slides harbouring concurrent mutations in the EGFR, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and PIK3CA genes at various allelic ratios, including low allele frequencies (1%), to mimic actual direct smears {28475299; 31021538}. In multicentre international studies, all participating laboratories successfully detected the mutations in the study set, with minimal variations despite the use of different sequencing platforms. Therefore, quantitative molecular reference slides are a useful tool for monitoring the performance of different multigene mutation assays, and this could lead to better standardization of molecular cytopathology procedures.

Procedures

A variety of molecular testing methodologies are used for interrogating NSCLC specimens (see #29404Table 2.06). These include mutation analysis methods ranging from single-gene-based assays, such as Sanger sequencing, real-time PCR, or multiplexed PCR assays, to broad and expanded molecular profiling approaches such as NGS, as well as testing for gene rearrangements using FISH, ICC, targeted real-time PCR, and NGS assays {25738220; 23551194}. In general, testing using a broad, expanded panel-based approach is recommended over single-gene-based testing approaches to ensure the adequacy of small thoracic specimens for the evaluation of all the clinically relevant molecular targets required for therapeutic decision-making [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2021 Mar 2]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]].

NGS is a massively parallel sequencing platform that can simultaneously screen (depending on the design of the assay) for a variety of genetic alterations, including single-nucleotide variations, indels, copy-number changes, gene fusions (including the ability to detect novel fusion partners), tumour mutation burden, and microsatellite instability. In general, RNA-based testing is superior to DNA-based testing for fusion detection using NGS.

Molecular targets for NSCLC are a rapidly evolving field, with nine biomarkers currently recommended by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, several of which are also recommended by the College of American Pathologists / International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer / Association for Molecular Pathology (CAP/IASLC/AMP) guidelines and endorsed by the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]] {29401004; 29355391}. #24273Table 2.07 summarizes some of the guideline-recommended molecular targets in NSCLC and the commonly used testing methodologies.

Other emerging molecular targets have been identified, including high-level MET amplification, ERBB2 (HER2) mutations, and tumour mutation burden, and they may potentially benefit some patients with NSCLC [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2021 Mar 2]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]]. Testing for these rare driver variants is recommended as part of a broader molecular profiling to identify patients who may benefit from a specific targeted therapy or may be eligible for clinical trials for these targeted agents.

Current guidelines recommend tissue-based testing for patients with NSCLC, with cell-free tumour DNA liquid biopsy testing considered in situations where there is insufficient material for testing or the patient is medically unfit for more-invasive tissue sampling {29355391}.

Diagnostic applications

Testing for predictive markers in NSCLC is performed as a standard of care to identify patients eligible for targeted therapy or immunotherapy, and it is based on studies showing improvements in overall survival when compared with conventional chemotherapy regimens {29355391} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]].

The presence of sensitizing mutations in EGFR (exons 18–21) predicts the response to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy {15118073; 15118125; 15329413}. Sensitizing EGFR mutations are detected in approximately 10% of White patients with NSCLC but can be found at a much higher prevalence (as high as 50%) in Asian patients. Patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion mutations are usually resistant to EGFR TKI, although a subset of exon 20 alterations confer TKI sensitivity {23328547}. Therefore, knowledge of the specific EGFR exon 20 mutation is critical for guiding therapeutic decisions.

BRAF p.V600E mutations are seen in a very small subset (1–2%) of patients with lung adenocarcinoma and respond to combination therapy using dabrafenib and trametinib {27283860} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]]. Although non-p.V600E mutations in BRAF are seen in patients with NSCLC, these are currently not targeted by specific therapeutic agents.

ALK and ROS1 fusions have been identified in a small subset of patients with NSCLC (5% and 2%, respectively) and show various fusion partners, with all fusion variants predictive of a response to appropriately targeted therapy {20979469; 22919003}.

NTRK1/2/3 fusions are seen in a wide range of solid tumours, including in 0.2% of patients with lung cancer. Patients with NSCLC harbouring NTRK gene fusions are eligible for treatment with NTRK inhibitors as a first-line or subsequent therapy option {29466156} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]].

The presence of MET exon 14–skipping mutations, seen in 3–4% of patients with NSCLC, predicts responsiveness to selective c-Met (MET) inhibitors {32877583} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]].

RET rearrangements are found in 1–2% of patients with NSCLC, and testing is recommended to determine eligibility for targeted therapy with selective RET inhibitors {32846060} [[National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). NCCN clinical practice guidelines in oncology (NCCN guidelines): Non-small cell lung cancer. Version 3.2022. Fort Washington (PA): NCCN; 2022 [accessed 2022 Apr 30]. Available from: https://www.nccn.org/professionals/physician_gls/default.aspx.]].

Unfortunately, the development of secondary resistance to first-line targeted treatment is inevitable. Molecular resistance mechanisms in general include on-target secondary mutations and off-target pathway activations; for example, EGFR TKI therapy can lead to acquired resistance mechanisms including an EGFR exon 20 p.T790M mutation in 60% of patients under first- or second-generation EGFR TKI therapy, and the acquisition of MET or ERBB2 amplifications accompanied by histopathological transformation to small cell carcinoma {18483355; 15737014}.

PDL1 testing and implications for therapy are covered elsewhere in this volume.

Basic terminology

Definition

Testing recommendation

Analytical threshold

Minimal input requirement for successful testing as determined by laboratory validation (LOD)

Analytical performance of an assay is measured by its accuracy, precision, analytical specificity, and analytical sensitivity (LOD); establishment of the LOD is critical for sequencing assays used to detect the presence of low-frequency variants (SNVs, indels) and is defined as the lowest allelic frequency at which the mutant alleles can be consistently detected in ≥ 95% of the test cases

Preanalytics

Collection, processing, and handling of specimens

Specimens must be of sufficient quality to be successfully analysed by molecular testing methods

Tumour fraction (T%) or neoplastic cell fraction

Percentage of tumour/neoplastic cell nuclei in a sample (total number of tumour/neoplastic cell nuclei relative to total number of all nucleated cells)

For NGS:

1. T% ≥ 20: reliable NGS readout

2. T% < 20: use one of the following:

a. single-target mutation analysis of high sensitivity (PCR-based methods such as Droplet Digital PCR)

b. NGS testing with enrichment of tumour cells (laser microdissection)

c. NGS with molecular barcodes that label each original DNA fragment within the sample and thereby increase analytical sensitivity and specificity

Input DNA/RNA

Minimum amount of DNA/RNA to obtain valid results

Nucleic acid input for NGS testing depends on the assay and platform used, gene panel size, target enrichment method, and DNA quality

LOD, lowest limit of detection; NGS, next-generation sequencing.

Molecular alteration

Commonly used testing methodology

EGFR mutations

·     Most common (~90%) EGFR sensitizing mutations include exon 19 deletion and p.L858R point mutation in exon 21; predictive of response to EGFR TKIs

·     Exon 20 mutations (usually insertions) are heterogeneous, with a subset that respond to TKI therapy and therefore require knowledge of specific mutation

·     EGFR p.T790M mutation is the most common resistance mechanism to first- and second-generation TKI therapy, detection of which warrants switching to third-generation TKIs

·     Sanger sequencing with tumour enrichment

·     Real-time PCR

·     Next-generation sequencing

ALK rearrangements

·     Most common fusion partner is EML4, but variety of fusion partners identified

·     Predictive of response to ALK TKIs

·     FISH using break-apart probes

·     Immunochemistry (D5F3 clone; 5A4 clone); may be used as a standalone test without confirmation

·     Next-generation sequencing

ROS1 rearrangements

·     Variety of fusion partners identified including CD74, SLC34A2, CCDC6, GOPC (FIG)

·     Predictive of response to ROS1 TKIs

·     FISH using break-apart probes

·     Immunochemistry (D4D6 clone); can be used as screening, but if positive requires confirmation by molecular test

·     Next-generation sequencing

BRAF p.V600E mutations

·     Activating mutations result in unregulated MAPK/ERK signalling

·     Predictive of response to combined therapy using BRAF and MEK inhibitors

·     Real-time PCR

·     Sanger sequencing with tumour enrichment

·     Next-generation sequencing

·     BRAF p.V600E antibody for immunochemistry not extensively validated

MET exon 14–skipping mutations

·     Predictive of response to MET TKIs

·     Next-generation sequencing

KRAS p.G12C mutations

·     Most commonly involving mutations in codon 12

·     Previously prognostic, but recently p.G12C mutations targetable

·     Real-time PCR

·     Sanger sequencing with tumour enrichment

·     Next-generation sequencing

RET rearrangements

·     Variety of fusion partners identified, including KIF5B, CCDC6, NCOA4

·     Predictive of response to oral RET TKIs

·     FISH using break-apart probes; sensitivity dependent on fusion partner (low sensitivity for NCOA4::RET)

·     Targeted real-time reverse transcriptase PCR; may not detect novel partners

·     Next-generation sequencing

NTRK1/2/3 fusions

·     Variety of fusion partners identified

·     FISH: requires three separate probe sets

·     Immunochemistry: high sensitivity, but may have underlying baseline expression in some tissues

·     Next-generation sequencing

PDL1

·     Expressed in tumour cells and inhibits T cell–mediated cell death

·     High PDL1 expression predictive of response to checkpoint inhibitor therapy

·     Immunohistochemistry using PDL1 antibody clones (please refer to <<3.2.0.1>>Immunocytochemistry, p. XXX)

TKI, tyrosine kinase inhibitor.
