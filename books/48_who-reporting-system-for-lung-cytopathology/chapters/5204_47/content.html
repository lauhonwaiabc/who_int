5.2.0.4 Solitary tracheobronchial papilloma

Definition

Pulmonary papillomas are divided into three types: (1) multiple papillomatosis, usually found in children, affecting the larynx and (rarely) the lower respiratory tract; (2) inflammatory endobronchial polyps, originating in the setting of chronic respiratory infection; and (3) solitary tracheobronchial papilloma (STBP), which is subclassified into squamous, glandular, and mixed squamous and glandular subtypes. This section focuses on STBP.

Clinical features and imaging

Patients with STBP can present with either wheezing or pneumonia due to airway obstruction or haemoptysis, although in some cases, STBP can be found incidentally on imaging, either as an endobronchial mass in the segmental bronchi or as a plaque-like mucosal lesion near the carina {9808125; 21971565; 21383960; 21325086; 7781378; 7674431}. Squamous cell STBP has been shown to be related to HPV infection and is commonly encountered in middle-aged smokers, with a male predominance, whereas the mixed squamous and glandular type is usually seen in non-smoker older men {9850375}. Squamous cell STBPs are usually exophytic masses, although inverted types have been reported. These tumours can spread to multiple sites within the tracheobronchial tree, recur, and progress to squamous cell carcinoma {11201373; 26730195}.

Histopathology

STBPs are subclassified as squamous, glandular, and mixed squamous and glandular types {31934202; 31779763; 21383960}. Squamous STBP can show a polypoid mass with a central core, covered by multilayered squamous epithelium that may show HPV koilocytic atypia and varying degrees of squamous dysplasia and transformation to squamous cell carcinoma. Glandular STBP can occur also as a polypoid mass, covered by tall columnar respiratory epithelium with prominent microvilli and usually lacking cellular atypia.

Key diagnostic cytopathological features

Squamous STBP
  • Loosely cohesive tissue fragments and scattered keratinizing and non-keratinizing squamous cells, some with anucleate squamous cells
  • Background of inflammatory and keratinous debris, but no cellular debris or tumour necrosis
  • Squamous cells with koilocytic atypia and (rarely) dysplastic changes
  • Most cases lack mitotic figures
Glandular STBP
  • Tissue fragments and singly scattered enlarged or hyperplastic columnar cells with retained cilia
  • Rare reports of mixed squamous and glandular STBP showing features of both squamous and glandular STBP

 

Reference(s): {21383960; 1054203; 1421008; 20301213}

Discussion and differential diagnosis

STBPs are rare neoplasms; cases have been reported on the basis of bronchial brushings and FNAB specimens, but they are difficult to diagnose solely from cytomorphology {21383960; 1054203; 1421008; 20301213}. Correlating imaging appearances with cytomorphology is essential, and core needle biopsy at rapid onsite evaluation (ROSE) should be recommended. The differential diagnosis of STBP includes squamous metaplasia, well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, well-differentiated adenocarcinoma, and low-grade mucoepidermoid carcinoma for glandular or mixed subtypes {21383960}.

Ancillary testing

HPV has been linked to the pathogenesis of STBP, most commonly HPV types 6 and 11 along with types 16 and 18 and other high-risk subtypes, which can be associated with transformation to higher-grade lesion {32309132; 7959664; 21383960; 12461047; 10526947; 24596531}.
