13.1.4.6 Lymphatic anomalies

Definition

Lymphatic anomalies are malformations of the lymphatic system that can present as localized (common lymphatic malformations [CLMs]) or generalized lesions (complex lymphatic anomalies [CLAs]). These anomalies were formerly referred to as lymphangiomas and lymphangiomatosis.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

LA90.12 & XA6452 Lymphatic malformations of small intestine

LA90.12 & XA1B13 Lymphatic malformations of large intestine

2E81.10 Disseminated lymphangiomatosis

Related terminology

Not recommended: lymphangioma; cystic hygroma; lymphangiomatosis; intra-abdominal cystic lymphangioma; haemangiolymphangioma.

Subtype(s)

Complex lymphatic anomaly

Generalized lymphatic anomaly; kaposiform lymphangiomatosis; Gorham–Stout disease; central conduction lymphatic anomaly

 

Localization

CLMs can arise throughout the GI tract, but they are most common in the small intestine, followed by the large intestine and oesophagus. In the peritoneal cavity, most occur within the mesentery {9306548;22696733}. Hepatic CLMs are very rare {25832463;34917222}. CLAs present with diffuse or multicentric lymphatic malformations in multiple organs, including the skin, soft tissues, bones, spleen, lungs, liver, and GI tract {31617676}.

Clinical features

Patients with CLM can present with anaemia from haemorrhage, intussusception, abdominal pain, or acute abdomen {9201081;8145105;36338654;34481132}. Some patients are asymptomatic, and their lesions are incidentally discovered {26336182}.

Overlapping features in CLA can be diagnostically challenging {23371338}. The clinical presentation varies depending on the affected organs. Cortical bone destruction is a key aspect of Gorham–Stout disease. Thrombocytopenia and thoracic involvement characterize kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA) {30542204}. Central conduction lymphatic anomaly (CCLA) can cause effusions of lymph or chyle, intestinal lymphangiectasia, and protein-losing enteropathy {26806875}.

Epidemiology

Intra-abdominal lymphatic anomalies are rare, accounting for < 1% of all lymphatic anomalies. They primarily present in children and young adults, but also across a wide age range, including older adults {4051731;10936792;22696733;26386077}.

Etiology

Most lymphatic anomalies are sporadic and associated with somatic mosaicisms involving genes of the PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR and RAS/MAPK/MEK pathways. In the majority of CLMs, a causative mutation is detected in the PIK3CA gene. Somatic PIK3CA mutations are encountered in generalized lymphatic anomaly {25681199;29217067}. Mutations in genes of the RAS/MAPK pathway have been identified in cases of CLA, including recurrent somatic pathogenic mutations in NRAS in patients with kaposiform lymphangiomatosis, KRAS mutations in Gorham–Stout disease, and ARAFvariants in CCLA {30542204;35202617;34166072}. Germline heterozygous kinase-dead mutations in the gene encoding EPHB4, which suppresses MAPK signalling in endothelial cells, have been identified in a subset of patients with CCLA {29905864}.

Pathogenesis

PI3K, particularly its catalytic subunit p110α, is a key regulator of vascular development. RAS/MAPK signalling, acting in concert with PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling, is essential for vascular development. The three RAS isoforms (HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS) cooperatively regulate lymphatic endothelial proliferation and lymphatic vessel growth. PIK3CAvariants are associated with hyperactivation of the PI3K signalling pathway, resulting in vascular malformations and abnormal growth of various tissues. The type of variant and the timing and location of these mutations likely contribute to the wide-ranging manifestations observed in PIK3CA-related disorders {34238334;34166072;39097232}.

Macroscopic appearance

CLM may be macrocystic (usually defined as > 10 mm), microcystic, or mixed. The macrocystic form, the classic type formerly known as cystic lymphangioma, may be unilocular or multilocular with thick vascular walls in some cases. The microcystic form is spongiform with thin vascular walls. Most cases show a combination of macrocysts and microcysts. Macrocystic lymphatic malformations tend to be circumscribed in contrast with the microcystic forms, which are usually infiltrative. The cystic contents may be clear, haemorrhagic, and occasionally chylous (particularly in patients with CCLA). Microcystic and macrocystic forms are also seen in CLA {15892005}.

Histopathology

CLM, generalized lymphatic anomaly, CCLA, and KLA display malformed lymphatic channels, often numerous, with irregularly muscularized walls. The mucosa, submucosa, or muscularis propria is expanded by dilated cystic spaces lined by a single simple layer of lymphatic endothelial cells, which are usually attenuated without cytological atypia. Some dilated spaces are partially invested by a layer of smooth muscle and/or underlying fibromuscular lymphatic vessel walls. The lymphatic spaces may contain clear fluid, sometimes with lymphocytes and foamy histiocytes, or blood. Deeper lesions may be associated with overlying mucosal clusters of dilated small lymphatics {15892005;21738815}. There are often scattered lymphoid aggregates, foci of granulation tissue, or xanthogranulomatous inflammation, particularly in mesenteric or retroperitoneal lesions, where florid reactive myofibroblastic proliferations can obscure the underlying lymphatic abnormality. Hepatic lesions are often multiloculated, with cystic dilatation of lymphatic vessels within hepatic parenchyma {25832463;34917222}. KLA is distinguished by the presence of perilymphatic clusters of hemosiderin-laden, spindle-shaped lymphatic endothelial cells {35202617}.

The lymphatic endothelium in CLM and CLA expresses D2-40, PROX1, CD31, ERG, and FLI1. Endothelial cells often display only focal CD34 expression and faint or focal expression of D2-40 in larger channels or cysts {26055853}. SMA is positive in smooth muscle cells surrounding the cysts. The smooth muscle component lacks melanocytic antigens, facilitating distinction from lymphangioleiomyomatosis (perivascular epithelioid cell tumour) {15892005}.

Cytology

Aspiration of non-haemorrhagic cysts usually reveals abundant lymphocytes and macrophages.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of causative mutations in affected tissues may clarify the diagnosis in complex cases. Deep next-generation sequencing analysis is required to identify low-level mosaicism that would be otherwise undetectable. Mutations can also be detected in amplified cell-free DNA from plasma, as well as cyst and effusion fluid of patients with CLA {31617676}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malformed and usually excessive lymphatic channels; associated spindle cell component in KLA; endothelial immunopositivity for D2-40 and PROX1; correlation with clinical features and imaging.

Desirable: molecular evidence of various gene alterations associated with lymphatic malformations, particularly in KLA if biopsy samples are not diagnostic.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Surgical resection, sclerotherapy, laser therapy, or a combination of these can provide local control and symptomatic relief to patients with CLM and CLA with recurrent effusions, infections, and pain. Generalized lymphatic anomaly, CCLA, and KLA require a multimodal approach for control, with inhibitors of the PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR and RAS/MAPK/MEK pathways. Patients with CLAs suffer from chronic pain, disabilities, and life-threatening complications including respiratory failure due to massive chylothorax, immune failure, sepsis, malnutrition secondary to prolonged fluid drainage, or haemorrhage due to coagulopathy {31617676}.

KLA currently has a high mortality rate {31030813}.
