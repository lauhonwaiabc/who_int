13.0.1 Mesenchymal tumours of the digestive system: Introduction

This chapter focuses on mesenchymal tumours most relevant to the digestive system. However, this introduction presents a more general overview of soft tissue pathology, as virtually any soft tissue tumour can arise at any site. For example, there are very rare descriptions of hepatic and gastrointestinal Ewing sarcoma {38767576} and small intestinal low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma {21677541}. Similarly, although intra-abdominal solitary fibrous tumour most commonly arises in the retroperitoneum or mesentery, it may occasionally arise in the GI tract also {38472638}. The full breadth of deep soft tissue tumours is covered in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series.

Incidence

The incidence of soft tissue sarcoma is fairly consistent at 30–50 cases per 1 million person-years {33630918;31055220;35796499} [[Ferlay J, Colombet M, Bray F. Cancer Incidence in Five Continents, CI5plus: IARC CancerBase No. 9 (Internet). Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2018. Available from: https://ci5.iarc.who.int. Bookref]]. The overall incidence in children and young adults is much lower than that in adults, estimated at 15 cases per 1 million person-years {39848113}. In toto, sarcomas account for 1% of adult malignancies {30231406} and 10–15% of paediatric malignancies, with an incidence in adolescents and young adults of 14.5 cases per 1 million person-years {25435119;27542645;39848113}. Some data suggest that overall deaths attributable to sarcomas are increasing globally {36346699}. Over 100 distinct sarcomas are currently recognized, with a wider variety presenting in adults compared with children. The overall incidence of benign soft tissue neoplasms is ≥ 100-fold higher, although some tumours (e.g. lipoma) are vastly more common than others.

Sarcomas arise mainly in the extremities (in particular, the deep soft tissue of the thighs), trunk, head and neck, and retroperitoneum. Mesenchymal tumours are relatively rare in the GI tract, far outnumbered by carcinomas {39588681;34294940}. Gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) is the most common sarcoma of the GI tract {23639648;19248970}, with an incidence of approximately 10 cases per 1 million person-years {20588273;29308530}. Diminutive clinically occult (seedling) GISTs are identified in as many as 35% of gastrectomy specimens; most never become clinically important {16996566}. Most GISTs are gastric (60%) followed by the small bowel (30%) {23639648}.

Other primary gastrointestinal sarcomas are rare; malignant gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumour is a key example {22592145}, but it can also be extraenteric {36934861}. Most other sarcomas are not specific to the GI tract, but some, such as leiomyosarcoma, are not unusual in visceral locations. Common benign mesenchymal tumours of the GI tract include lipomas, leiomyomas, vascular lesions, and nerve sheath tumours. Haemangiomas and vascular malformations are by far the most common hepatic mesenchymal tumours; primary hepatic sarcomas are rare and include angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, and epithelioid haemangioendothelioma.

Etiology and pathogenesis

Most soft tissue neoplasms arise spontaneously, with an unknown cause, but a clear etiology is established for a subset. Viruses associated with soft tissue tumours include EBV (associated with some smooth muscle tumours in immunosuppressed patients) {16330945} and KSHV/HHV8 (associated with Kaposi sarcoma) {9044510}. Angiosarcoma can complicate longstanding lymphoedema, in particular after radical mastectomy (as seen in Stewart–Treves syndrome). Sarcomas (most commonly angiosarcoma and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour) can also arise in fields of prior therapeutic irradiation {9279573}. Associations with chemical exposure, immunosuppression/transplantation, and chronic tissue irritation have been described in a subset of sarcoma cases {18514694}. Soft tissue tumours (both benign and malignant) are associated with several inherited syndromes also, including Maffucci syndrome (associated with chondroid and vascular tumours), tuberous sclerosis (associated with renal and hepatic angiomyolipomas), and PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (Cowden syndrome) (associated with lipomas, nerve sheath tumours, and vascular malformations involving the GI tract). These syndromes are also discussed in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume of this series. Desmoid fibromatosis can be seen in the setting of familial adenomatous polyposis with germline (constitutional) APC pathogenic variants {28668823;20301519}. GISTs are usually sporadic, although germline KIT or PDGFRA pathogenic variants (familial GIST), NF1 variants (neurofibromatosis type 1), and SDH gene variants (including Carney–Stratakis syndrome) account for a subset of tumours {27600498;24840526;27437068}.

The line of differentiation of most sarcomas is unknown, and precursor or in situ lesions are not recognized. The exception is GIST: somatic alterations in KIT and PDGFRA, believed to arise in precursors involving the interstitial cells of Cajal, are identified in the vast majority of GISTs. Small precursor lesions can be seen, and hypertrophy of the interstitial cells of Cajal is noted in families with inherited predisposition to GISTs {28632504}. Somatic genetic alterations such as recurrent chromosomal translocations drive the pathogenesis of many types of sarcomas, particularly those arising in children and young adults, but how and in what cell these somatic genetic alterations arise remains unknown.

Clinical features

Presentation of gastrointestinal mesenchymal tumours is often site-specific. Distal gastric lesions can be associated with gastric outlet obstruction, and small intestinal tumours can manifest with intussusception {23426127}. Several tumour types present as painless masses with variable growth rates. GISTs and other gastrointestinal and hepatic mesenchymal neoplasms are detected either incidentally or present with abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, anaemia, or an abdominal mass. The vast majority of benign soft tissue tumours are < 50 mm {26839096}. Malignant tumours can attain larger sizes, particularly in the abdominal cavity and retroperitoneum, where tumours may be quite large before becoming symptomatic. In most cases, sarcoma involvement of digestive system organs is secondary, such as direct invasion of the pancreas by retroperitoneal liposarcoma, or bowel entrapment by mesenteric fibromatoses. The liver is also a common site for metastatic sarcomas of many types.

Because sarcomas are rare, consensus guidelines suggest referral of patients with presumed sarcomas to multidisciplinary centres before biopsy or surgery for optimal diagnosis and management {38734790;35830886}.

Histopathology

Histological features in part depend on the underlying pattern of genomic alterations. Sarcomas with complex genomic alterations, such as leiomyosarcoma or angiosarcoma, generally exhibit nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic activity, and necrosis to varying degrees. Some benign tumours can also show one or more of these features; for example, nuclear atypia can be seen in schwannomas. Tumours with specific or simple genomic alterations, such as most translocation-associated sarcomas and GISTs, generally show uniform nuclear morphology. In these types of tumours, increased mitotic activity is the key indicator of malignant behaviour, and necrosis is also often a poor prognostic sign. Immunohistochemistry or molecular studies can be essential to confirm the diagnosis of GIST and other mesenchymal neoplasms. GI tract solitary fibrous tumours appear as they do elsewhere in the body, with characteristic prominent, thin, branching, often ectatic blood vessels; wiry collagen; variable arrangements of spindle cells; and nuclear expression of STAT6.

Genetics

The role of structural alterations and oncogenic mutations is increasingly recognized in both benign and malignant soft tissue tumours. Many benign soft tissue tumours feature diploid karyotypes with characteristic chromosomal alterations (e.g. HMGA2 rearrangement in lipomas), or limited foci of deletion (e.g. RB1 locus on 13q14). Some entities are characterized by oncogenic driver mutations (such as PDGFRA in inflammatory fibroid polyp), while others may be driven by a variety of mutations (e.g. schwannoma {37436963}). In sarcomas, overall two genetic classes exist {15111298}: simple-karyotype sarcomas associated with a recurrent mutation or translocation/rearrangement (e.g. synovial sarcoma) {37972927}; and complex-karyotype sarcomas with numerous non-recurrent chromosomal aberrations, and recurrent mutations/deletions mainly confined to common tumour suppressor genes (including TP53, RB1, and ATRX) {35705560}. Rarely, specific subtypes of tumours that are usually genomically complex can show simpler genomic alterations and recurrent rearrangements, such as PLAG1 alterations in myxoid leiomyosarcoma {38656544}.

Mesenteric (desmoid) fibromatosis, locally aggressive but non-metastasizing, is generally associated with a single somatic mutation in CTNNB1 and (much less commonly) APC {28578783;28961825}. GIST shows a characteristic molecular progression usually initiated by activating mutations in KIT or PDGFRA, followed by additional alterations {28632504;37477937}.

Diagnostic procedures

Diagnostic investigation includes clinical assessment of the size and depth of invasion of the tumour, the use of endoscopy and imaging modalities (CT and MRI), and biopsy. Imaging can be used to assess the extent of a primary tumour, to determine its relationship to normal structures, and to identify metastases. Small lesions of ≤ 10–15 mm diameter can be completely excised on endoscopy. Larger lesions (and all deeper-seated tumours) necessitate diagnostic sampling. Core needle biopsy (often image-guided and preferably using a larger-bore needle) can provide diagnostic information on malignancy, subtype, and grade, with high sensitivity and specificity {35115589;38086314}. Open biopsy and cytology are less commonly used, and endoscopic biopsy is often used for gastrointestinal mesenchymal tumours.

Tumour behaviour

The Soft tissue and bone tumours volume recognizes four tumour behaviour categories: benign, intermediate (locally aggressive), intermediate (rarely metastasizing), and malignant (see #44802Box 13.01). Benign tumours are usually cured by local excision and rarely recur locally (and any recurrences are non-destructive). Intermediate tumours can be either locally aggressive (e.g. fibromatosis, which locally infiltrates surrounding tissues) or rarely metastasizing (tumours with a very low [< 2%] but definite risk of metastasis, e.g. plexiform fibrohistiocytic tumour). Malignant tumours such as leiomyosarcoma and angiosarcoma infiltrate and recur locally and commonly metastasize (i.e. in > 20% of cases). GISTs range from clinically insignificant, small benign lesions to aggressive sarcomas.

Grading

Grading is an attempt to predict clinical behaviour on the basis of histological variables, but it can only be performed using material from primary untreated neoplasms. It is not applicable to all sarcomas; for example, angiosarcomas, clear cell sarcomas, and epithelioid sarcomas are always considered to be high-grade. The most widely used system for grading soft tissue sarcomas is the three-tiered system developed by the French Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) {17090186;24378389}. It uses a combination of tumour differentiation, mitotic count, and necrosis to categorize tumours as being of low, intermediate, or high grade. However, this system is not applicable to GISTs.

Staging

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM staging systems are widely used [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]] [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. Sarcoma staging incorporates histological grading where relevant, and site-specific criteria, along with histological type, extent of lymph node involvement, and presence or absence of distant metastasis. Among intra-abdominal tumours, visceral primaries are staged according to the extent of invasion beyond the organ of origin, while mesenteric, pelvic, or peritoneal sarcomas are staged according to retroperitoneal size criteria. Specific criteria are applicable to GISTs.

Prognosis and predictive factors

Complete excision is the most important factor in preventing local recurrence. Factors generally associated with a greater risk of metastasis are larger tumour size and higher grade; peritoneal seeding may result from intraoperative tumour rupture. In some instances, histological type / subtype alone is predictive, but one of the principal factors in assessing prognosis and determining management is histological grade. Site is also important, because low-grade sarcomas in sites where complete surgical excision is difficult generally have a worse outcome than do similarly staged tumours in the extremities {35901187;38225730}. A risk assessment system incorporating anatomical site, mitotic count, and tumour size is applied to GISTs in order to predict malignant behaviour and to select patients for adjuvant targeted therapy {36073677}. Site-specific risk assessment scores are not applicable to the rare cases of solitary fibrous tumour arising in the GI tract, as tumours are biologically similar to other intra-abdominal solitary fibrous tumours and should be managed according to site-agnostic risk criteria {28731041}. More broadly applicable molecular approaches for prognostic and therapeutic prediction continue to evolve {36430703;35705560;38488807}.
