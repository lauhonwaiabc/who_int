4.1.2.1 Non-ampullary adenocarcinoma

Definition

Non-ampullary duodenal adenocarcinoma is an invasive epithelial neoplasm of the duodenum not involving the ampulla and showing glandular differentiation.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B80.00 Adenocarcinoma of duodenum

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); poorly cohesive carcinoma / signet-ring cell carcinoma (84901/3); medullary carcinoma (85100/3); adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3)

Localization

In the small intestine, the duodenum is the tract most frequently involved by adenocarcinoma, accounting for approximately 50–60% of cases {15274064;20715162;38062562}. Involvement of the first and second portions of the duodenum is more common than that of the third and fourth portions {25310836}. For duodenal cancers occurring in the vicinity of the ampulla, the lack of ampullary involvement by gross and microscopic examination is the criterion to define non-ampullary duodenal cancers {27739441;25310836}. Ampullary adenocarcinomas are discussed separately (see section Ampullary adenocarcinoma).

Clinical features

The clinical presentation and diagnosis of duodenal adenocarcinomas is usually delayed, with many cases presenting at advanced stages. Lymph node involvement and/or distant metastases are reported in more than two thirds of all patients {15274064;27739441}. Patients with low-stage tumours are generally asymptomatic, but occult bleeding may occur in 30–40% of cases {25310836}. The symptoms are relatively nonspecific, with abdominal pain being the most common symptom at presentation {15274064}. Duodenal adenocarcinoma is often a diagnosis of emergency, with intestinal obstruction reported in approximately one third of patients at presentation {15274064}. Most patients with duodenal adenocarcinoma are diagnosed by upper endoscopy {15274064}.

Epidemiology

Adenocarcinomas of the small intestine account for 30–45% of all small bowel malignant tumours {20647399;27170100;30884915}. However, they are remarkably rare, accounting for 3% of GI tract cancers {26487334;21461167} and 3600 new cases of small intestine adenocarcinoma diagnosed annually in Europe {22169462}.

In the small intestine, the duodenum is the most common tumour site (60%) {19106677;30884915}. Much of the increase in frequency reported in adenocarcinoma of the small bowel is due to an increase in duodenal tumours {22187121}. Men are affected slightly more frequently than women {19064190;28830268}, and the median patient age is approximately 60 years {19064190;20334897;30884915}.

Etiology

The predilection for the duodenum, particularly in the vicinity of the ampulla/pylorus, is attributable to exposure to acid and bile. Smoking, alcohol consumption, and specific diets have also been associated with small intestine adenocarcinoma {9052748}. Rarely, duodenal adenocarcinomas arise in the setting of chronic inflammatory disorders, such as coeliac disease and Crohn disease {23243535;31388669}.

Hereditary cancer syndromes can predispose to small intestinal adenocarcinoma. Lynch syndrome due to germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes is associated with an increased risk of small intestinal adenocarcinoma {27258561}. The reported cumulative lifetime risk of small intestinal adenocarcinoma in Lynch syndrome ranges from 1% to 12% {23091106;21642682}. Between 5% and 20% of small intestinal adenocarcinomas are MMR-deficient and/or show a high level of microsatellite instability (MSI-H) and require further evaluation for Lynch syndrome {27258561;28934760}. Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) primarily affects the non-ampullary duodenum in most cases {32535190}. Duodenal adenocarcinoma is the main cause of cancer-related death in patients with FAP who have undergone proctocolectomy, and individuals with FAP have approximately a 5% chance of developing duodenal adenocarcinoma {11950808}. The small bowel has the highest relative risk (520) of cancer development in Peutz–Jeghers syndrome {11113065}.

Pathogenesis

Non-ampullary duodenal cancers are less commonly associated with an adenoma component than are colorectal cancers. In addition, some are accompanied by background gastric-type epithelial alterations {27739441;25310836}. All these suggest a gastric metaplasia–dysplasia–carcinoma sequence in some tumours {25310836;28321513;26421956}, presumably induced by the local chemical factors, including acid. A lower but statistically significant association (relative risk of 10 when compared with the healthy population) with coeliac disease has been documented in the literature {12404215;12702783}.

Most recent molecular studies of small intestinal adenocarcinoma have grouped adenocarcinomas of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum together {24797764;29522538;28617917;26892442}, although a few studies have specifically evaluated duodenal adenocarcinomas {31324814;38062562}. The most common alterations in small intestinal adenocarcinoma involve TP53 (41–58.4%), KRAS (43–53.6%), APC (13–26.8%), SMAD4 (9.6–17.4%), PIK3CA (8.4–16.1%), CDKN2A (14.5%), ARID1A(12.3%), BRAF (6–11%), ATM (7.6–8%), FBXW7 (5–6.9%), GNAS (6.5%), ERBB3 (6.3%), NF1 (6.0%), CTNNB1(5.7%), MDM2 (5.7%), and PTEN (5.7%). Most BRAF mutations are non–p.Val600Glu (V600E) {29522538;28617917;26892442}. ERBB2 (HER2) amplification and point mutations are identified in 2.2% and 8.2–14% of tumours, respectively {24797764;28617917;23703294}. Activating receptor tyrosine kinase rearrangements can be seen in 1% of tumours and include EML4::ALK, GOPC::ROS1, and FGFR2::CCAR1 {28617917}. Duodenal adenocarcinomas are enriched for mutations in CDKN2A and alterations in ERBB2 {24797764;28617917}. The rate of MSI-H / MMR deficiency in small intestinal adenocarcinoma ranges from 7.6% to 21.6%; this wide range is most likely affected by the stage groups included between studies, with higher rates of MSI-H / MMR deficiency seen in lower-stage tumours {24797764;27258561;28617917;29522538;31576398}. Tumours associated with coeliac disease have a higher rate of MSI-H (50–73%) {15466202;20179237;29522538}. Undifferentiated carcinomas with rhabdoid morphology are characterized by alterations of SMARC genes {26551623}.

Macroscopic appearance

Duodenal adenocarcinomas have a polypoid component in about a third of cases, but a substantial proportion of cases show plaque-like growth {27739441}.

The macroscopic examination is dictated by the likely clinical features and associations of this cancer: advanced stage at presentation {15274064} and the etiological associations with polyposis syndromes {29998424}. These features, as well as the high chance of serosal involvement and the frequent extension to neighbouring organs, should be considered before gross dissection.

Histopathology

Non-ampullary duodenal adenocarcinomas can be broadly categorized into tubular-type adenocarcinoma NOS, and non–tubule-forming subtypes of adenocarcinomas that resemble their counterparts in the colorectum and include mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, poorly cohesive carcinoma, medullary carcinoma, and undifferentiated carcinoma {27739441;29998424} (see section Colorectal carcinoma for further explanation).

Duodenal adenocarcinomas with a predominant tubular growth pattern often have a spectrum of morphological patterns and immunophenotypes {25310836;27739441;38062562}. Approximately 40–50% of duodenal adenocarcinomas have a gastric or pancreatobiliary histomorphology and express gastric (MUC5AC and MUC6) or pancreatobiliary (MUC1) differentiation markers, respectively {25310836;27739441}. Intestinal-type adenocarcinoma resembling colorectal adenocarcinoma is less frequent and expresses the intestinal differentiation markers CDX2 and MUC2 {25310836;27739441;38062562;24603585}. In one study, nearly half of duodenal adenocarcinomas had a mixed histomorphology and immunophenotype. MMR deficiency can be identified in medullary carcinoma but can also be seen in other histological types, including intestinal-type adenocarcinoma {27739441;38062562;31576398}.

Direct involvement of the duodenum by a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma or ampullary adenocarcinoma can mimic a primary duodenal adenocarcinoma when it infiltrates into and colonizes the mucosa, acquiring a morphology closely simulating that of an adenoma {21412071}. Careful examination of the gross macroscopic findings and review of clinical and imaging information are necessary when assigning the primary site to the duodenum.

Cytology

Cytopathological analysis (FNA or brushing) is seldom employed for duodenal tumours, with rare exceptions in the ampullary region in order to analyse an associated deep-seated lesion (see section Ampullary adenocarcinoma).

Diagnostic molecular pathology

MMR protein immunohistochemistry and/or microsatellite instability determination are required to evaluate a possible hereditary cause of duodenal adenocarcinoma; they are also useful for selection for immunotherapy {26028255}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a glandular epithelial neoplasm in a non-ampullary location of the duodenum, showing unequivocal evidence of invasion.

Staging

Duodenal adenocarcinoma is pathologically staged according to the latest edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The overall survival associated with duodenal adenocarcinomas is dependent on tumour stage and appears to be worse than that associated with colorectal cancer {27739441;25310836;24139211;22187121}. However, it is substantially better than that for pancreaticobiliary cancers {22350721;27739441}. The number of lymph nodes retrieved and analysed is in itself a strong prognostic indicator, with a cut-off point of 8 lymph nodes to improve survival {20715162;22187121;35402228}. Histological subtyping of tubular-type (glandular) duodenal adenocarcinoma into intestinal, gastric, or pancreatobiliary is not significantly associated with overall survival {27739441}.

PD1 inhibitor immunotherapy may improve overall survival in patients with MSI-H and/or MMR-deficient tumours {33883178}. In one multicentre study, 50% of patients with advanced MSI-H small bowel adenocarcinoma achieved partial response to PD1 inhibitor immunotherapy and were alive without disease progression {33883178}.

ERBB2 (HER2) evaluation is also of use for selection for ERBB2-targeted therapy {37870536}.
