12.1.1 Haematolymphoid tumours of the digestive system: Introduction

The GI tract may be involved secondarily by spread from lymphomas arising elsewhere in the body but is also one of the most common sites of primary extranodal lymphomas. Primary GI tract lymphomas account for approximately 1–8% of GI tract malignancies and 30–40% of extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) {1835586;2767109;21798075;10922409;12733145;5007387}, although classification and definitions have changed over time. Primary extranodal lymphomas of the digestive system are generally defined as having dominant GI tract or hepatobiliary involvement at presentation with at most only minor nodal involvement {1835586;21798075}. Any involvement of these organs must be distinguished from secondary involvement by systemic lymphoma.

A wide range of primary extranodal lymphomas occur in the GI tract with B-subtypes of NHL accounting for the majority {21798075;29505542;26632732;12733145}. Some haematological malignancies are specific to the digestive system, only rarely or never occurring at other sites, such as duodenal-type follicular lymphoma, indolent NK-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, indolent T-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, monomorphic epitheliotropic intestinal T-cell lymphoma, intestinal T-cell lymphoma NOS, hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma, and EBV-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma.

Others can occur at a diverse range of systemic sites but are sufficiently common in the GI tract or hepatobiliary system as primary extranodal lymphomas to be included in this sixth-edition WHO classification of digestive system tumours. These include extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, extranodal/primary digestive follicular lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and Burkitt lymphoma. A summary of the primary and common haematolymphoid neoplasms involving the digestive tract covered in this volume is listed in #49880Table 12.01. Other haematological neoplasms may also involve the digestive system especially as part of more widespread systemic disease; for those, the reader is referred to the fifth-edition WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours.

A range of non-lymphoid haematological neoplasms can also involve the digestive system including, but not limited to, systemic mastocytosis and EBV-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, the latter of which primarily involves the liver or spleen and less commonly the GI tract {33264138}. A new addition to the WHO classification of digestive system tumours is the reactive lymphoid proliferation EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer, which arises in patients with immune deficiency or dysregulation and is an important differential diagnosis for atypical lymphoid proliferations.

While most primary GI tract lymphoproliferative disorders involve the GI tract focally, some such as mantle cell lymphoma, follicular lymphoma, and enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma commonly show multicentric involvement {21798075;20084529;23313469}.

Gastrointestinal tract

Although there is some geographical variation in the frequency and anatomical distribution of primary GI tract lymphoma, the most common site is typically the stomach (~50–70%), followed by small intestine (~20–30%) and large intestine (~10%), with the ileocaecal region being a common site and the rectum and oesophagus only rarely involved {11559725;15480663;29505542;9378552;26632732;21798075;12733145}. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is the most common type of primary GI tract lymphoma accounting for 55–70% of primary GI tract lymphomas {11559724}, followed by extranodal marginal zone lymphoma, which accounts for 4% of gastric malignancies {36792000}, 2–10% of primary gastrointestinal lymphomas {25639480;28288721;30315345}, 30–60% of primary gastric lymphomas, and 2–28% of primary intestinal lymphomas {1347858;1568573;25639480;29225710}. Mantle cell lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma each account for < 5% of GI tract lymphomas (with some geographical variations) and mostly occur in the intestines {11559724;12733145;29505542;21798075;26632732;26988370}. T-cell lymphomas account for approximately 15% of GI tract non-Hodgkin lymphoma {11559725;21798075;26632732;12733145}, with enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma and monomorphic epitheliotropic intestinal T-cell lymphoma being the most common in Europe and the USA {21566094}, and intestinal T-cell lymphoma NOS being relatively more common (ranging from 15% to 58%) in Asian countries {39121027;36327820;35551151;34072328;21798075}.

Hepatobiliary system

The liver is a major reticuloendothelial organ, but primary hepatic lymphoma (PHL) is much less common than secondary involvement, which occurs in 40% of all patients with lymphoma. PHL accounts for only 0.4% of all extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphomas and < 0.1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas {5007387;28660145}. Both HCV and to a lesser extent HBV have been linked to PHL in epidemiological studies {9684927;12668970;22395482;21780099}. Non-hepatotropic viruses such as HIV and other causes of immunodeficiency (posttransplant setting) or autoimmune conditions have also been associated with PHL {22395482}. The most common PHL is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, accounting for approximately 80% of cases, followed by MALT lymphoma {22395482;27746888;28660145}, although study sizes are small. The liver is a characteristic primary site for the occurrence of EBV-positive inflammatory follicular dendritic cell sarcoma (previously known as inflammatory pseudotumour–like follicular/fibroblastic dendritic cell sarcoma {11395549}). Primary lymphomas of the pancreas, gallbladder, and extrahepatic bile ducts are very rare, with the most common being diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and extranodal marginal zone lymphoma {22134518}.

Mast cell disease

Neoplastic infiltration of the GI tract by mast cells (systemic mastocytosis) can sometimes be associated with symptoms attributable to the release of mast cell mediators and is characterized by morphologically identifiable aggregates of atypical mast cells, which usually show aberrant expression of CD25, in addition to traditional mast cell markers such as tryptase and KIT {36853780}. While mast cells may play a role in some patients with GI tract dysfunction, their absolute numbers and function overlap with healthy controls, and there is insufficient evidence to support a role for mast cell count assessment outside the assessment of systemic mastocytosis {36853780}.
