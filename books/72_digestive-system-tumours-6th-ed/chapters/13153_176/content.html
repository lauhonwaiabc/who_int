13.1.5.3 Granular cell tumour

Definition

Granular cell tumour is a benign neoplasm showing neuroectodermal differentiation and composed of epithelioid cells of Schwannian derivation with distinctive lysosome-rich granular cytoplasm.

ICD-O coding

95800/0 Granular cell tumour, NOS

95800/3 Granular cell tumour, malignant

ICD-11 coding

2E92.0 & XH09A9 Granular cell tumour, NOS, of oesophagus

2E92.4Y & XH09A9 Granular cell tumour, NOS, of large intestine

2C1Z & XH90D3 Granular cell tumour, malignant, of digestive organs

Related terminology

Not recommended: granular cell schwannoma; granular cell nerve sheath tumour; granular cell myoblastoma; Abrikossoff tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Up to 10% of granular cell tumours arise in the GI tract. Within the GI tract, the most common site is the oesophagus, followed by the large intestine and perianal region {25882927;16487364;20661017;8784020}. Within the oesophagus, about 60% of cases are located distally, with the remainder evenly divided between the proximal and middle portions. In the large intestine, tumours most often arise in the right colon {20661017}. Rare cases involve the stomach, small intestine, gallbladder, and bile ducts.

Clinical features

Most gastrointestinal granular cell tumours are small and are discovered incidentally during endoscopy for other reasons. The majority of cases are solitary and superficial, but multifocal examples occur. Congenital granular cell tumours of the gingiva have distinct clinicopathological features and are addressed separately (see the fifth-edition Paediatric tumoursvolume [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Paediatric tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 7). https://publications.iarc.who.int/608. Bookref]]).

Epidemiology

Granular cell tumours in the GI tract are rare and account for approximately 8% of all granular cell tumours {6246310;3030172}. They usually occur in adults in the fourth to sixth decades of life, although they can be encountered at any age. Some reports describe a predilection for the female sex and for African Americans {8424442}.

Etiology

Multiple granular cell tumours have been reported in association with various syndromes – such as neurofibromatosis type 1, Noonan syndrome (short stature, distinctive facial features, heart defects, bleeding problems, and skeletal abnormalities), and LEOPARD syndrome (multiple lentigines, electrocardiographic conduction abnormalities, ocular hypertelorism, pulmonic stenosis, abnormal genitalia, retardation of growth, and sensorineural deafness; also known as Noonan syndrome with multiple lentigines) – mostly characterized by aberrant signalling within the RAS/MAPK pathways through inactivating mutations {19054014}. However, most reported syndromic cases do not affect the GI tract.

Pathogenesis

Genomic studies have shown mutually exclusive, clonal, inactivating somatic mutations in the endosomal pH regulators ATP6AP1 and ATP6AP2 in 72% of granular cell tumours {30166553}. Inactivating mutations in these two genes are probably oncogenic drivers of granular cell tumours and underpin the genesis of the intracytoplasmic granules that characterize them, providing a genetic link between endosomal pH regulation and tumorigenesis.

Macroscopic appearance

The tumours typically appear as small yellowish nodules with an intact overlying mucosa. Tumour size ranges from 1 to 30 mm.

Histopathology

Gastrointestinal granular cell tumours typically involve the lamina propria or submucosa, and are poorly marginated or circumscribed lesions composed of large monotonous epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm. The cells are arranged in sheets, nests, lobules, or trabeculae within a variably dense fibrous stroma. The overlying squamous epithelium in oesophageal tumours typically shows acanthosis and (sometimes) striking pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia. The tumour cells may appear polygonal to somewhat elongated/spindled. The nuclei are small and uniform, typically slightly hyperchromatic with inconspicuous nucleoli, and are often eccentrically located. Occasional tumours (usually colonic) show degenerative nuclear changes. Multinucleation, nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, and mitotic figures are uncommon features.

Clinically malignant granular cell tumours in the GI tract are exceedingly rare, and their histological features vary from morphologically bland to overtly sarcomatous. Features of malignant tumours include prominent spindling, vesicular nuclei with prominent nucleoli, high N:C ratio, increased mitotic activity (> 2 mitoses/2 mm2), and necrosis.

Immunohistochemically, the tumour cells are positive for S100, SOX10, nestin, calretinin, and in a subset of cases, CD57 {16487364}. Expression of TFE3 is common, but TFE3 rearrangements are absent {26009539}. Because of their abundant lysosomal cytoplasmic content, tumour cells usually express CD68, CD63 (NKI/C3), and NSE.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: plump cells with copious, distinctly granular eosinophilic cytoplasm.

Desirable: strong S100 and/or SOX10 positivity.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The vast majority of gastrointestinal granular cell tumours are benign and do not recur, even after incomplete excision. Exceptionally rare malignant (usually oesophageal) granular cell tumours pursue a locally aggressive course and metastasize.
