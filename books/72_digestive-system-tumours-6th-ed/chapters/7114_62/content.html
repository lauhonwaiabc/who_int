7.1.1.4 Colorectal hamartomatous polyps: Juvenile polyp

Definition

Juvenile polyp is a benign polypoid lesion characterized by expanded inflammatory stroma with cystically dilated glands lined by colonic epithelium, which may or may not be dysplastic.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

DB35.3 & XA4ZR7 Hamartomatous polyp of colorectum

Related terminology

Acceptable: sporadic juvenile retention polyp.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Sporadic juvenile polyps are often solitary and occur in the rectosigmoid {17768394}. Colonic juvenile polyps in the context of juvenile polyposis syndrome occur throughout the colon; their number varies between patients and even within families from < 10 to > 100 {35487765}.

Clinical features

Sporadic juvenile polyps can present with painless rectal bleeding or rectal prolapse of a polyp. Many of these polyps outgrow their blood supply, become ischaemic, and autoamputate. A typical history associated with a solitary juvenile polyp is the asymptomatic passing of a polyp in an infant’s diaper {35487765}.

Juvenile polyposis syndrome is characterized by the presence of multiple juvenile polyps. The clinical features of juvenile polyposis syndrome are discussed elsewhere (see section Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4)). The diagnosis of juvenile polyposis syndrome requires the presence of more than five juvenile polyps in the colorectum, or the presence of juvenile polyps throughout the GI tract, or the presence of any number of juvenile polyps in an individual who has a family history of juvenile polyposis syndrome {36717037}. The histopathological appearance of the polyps associated with juvenile polyposis syndrome can be indistinguishable from their appearances in other hamartomatous polyposis syndromes, in particular those in patients with PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (see section PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PTEN)). The features of these syndromes should, therefore, be carefully sought when evaluating a patient with suspected juvenile polyposis syndrome {17768394;28901964}.

Rare newborns (1 in 1 million) develop a severe form of juvenile polyposis, known as juvenile polyposis of infancy, due to a large germline (constitutional) deletion in the long arm of chromosome 10 (10q23) encompassing both the BMPR1Aand PTEN genes. This disease presents in the first 2 years of life with diarrhoea, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, refractory anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, and enteropathy with massive juvenile polyposis. Outcome is poor and death usually occurs at a young age.

Epidemiology

The presence of isolated sporadic juvenile polyps in the colorectum is relatively common; as many as 2% of children aged < 10 years are reported to have an isolated juvenile polyp {16206494}. The epidemiology of juvenile polyposis syndrome is discussed elsewhere (see section Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4)).

Etiology

The presumed etiology of juvenile polyps depends on clinical context. The etiology of sporadic juvenile polyps is not known. Syndromic juvenile polyps are a phenotypic expression of germline pathogenic variants in either SMAD4 or BMPR1A in case of juvenile polyposis syndrome or PTEN in PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (see section Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4) and section PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PTEN)). Isolated reports of patients with SMAD4 variant mosaicism in juvenile polyposis suggest a possible explanation for apparent incomplete penetrance in rare patients {38695688;37950802}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of juvenile polyps is poorly understood but thought to be a consequence of altered stromal-epithelial interaction {15017003;24200851}.

Macroscopic appearance

Juvenile polyps regardless of context are typically spherical with a smooth surface and erosion.

Histopathology

Small syndromic juvenile polyps have histopathological features indistinguishable from sporadic juvenile polyps. Juvenile polyps characteristically show cystically dilated glands with inspissated mucin, embedded in abundant oedematous stroma rich in inflammatory cells. They can be indistinguishable from inflammatory or retention polyps {6195073} that may mainly occur in adults. Inflammatory polyps that occur in the context of inflammatory bowel disease (often called pseudopolyps or postinflammatory polyps) have a different morphology, lacking the cystic dilatation {28321155}. Foci of low-grade dysplasia are seen in approximately 10% of colorectal juvenile polyposis syndrome polyps, while < 1% of colorectal syndromic polyps show high-grade dysplasia {22965402}. Dysplasia is not seen in sporadic juvenile polyps {17768394}. Given the challenges in discerning between these polyps, without genetic confirmation of juvenile polyposis or PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome, it is often appropriate to issue a descriptive diagnosis and suggest adjunct investigations in relevant cases {20924281;26721304}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Demonstration of germline pathogenic variants in SMAD4, BMPR1A, or PTEN can aid in the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a polypoid lesion characterized by expanded inflammatory stroma with cystically dilated glands lined by colonic epithelium.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis depends on clinical context (see section Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4) and section PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PTEN)). Sporadic juvenile polyps are not associated with an increased cancer risk {17768394;31765335;34839308}.
