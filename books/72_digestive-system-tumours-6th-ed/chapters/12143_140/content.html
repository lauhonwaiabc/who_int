12.1.4.3 Enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma

Definition

Enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL) is an aggressive primary intestinal T-cell lymphoma of intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs), which exhibits variable cellular pleomorphism and occurs in individuals with coeliac disease.

ICD-O coding

97171/3 Enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2A90.7 & XA6452 Enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma of small intestine

Related terminology

Not recommended: type I enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The small intestine is involved in about 90% of cases: most frequently the jejunum, but other segments of the small bowel, and less commonly the stomach and colon, may be affected {21566094;10673521;20197551;23313469}. Multifocal lesions are present in 32–54% of cases {10673521;23313469}. As many as one third of EATLs evolving from refractory coeliac disease type 2 (RCD2) may initially manifest at extraintestinal locations (e.g. skin, lymph nodes, spleen) {23313469}. Dissemination to extragastrointestinal sites is most often to mesenteric and abdominal lymph nodes, but other organs, including the bone marrow, lung, or liver, may also be involved {8583077;23313469;21566094}. Widespread disease (Lugano stage II–IV) is observed in 43–70% of patients at diagnosis {21566094;23313469;20197551}.

Clinical features

Abdominal pain, diarrhoea (due to malabsorption), and weight loss are the most common symptoms and signs. Almost half of the patients present with an acute abdominal emergency due to small bowel perforation or obstruction. Many patients are known to have adult (or childhood)-onset coeliac disease, but coeliac disease is frequently diagnosed only at the time of EATL diagnosis. The duration of symptoms before diagnosis of EATL varies widely (from weeks to years) {10673521} but is < 3 months in most cases {20197551}. About 50% of patients have a prodromal phase of unresponsiveness to a gluten-free diet before the development of EATL, referred to as RCD {23313469}, which is sometimes associated with small intestinal ulceration (ulcerative jejunitis) {9250161;10381521}. Such patients usually present with anorexia, severe malnutrition, and hypoalbuminaemia. B symptoms (other than weight loss) are present in a third of patients with EATL, and a significant proportion have anaemia, elevated LDH, or hypercalcaemia {20197551;21566094;23313469}. A haemophagocytic syndrome has been reported in 16–40% of cases {22100722;23313469}.

Refractory coeliac disease

Approximately 0.3–1% of adults with coeliac disease have persistent villous atrophy and fail to improve clinically or develop recurrent malabsorptive symptoms and signs despite adhering to a strict gluten-free diet for > 12 months. Upon exclusion of other gastrointestinal disorders and coeliac disease–related malignancies, these patients are considered to have RCD {17470479;24387637;24917550}. Two subtypes of RCDs are recognized, according to the absence (type 1, RCD1) or presence (type 2, RCD2) of abnormal IELs {33707055}.

RCD1

The majority of IELs are of TCRαβ lineage and express sCD3 and CD8 (i.e. normal immunophenotype). TR rearrangement analysis usually shows polyclonal populations; monoclonality may be detected in a small proportion of RCD1 cases but is often transient. The etiology of this condition is unknown, but surreptitious gluten ingestion is suspected in some patients. Variably sized clonal expansions of mucosal T-cell subsets harbouring somatic mutations (STAT3, DDX3X) have been identified {40367192}. Mucosal pathological changes are the same as in uncomplicated coeliac disease. Therefore, differentiating RCD1 from active coeliac disease due to ongoing gluten ingestion can be challenging, as both present similarly, and detecting trace or inadvertent gluten exposure is often difficult. The symptoms of RCD1 are milder than that of RCD2 {19014942}, and the course is generally benign (5-year survival rate: 80–96%), with a low risk of EATL development (3–14%) {19014942;17470479;24387637;18996383}.

RCD2

The IELs usually express cCD3 and NK antigens, especially NKp46 (NCR1), but lack sCD3, CD8, and T-cell receptors {10381521;30448772;9496937}. Other immunophenotypes (CD8+, sCD3+, and/or TCRαβ+ or TCRγδ+) can occasionally be seen {18360280;12171959;33544565}. The immunophenotypic and molecular profiles of the majority of RCD2 cases suggest an origin from an innate lymphoid cell subset with variable commitment to a T-cell lineage, which resides in the small intestinal epithelium {22364936;22760007;27612641}. CD30 expression is considered an indicator of transformation to EATL {12171959}. Flow cytometry is the best modality to assess IEL phenotype, and a cut-off point of > 20% abnormal cells has been shown to have a high sensitivity in detecting RCD2 {18024205}. For immunohistochemistry, a threshold of > 50% CD8− IELs (of all CD3ε+ IELs) is recommended for the classification of RCD2, since CD8− TCRγδ+ IELs are frequently increased in coeliac disease (and RCD1), often accounting for > 20% of all IELs, and can be mistaken for the aberrant IELs characteristic of RCD2{25062848;22810901}. A threshold of > 25 NKp46+ IELs per 100 epithelial cells has been reported to be a reliable indicator of RCD2 {30448772}. Clonal TR rearrangements can be demonstrated in most cases. However, about 70% of cases with clonal rearrangements have incomplete or non-functional TR rearrangements, and 25% lack clonal TR rearrangements {22364936;29703761;27612641}. The majority of cases have somatic genetic alterations that overlap with EATL (see below). Villous atrophy is usually severe in RCD2. The abnormal IELs lack significant cytological atypia, and they can disseminate along the entire GI tract (or to extragastrointestinal sites), with frequent infiltration of the lamina propria {12524401;19444812}. Mucosal ulcers are observed in some cases (ulcerative jejunitis). RCD2 is considered a cryptic lymphoma of IELs or EATL in situ and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality (5-year survival rate: 44–58%) and a high propensity to transform to EATL (30–52% within ~5 years) {19014942;17470479;24387637;18996383}.

Epidemiology

EATL constitutes about 3% of peripheral T-cell lymphomas and accounts for two thirds of all primary intestinal T-cell lymphomas reported in Europe and the USA; it is extremely rare in Asia {21566094}. It usually occurs in the sixth and seventh decades of life (median age: 61 years), with a slight male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.04–2.8:1) {21566094;10673521;23313469;20197551;18618372}. All patients have the genetic background of coeliac disease. Therefore, EATL is more common in regions with a high seroprevalence of coeliac disease, particularly Europe (0.05–0.14 cases per 100 000 population) {15825131;20197551;18618372} and the USA (0.016 cases per 100 000 population) {22169928}.

Etiology

EATL is strongly associated with coeliac disease. Evidence of an association between coeliac disease and EATL comes from the following: identical HLA-DQ2 haplotypes of patients with coeliac disease and EATL, the presence of gluten sensitivity in patients with EATL, the protective effect of a gluten-free diet, and case series showing progression from coeliac disease through RCD to EATL with clonal identity {7558926;3094712;2707633;19996326}. Homozygosity for HLA-DQ2 alleles, observed in 53–56% of patients with EATL (compared with 21% of patients with uncomplicated coeliac disease), and older age, both connoting increased exposure to gluten, are risk factors for the development of EATL {16527694;23313469}.

Pathogenesis

Small intestinal IELs are the postulated normal counterparts of EATL, based on immunophenotypic identity {3260750;10381521;23313469}. The lymphomas were thought to originate from TCRαβ IELs, but studies have suggested derivation from multiple IEL subsets. Virtually all cases harbour clonal TR rearrangements, which are identical to those in antecedent RCD2 when present {9250161;7856760;11709515}. Besides developing in a stepwise manner with an intermediate RCD2 phase, EATL can also arise de novo {23313469;11709515}. Similarities in the genomic landscapes of RCD2 and EATL indicate shared oncogenic mechanisms, initiated by highly prevalent mutations in members of the JAK-1/STAT3 signalling axis (80% of RCD2 and 90% of EATL cases) {33544565;33579790;27600764;28424246}. Epigenetic regulators (TET2, KMT2D) and DDX3X, as well as NF-κB (TNFAIP3 [A20], TNIP3), DNA damage/repair (POT1, TP53), and immune escape (CD58, FAS, B2M) pathway genes, are also recurrently mutated in RCD2 and EATL {33579790;33544565;28424246}.

The mutation spectrum of RCD2-related EATL overlaps with that of de novo EATL, but TNFAIP3 mutations have so far only been observed in the former type. The genetic features of EATL and monomorphic epitheliotropic intestinal T-cell lymphoma (MEITL) overlap, although some alterations are differentially enriched in these two diseases (e.g. JAK1 and STAT3 in EATL; and JAK3, STAT5B, and SETD2 in MEITL) {27600764;33579790;26854024;28424246}.

The identification of recurrent gain-of-function JAK1 and STAT3 mutations highlights the role of chronic inflammation in coeliac disease–associated lymphomagenesis. These mutations provide transformed IELs with a selective growth advantage and enhance cytotoxicity in the inflamed coeliac mucosa, which has an abundance of JAK/STAT-activating cytokines (IL-15 produced by stressed enterocytes and lamina propria macrophages; and IL-2, IL-21, TNF, and IFN-γ released by gliadin-responsive CD4+ T cells) {12949719;27612641;22760007;28049849}. Loss-of-function TNFAIP3 mutations, resulting in NF-κB activation, may sensitize clonal IELs to the activating and prosurvival signals provided by cytokines released by CD4+ T cells {28049849}. Protracted inflammation could lead to DNA damage and the acquisition of genomic alterations, further fuelling EATL progression {27612641;20440074;22364936;28049849}.

Comparative genomic hybridization analysis has revealed a variety of chromosome aberrations in EATL, including segmental amplifications of chromosome 9q34 or deletions at 16q12.1 in > 80% of cases, and gains at 1q and 5q34-q35.2; some of these changes have also been observed in RCD2 {17484883;12851869;19014942;33579790}. These abnormalities, however, are not specific to EATL, as they can be present in other types of intestinal T-cell lymphomas, including MEITL.

Macroscopic appearance

The intestinal tumour can manifest as circumferentially oriented ulcers, ulcerated nodules, plaques, or strictures, and less commonly as an exophytic mass. The uninvolved mucosa can be thin, with loss of mucosal folds.

Histopathology

EATLs can display different patterns of intestinal involvement, ranging from scattered single or variably sized clusters of atypical cells in the lamina propria and/or submucosa to transmural infiltrates of pleomorphic medium-sized and large lymphoid cells exhibiting immunoblastic or anaplastic morphology. The tumours may have a pronounced inflammatory background consisting of histiocytes, eosinophils, small lymphocytes, and plasma cells, obscuring the neoplastic cells. Intraepithelial spread may be striking. Angioinvasion and angiodestruction are commonly seen. Mucosa adjacent to the tumour usually shows villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and intraepithelial lymphocytosis – histopathological features of coeliac disease. These coeliac disease–associated alterations are variable: more pronounced in the upper jejunum and less so in the distal small intestinal segments {15643667;23313469}.

The most common immunophenotypic profile is CD3+, CD5−, CD7+, CD4−, CD8−, CD56−, and CD103+; CD2 expression can vary. Cytotoxic granule proteins (TIA1, granzyme B, perforin) are generally expressed. T-cell receptor expression is mostly absent {18815285}. Approximately 25% of EATLs are CD8+ (primarily de novo tumours), and rare cases express TCRγδ {7856760;23313469;26462278}. Lymphomas with large-cell or anaplastic morphology are often CD30+ and at times EMA+; ALK expression is extremely rare {9811340;23313469;26531107}. The Ki-67 labelling index is usually > 50% {7856760}. In situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) is negative in the neoplastic cells.

Differential diagnosis

Differentiating EATL from MEITL can be challenging, because the lymphoma cells in some cases of MEITL may be pleomorphic or exhibit an atypical immunophenotype (CD8− and/or CD56−), and the villi at a distance from the tumour can be broad and show increased small IELs. The clinical features (no malabsorption and negative coeliac serologies), usually monomorphic lymphomatous infiltrate with few admixed inflammatory cells, lack of villous atrophy in the uninvolved small intestinal mucosa, and MATK expression can help support the diagnosis of MEITL (see #49464Table 12.03). Similarly, intestinal T-cell lymphoma NOS and secondary intestinal involvement by PTCLs, such as extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, can be distinguished from EATL by the clinical presentation, an absence of epitheliotropism, and differences in immunophenotype. Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma can involve or be localized to the GI tract, display anaplastic cytomorphology, and express CD103 {31392053}; so the evaluation of demographic information and HTLV-1 serology is paramount in excluding this possibility.

Cytology

Cytologically (e.g. effusion), the neoplastic cells are pleomorphic and medium to large, with round or oval vesicular nuclei, invariably prominent nucleoli, and moderate to abundant pale-staining cytoplasm. The cytoplasm may show azurophilic granules with a Giemsa stain.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a lymphoma with the bulk of disease localized to the GI tract; infiltration by pleomorphic medium-sized to large lymphoid cells; a variable inflammatory background, often including many eosinophils and histiocytes; uninvolved intestinal mucosa showing features of coeliac disease (villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, intraepithelial lymphocytosis); T-cell lineage, often with a CD4−/CD8− phenotype and expression of cytotoxic markers (TIA1, granzyme B, perforin).

Desirable: CD30 positivity (predominantly in cases of large cell or anaplastic morphology) is desirable; in problematic cases, the presence of JAK1 and/or STAT3 SH2 domain hotspot mutations may help in differentiating EATL from MEITL.

Staging

EATL is staged according to the Lugano classification, which has been adopted by the ninth-edition Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification system {25113753} [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for EATL is very poor because of the frequent multifocal nature of the disease, high rate of intestinal (or extraintestinal) recurrence, and poor nutritional condition of patients, especially those with preceding RCD2. The median overall survival is < 10 months. Patients with de novo EATL have better outcomes than those with RCD2-related EATL (5-year overall survival rate: 50% vs 0–8%) {23313469;17470479}. The use of novel aggressive chemotherapy regimens followed by autologous stem cell transplantation has been shown to enhance survival {20197551;23361910}, and patients with CD30-positive EATLs have shown promising responses to CD30-targeted therapies {23411380;33259598}.

Prognostic factors are not well established. Recently, the EATL Prognostic Index was introduced, which reportedly performs better than the International Prognostic Index and another prognostic model for T-cell lymphomas (the Prognostic Index for Peripheral T-cell Lymphoma [PIT]) {25779949}.
