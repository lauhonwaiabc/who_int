8.1.1.1 Anal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia

Definition

Anal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN) is a non-invasive neoplasm of the anal canal and perianal region.

Anal squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) are HPV-associated non-invasive neoplasms of the anal canal and perianal region.

Localization

Anal SILs are localized to three sites: skin (> 50 mm from the anal opening visualized with gentle traction on the buttocks), perianal (completely visualized with gentle traction on the buttocks and within 50 mm of the anal opening), and intra-anal (anal canal), often arising in the anal transformation zone.

Clinical features

SILs are often asymptomatic but may present as a solitary or multiple cauliflower-like mass (typically classic condyloma acuminatum or giant condyloma acuminatum) with associated anal discomfort or pruritus especially in classic condyloma. Giant condyloma (Buschke–Löwenstein tumour) present with a large pushing mass, which may arise from a longstanding classic condyloma acuminatum. Tumour growth is usually slow but may be rapid in immunocompromised individuals {35562936}. It is often associated with fistulas, abscesses, bleeding, and displacement of adjacent tissue {35562936}.

Although routine screening for anal SIL is not performed for average-risk populations, presence of condylomas should prompt further evaluation for HPV-related disease. Anoscopy for assessment of lesion extent is warranted. Screening with anal cytology is often performed for high-risk populations (men who have had sex with men with HIV or otherwise, individuals with HIV aged 35 years or older, women with history of HPV-related genital cancers and precancers, solid-organ transplant recipients, and other immunocompromised persons), followed by high-resolution anoscopy and biopsy to confirm diagnosis. Either 3% or 5% acetic acid is applied to the anal canal during anoscopy, and the dysplastic epithelial cells absorb the acetic acid, resulting in a scaly white appearance with punctations, mosaicism, and irregular vascularity. Lugol’s solution, an iodine-based solution absorbed by healthy tissue and not by dysplastic tissue, can also be applied to the anal canal mucosa to aid in detection.

Epidemiology

Classic condyloma remains an uncommon disease, but its incidence has been increasing at a steady rate over the past several decades and occurs most frequently in patients in their twenties {26602622;30397396}. The prevalence of classic anogenital condyloma is similar in men and women, and classic anal condyloma is more common in young men who have sex with men and HIV-positive individuals. Giant condyloma tends to occur more frequently in men in their forties, presenting an M:F ratio of 2.7:1, but may occur in paediatric patients also {35562936}. The epidemiology of anal HSIL is similar to that of anal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), which is described in section Anal squamous cell carcinoma.

Etiology

SIL of the anus is caused by HPV infection {15241823;19960431}. Low-risk HPV genotypes mediate the development of low-risk lesions, whereas high-risk HPV genotypes, most commonly HPV16 and HPV18, cause high-risk lesions {19838162;20730855}. Classic condyloma and giant condyloma are linked to the low-oncogenic-risk subtypes HPV6 and HPV11 in > 90% of cases {35562936}. Risk factors for acquiring the virus include immunodeficient state (HIV infection, after solid-organ and bone marrow transplantation) and increased contacts (unprotected sex with multiple partners and anal intercourse) {25597932}. Smoking is an additional risk factor. Conditions with inborn errors of immunity may predispose to multiple condylomas {35562936}.

Although most anal intraepithelial neoplasias (AINs) are related to HPV, a subset arise independently of HPV, and these are analogous to TP53-mutant and TP53-wildtype intraepithelial neoplasia in the vulva {35437330}. Differentiated intraepithelial neoplasia is a non-invasive precursor of HPV-independent SCC of the anus exhibiting atypia of the basal and parabasal squamous epithelium and harbours TP53 mutations (TP53-mutant) {26402383;35437330}, whereas HPV-independent, TP53-wildtype verruciform acanthotic intraepithelial neoplasia exhibits hyperkeratosis and hypergranulosis with or without cytoplasmic pallor of the upper epithelial layers (TP53-wildtype) {35437330;39863109}.

Pathogenesis

HPV infection has two possible fates: transient productive infection, which gives rise to low-risk lesions, and proliferative oncogenic infection, which produces high-risk lesions {28964706;26193301}. Low-risk lesions do not progress to high-risk lesions in most instances. The risk of progression from a high-risk lesion to SCC is influenced by HPV genotype, immune status, and other factors {15241823;26193301;19838162;22427679}.

Macroscopic appearance

Classic condyloma appears as a warty tumour with an irregular surface. Giant condyloma may grow to > 100 mm and often has deep local extension with displacement of surrounding tissues {35562936}. Anal SIL may present as a plaque-like or verrucous lesion but is often not discernible with the naked eye, requiring high-resolution anoscopy.

Histopathology

HPV-associated preinvasive lesions of anal SCC have been associated with a variety of terminologies resulting in non-uniform and confusing diagnoses. In addition, the three-tiered dysplasia terminology was associated with poor reproducibility, particularly with the intermediate category of dysplasia/AIN {22742517}. The Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) standardization project proposed a simple, unified two-tiered terminology for lesions of the anogenital tract. This terminology reflects the current understanding of HPV biology and facilitates clear communication across specialties and is now widely used in the USA {22742517}. LSIL includes what were previously classified as mild dysplasia, anal intraepithelial neoplasia I (AIN I), anal squamous intraepithelial lesion I, and condyloma acuminatum. Older terms, including “condyloma acuminatum”, may be included in parentheses {22742517}. Histologically, LSIL shows cytological atypia and mitotic figures in the lower third of the epithelium only, and it is associated with koilocytotic atypia. Condyloma acuminatum, when included under LSIL, is a papillomatous exophytic growth with marked epithelial thickening, broad rete pegs, parakeratosis, and koilocytotic atypia. Anal condylomas should be thoroughly sampled, as both LSIL and HSIL may be seen in anal condylomas due to infection with multiple HPV subtypes {25510243;29178840;30636708}. HSIL includes lesions that were previously classified as moderate dysplasia, severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, Bowen disease, and bowenoid papulosis. Microscopically, HSIL shows involvement of two thirds or more of the squamous epithelium by marked cytological atypia, mitotic figures in the upper two thirds of the epithelium, atypical mitotic figures, and loss of nuclear polarity. p16 immunohistochemistry is characteristically diffusely positive and can distinguish HSIL from reactive changes {22742517}. The term “Bowen disease” refers to perineal and perianal plaques characterized by histological features of HSIL with underlying chronic inflammation. The term is no longer encouraged, to avoid confusion. Bowenoid papulosis also shows the same histological features as HSIL and is located in the perianal skin. However, bowenoid papulosis presents as papules and classically has a more indolent course, with reported regression {6315601}. This term is also no longer encouraged in pathology reports, as it is a clinical diagnosis. Histologically, Bowen disease and bowenoid papulosis cannot be distinguished from HSIL involving the anus.

Analogous to the terminology for HPV-independent preinvasive lesions in the vulva, the terminology for HPV-independent preinvasive lesions in the anal canal is emerging and currently not codified {35437330}.

Cytology

For anal exfoliative cytology, the specimen swab should sample the entire length of the anal canal mucosa {36626881}. Anal cytology should be reported as per the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (BSRTC) and adequacy criteria observed {36626881}. A normal smear does not have abnormal squamous cells. Abnormal smears can be categorized as LSIL, HSIL, or atypical squamous cells of uncertain significance. This last category includes atypical cells that do not meet the criteria for LSIL or HSIL. LSIL typically shows koilocytotic atypia and > 3-fold nuclear enlargement in superficial squamous epithelial cells. HSIL shows more severe nuclear atypia and involves more immature-appearing cells {36626881}. The sensitivity and specificity of anal cytology for HSIL and SCC were 70% and 73%, respectively, in one large study {29659752}. There is modest correlation between biopsy results and cytology, indicating that cytology should be combined with other methods for screening {29659752}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

High-risk HPV inactivates tumour suppressor proteins p53 and RB1 in host cells via oncoproteins E6 and E7, respectively, resulting in compensatory upregulation of the tumour suppressor protein p16. Low-risk HPV E7 proteins do not induce p16 overexpression. As a result, p16 is overexpressed in nearly all HSILs and some LSILs associated with high-risk HPV. A positive p16 immunohistochemical stain is defined as strong, continuous block-like staining that involves basal keratinocytes and extends beyond the lower third of the epithelium {22742517}. Application of p16 immunohistochemistry is useful in distinguishing HSIL from anal transition zone and in informing the diagnosis of HSIL in morphologically equivocal cases {22742517}. HPV subtyping by PCR or by RNA in situ hybridization can also be helpful, particularly in distinguishing LSIL from negative/reactive lesions {28403015}. Absence of p16 overexpression in conjunction with overexpression of p53 on immunohistochemistry is useful in the diagnosis of HPV-independent TP53-mutant AIN, but this is not useful in distinguishing HPV-independent TP53-wildtype AIN from histological mimics.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

LSIL

Essential: cytological atypia in the lower third of the anal squamous epithelium or koilocytic changes.

HSIL

Essential: marked cytological atypia in at least two thirds of the thickness of the anal squamous epithelium.

Desirable: strong, continuous, block positive p16 staining.

 

Staging

HSIL is staged as Tis.

Prognosis and prediction

Most cases of LSIL will regress and not progress further in immunocompetent hosts {22742517}. Consequently, LSIL is considered a preinvasive lesion rather than a precursor of SCC. Small classic condylomas can regress and be treated conservatively. Those located in the anal canal and distal end of the rectal ampulla require surgery {38677817}. Large classic condyloma and giant condyloma do not regress spontaneously and are prone to recurrence and malignant transformation {35562936}. Recurrence of anal condyloma varies depending on the treatment modality {38677817}. The prevalence of high-grade dysplasia and carcinoma in patients with pre-existing anal condyloma is higher in HIV-positive patients {29183133}.

HSIL has a high risk of recurrence and may progress to (and is a precursor of) invasive SCC, particularly in men with HIV who have sex with men {28252530}. Treatment options for anal HSIL include ablation with electrocautery or infrared coagulation, various topical therapies, or expectant management {35704479}. Because all treatment options carry a high recurrence rate (varying from 10% to 75%), patients require close posttreatment surveillance {35704479}. The Anal Cancer / HSIL Outcomes Research (ANCHOR) study, a randomized controlled trial of 4459 HIV-positive patients aged > 35 years with biopsy-proven HSIL, demonstrated a statistically significant difference in progression to SCC in the treatment group compared with the active monitoring group {35017115}. The cumulative progression to cancer at 48 months was 0.9% in the treatment group compared with 1.8% in the active monitoring group. HPV vaccination before HPV exposure is recommended to prevent disease {35017115;34780705}.

The risk of recurrence and progression of HPV-independent TP53-mutant and TP53-wildtype AIN is not well defined, and there are no evidence-based recommendations on the management of this disease whether it arises in the perianal skin or anal canal.
