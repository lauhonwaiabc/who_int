4.1.2.2 Ampullary adenocarcinoma

Definition

Ampullary adenocarcinoma is an invasive tumour with glandular differentiation arising in the ampulla of Vater.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C16.0 Adenocarcinoma of ampulla of Vater

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); medullary carcinoma (85100/3); mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); poorly cohesive carcinoma / signet-ring cell carcinoma (84901/3); tubular adenocarcinoma, intestinal type (81445/3); tubular adenocarcinoma, pancreatobiliary/gastric type (81630/3); tubular adenocarcinoma with mixed features or hybrid features (82113/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3)

Localization

Ampullary adenocarcinomas can arise from (and have their epicentre in) the various compartments of the ampulla {23026934}. Ampullary duodenal adenocarcinomas arise from the duodenal surface of the ampulla (often in association with an adenoma). In contrast, intra-ampullary adenocarcinomas have their epicentre within the lumen of the ampulla and therefore result in only minimal changes on the ampullary duodenal surface. Intra-ampullary adenocarcinomas are further subdivided as those that arise from intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm (IAPN) and those of ampullary duct origin that arise from flat dysplasia.

In some cases, the precise origin within the ampulla cannot be determined.

Clinical features

Unlike adenocarcinomas in other segments of the small intestine, ampullary adenocarcinomas are often detected at a relatively low stage because of the symptoms and signs caused by the obstruction of the bile duct, leading to jaundice. Occasionally, pancreatic injury / insufficiency due to blocked pancreatic outflow may also occur {23026934;20871215;21084962}. On imaging, ampullary carcinoma of ampullary ductal origin is often recognized by secondary obstructive changes such as the double-duct sign, while the subtle ampullary tumour itself may be difficult to appreciate. Ulcerated carcinomas may present with gastrointestinal bleeding and occult blood in the stool.

Epidemiology

Ampullary adenocarcinoma accounts for 6–9% of all malignancies occurring in the periampullary region (pancreatic head, common bile duct, ampulla, and non-ampullary duodenum). Approximately 15% of pancreatoduodenectomies are performed for ampullary cancers {23026934;25783680;20871215;29518820}. The age-adjusted incidence rate varies from 0.30 to 0.80 cases per 100 000 person-years {19697352;33712346}, with men being slightly more frequently affected {33712346;39286671}. The median patient age is in the sixties {25783680;39492830;34590592}. In patients who are very young, genetic predisposition such as Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis should be considered {34590592}.

Etiology

For the vast majority of ampullary adenocarcinomas, a specific etiology is not evident. Inflammatory diseases (e.g. Crohn, coeliac), which have been documented as predisposing factors for adenocarcinomas of other parts of the small bowel, account for only a small percentage of cases in the ampulla {28694132;34704127;12702783}. Hereditary conditions such as familial adenomatous polyposis and Lynch syndrome are also relevant associations {11677205;34873870}. Aspirin and light alcohol consumption are likely protective {35894762}.

Pathogenesis

Considering that various chemical milieus (pancreatic enzymes, bile, duodenal contents) converge in this area with different epithelial linings, local toxic factors and vulnerabilities of the different compartments are believed to play a role in carcinogenesis. The association with low insertion and prior cholecystectomy {32336556;35894762} also points to chemical milieu alterations in the pathogenesis of ampullary cancers. Information specific to carcinogenesis related to IAPN is not available; however, IAPNs can progress to invasive carcinomas with aggressive features {38938087}.

Ampullary duodenal adenocarcinoma commonly develops from intestinal-type adenoma through the accumulation of somatic mutations in select genes. The most frequently somatically altered genes in ampullary adenocarcinoma are, in descending order, TP53, KRAS, APC, SMAD4, and CDKN2A. APC mutations are observed more frequently in intestinal-type ampullary adenocarcinoma, whereas for KRAS mutations, the difference between intestinal and pancreatobiliary subtypes is not significant {31256008}. A proportion of cases harbour ERBB2 amplification without downstream KRASmutation {25975284;28078121;31256008}.

The ampulla is also one of the common sites for familial adenomatous syndrome–related adenocarcinomas, although such cases account for a very small percentage of ampullary cancers.

Macroscopic appearance

To establish whether a tumour is of ampullary origin (and to exclude infiltration by tumours from the immediately neighbouring bile duct and pancreas), proper dissection of pancreatoduodenectomy specimens is crucial. Because the documentation of the epicentre of the cancer within the ampulla is an essential diagnostic criterion, the specimen dissection technique used should provide detailed views of the ampulla and adjacent structures, i.e. the common bile duct, pancreatic duct, pancreas, and duodenum. Examining the duodenum-facing surface of the ampulla (ampullary duodenum) and determining the epicentre of the mass is a crucial part of this macroscopic evaluation.

Ampullary duodenal carcinomas typically present as large ulcerating or vegetating masses readily visible in the duodenal mucosa. Polypoid nodular components correspond to adenomas, beneath which the infiltrating carcinoma may hide. The ampullary orifice can be obscured and is often engulfed by the mass.

In ampullary carcinomas arising from an IAPN, polypoid, nodular granular material fills the ampullary cavity. From the duodenal perspective, IAPN-associated carcinomas can present as mucosa-covered bulges, often with a centrally located dilated ampullary orifice, from which granular nodular material may protrude.

Carcinomas of ampullary ductal origin are also intra-ampullary, and they often appear, from the duodenal perspective, as puckering or mucosa-covered button-like elevations of the ampulla, some with subtle ulceration. On cut section, the ampulla typically reveals poorly defined sclerosing thickening of the intra-ampullary ducts, leading to circumferentially constrictive lesions.

Histopathology

The vast majority of ampullary adenocarcinomas, regardless of site-specific classification, are gland-forming (tubular) adenocarcinomas {27739441}.

Historically, the direction of differentiation of ampullary adenocarcinomas has been expected to fall into one of two distinct histological categories: pancreatobiliary or intestinal. However, about 40% of cancers have mixed or hybrid phenotypes, and this is also reflected in their associated immunoprofiles {27739441;27586202}. There are also a substantial number of cases with heterogeneous immunoprofiles {24832159;23439753;28505002;21084962}. This is not surprising, considering that the ampulla is a transitional region with distinctive cellular compartments, the small intestinal epithelium is not truly colonic and the ampullary duct is not exactly identical to pancreaticobiliary ducts. Nevertheless, panels of immunohistochemical markers including MUC1, MUC2, and CDX2, while not entirely specific, can aid in the classification of tubular adenocarcinomas as predominantly intestinal type (MUC2+, CDX2+) or pancreatobiliary type (MUC1+) in a substantial proportion of cases {24832159;23439753;28505002}.

Intestinal-type adenocarcinoma

These adenocarcinomas are morphologically similar to adenocarcinomas of the colon {27586202}. The neoplastic glands are composed of tall columnar cells with elongated, pseudostratified nuclei. Scattered goblet, Paneth, and neuroendocrine cells may be present {27586202}. A substantial proportion are associated with an intestinal-type adenoma, and these cancers more commonly arise from the ampullary duodenum {23026934}. Of note, even histologically conventional intestinal-type adenocarcinomas in this region are often immunophenotypically distinct from true colonic intestinal-type adenocarcinomas (see below) {27586202}. For example, CK7 and CK20 coexpression is not uncommon, and some cancers may be predominantly CK7-positive. Similarly, the MUC1, MUC2, and CDX2 profile may sometimes not match with the histological phenotype, showing the opposite of the expected pattern.

Pancreatobiliary-type or gastric-type adenocarcinoma

Similar to pancreatic ductal and extrahepatic biliary tract adenocarcinomas, these tubular carcinomas are composed of relatively small neoplastic glandular units widely separated by desmoplastic stroma. The neoplastic epithelium is usually a single layer of cuboidal to low columnar cells, without substantial nuclear pseudostratification, but with more pleomorphism than in the intestinal type {27586202;23026934}. Although carcinomas with gastric-like mucin (histologically and immunophenotypically) have been designated as gastric type, they have significant overlap, both morphologically and immunohistochemically, with pancreatobiliary-type adenocarcinomas and are therefore currently regarded as part of this group {28505002;21084962}.

Tubular adenocarcinoma with mixed or hybrid features

Many ampullary carcinomas are composed of a mix of components with distinct intestinal and pancreatobiliary features, both histologically and immunohistochemically {24832159;27739441;27586202;28505002}. They should be classified as tubular adenocarcinomas with mixed features, with the predominant direction of differentiation noted. There are also carcinomas in which the overall histomorphology is ambiguous or hybrid, showing features of both types within individual cancer glands. Immunohistochemical studies using antibodies to MUC1, MUC2, CDX2, and CK20 may be helpful in determining the predominant direction of differentiation {27739441;27586202;28505002;24832159}. However, even when using this panel, as many as 30% of ampullary carcinomas have hybrid features and do not fit into one of the specific cell lineages, and they therefore have to be classified as “tubular adenocarcinoma NOS” {28505002;24832159}.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma

Mucinous adenocarcinoma (with > 50% stromal mucin deposition) is proportionally more common in the duodenum than in the ampulla. In the ampulla, some degree of extracellular mucin deposition is seen in 15% of cases, with two thirds of these qualifying as mucinous adenocarcinoma {19697352}. Strips and glands of neoplastic epithelium float in stromal pools of mucin. The epithelium is often columnar with intestinal phenotype (also with diffuse CDX2 and MUC2 expression). Scattered signet-ring cells may be seen floating within the stromal mucin but not infiltrating the stroma as individual cells or cords – an important distinction from poorly cohesive cell carcinoma discussed below. Mucinous adenocarcinoma in the ampulla most commonly arises from adenomas of the ampullary duodenum but occasionally may also be seen in association with intra-ampullary tumours. Ampullary mucinous adenocarcinomas do not usually fulfil the criteria of pure colloid carcinoma, as they also have significant areas of stromal invasion devoid of stromal mucin, and they do not seem to confer the survival advantage of pure colloid carcinomas as described in the pancreas {12717243;37378903}.

Poorly cohesive cell (signet-ring cell) carcinoma

These cancers consist of poorly cohesive neoplastic cells that infiltrate as single cells or in small cords and clusters. Signet-ring morphology is often seen at least focally. Poorly cohesive cell carcinomas are relatively uncommon in the small bowel and often lack an adenomatous/preinvasive component {19697352;26057354;25202392;31612756;36407266}. They are usually advanced at diagnosis and behave more aggressively.

Medullary carcinoma

Medullary carcinomas are akin to their colonic counterparts and constitute 3% of ampullary cancers {27739441;36502926}. They are characterized by large nodules of neoplastic cells with pushing borders and syncytial growth with associated Crohn-like inflammation. Like in the colon, they are strongly associated with microsatellite instability and PDL1 expression and behave less aggressively despite their often large size {32857459}.

Adenosquamous carcinoma

Adenosquamous carcinoma is extremely rare, accounting for 1% of all ampullary carcinomas {26420726;23721111}. Like in the pancreas, a carcinoma is qualified as adenosquamous only if the squamous component accounts for > 30% of the tumour. However, it is recommended that a small squamous component, if convincing, be mentioned in diagnostic reports, as this appears to be associated with shorter survival {35118421}. The squamous component expresses squamous-type cytokeratins, along with p63 and p40. A pure squamous cell carcinoma should raise the possibility of metastasis to the ampulla.

High-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) and mixed carcinomas

Poorly differentiated NECs (both small and large cell types) and mixed carcinomas containing an adenocarcinoma component can arise in the ampulla {35553369;35892508;36536502} (see section Duodenal and ampullary neuroendocrine neoplasms). Of note, NECs in the ampulla are often associated with an adenomatous preinvasive component.

Undifferentiated carcinoma

Most carcinomas with undifferentiated (non-tubular) morphology are regarded as a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma with a solid, sheet-like growth pattern. Abortive evidence of glandular differentiation (some resembling acinar carcinoma) is demonstrable in these otherwise undifferentiated cells. In some, a glandular precursor lesion and/or an adenocarcinoma component establishes a glandular lineage.

There are, however, undifferentiated carcinomas that have other characteristics that place them into a more specific category. Undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells, as described in the pancreas, can occasionally occur in the ampulla {27508975}. Metastatic or direct involvement by a pancreatic or bile duct primary should be excluded {27508975}. The multinucleated giant cells in these tumours are histiocytic and non-neoplastic and label positively for the histiocytic marker CD68. In contrast, the neoplastic mononuclear cells, which often have a sarcomatoid appearance, may be keratin-positive and often show a mutant p53 pattern {28722124}.

Undifferentiated carcinomas with a distinct rhabdoid phenotype are also being recognized increasingly in the ampulla {26551623;34812766;38576599}. These tumours are typically large and show characteristic rhabdoid cells with abundant intracytoplasmic, eosinophilic rhabdoid bodies. The tumour cells are often dyscohesive and embedded in a myxoid matrix. Pleomorphic giant or spindle cell differentiation, as well as areas of glandular differentiation, may also be seen. Loss of nuclear immunolabelling for SMARCB1 (INI1), a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex, is characteristic. These tumours can have loss/coinactivation of multiple members of the SWI/SNF family of proteins, including SMARCA4, SMARCA2, and/or SMARCB1, indicating an association between SWI/SNF deficiency and rhabdoid phenotype. Some have ARID1A abnormalities.

 

Cytology

As ampullary adenocarcinomas arising in the ampullary duodenum are readily accessible for direct endoscopic biopsy, they seldom undergo aspiration biopsy. In contrast, IAPN-associated carcinomas are often covered by non-neoplastic ampullary duodenal mucosa and thus may be sampled with the aid of aspiration biopsy {24949343;27203738;38938087}. Features suggestive of adenocarcinoma include abnormal cellular arrangement with 3D cell clusters, crowding, loss of polarity, and overlapping nuclei, as well as atypical cellular features such as high N:C ratio, nuclear pleomorphism, irregular nuclear membrane, and prominent nucleoli. In formulating the diagnosis, the WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology may be utilized, acknowledging that it has not been tested and verified for this specific site [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Mismatch repair protein immunohistochemistry and/or microsatellite instability determination are required to evaluate a possible hereditary cause of ampullary adenocarcinoma, and they are also useful for selection for immunotherapy {26028255}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a glandular epithelial neoplasm in ampullary location, showing unequivocal evidence of invasion.

Staging

Ampullary adenocarcinomas are staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification systems [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]] [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]]. Careful macroscopic examination and dissection of resection specimens is crucial in order to achieve proper staging. Given the complex anatomy of the ampulla and depending on which segment of the ampulla the tumour arises from, even a minuscule invasive carcinoma may have to be assigned to pathological stage T2 or even T3, such that reporting of the size of invasive carcinoma is recommended {34417142;38938087}.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with a resected ampullary carcinoma have an overall 5-year survival rate of 40–70% {30628913;32857459;33712346;37149485;39402320;39492830}. In well-characterized cohorts of invasive ampullary carcinomas diagnosed according to updated criteria, the 5-year survival rate is 55% and as such much better than that of pancreatic and common bile duct cancers. Factors contributing to this difference include relative abundance of the preinvasive component, smaller size of the invasive component, lower rate of lymph node metastasis, higher resectability, and common occurrence of less aggressive histological phenotypes (medullary, mucinous) in ampullary cancers {39402320}. In patients without distant metastases, the strongest independent prognostic factor is lymph node metastasis, which occurs in up to 60% of cases {30628913;31141112;33449948;38155394;39402320}. Extranodal extension of lymph node metastasis has been reported as a predictor of poorer survival {37088435}. Further prognostic factors include perineural and vascular invasion, margin status, tumour budding, depth of invasion into the pancreas, and MUC5AC expression {20871215;28505002;30628913;37149485;38155394;38893239;39095625}. The overall size of the neoplastic mass that includes both the non-invasive and invasive components has marginal significance as a prognostic factor; however, emerging evidence suggests that the size of the invasive carcinoma is an independent prognosticator {38938087}. Carcinomas of ampullary ductal origin are often aggressive. This may be related to the fact that they are often of pancreatobiliary type and have reached an advanced T-stage at the time of presentation due to their ready access to the periduodenal soft tissue, despite their small size {23026934;21084962}. In general, carcinomas of predominantly pancreatobiliary type are more aggressive than those of intestinal type. However, in well-defined cohorts, the difference in behaviour is not found to be as striking as previously believed, and intestinal/pancreatobiliary type does not seem to be an independent prognostic factor {27586202;28505002;36272870;30628913}. Of note, even ampullary carcinoma of pancreatobiliary type has a substantially better prognosis than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma {27586202;39402320}. Grade also has some prognostic value {32808236;37149485}. In particular, some poorly differentiated non-tubular cancers, such as poorly cohesive cell and undifferentiated carcinoma, often present at an advanced stage and behave aggressively {31612756}.

Molecular tests are recommended for identifying patients who may benefit from personalized treatment. As many as 10–15% of ampullary carcinomas are mismatch repair–deficient {17653761;32857459;38439030}. Hence, routine immunohistochemical labelling for mismatch repair proteins or PCR for microsatellite instability is recommended with a view to immunotherapy based on PDL1 inhibition {37433437}. Next-generation sequencing of tumour tissue to identify somatic mutations that produce therapeutic targets is recommended for patients with advanced ampullary cancer, including but not limited to fusions (ALK, FGFR2, NRG1, NTRK genes, RET, ROS1), somatic mutations (BRAF, BRCA1/BRCA2, KRAS, PALB2), and amplifications (ERBB2) {26997442;37433437;38845233;39123483}.
