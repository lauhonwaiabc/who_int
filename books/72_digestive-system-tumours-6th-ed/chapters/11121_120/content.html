11.1.2.1 Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma

Definition

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is an invasive epithelial neoplasm that has glandular (ductal) differentiation, usually demonstrates luminal and/or intracellular mucin production, and lacks a substantial component of any other histological type.

ICD-O coding

85000/3 Duct adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C10.0 & XH7KH3 Duct adenocarcinoma, NOS, of pancreas

Related terminology

Acceptable: ductal adenocarcinoma; tubular adenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); colloid carcinoma (84800/3); hepatoid carcinoma (85760/3); medullary carcinoma (85100/3); poorly cohesive carcinoma / signet-ring cell carcinoma (84901/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3); undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells (80352/3)

Localization

Two thirds of PDACs arise in the head of the pancreas, and the remainder in the body or tail of the gland {30264642} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. The vast majority of PDACs are solitary, but multifocal disease can occur [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]] {27539084}. Very rarely, heterotopic pancreatic tissue can give rise to pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) and even to invasive carcinoma {28368927;39613894}.

Clinical features

Clinical features are often nonspecific and include unintentional weight loss, back pain, jaundice, decreased appetite and indigestion, changes in bowel habits, and fatigue {28404200}. New-onset diabetes mellitus (type 3c) may be an early manifestation of PDAC {28404095;29723506;34358720}. Rarely, depression may be a presenting symptom {37746894}. Symptoms of advanced disease are related to liver metastasis, invasion of adjacent organs (e.g. the duodenum), or spread to the peritoneal cavity (ascites). Patients occasionally present with migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau syndrome) and rarely with acute pancreatitis {28675831;28099352;39387458}.

PDACs frequently directly spread into retroperitoneal tissues and locally into peripancreatic organs. Lymph node metastases are common, and the nodes involved depend on the location of the primary tumour {17981748}. The liver is the most common distant organ involved, but lung, bone, and adrenal metastases can also be seen {28338509} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Cerebral metastases can occur but are uncommon. Lung metastases may histologically resemble primary pulmonary mucinous adenocarcinoma {23887294}.

Multidetector CT with dual-phase or multiphase dynamic contrast using early arterial, pancreatic, and late venous phases is one of the best imaging modalities for the pancreas, the surrounding vasculature, and the other abdominal organs {34467768}. PDAC usually appears as an irregular solid hypodense mass with abrupt cut-off and upstream dilatation of the main pancreatic duct. The double-duct sign (dilatation of both the biliary and the pancreatic ducts) is characteristic of carcinoma of the head of the pancreas, but may also be observed in non-neoplastic conditions including autoimmune pancreatitis {20396913;34467768;33586343}. MRI may be more sensitive than CT for the detection and evaluation of liver metastases {21693662}, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography provides excellent resolution of the pancreatic duct system. EUS allows high-resolution imaging of the pancreas and surrounding lymph nodes, vessels, and ascites; it also allows tissues to be sampled (EUS-FNA and EUS-FNAB), which remains the gold standard for diagnosis. PET may have diagnostic value, especially in the evaluation of enlarged lymph nodes or of persisting masses after therapy {26099670}. The serum markers CA19-9 and CEA are not useful for screening of asymptomatic individuals but can be used to monitor established disease {23331006}.

Epidemiology

The number of cases of PDAC is increasing, as shown by the 510 566 new cases that occurred worldwide in 2022 {38572751}. This makes PDAC the 12th most common malignancy, with age-adjusted incidence rates for males and females, respectively, of 8.6 and 5.9 cases per 100 000 person-years in high-income regions, and 3.3 and 2.4 cases per 100 000 person-years in low- and middle-income countries {28600530}. The median age at diagnosis is 69 years, with a predominance of cases occurring in men (M:F ratio: 1.2:1) {38615292;28600530}. PDAC accounts for 467 005 deaths per year, with a mortality rate of 5.0 deaths per 100 000 population in men and 3.5 deaths per 100 000 in women. It is currently the sixth leading cause of cancer mortality in both sexes combined {38572751}. If the mortality rates continue with this increasing trend, PDAC might become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the USA by 2030 {33804776}.

The 1-year overall survival rate of patients with PDAC is very low (24%), and the 5-year overall survival rate is even lower (13%). The incidence of PDAC varies among countries, with the highest age-standardized incidence rates in Europe and North America and the lowest rates in Africa and central Asia. Survival and mortality rates are very poor and show a mortality-to-incidence ratio of 0.9. Therefore, PDAC shows continuously increasing incidence and mortality rates {38572751;24840647}. Mortality rates of patients with PDAC in low- or middle-income countries are about 4–5 times as high as those in high-income countries, which means PDAC treatment needs to be internationally standardized as soon as possible {33804776}.

Risk factors for developing PDAC include family history, genetic disorders, complications, and preferences. Early diagnosis is important to improve the prognosis for patients with PDAC, but to achieve early diagnosis, it is extremely important to have a solid understanding of associated risk factors {33804776}.

Etiology

The most firmly established preventable risk factor for PDAC is tobacco smoking. The risk in smokers is 2–3 times higher than in non-smokers, and a dose–response relationship and a favourable effect of quitting smoking have been shown in many studies {18193270;19790196;37924460}. The proportion of cases of PDAC attributable to tobacco smoking has been estimated to be 20–30% in men and 10% in women {15285078}. Passive (second-hand) smoke and the use of smokeless tobacco products have also been linked to PDAC {39399427;23719601;21245160}.

Body habitus and dietary factors have been suggested to be related to PDAC, including obesity, low physical activity, high intake of (saturated) fats, and low intake of vegetables and fruits {36429506;26347100;26174790;28922784;22875754}. A positive association between alcohol consumption and PDAC has been reported in some, but not all, studies that have addressed this question, and the current consensus supports a possible weak effect of heavy alcohol consumption {19258474;20349126}.

Several medical conditions are associated with subsequent risk of PDAC, most notably chronic pancreatitis and longstanding diabetes mellitus {18061176;28762376}. A longstanding history of pancreatitis has been shown to increase the risk 2- to 10-fold, and the risk is particularly high in individuals with hereditary pancreatitis {9091646;22767586;16083707;18395079}. An increased risk has also been shown in several studies of patients with longstanding diabetes mellitus; the relative risk is likely in the range 1.5–2 {25275365;25057164}. Although studies are conflicting, patients with a history of a gastrectomy may have an increased risk of PDAC {3028929;23970016;22674223}. In addition, Helicobacter pylori infection increases the risk of PDAC, while high-dose aspirin consumption may reduce the risk {25951801;26958813;24969230;27999143}.

Some of the features of the descriptive epidemiology of PDAC (i.e. a high incidence among Black people in the USA, and a higher risk among men and urban residents) can be explained by differences in smoking habits, diabetes, and obesity {19723915;24203988;23622135}.

Individuals with type O blood have a lower risk than those with type A or B {19276450;23622135}. For further details on genetic predisposition, see section Hereditary and familial pancreatic cancer.

Pathogenesis

The mechanisms by which the environmental and chronic disease-related factors described in the Etiology subsection lead to neoplastic progression are well defined. For example, the mechanisms by which smoking, one of the leading risk factors of PDAC, increases the PDAC risk are likely multifactorial and include DNA damage as well as inflammation and fibrosis {22623649;28454347}. Cigarette smoke contains carcinogens that damage DNA, such as 4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (NNK) and benzo[a]pyrene (BaP), and as discussed below, somatic DNA mutations are known to drive the formation of PDAC {8342602;19351817}. Repeated episodes of inflammation, injury, and repair may explain the contribution of longstanding chronic pancreatitis to the development of PDAC {21665147}.

PDAC is essentially a genetic disease, caused by inherited (germline) deleterious variants and acquired somatic mutations. The inherited deleterious variants are described in section Hereditary and familial pancreatic cancer. The main somatic genetic drivers of PDAC were discovered in the late 1980s and the 1990s, and the exome of PDAC was first sequenced in 2008 {8553070;7726912;3047672;1764370;18772397}. Several large publicly funded next-generation sequencing efforts, such as the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have further defined the comprehensive molecular landscapes of PDAC {26909576;28810144}. These studies have confirmed four so-called mountains (i.e. the most common recurrent abnormalities in the PDAC genome): oncogenic mutations of KRAS (chromosome 12p), as well as loss-of-function mutations and/or deletions of the tumour suppressor genes TP53 (chromosome 17p), SMAD4 (DPC4) (chromosome 18q), and CDKN2A (P16) (chromosome 9p).

Oncogenic point mutations of KRAS at codon 12, 13, or 61 are found in > 90% of PDACs. The resulting constitutive activation of the RAS signalling pathway has a profound impact on cell-autonomous growth and survival, as well as paracrine effects on the tissue microenvironment that enable neoplastic cells to escape the immune system {22628411}. As described in the section on PanIN lesions, KRAS mutations are one of the earliest genetic alterations in the multistep progression from non-neoplastic cells to PDAC, and animal models have validated the seminal role of KRAS in PDAC initiation and maintenance {14706336;15894267;22541435;22232209;38693266}. Exciting preliminary results from targeting the protein product of the KRAS gene have been reported. KRAS-wildtype tumours often harbour other potentially actionable genetic alterations including gene fusions involving genes such as BRAF, FGFR2, ALK, RET, and NRG1, as well as amplifications of FGF3, ERBB2, FGFR3, NTRK genes, and MET {35302596;39729677;33148671}. Many of these alternative drivers activate the MAP kinase signalling pathway, a downstream RAS effector.

The CDKN2A (P16) tumour suppressor gene is inactivated in 40% of PDACs by homozygous deletion, in 40% by loss of one allele coupled with an intragenic mutation in the other, and in an additional 15% by hypermethylation of the CDKN2A (P16) promoter {7726912;9242437}. Inactivation of p16 removes an important cell-cycle regulator. The MTAP gene, on chromosome 9p21, is frequently included within homozygous deletions of the CDKN2A (p16INK4A) gene, and MTAP, a key enzyme associated with the salvage of methionine and adenine, is deficient in 20–30% of PDACs and may serve as a therapeutic target {39293516;15662124;39282709;34385182}.

The TP53 tumour suppressor gene is inactivated in 75% of PDACs by loss of one allele coupled with an intragenic mutation in the second allele {1764370;8187092}. The TP53 gene codes for a protein, p53, that has a number of functions including DNA repair, cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and senescence. The SMAD4 (DPC4) tumour suppressor gene is inactivated in 55% of PDACs, in 35% by homozygous deletion, and in 20% by loss of one allele coupled with an intragenic mutation in the second allele {8553070}. The SMAD4 protein functions in the TGF-β pathway.

In addition to these genes, a large number of genes are altered at a lower prevalence in PDAC (genomic “hills”). Many of these genes code for proteins involved in DNA repair, chromatin remodelling, and or axon guidance, some of which delineate clinically important groups that may generate responses to specific therapies. For example, mutations of genes involved in DNA repair (most commonly deleterious germline BRCA2 variants, but also other somatic mutations in genes such as PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, and RAD51) can be found in as many as 15–20% of PDACs, and cancers harbouring these mutations may be particularly sensitive to agents such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors {25719666;27768182}.

Similarly, defects in epigenetic drivers that regulate chromatin accessibility, and therefore gene expression, are found in almost 40% of PDACs, including abnormalities of the COMPASS-like complex (KMT2C [MLL3], KDM6A) and the DNA-binding helicase multiprotein complex known as the SWI/SNF complex – ARID1A, PBRM1, SMARCA4 (BRG1) {26909576;28810144}. Defects in these chromatin regulators cause widespread transcriptional deregulation in PDAC thus promoting tumour growth and metastases {29533787;25792600}. For example, ARID1A belongs to the SWI/SNF family of chromatin remodellers, and mutations in ARID1A are found in 5–10% of PDAC {28810144}. ARID1A is known to function as a tumour suppressor gene, and its expression levels correlate with tumour stage and differentiation, but not with lymph node or distant metastasis {29486633;35512587}.

In addition to the extensive molecular alterations in neoplastic cells, interactions of neoplastic cells with non-neoplastic cells also play a crucial role in tumorigenesis as well as dissemination of PDAC {34890551;39007872}. The multifaceted contribution of the complex tumour microenvironment to PDAC progression, metastasis, and treatment response are becoming increasingly evident and indicate dynamic interactions between the tumour cells and their surroundings {33727309;36792355;39007872}.

Macroscopic appearance

PDACs form firm and poorly defined yellowish-white firm solid masses, usually without haemorrhagic necrosis but occasionally with microcystic or macrocystic areas. Most resected carcinomas of the pancreatic head (60%) are usually between 20 and 40 mm (mean: 35 mm), whereas carcinomas of the body/tail are usually somewhat larger {28802189;23472829}. Cancers < 20 mm are uncommon (10–15%) {28802189;23472829}. Carcinomas in the pancreatic head typically lead to stenosis and upstream dilatation of the common bile duct and/or the main pancreatic duct (double-duct sign on imaging), and this in turn causes fibrosclerotic atrophy of the upstream non-neoplastic pancreatic tissue (obstructive chronic pancreatitis). Moreover, most PDACs in the head infiltrate surrounding structures, including the ampulla of Vater, the duodenum, common bile duct, peripancreatic and retroperitoneal tissues, and the superior mesenteric vessels {28439733}. Carcinomas in the pancreatic body or tail typically do not involve the common bile duct, but may infiltrate the celiac artery, the stomach, left side of the colon, spleen, and left adrenal gland.

For accurate staging of resected PDACs, the use of protocols for the standardized dissection and sampling of tumour tissue is recommended {34889852} [[Burgart LJ, Chopp WV, Jain D. Protocol for the examination of specimens from patients with carcinoma of the pancreas. Version 4.2.0.0. June 2021. Bookref]]} [[Verbeke C, Brosens L, Campbell F, et al. Carcinoma of the exocrine pancreas histopathology reporting guide. 1st ed. Sydney (Australia): International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting; 2020. Bookref]]} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]].

Histopathology

Histopathological appearance

Most PDACs are moderately differentiated and form duct-like glandular structures, which haphazardly infiltrate the pancreatic parenchyma and elicit a prominent desmoplastic stromal response [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. The neoplastic glands can contain necrotic debris, and in some instances, the neoplastic cells incompletely line glandular lumina (incomplete lumina). Variation in the size and shape of the nuclei is common, and the finding of a nucleus four times bigger than another nucleus in the same gland supports the diagnosis of PDAC (the 4:1 rule) {7223929}. The stroma is hypovascular and composed of collagen fibres, which are often interspersed with fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, scattered lymphocytes, and macrophages. The neoplastic cells stain with Alcian blue, mucicarmine, and PAS.

The neoplastic cells may infiltrate non-neoplastic structures, such as nerves, vessels, and even pre-existing ducts, and into the peripancreatic adipose tissue {29684363;31700162}. Perineural invasion, observed in > 70% of surgically resected PDACs, is a highly tumour-specific finding and occurs within and can extend beyond the pancreas {1314766;17895841;37291275}. Although non-neoplastic glandular inclusions in nerves can happen, particularly in cases of longstanding chronic pancreatitis, this finding is exceedingly rare. Lymphatic invasion is common, and venous invasion is observed in two thirds of surgically resected PDACs {22082604;29302820;32948429;29684363;31700162}. When PDAC invades veins, it often replaces the vascular endothelium and relines the inner wall of the vessels, a feature that can mimic PanIN {22082604}. The reduced blood flow caused by venous invasion may contribute to the development of the desmoplastic stroma {33253282}. A gland next to a muscular vessel is also a histological clue to the diagnosis of PDAC {15105649}. PDAC may also invade back into non-neoplastic ducts, a process called cancerization of the ducts (COD) {26520652;30212393;40143803}. Histologically, COD can mimic high-grade pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HG-PanIN), and differentiating between them can be challenging (see section Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia). COD is suspected when there is immediately adjacent invasive carcinoma, which is histologically similar to the duct lesion. COD will frequently show an abrupt transition between the marked dysplasia of the neoplastic cells and the complete absence of dysplasia in the normal duct epithelium, although circumferential duct involvement can be seen. Suspected COD was identified on H&E sections in 89% of the cases in one study {30212393}. The neoplastic cells in COD are often better differentiated than the neoplastic cells in the stroma [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. In addition to being diagnostically useful features, perineural invasion, venous invasion, and COD are all pathways by which the invasive carcinoma can reach far beyond the main neoplastic mass {2819857}. The infiltrating neoplastic glands may also be intimately associated with non-neoplastic islets or single islet cells {33440856}. A gland in an islet, however, is not specific for cancer, and only in exceptional cases do the neuroendocrine cells constitute a truly neoplastic component of the ductal carcinoma. Most PDACs obstruct the larger pancreatic ducts producing marked upstream duct dilatation and fibrotic atrophy of the upstream parenchyma, often with intense clustering (aggregation) of the remaining non-neoplastic islets. Some neoplastic glands may lie individually within fatty tissue (naked glands in fat) or invade the wall and mucosa of the duodenum and the ampulla of Vater.

Morphological patterns of ductal adenocarcinoma

Histological subtypes with distinct prognostic or molecular features are discussed in detail below, under the subheading Histological subtypes. There are also histological patterns of conventional PDACs that are not considered distinct subtypes because they do not alter prognosis and do not appear to have distinct molecular or clinical features.

Perhaps the most important histological pattern of PDAC that one should be aware of is the large duct pattern, which is characterized by neoplastic glands measuring > 0.5 mm and sometimes having a deceptively bland-looking morphology {15920540;28145465;22056954;35667635;22367300}. This pattern can mimic the branching of an intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), until one recognizes that the glands do not conform to the branching pattern of the normal duct system and instead are haphazardly arranged, and that the neoplastic cells are dysplastic.

PDAC with a foamy gland pattern is also important to recognize as this pattern can be a clue to the diagnosis {10757396;37078931}. The neoplastic cells of foamy gland adenocarcinoma have a lacy-looking microvesicular cytoplasm, basally located hyperchromatic (and often raisinoid) nuclei reminiscent of gastric foveolar epithelium, and a brush border–like apical condensation zone at the luminal side of the neoplastic cells. These cells look deceptively bland, particularly on frozen sections or EUS-FNAB samples {10757396}. A clear cell subtype that resembles metastatic renal carcinoma has also been described.

A micropapillary pattern, originally described in the breast and later in the urothelium, is composed of small solid nests of cells suspended within stromal lacunae and can be seen in the pancreas as a focal finding {16007065}. This pattern is often associated with prominent intraepithelial neutrophilia {34055090}. Data on the survival of patients with predominantly micropapillary-pattern PDAC are still unclear {16007065;34055090}.

Immunohistochemistry

To date, there is no immunohistochemical marker that unequivocally distinguishes PDAC from non-neoplastic glands in the pancreas, nor is there a marker that distinguishes PDAC from extrapancreatic mucin-producing adenocarcinomas, notably bile duct and duodenal adenocarcinomas. CK7, CK8, CK18, and CK19 are consistently expressed in PDAC [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Other markers that are usually positive are CEA, CA19-9, CA125, B72.3, DUPAN-2, MUC1, and MUC5AC (but not MUC2) {25906442;30929285}. However, some of these markers also label the apical cell membranes of non-neoplastic duct cells, particularly in chronic pancreatitis. PDACs are usually negative for vimentin, neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin A), and acinar markers (trypsin, BCL10) {19066953} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Nuclear expression of SMAD4 (DPC4) and p16 (CDKN2A) is lost in 55% and 75% of the PDACs, respectively {23470568;23344532;10623651}. The expression of p53 is altered in 75–80% of the PDACs with either nuclear overexpression or completely absent expression (null pattern) {28145465;23470568} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. The loss of the SMAD4 (DPC4) protein is relatively specific to invasive cancer and can therefore serve as a diagnostic aid in the histopathological evaluation of pancreatic and extrapancreatic biopsies {10623651}. For example, if a patient with a PDAC is found to have an adenocarcinoma in the lung, concordance of SMAD4 status of the lung and pancreatic lesions can be used to distinguish between two primaries and a primary PDAC metastatic to the lung {17854080}.

Transcriptomic profiling has defined patterns of PDAC characterized by distinct gene signatures, therapeutic responsiveness, and natural history {28810144;26909576}. Specifically, PDACs can be divided into a basal-like subtype (also known as the squamous or quasimesenchymal subtype) with CK5 expression and loss of GATA6, and a classic subtype with intact GATA6 expression {31932696;32686876;32156747;36788090}. This distinction, however, has blurred lines, as individual PDACs can have both subtypes.

Grading

PDACs are graded as well, moderately, or poorly differentiated on the basis of the combined assessment of the degree of glandular differentiation, mucin production, mitotic activity, and nuclear features {10861575;15897739} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]] (#49536Table 11.02). In well-differentiated carcinomas, the neoplastic glands usually consist of cuboidal or columnar neoplastic cells with basally oriented, uniform round to oval nuclei and little pleomorphism. Although in isolation it may be difficult to recognize these glands as neoplastic, they are recognizable as such by their pattern of growth (perineural invasion, vascular invasion, gland adjacent to a muscular vessel, etc.).

In moderately differentiated adenocarcinomas, the intratumoural heterogeneity is more conspicuous, with abundant glands forming cribriform, papillary, micropapillary, and/or gyriform patterns {22367300;28145465}. Foci of smaller and more irregular glands and rare individual pleomorphic cells are found at the leading tumour borders. The neoplastic cells are columnar to cuboidal. The cytoplasm of the neoplastic cells varies from slightly basophilic to eosinophilic, but sometimes foamy or even clear. Occasionally, tumour cells have nuclear pleomorphism, with nuclei four times larger than other nuclei in the same gland {7223929}. The nucleoli are often distinct, and mitoses can be seen.

Poorly differentiated PDACs are heterogeneous, and they are composed of solid or cribriform sheets of neoplastic cells, and individual pleomorphic cells embedded in loosely arranged stroma {28145465}. Foci of necrosis and haemorrhage may occur. The pleomorphic neoplastic cells (occasionally with squamoid or spindle cell differentiation) are not polarized, produce little or no mucin, and have many atypical mitoses.

If there is intratumoural heterogeneity (i.e. variation in the degree of differentiation and mitotic activity), the higher grade is assigned {10861575;15897739}.

Differential diagnosis

PDAC must be distinguished from advanced chronic pancreatitis. The most relevant criteria for the differential diagnosis are listed in #43533Table 11.03. Distinction between PDAC and autoimmune pancreatitis is particularly challenging in EUS-FNAB because of similar inflammatory and fibrous backgrounds {32767550;34820715}. In contrast to PDAC, the nuclei of epithelial cells in chronic pancreatitis are small, round, and polarized, and lack anisonucleosis and hyperchromasia [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]]. Differential diagnosis of PDAC from other primary neoplasms of the pancreas is not usually difficult because of characteristic invasive growth of duct-forming structures in PDAC. However, immunolabelling can be helpful in difficult cases (#43534Table 11.04).

Histological subtypes
Adenosquamous carcinoma

The squamous component should account for ≥ 30% of the neoplasm in order for it to qualify as adenosquamous carcinoma. When this threshold is used, adenosquamous carcinomas account for only 1–4% of exocrine pancreatic malignancies {39425752;26380056}. Macroscopically, most adenosquamous carcinomas are infiltrative, yellowish-white to grey, firm masses {11353055}. Central necrosis and cystic degeneration are common. Some adenosquamous carcinomas are deceptively well demarcated (radiologically detected as ring-enhancing) {24854619}. Histologically, the adenocarcinoma component forms glandular structures, often with intracellular or luminal mucin. Squamous differentiation is characterized by solid clusters or sheets of polygonal cells with distinct cellular borders, prominent intercellular junctions, dense eosinophilic cytoplasm, and varying degrees of keratinization. When squamous differentiation is extensive, sampling often reveals glandular differentiation or histochemical evidence of at least focal mucin production. Pure squamous cell carcinoma of the pancreas is extremely rare, and a metastasis from another site (e.g. lung) should be excluded if a neoplasm only has squamous differentiation. As expected, the squamous component often expresses p63, p40, and high-molecular-weight cytokeratins {19270646;30428345}. Almost all cases harbour somatic KRAS mutations, and they also are highly enriched for SMAD4, CDKN2A, and TP53 mutations, 3p loss, and mutations in the RNA surveillance gene UPF1 {28722109;24859531;19270646;26380056}. Transcriptome-based studies confirm that adenosquamous carcinomas belong to the basal-like category of PDAC {26909576}. Compared with conventional PDAC, this subtype is usually larger at the time of diagnosis and is more likely to have spread to lymph nodes, to be poorly differentiated, and to arise in the pancreatic tail {33622577}. Adenosquamous carcinomas appear to have a worse prognosis than pure PDACs even in resected cases, with a median survival time of about 9 months {33622577;21082275;11353055;19801164;24518509;21816433;36850060;39425752}. The presence of any squamous component in the neoplasm appears to portend a poorer prognosis {19801164;39422835}.

Colloid carcinoma

Colloid carcinomas represent about 2–3% of all PDACs and are mucin-producing non-cystic invasive carcinomas in which ≥ 80% of the neoplastic epithelium is suspended in extracellular mucin pools {39425752}. Most arise in association with intestinal-type IPMNs {11145249;11756769;29500184;21422912}. Rarely, colloid carcinomas can arise in association with intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms {30986801;38950698}. Grossly, colloid carcinomas tend to be large, well-demarcated, and gelatinous. Histologically, the neoplastic epithelium within the pools of extracellular stromal mucin forms strips (either free-floating or attached to the surrounding fibrous stroma), or glands, or can be free-floating individual cells. Some floating cells may have a signet-ring morphology, but the presence of extracellular mucin pools with floating neoplastic cells supports the diagnosis of colloid carcinoma over poorly cohesive / signet-ring cell carcinoma. The neoplastic cells floating in pools of stromal mucin lie outside of the normal branching pattern of the duct system, and this pattern, together with other diagnostic features of invasion, such as perineural invasion, helps distinguish a colloid carcinoma from extruded stromal mucin {18824892;11145249;29307199;34518632}. The neoplastic cells of colloid carcinoma have intestinal differentiation; there is strong expression of CDX2 and MUC2, which are not substantially expressed in conventional PDAC {12379756}. Compared with conventional PDAC, this subtype of PDAC is enriched in defective mismatch repair / microsatellite instability (MSI) {29158190;32350089}. Furthermore, this subtype is also associated with deleterious germline variants in the ATM gene {31285527}. Colloid carcinomas have a substantially better prognosis than conventional PDACs {11145249;20142731;25840541;21508421;39425752;12717243}.

Hepatoid carcinoma

Hepatoid carcinoma of the pancreas is an extremely rare neoplasm defined as a carcinoma in which ≥ 50% of the neoplastic cells have histological and immunohistochemical evidence of hepatocellular differentiation {29961402;39425752}. These carcinomas are composed of large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. An associated component of PDAC, neuroendocrine or acinar cell carcinoma, may be present. Some immunohistochemical markers may be of help in supporting the diagnosis of hepatoid carcinoma, including AFP, CD10, and HepPar1, but they are not specific, and histology remains the primary diagnostic criterion {34562502}. Indeed, AFP can also be observed in pancreatoblastomas and acinar, neuroendocrine, and ductal neoplasms without hepatoid morphological features {10987254}, HepPar1 can be expressed by intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasm of the pancreas, and CD10 expression is also not entirely specific {10987254}. FISH for albumin and immunohistochemistry for arginase are more specific but less sensitive markers of hepatocellular differentiation {27425386}. A metastasis from an occult hepatocellular carcinoma to the pancreas must be excluded before establishing the diagnosis of a primary hepatoid carcinoma of the pancreas. Moreover, it is essential to highlight that other tumours arising in the pancreas can show hepatoid morphology, including well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) and solid pseudopapillary neoplasms; thus these differential diagnoses also must be ruled out {32488621;35359182}. Data on the prognosis for hepatoid carcinomas are still limited, but nodal and distant metastases appear associated with a worse prognosis {29961402;39425752}.

Medullary carcinoma

These are poorly differentiated carcinomas with limited gland formation, a pushing border, and syncytial growth, frequently with abundant tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes {10793075}. Medullary carcinoma is composed of sheets and nests of poorly differentiated epithelial cells that largely lack overt gland formation. The periphery is circumscribed, pushing rather than infiltrating the surrounding stroma. Intercellular borders are indistinct, producing a syncytial growth pattern, and tumour-associated inflammatory infiltrates can be seen, both within the stroma and admixed with the neoplastic cells (tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes) {9626054;10793075}. Medullary carcinomas are more common in the ampulla and duodenum and are exceedingly uncommon in the pancreas; therefore, before this diagnosis is rendered, origin in the adjacent GI tract should be excluded. Medullary carcinomas may arise sporadically or in the setting of Lynch syndrome {11306499;10793075;39235819} and are typically associated with defective mismatch repair / MSI {10938395;10793075;32350089}. Furthermore, medullary carcinomas often have wild type for KRAS, SMAD4, and TP53 genes {14507635;9626054;12615714;11248065;11306499;32350089;32933947}. Some poorly differentiated carcinomas with EBV infection of the neoplastic cells histologically resemble medullary carcinoma {10793075}. Despite poor differentiation, the prognosis for patients with medullary carcinomas may be somewhat better than for those with conventional PDACs {10793075;11306499;32350089;39235819;39425752}. Based on the presence of MSI, medullary carcinomas may be responsive to treatment with immunotherapy {26028255;32933947;39235819}.

Poorly cohesive / signet-ring cell carcinoma

This is a rare histological subtype of PDAC, composed of poorly cohesive / signet-ring cells resembling the corresponding tumour type more commonly encountered in the stomach {18223216;37355152}. By definition, ≥ 80% of the neoplasm needs to consist of individual, poorly cohesive cells, often with intracellular mucin vacuoles that peripherally displace the nuclei (signet-ring cell). The diagnostic threshold is clinically important, since tumours with ≥ 80% of poorly cohesive / signet-ring cells have a very poor prognosis {37355152;39425752}. A variable amount of extracellular mucin can be present, and the expression of E-cadherin may be lost at immunohistochemistry {18223216}. The molecular profile of this rare PDAC subtype is similar to that of conventional PDAC; of these, EGFR alterations, RET::CCDC6 fusions, and MSI have been recently reported as potential targets for precision therapy {37355152}. A carcinoma with this morphology in the pancreas should be presumed to be metastatic (from the stomach or breast in particular) before it is classified as a primary of the pancreas. Poorly cohesive / signet-ring cell carcinomas are highly aggressive neoplasms with a poor prognosis; the lungs are a common distant metastatic site {37355152;39425752}.

Undifferentiated carcinoma

Undifferentiated carcinoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm in which a substantial component (≥ 80%) of the neoplasm does not have a definitive direction of differentiation. Typically, these neoplasms have a more solid or diffuse sheet-like growth pattern without overt glandular differentiation {20542529;37548077}. Unlike PDACs, undifferentiated carcinomas are poorly cohesive and hypercellular, and they often have only scant stroma. Immunohistochemically, most of these carcinomas express vimentin and usually also keratin but not E-cadherin {9713360;11436166;18223216;32661742;37548077}. The prognosis is extremely poor, with an average survival time of just 5 months {9713360;11436166;39425752}. If the threshold for the diagnosis of undifferentiated carcinoma is not reached, the diagnosis should nonetheless clearly report the presence of such component. Three morphological patterns of undifferentiated carcinoma have been recognized: anaplastic, sarcomatoid, and carcinosarcoma.

Anaplastic undifferentiated carcinomas are composed of pleomorphic mononuclear cells admixed with bizarre-appearing giant cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm {20542529}. The cells are non-cohesive, and an inflammatory infiltrate that includes neutrophils may be prominent, with emperipolesis of neutrophils within the cytoplasm of the neoplastic cells. Keratin expression is typically present but can be very focal.

Sarcomatoid undifferentiated carcinomas are composed of neoplastic cells with spindle cell morphology, which may contain heterologous elements including bone and cartilage {37548077}. By definition, ≥ 80% of the neoplasm displays spindle cell features, with or without heterologous differentiation. These tumours have a molecular profile very similar to that of conventional PDAC, with some cases having a higher rate of KRAS amplifications and less frequent SMAD4 alterations {35850360;38723231}.

A rare but important subgroup of this entity is the undifferentiated carcinoma with rhabdoid cells {25031015;25103069}. These tumours are characterized by diffuse sheets of neoplastic cells with paranuclear eosinophilic inclusions (rhabdoid cells resemble rhabdomyoblasts but lack true muscle differentiation), often with some degree of non-cohesiveness and a myxoid matrix. They can have pleomorphic giant cells, spindle cell areas, and tubular components. Immunohistochemically, loss of nuclear expression of SMARCB1, SMARCA2, or SMARCA4, which are core subunits of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex, are found in the majority of these tumours {25103069;39094734}. Tumours with SMARCB1 deletions appear to be frequently KRAS-wildtype {25103069;39094734}.

Carcinosarcomas have a biphasic pattern, containing both sarcomatoid elements (with or without heterologous elements) and components with obvious epithelial morphology, which can be conventional PDAC. Each component should arbitrarily constitute ≥ 30% of the neoplasm to qualify as carcinosarcoma, and each component is immunophenotypically similar to its pure counterpart. The prognosis for pancreatic carcinosarcoma is very poor, and the number of metastatic lymph nodes appears to impact survival indices {37524640}.

Undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells

This distinctive neoplasm occurs mostly in the pancreas but rarely also in the bile ducts and other organs. It contains three cell types: distinctive but non-neoplastic osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells, a non-neoplastic mononuclear histiocytic component, and markedly atypical neoplastic mononuclear cells {28371566;28722124;9777987}. The osteoclast-like multinucleated cells express histiocytic markers and lack epithelial differentiation, and they are often found in areas adjacent to haemorrhage or necrosis {27508975;28371566;28722124;30031096}. The mononuclear histiocytic component may be inconspicuous by routine histology, but the abundance of these cells is easily demonstrated by immunohistochemical labelling for histiocytic markers {11070115;30031096}. The neoplastic cells vary from spindle-shaped to epithelioid and are often very large and pleomorphic. The neoplastic cells can express keratin and E-cadherin, but the expression of these proteins is often very focal, and may be lost in some cases {9529019;32661742}. These tumours have a mutation profile strikingly similar to that of conventional PDAC, with prevalent KRAS and TP53 mutations {11070115;28722124;9777987}. Along with the commonly associated presence of a conventional PDAC or glandular precursor component, the molecular profile provides clear evidence of the relationship of undifferentiated carcinomas with osteoclast-like giant cells to PDAC {11070115;9713360;9529019;9777987;28722124}. The clinical behaviour of this tumour type in cases with an associated glandular component appears to be very similar to that of conventional PDAC, whereas pure forms without glandular components have a slightly better prognosis, sometimes with very long survival {28722124;27508975;39425752}. Expression of PDL1 in tumour cells seems to be another prognostic moderator, being associated with poorer prognosis {30031096;32424767}.

Cytology

The cytological diagnosis of adenocarcinoma is most often obtained by FNA (typically EUS-guided FNA) or ultrathin needles (fine CNB), the latter of which has improved diagnostic yield and accuracy {28988195;35124072;29401169}. Brushing cytology is inferior to EUS-guided FNA and FNAB for the diagnosis of malignancy {24559784}. Ancillary tests performed on bile duct brushing specimens, such as FISH {26327129} and next-generation sequencing, may add value to cytology for the detection of malignancy but are rarely performed in routine practice {26596524;30971436}.

PDACs are distinguished from other solid neoplasms of the pancreas on the basis of both smear pattern and cytomorphology. The smear pattern of adenocarcinomas is one of scattered cellular glandular clusters admixed with single cells, in contrast to the diffuse, uniform polygonal cell smear pattern produced by the monotonous tumour cells of parenchyma-rich, stroma-poor neoplasms such as acinar cell carcinomas and well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Moderately poorly differentiated PDACs are recognized by overt features of malignancy. Nuclei are enlarged and hyperchromatic and have irregular nuclear membranes; the cytoplasm ranges from scant and non-mucinous to abundant and mucinous [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]] [[Pitman MB, Layfield LJ. The Papanicolaou Society of Cytopathology system for reporting pancreaticobiliary cytology: definitions, criteria and explanatory notes. Cham (Switzerland): Springer International Publishing; 2015. Bookref]]. Subtypes such as adenosquamous carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, and undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells can also be recognized by unique cytological features that recapitulate the histology [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Well-differentiated adenocarcinomas are more challenging because the aspirated ductal cells can be difficult to distinguish from atypical but reactive glandular cells in pancreatitis, as well as from gastrointestinal contamination from the EUS-guided biopsy procedure {17427224;16224213;19249774}. Cytological criteria for well-differentiated adenocarcinoma include irregular spacing of cells in a cohesive group, anisonucleosis of 4:1 in a single group, parachromatin clearing, irregular nuclear membranes, and finely vacuolated lacy-looking cytoplasm [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Morphological features supportive of a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma in EUS-FNAB include atypical epithelium wrapping around the edges of desmoplastic stroma, structurally atypical epithelium including cribriform pattern, long strips of epithelium with significant nuclear pleomorphism, enlarged nuclei with loss of polarity, prominent nucleoli, and irregular nuclear membrane {37078931}. Cells within tissue fragments are often easier to evaluate than are detached cells.

Staging

PDAC is pathologically staged according to the ninth edition (2025) of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. A slightly modified classification is proposed by the Japan Pancreas Society {39074998}.

Neoadjuvant chemoradiation or chemotherapy is increasingly used as a treatment option. In these cases, the pathological assessment of response to neoadjuvant therapy is estimated using a tumour response scoring system {29334562;30946090}. Several tumour response scoring systems have been reported, but there is currently no universally accepted system [[Burgart LJ, Chopp WV, Jain D. Protocol for the examination of specimens from patients with carcinoma of the pancreas. Version 4.2.0.0. June 2021. Bookref]] {22028089}.

Prognosis and prediction

PDAC is fatal in almost all cases. The mean survival time of untreated patients is 3–5 months. Although surgical resection is the only curative treatment for PDAC, only 10–20% of patients have surgically resectable carcinomas at the time of diagnosis, and the mean survival time after surgical resection is 10–20 months {31841792;39798179}. The 5-year survival rate for all patients is 13% {38230766}, which is a modest improvement from that over a decade ago {39043316}. Carcinomas of the body or tail of the pancreas tend to present at a more advanced stage than those of the head {16546491}. Patients from rural areas, with a lower socioeconomic status, and of Black race are less likely to undergo resection and have higher mortality rates {37233399;31212101;33844130}.

Stage is the most important prognosticator. T stage is primarily driven by tumour size, as survival time is longer in patients with small carcinomas than it is in patients with large carcinomas {18640931;21116727}. T4 is defined by extension beyond the pancreas to involve the coeliac axis or superior mesenteric artery {27163957;27322188}.

Lymph node metastases substantially worsen prognosis {29068800;38677773;38953216;38825047;39286904}. The lymph node ratio (the ratio of the number of nodes harbouring a metastasis to the total number of nodes examined) can also be used to predict survival after surgery {28338509;30061263;31082915}. At least 12 lymph nodes must be examined for proper staging {22421988;27644962}.

Distant metastases are significantly associated with poor prognosis {33009477;38710910}. Lung-only metastases are associated with better outcomes compared with liver-only or peritoneum-only metastases {26372811;28338509;37870253;38969544}.

Patients with no evidence of residual tumour after surgical resection (R0) have a more favourable prognosis than do patients with R1 disease {26200098;27918310;29068800;31454073;38303519;38607450}. Because involvement of the retroperitoneal resection margin, which includes the superior mesenteric artery margin or uncinate margin, is one of the factors determining survival, careful sampling and examination of this area in pancreatoduodenectomy specimens is recommended {24281214;18351300}. In addition to the direct cancer invasion at a margin, cancer within 1 mm of a margin also correlates with poor prognosis, and distance to the closest margin should be documented {26200098;29068800}.

Other prognostic features include tumour grade, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion {29068800;31082915;28628722;31454073;33086324}. Cytological or molecular detection of PDAC in peritoneal washing fluid is a poor prognostic indicator {27840175;39289461}. Other reported prognosticators include serum CA19-9 levels {22411488;23991810;31082915}, obesity {19289659;20665496;25266049}, race {23534717;25872128}, absence of adjuvant therapy {31082915;28338509;38825047}, SMAD4 gene status of the tumour cells {19584151;23360922;28145465}, and circulating tumour DNA levels {31142500;34876329}.

The majority of surgically resected PDACs recur within 2 years after surgery, most commonly locally in the bed of the pancreas and more distantly in the liver {19131205;31454073;37870253}. The peritoneal cavity and lymph nodes are also common sites of disease recurrence {19131205;28338509;31454073}.

Combination adjuvant chemotherapy with gemcitabine-based regimens, or FOLFIRINOX (a combination of folinic acid [calcium folinate], fluorouracil, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) significantly improve the overall survival of patients (~20% 5-year overall survival rate, vs 10% in patients with observation alone) {24104372;27265347;28129987;30575490}. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy given to patients with resectable, borderline resectable, or locally advanced PDACs prolong survival time, but further studies are necessary to establish standardized regimens {35084987;34334656;39329454}. As highlighted in #49539Table 11.05, some of the deleterious germline variants and somatic mutations that drive PDAC, while not diagnostically useful, are therapeutically targetable. Other markers that may help guide therapy include ERBB2 (HER2) and claudin-18 expression {19432892;32925194}.
