10.1.2.2 Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma

Definition

Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) is a primary malignant epithelial neoplasm arising in the extrahepatic bile ducts.

ICD-O coding

81600/3 Cholangiocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C15.0 & XH7M15 Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: carcinoma of the extrahepatic bile ducts.

Subtype(s)

Adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); mucinous carcinoma (84800/3); micropapillary carcinoma (82650/3); enteroblastic carcinoma (85762/3); hepatoid carcinoma (85760/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3)

Localization

The carcinomas arise in the extrahepatic bile ducts. Those arising in the left and right hepatic ducts and at the bifurcation are referred to as perihilar cholangiocarcinomas. Carcinomas arising in the common hepatic duct and distal common bile duct are referred to as distal cholangiocarcinomas.

Clinical features

Most patients present with signs and symptoms related to biliary obstruction, such as jaundice and pruritus. If cholangitis develops, symptoms may include chills and fever. Other common symptoms include abdominal pain, malaise, anorexia, and weight loss. In rare cases, the cancer may be detected incidentally through abnormal liver function tests or imaging performed for other reasons.

The most useful imaging techniques for assessing eCCA include multiphase CT or MRI with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography. These techniques help evaluate the primary tumour, including its relation to local vasculature, and assist in detecting metastasis {37433432;36372281;39232586;37770126}. eCCA is typically indicated by dilatation of the upstream bile ducts, although the tumour itself may be difficult to delineate, particularly if it is non–mass forming. 18F-FDG PET is valuable for detecting lymph node and distant metastases {32871859}.

EUS and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography are used to confirm the presence and localization of the tumour, as well as to obtain EUS-guided FNA or biopsy specimens from the primary tumour and regional lymph node metastasis. Additionally, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography–directed brushings may be performed. Diagnostic accuracy can be further enhanced by cholangioscopy with intraductal forceps biopsy {32887436}.

Epidemiology

The age-standardized incidence rate of eCCA varies worldwide, ranging from < 0.2 to > 2.24 cases per 100 000 person-years. The highest rates are observed in men in Asian countries, particularly in the Republic of Korea, Thailand, and Japan {32129902}. In some countries, the incidence has increased {32129902;35485392;35909086}, while in others it has either decreased or remained stable over the past few decades {24614916;30633038;34533900}. While in western Europe the incidence of eCCA has remained stable or even decreased, in the USA it has risen significantly over the past four decades {32129902;37797807}. Most patients with eCCA are in their seventh or eighth decade of life (median age: 72 years). Unlike in gallbladder carcinoma, there is a slight male predominance (M:F ratio: 1:0.67–0.92) {29632056;36581813;35485392;35909086}.

Etiology

Biliary conditions that lead to chronic inflammation, such as primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), bile duct cysts, Caroli disease, and cholelithiasis, are well-established risk factors for carcinoma of the eCCA {31536748}. In south-east Asia, endemic parasites (in particular Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis) are the primary etiological factors. Additional factors that confer lower but still significant risk include cirrhosis, inflammatory bowel disease, and HBV and HCV infections. Variant anatomy of the biliary system, in particular a low junction of the cystic duct and the hepatic duct, has been associated with distal bile duct cancer {32336556}. Recent analyses also suggest that type 2 diabetes mellitus, alcohol consumption, and smoking may be associated with an increased risk {29049401;30299315;31536748}. Occupational exposure to pesticides and organic solvents, particularly those used in the offset printing industry, has been identified as a plausible risk factor {27371662;35763622}. It is important to note that most patients with eCCA have no identifiable risk factors.

Pathogenesis

eCCA develops through a multistep carcinogenic process, either from precursor lesions or directly from the non-dysplastic epithelium. Four types of precursor lesions are recognized: biliary intraepithelial neoplasia (most common); intraductal papillary neoplasm; intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasm; and intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm (see respective chapters). During this carcinogenic process, some gene mutations can occur regardless of the type of precursor lesions (e.g. KRAS, TP53), while other molecular alterations are more specific to particular precursor lesions (e.g. GNAS, STK11, PRKACA, PRKACB) {36800543;31676785}.

Macroscopic appearance

eCCA typically forms a poorly defined tumour and is grossly classified into three types: sclerosing, nodular, and papillary. The sclerosing type is most common and is characterized by annular constrictive thickening of the bile ducts.

Histopathology

Cancer cells are arranged in well-formed glands, irregular fused tubules, solid nests, or single cells, set against the background of a partially inflamed, desmoplastic stroma. Poorly defined infiltrative growth, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural infiltration are common.

Histologically, most cases show pancreatobiliary differentiation, although intestinal differentiation may also develop, particularly in the lower part of the common bile duct or in cases with underlying biliary disease (e.g. PSC, choledochal cyst). Based on the degree of cytoarchitectural atypia, eCCA is classified as well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, and poorly differentiated, similar to ductal adenocarcinoma of the pancreas {34379823}.

The diagnosis of eCCA by intraductal forceps biopsy is often challenging because of small sample size, mild cellular atypia, and associated inflammation. A diagnostic clue is the presence of minute cell nests in the stroma, which can be highlighted by immunostaining for cytokeratins. Immunostaining for p53, S100, and SMAD4 may also assist in the diagnosis of cancer.

Histological subtypes

Histological subtypes of eCCA include adenosquamous, mucinous, micropapillary, enteroblastic, hepatoid, and undifferentiated carcinomas {25109922;38301963;23801851}. Pathological features of these subtypes correlate with those of large duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as described in the respective chapters. Of note, multiple histological subtypes can be observed within the same tumour.

Cytology

Features suggestive of malignancy, as opposed to reactive cholangiocytes, include pleomorphic nuclei, irregular nuclear membranes, coarse chromatin, 3D cell clusters, loss of polarity, and a necrotic background. Another helpful feature is the presence of two distinguishable populations of cells: one benign and the other clearly neoplastic.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although molecular tests are not essential for the diagnosis of eCCA, there is significant oncological interest in these tests. IDH1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions, known drug targets in small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, are typically absent in eCCA. ERBB2 (HER2) amplification is observed in 15–20% of eCCAs {38709907}. Mismatch repair–deficient / microsatellite-unstable–high neoplasms account for 2% of eCCAs {33998775}. Alterations in other drug target genes (e.g. BRAF, ROS1, MET, and RET) are rarely identified in eCCA.

FISH analysis for chromosomal polysomy or 9q loss can enhance the sensitivity for detecting malignancy in cytology specimen {26596524;22100297;39518600}. Sequencing of biopsy or cytology specimens can also be a powerful diagnostic tool {28970292}. Detection of mutations in late-phase cancer–related genes (e.g. TP53, SMAD4, CDKN2A) supports the diagnosis of carcinoma, while isolated mutations in early-phase genes (e.g. KRAS, GNAS) can be identified in precursor lesions or even in cases without dysplasia or malignancy, requiring careful clinicopathological correlation {32839152}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an invasive epithelial neoplasm with biliary differentiation arising within the extrahepatic biliary tract.

Staging

eCCA is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification, which applies to tumours of the perihilar bile duct or distal extrahepatic bile ducts, depending on the location of the tumour.

Prognosis and prediction

The stage of disease at presentation is the most critical prognostic factor {29999515;32169826}. While the median survival for all patients is 18.5 months, advanced-stage eCCA is associated with a median overall survival of 11.8 months {36588169;37040087}. Resectability is another crucial factor, with the 5-year overall survival rate ranging from 11% to 31% {27026485;33879742}. For unresectable cases, this survival rate drops to nearly 0% {23059502;34314644;35787702}. Surgery remains the only curative option for non-metastatic eCCA {36588169;37433432}, although the addition of chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy improves survival compared with surgery alone.

Key microscopic factors associated with poor prognosis include lymph node involvement, poor differentiation, vascular invasion, perineural infiltration, pancreatic and duodenal invasion, and positive surgical margins {27469329;39390920}. Additionally, tumour size, age, pathology grade, and T stage are independent risk factors for metastasis {33879742}.

The presence of risk factors such as higher T stage, lymphovascular invasion, and perineural invasion also increases the likelihood of local and distant recurrence following resection {38177560}.
