9.1.2.2 Hepatoblastoma

Definition

Hepatoblastoma (HBL) is a primary malignant tumour that recapitulates the developmental stages of the liver and consists of either epithelial or epithelial and mesenchymal components.

ICD-O coding

89700/3 Hepatoblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2C12.01 Hepatoblastoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Pure fetal hepatoblastoma with low mitotic activity (well-differentiated fetal hepatoblastoma) (89704/3); fetal hepatoblastoma, mitotically active (crowded fetal hepatoblastoma) (89705/3); pleomorphic hepatoblastoma, poorly differentiated (89706/3); embryonal hepatoblastoma (89707/3); small cell undifferentiated hepatoblastoma, SMARCB1 (INI1) retained (positive) (89708/3); cholangioblastic hepatoblastoma (8970A/3); epithelial mixed hepatoblastoma (8970B/3); mixed epithelial and mesenchymal hepatoblastoma without teratoid features (8970D/3); mixed epithelial and mesenchymal hepatoblastoma with teratoid features (8970E/3) (see #49782Box 9.02)

Localization

HBL usually arises as a solitary mass affecting the right lobe (55–60% of cases), the left lobe (15–20%), or both lobes of the liver {6303939}. Multifocal lesions at presentation do occur. Metastases at diagnosis, usually to the lungs, are present in approximately 20% of cases {11042582;33849474}.

Clinical features

Most commonly, an abdominal mass is incidentally discovered by family members or the paediatrician; weight loss, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain may also be present. Liver enzyme levels are generally normal. Serum AFP is frequently markedly elevated (to thousands or even millions of nanograms per millilitre) and is only rarely below 100 ng/mL, without any clear association with specific histological subtypes {36672416}. Thrombocytosis with platelet counts > 450 000/μL is frequent.

HBLs usually appear as well-defined, lobulated, heterogeneous, mildly echogenic masses on ultrasound, and may show calcifications and areas of haemorrhage. Imaging studies serve as the modality for pretreatment extent of disease (PRETEXT) staging of these tumours (see #49784Box 9.03) {32602829;29427028}. On CT, HBLs appear as well-defined hypodense or occasionally isodense masses with intratumoural cystic changes, haemorrhage, and necrotic foci after chemotherapy. On MRI, most tumours are heterogeneous, hypoenhancing, T2-hyperintense, and T1-hypointense lesions, and they are hypointense with gadolinium in the hepatobiliary phase {29427028}.

Epidemiology

HBL accounts for about 0.5–2% of all paediatric tumours, and its overall incidence is increasing worldwide {30984908}, with an increase from 1.89 cases per 1 million in 2000 to 2.16 cases per 1 million in 2015 in the USA alone. The increase is significant in boys, with an overall M:F ratio of 2:1. The incidence is highest in children aged 0–1 year (11.19 cases per 1 million), followed by those aged 2–4 years (5.66 cases per 1 million) and 5–18 years (0.18 cases per 1 million) {31651371}. The majority (80–90%) of HBLs occur between 6 months and 5 years of age (median age: 18 months). They may be congenital. HBLs can also occur in older children, and (rarely) in adults {22692949}.

Etiology

Several congenital abnormalities and constitutional genetic syndromes have been associated with HBL {32469081} (see #49783Box 9.04). An increased incidence, warranting surveillance, is seen in patients with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, and other syndromes {30270492;22692949;16126064}. However, the majority of HBLs are sporadic. An association with prematurity and very low birth weight (< 1500 g) is well documented {16510543;18392049;22692949}. Other associations include maternal smoking, infertility treatment, and younger maternal age {16510543;22692949}.

Pathogenesis

HBLs are thought to arise from progenitor cells (hepatoblasts) with the capacity to differentiate into various cell types, giving rise to the histological spectrum of this tumour. In general, HBLs are tumours that have relatively stable genomes with a very low rate of somatic mutations {29875405}. The WNT/β-catenin pathway appears to be central to HBL development, with as many as 90% of cases harbouring CTNNB1 mutations, resulting in upregulation of downstream WNT targets {27938502;17006932}. Other pathways reported to be involved in HBL pathogenesis and potentially implicated in its clinical behaviour include the NOTCH, sonic hedgehog, PI3K/AKT, EGFR (HER1), and Hippo/YAP1 pathways, among others {17006932;19200579;25135868;27910913;27775819;24837480}. NFE2L2 mutations have been documented in HBL (2–5%), as well as mutations in the TERT promoter, both associated with a poor prognosis {27775819}. Molecular characterization of tumours in the provisional category of hepatocellular neoplasms (HCNs) NOS demonstrated greater genomic instability than HBL but lower than hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), as well as recurrent mutations (most commonly of the TERT promoter), copy-number abnormalities, and alterations in cancer pathways (e.g. PI3K/AKT, mTOR) that are commonly seen in HCC, indicating the value of molecular testing for the accurate diagnosis of these tumours {35577029}.

Gene expression and profiling studies demonstrated HBL molecular subtypes of potential clinical significance {19061838;27775819}. More recently, epigenomic and multiomic profiling studies documented genome-wide dysregulation of RNA editing in HBL and identified additional epigenomic risk-prognostic clusters, differentially methylated regions, and regulatory genes of potential prognostic and therapeutic relevance {32240714;34538872;33893148;38330765}.

Macroscopic appearance

HBLs are usually single, well-circumscribed, lobulated, tan masses arising in a normal liver. They may be grossly heterogeneous and are often surrounded by a pseudocapsule. After therapy, the tumours usually show cystic degeneration, with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis, as well as a more accentuated pseudocapsule. Large-vessel invasion can be seen. Areas of calcification or osteoid formation may be present {39836187}.

Histopathology

HBLs are classified according to the constituent components present (see #49783Box 9.04) as either epithelial HBL or mixed epithelial–mesenchymal.

Well-differentiated fetal HBL (pure fetal with low mitotic activity) is characterized by uniform polygonal cells with a low N:C ratio and few mitoses {21768450;6303939}, arranged in trabeculae that are 2 or 3 cells thick. The cytoplasm is variably clear to eosinophilic and finely granular, and frequently a light-and-dark cell pattern is seen. Necrosis or haemorrhage is not present. This diagnosis can only be made on a primary tumour resection when the entire lesion can be evaluated, and not on a biopsy or after therapy. This HBL subtype is associated with favourable prognosis, and these are generally low-stage tumours {30975630}.

Crowded fetal HBL (fetal HBL with mitoses) is characterized by fetal areas showing polygonal cells with dense eosinophilic to amphophilic cytoplasm and round, centrally placed nuclei with inconspicuous to prominent nucleoli; there is no pleomorphism, and the cells are arranged in trabeculae. These areas frequently merge imperceptibly into embryonal areas. Extramedullary haematopoiesis is frequently present.

Embryonal HBL is the most common epithelial pattern and is characterized by smaller cells with a high N:C ratio, arranged in sheets or nests, that may merge with fetal cells. The nuclei are angulated. Nucleoli and mitoses are frequent. The tumour cells are frequently arranged in rosettes and tubular forms. Extramedullary haematopoiesis may be seen.

The macrotrabecular pattern of HBL is characterized by trabeculae > 5 cells thick, mimicking the pattern seen in HCC. These cells can have either a fetal or an embryonal phenotype. The macrotrabecular pattern of HBL should not be mistaken for HCC, especially on a biopsy {24008558}.

The pleomorphic epithelial (poorly differentiated) pattern is characterized by fetal or embryonal cells showing nuclear pleomorphism, including giant cells or frank anaplasia with large prominent nucleoli. These areas are noted occasionally in posttherapy resections and have been reported to be associated with chemoresistance and poor outcome, but they can also be seen in biopsies.

Both the macrotrabecular and pleomorphic patterns have been reported in the HCN-NOS category tumours and maybe associated with chemoresistance and unfavourable prognosis {35577029}.

The small cell undifferentiated (SCUD) pattern, also designated as blastemal in some instances, is believed to represent primitive, undifferentiated cell patterns. The category of pure SCUD HBL is no longer accepted, and tumours showing loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) nuclear staining should be classified as malignant rhabdoid tumours. It is more common for the SCUD pattern to present as scattered small nests within other areas of the tumour. Cells have scant cytoplasm and stippled chromatin with variable mitoses and are arranged in different patterns, sometimes forming sheets and nests of small round to ovoid cells {34874751}. Contrary to earlier reports, recent studies have confirmed that the presence of a small-cell component retaining SMARCB1 (INI1) expression is not associated with an adverse prognosis, as previously reported {34874751}.

Cholangioblastic HBL is characterized by bile duct–like structures at the periphery or intermixed with other epithelial areas. In contrast to reactive biliary proliferation seen after therapy, tumour cholangioblastic areas are positive for nuclear β-catenin. A squamous component with keratin production may also be seen occasionally {12228905}.

Mixed HBL is characterized by epithelial elements associated with stromal derivatives, including osteoid and other mesenchymal elements (e.g. spindle cells [blastema], cartilage, rhabdomyoblastic and adipose tissue). Some of the mesenchymal elements are detected on imaging, so the presence of calcifications is suggestive of mixed HBL at diagnosis {24008558}. Posttherapy specimens may show cells differentiating into osteoid, which is still considered neoplastic. The presence of a mixture of heterologous components – including endoderm, neural elements (e.g. glial tissue), and neuroectodermal derivatives (e.g. neuromelanin or primitive neuroepithelial rosettes) – warrants the diagnosis of a teratoid HBL. Rare examples of neuroendocrine differentiation and yolk sac–like glands in teratoid areas, all demonstrating nuclear expression of β-catenin, have been documented in teratoid HBLs.

Posttherapy resections may show areas of differentiation and chemotherapy effects including necrosis, haemorrhage, peliosis-like spaces, and extensive bone formation. Large areas of fibrous reaction, histiocytic infiltrate, and haemosiderin pigment may be seen. It is important not to mistake haemosiderin-laden cells for melanin pigment.

Immunohistochemistry to demonstrate β-catenin nuclear expression in the different components is important diagnostically, usually demonstrating strong positivity in the embryonal, crowded fetal and mesenchymal components, but sometimes weak or only focal positivity in fetal areas {20118773}.

Additional immunohistochemical stains may be helpful to identify different components, as shown in #53685Table 9.10, but are not diagnostically useful. Arginase and HepPar1 are generally positive but do not help distinguish from non-neoplastic liver. Most HBLs are positive for glypican-3 GPC3, generally staining epithelial components, but this marker may also be positive in HCCs, macroregenerative nodules, and infant livers {18536657}. The primitive marker SALL4, highlights the embryonal component. Ductular and cholangioblastic components are generally positive for CK19 and CK7.

There is a subset of challenging-to-classify paediatric hepatocellular tumours, showing histological features of both hepatoblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, currently labelled as HCN-NOS in international paediatric clinical trials, for investigative purposes. Several recent studies have demonstrated that these tumours are biologically likely to represent progressed hepatoblastomas distinct from HCCs, requiring further investigation.

Differential diagnosis

For the differential diagnosis of HBL, see #53686Table 9.11.

Well-differentiated HCC is differentiated from fetal hepatoblastoma by the presence of thickened trabeculae, higher N:C ratios, and the absence of light and dark areas.

Rhabdoid tumour may resemble SCUD HBL. Rhabdoid tumour consists of sheets of epithelioid cells with an eccentric nucleus and prominent PAS-positive intracytoplasmic inclusions. These tumours show loss of SMARCB1 (INI1) expression, corresponding to deletion or inactivation of SMARCB1 on chromosome 22 {19200579;34874751}.

Cytology

Cytology is not suitable for the diagnosis of hepatoblastoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

About 90% of HBLs carry CTNNB1 mutations, and the remainder may be associated with germline APC mutations or with mutations in other WNT pathway genes. Mutation testing of the TERT promoter and of NFE2L2 and other prognostically relevant genes may be useful in aggressive tumours {33125945;35577029}, particularly for the diagnosis of HCN-NOS and tumours demonstrating chemoresistance.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • For epithelial HBL: combinations of neoplastic epithelial cells resembling fetal/embryonal liver cells, small undifferentiated cells, duct-like structures arranged variably as sheets, nests, rosettes, glands, or trabecula
  • For mixed epithelial–mesenchymal HBL, non-teratoid: epithelial and stromal components (including spindle cells, osteoid, or other components)
  • For mixed epithelial–mesenchymal HBL with teratoid features: epithelial and stromal components with heterologous elements including endoderm, neuroectodermal derivates, melanin-containing cells, and/or other elements

Desirable:

  • CTNNB1 mutation or nuclear β-catenin expression by immunohistochemistry
  • for HCN-NOS: TERT promoter mutation and/or other HCC-associated mutations

Staging

The pretreatment extent of disease (PRETEXT) system is used for the staging of HBL and has been adopted for risk stratification in the most recent PHITT trial [[ClinicalTrials.gov (Internet). Bethesda (MD): U.S. National Library of Medicine; 2020. Identifier NCT03017326, Paediatric Hepatic International Tumour Trial (PHITT); first posted 2017 Jan 11 updated 2020 Nov 6. Available from: https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03017326. Bookref]] {29427028;17186233}. It describes the extent of tumour involvement of surgical liver segments and is reflective of the extent of involvement of the liver. Annotation factors include venous and extrahepatic tumour, as well as metastasis.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year survival rate for the combined stages of HBL is 60–70%. The survival rate is > 80% for patients with surgically resectable tumours after chemotherapy, but it is worse for children with higher-stage, chemoresistant, or metastatic tumours. Pure well-differentiated fetal hepatoblastoma with low mitotic activity has a favourable prognosis, and children with these tumours do not require chemotherapy if the tumour is completely resectable {21768450}.

Univariate analyses have identified several prognostic factors that affect outcome in children with HBL {24322718}. Five backbone groups have been identified based on three traditional prognostic factors, namely, the PRETEXT group, the serum AFP level, and the presence of metastases at diagnosis. Further stratification was achieved by superimposing additional factors on these backbone groups, specifically, PRETEXT annotation factors, age at diagnosis (< 3 years, 3–7 years, and ≥ 8 years), and AFP levels (≤ 100 µg/L and 101–1000 µg/L). This has led to a refined system for risk stratification that also guides therapy {27884679}.
