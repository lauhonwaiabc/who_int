3.1.1.3 Gastric dysplasia

Definition

Gastric dysplasia is an unequivocal neoplastic lesion of the gastric epithelium without stromal invasion.

ICD-O coding

81480/0 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade

81480/2 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.1 & XH6AF9 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade, of stomach

2E60.2 & XH28N7 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade, of stomach

Related terminology

Acceptable: intraepithelial neoplasia.

Subtype(s)

Intestinal-type dysplasia/adenoma (low-grade, 81441/0; high-grade, 81441/2); foveolar-type (gastric-type) dysplasia/adenoma (low-grade, 82105/0; high-grade, 82105/2); serrated dysplasia/adenoma (82130/2); crypt dysplasia (low-grade, 81482/0; high-grade, 81482/2)

Localization

The anatomical distribution of gastric dysplasia varies based on the underlying mucosal characteristics. In patients with background mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, the lesions arise predominantly in the incisura and antrum, while only approximately 10% of such lesions are found in the body and fundus. This distribution pattern applies not only to intestinal-type dysplasia but also to a subset of foveolar-type dysplasia arising in Helicobacter pylori–associated gastritis {12920223;21107615;18300795;32533343}.

In contrast, foveolar-type dysplasia occurring in H. pylori–naïve patients develops almost exclusively in the oxyntic mucosa of the gastric body/fundus. The same anatomical predilection is observed in foveolar-type dysplasia arising from polyps in the context of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of the stomach (GAPPS) {35968980;12360042;37852192}.

Clinical features

Dysplasia is usually asymptomatic. However, some patients may experience symptoms related to the underlying disease. Bleeding, anaemia, or dyspepsia may result from large or ulcerated lesions.

Endoscopically, gastric dysplasia presents as a flat, depressed, or elevated (polypoid) lesion, along with erosion/ulceration and colour tone change. Polypoid and sometimes even flat dysplasias have also been referred to as adenomas (intestinal, foveolar, pyloric-gland, and oxyntic-gland types; see the respective sections), at variance with the concepts that most examples of gastric dysplasia are related to prior injury and repair and that some of them can form visible lesions {40112834}.

A diagnosis of dysplasia can be made on targeted biopsies from mucosal endoscopic abnormalities. Forceps biopsies frequently underestimate the neoplastic grade when compared with complete endoscopic resections, particularly in cases of depressed, erythematous, or large lesions (see the subsection on grading, in Histopathology, below).

Modern endoscopic technologies, including combined magnifying endoscopy and narrow-band imaging, have substantially improved endoscopy assessment, which has made it possible to detect dysplastic lesions more accurately, paying attention to the structure of surface epithelium and subepithelial microvasculature {26668793;32200589}.

Epidemiology

Most cases of intestinal-type dysplasia, like many gastric cancers (GCs) often arise from H. pylori–induced chronic atrophic gastritis. In fact, the prevalence of intestinal-type dysplasia follows the same geographical distribution as that of GC; namely, a high incidence in parts of Asia (China: 3–10%) and a much lower risk in North America (USA: < 1%) and parts of Europe {38438000;40018952}. Most patients (> 70%) are older than 60 years and male.

Recent Japanese studies have identified a higher prevalence of sporadic foveolar polypoid lesions (foveolar-type adenomas), particularly in H. pylori–naïve individuals {38837872;34043063;35968980}, described as the raspberry subtype, which exists alongside the previously established flat-subtype foveolar dysplasia. Foveolar-type adenomas seem uncommon in the USA, where a single population study showed they occurred at a rate less than one quarter of that of intestinal-type adenomas {12360042}. The median age of patients with foveolar-type adenomas tends to be lower than that of patients with intestinal-type dysplasia, and most patients are male, although the M:F ratio is lower {35968980}.

Similar lesions (foveolar-type adenomas) are also frequently observed in patients with FAP {24525509;12360042;37852192}.

Etiology

The established risk factors overlap with those of GC; H. pylori gastritis is the key environmental etiology. While autoimmune gastritis has traditionally been considered a host-mediated risk factor for dysplasia and cancer, recent data indicate that these cases result from concurrent or previously undetected H. pylori infection {35772926}.

The etiology of sporadic foveolar-type dysplasia is unclear. Some cases have been observed in patients previously infected with H. pylori with intestinal metaplasia {18300795;32533343}. However, the etiology of the subset developing in H. pylori–naïve gastric mucosa remains unknown.

Both intestinal and foveolar dysplastic lesions can arise from hyperplastic polyps. Intestinal and foveolar dysplasia can also develop in association with genetic syndromes, particularly FAP and GAPPS, but also in juvenile polyposis, Lynch syndrome, and McCune–Albright syndrome {25390638;24518125;28188442}. For more information, see the sections on associated genetic disorders (e.g. section Familial adenomatous polyposis and section GAPPS (APC promoter) and other fundic gland polyposes).

Pathogenesis

The molecular profile of gastric dysplastic lesions varies according to the phenotype. Molecular alterations seen in invasive GC are also detected in intestinal-type gastric dysplasia. These abnormalities include chromosomal instability, microsatellite instability, and CpG island methylation. The presence of EBV infection remains debated {27236438;28532700;34537878;35925389}.

TP53 variants and p53 overexpression are detected at high frequencies in high-grade dysplasia (HGD), whereas they are absent in low-grade dysplasia (LGD) {30474112;24272205}. Accordingly, HGD shows a higher frequency of somatic copy-number variations than LGD {39133395}. APC mutations exist at high frequencies in LGD and HGD (~70%) but are less frequently detected in intramucosal carcinoma (4–20%) {12163385;27514024;30474112}.

Different alterations have been observed in flat and raspberry-like patterns of sporadic foveolar-type dysplasia developing in H. pylori–naïve stomachs. The flat-type adenomas present with variants in APC and KRAS; mutations of CTNNB1 have not been reported {12920223;23208952;35968980}. Acquisition of GNAS mutations may be involved in the progression of flat-type dysplasia to pyloric gland adenoma. On the other hand, raspberry-like foveolar-type adenomas have been associated with a single-nucleotide variant in KLF4 (NM_004235.6:c.1322A>C). This gene is a transcription factor involved in various biological processes, such as maintenance of homeostasis and apoptosis. Gene variants induce gastric foveolar epithelial tumorigenesis and activate both cell proliferation and apoptosis {35750256;36729478}.

Macroscopic appearance

Endoscopically, gastric dysplastic/adenomatous lesions present as flat, depressed, eroded/ulcerated, or protruding/nodular/polypoid lesion with colour tone alterations. The lesions are usually solitary and < 20 mm in size; larger lesions accounted for 20% of the cases in one multicentre series {21107615;19421245}.

Foveolar dysplastic lesions developing in H. pylori–naïve stomachs present as solitary polyps (raspberry-like and flat types) predominantly arising in the oxyntic mucosa of the gastric body/fundus {35968980}. The flat type appears as a pale sessile lesion typically exceeding 10 mm in diameter. The raspberry-like types are smaller polyps characterized by a bright red, finely granular surface resembling a raspberry {35968980;38837872;34043063}. The latter can pose diagnostic challenges, particularly when differentiating them from hyperplastic polyps in patients without H. pylori infection {35968980;37543537}.

Histopathology

The two most common subtypes of dysplasia, which arise from the surface epithelium, are intestinal-type dysplasia, which develops from epithelium with intestinal metaplasia, and foveolar-type dysplasia, which predominantly emerges from native epithelium. These subtypes are distinguished by their unique cytological, architectural, clinical, epidemiological, and molecular characteristics.

Dysplastic lesions arising from the deep glandular (pyloric and oxyntic) compartments of the gastric mucosa are described in section Gastric pyloric gland adenoma and section Oxyntic gland adenoma. See #54428Table 3.02.

There is ongoing debate about the use of the term “adenoma”. Some argue that gastric adenomas occurring outside of a polyposis setting should be classified as polypoid dysplasia rather than as true sporadic adenoma. Others suggest applying the term “adenoma” to all protruding/nodular/polypoid dysplastic lesions, regardless of the status of the surrounding mucosa. For consistency and clarity, in current practice, the terminology “polypoid dysplasia/adenoma” (both intestinal- and foveolar-type) is appropriate for describing a circumscribed polypoid lesion detected during endoscopy, while “dysplasia” usually refers to endoscopically flat or poorly defined lesions.

Intestinal-type dysplasia manifests as a neoplastic proliferation of tubules, tubulovillous structures, or villous fronds lined by columnar cells showing intestinal differentiation with elongated overlapping and hyperchromatic nuclei. The cytoplasm is variably mucin-depleted and eosinophilic. Terminal differentiation towards absorptive-type cells, goblet cells, endocrine cells, or even Paneth cells can be detected.

Foveolar-type dysplasia shows elongated epithelial fronds, with papillary infoldings and/or serrated glandular structures lined by cuboidal to low columnar cells resembling gastric foveolar cells. The nuclei are round to oval and display variably prominent nucleoli. The cells contain pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and have a distinctive apical neutral mucin cap (PAS-positive, Alcian blue–negative) that differentiates foveolar-type dysplasia/adenomas from pyloric gland adenomas.

In H. pylori–naïve stomachs, the raspberry-like polyps display irregularly shaped tubulopapillary structures with a well-vascularized broad stroma and less-prominent underlying oxyntic tissue. The flat type is composed of long papillary projections with thin fibrovascular cores. In most cases, the preserved subjacent oxyntic mucosa exhibits glandular cystic dilatation {35968980;34043063;38837872}.

The subtyping of dysplastic lesions primarily relies on morphology alone, rather than on immunophenotype. However, given the heterogeneity of foveolar dysplastic lesions, the role and necessity of immunohistochemistry in their evaluation may require further assessment. Intestinal-type dysplasia is immunoreactive for intestinal markers, such as MUC2, CD10, and CDX2 {11668493;19820687}, while gastric-type dysplasia may show expression of foveolar (MUC5AC) and/or pyloric (MUC6) mucins {18300795;25146833}. Hybrid (or mixed) cases also occur, with both intestinal and gastric marker expression, as well as null cases, negative for all markers. While immunophenotypically pure foveolar lesions are detected in H. pylori–naïve stomachs, a high proportion of cases developing in patients previously infected with H. pylori and intestinal metaplasia express a hybrid immunophenotype {18830123;35968980;18300795;32533343}.

Some cases showing a prominent Christmas tree–like serrated configuration have been reported as serrated dysplasia/adenoma and are characterized by frequent association with adenocarcinoma {23404616;26468393}.

Crypt dysplasia shows dysplasia limited at the basal portion of gastric intestinalized “crypts”, showing abrupt transition from the adjacent pits/glands, but preserving surface maturation. It has been reported at the periphery of traditional neoplasia in 21–49% of cases, and may be an independent predictor of cancer progression {21677540;26336971;24529328}.

Grading

Regardless of the morphological type, dysplasia is graded as either LGD or HGD {10680883;10896917;23809241;28532700}.

In LGD, the glandular architecture is relatively well preserved, with minimal disarray. The neoplastic cells show mild to moderate cytological atypia. The nuclei are hyperchromatic; they are elongated (cigar-shaped) in intestinal-type dysplasia and round to oval in foveolar-type. In both types, the nuclei maintain polarity and are basally located. The mitotic activity is mild to moderate.

HGD displays cuboidal to columnar cells with prominent cytological atypia, enlarged nuclei with a high N:C ratio, and commonly prominent amphophilic nucleoli. Loss of nuclear polarity and extension of atypical nuclei to the luminal surface is common. Mitoses (including atypical forms) are frequently detected. The glandular architecture of HGD is usually more distorted with back-to-back glands. It is worth underscoring that most polypoid foveolar dysplastic lesions arising in H. pylori–naïve patients are low-grade {35968980}, in contrast to the lesions diagnosed in inflamed stomachs {18300795}.

Diagnostic criteria for gastric dysplasia and adenocarcinoma have historically differed between Japanese pathologists and those in the USA and Europe and the Republic of Korea {10328081;10457028;22076218;21677827}. Although Japanese pathologists diagnose adenocarcinoma based solely on cytoarchitectural atypia, international consensus now is that marked glandular crowding, irregular gland budding, and cribriforming warrant a diagnosis of intramucosal adenocarcinoma, even without a stromal reaction {16952035;25310837} [[Odze RD, Montgomery EA, Wang HH, et al. Tumors of the esophagus and stomach. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023. (AFIP atlas of tumor pathology, series 4; fascicle 28). Bookref]].

Biopsy-based dysplasia diagnoses are frequently upgraded on endoscopic resection. For example, in a study of 293 patients with LGD, 18.7% were upgraded to HGD or GC on endoscopic mucosal resection {20623445}. Another multicentre study found that 63% of LGD biopsies were later upgraded to HGD or cancer {29094455}. Even among HGD cases, endoscopic submucosal dissection revealed deeper invasion in some patients {25310837}, highlighting the limitations of biopsy-only diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis

The term “indefinite for dysplasia” indicates a provisional diagnostic category rather than a distinct biological condition. This classification is used when histological features are insufficient to reliably distinguish between dysplasia and reactive or regenerative changes {22523210}. Although regenerative atypia can mimic dysplasia due to its cellular abnormalities and occasional atypical mitotic figures {17557866}, non-dysplastic changes typically have distinguishing characteristics: they show limited cytoarchitectural alterations, primarily affect the surface epithelium and neck, and demonstrate gradual transition to normal mucosa {17557866}. Antral erosive reactive gastropathy resulting in severe reactive changes can be distinguished from dysplasia by noting maintained cell-to-cell polarity such that four layers can be detected (apical mucin cap, base of the cap, row of cytoplasm, and row of nuclei {32841414}). In rare circumstances, distinction between HGD and extremely well differentiated intramucosal adenocarcinoma showing preserved surface epithelium and limited architectural glandular distortion {39362133} (e.g. crawling-type adenocarcinoma) can be virtually impossible on scant superficial biopsies {23723017;22865191;38584451} (see section Gastric adenocarcinoma). The latter carcinomas show irregularly fused glands, pit and glandular anastomoses, distended glands, discohesive cells, abortive glands, and glandular outgrowth {23723017}. An “indefinite” designation is commonly applied for small or poorly oriented biopsy specimens complicated by significant inflammation {10680883;17557866}. Evaluating serial histological sections or obtaining additional tissue samples may be necessary to reach a definitive diagnosis.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

No markers have yet been validated for routine clinical use.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an unequivocal intraepithelial neoplastic lesion (non-polypoid or polypoid); no evidence of invasion; low-grade to high-grade cytological and architectural changes; gastric intestinalized glands (intestinal phenotype) or cells presenting with a distinctive apical neutral mucin cap (foveolar-cell phenotype).

Staging

HGD is staged as pTis.

Prognosis and prediction

Intestinal-type gastric dysplasia significantly increases the risk of developing GC {1842372;28532700}, and histological grade serves as an indicator for disease progression {12865267}.

A nationwide cohort study from the Netherlands showed an annual incidence of GC of 0.6% and 6% within 5 years of a diagnosis of LGD and HGD, respectively {18395075}. In a prospective centralized study involving only patients with endoscopic follow-up > 12 months, the overall progression rate of LGD was 15% (progression to cancer: 9%). HGD progressed to invasive GC in 69% of the cases. “Indefinite for dysplasia” lesions only progressed to LGD (in 11% of the cases) {12865267}. In a recent retrospective multicentre study, only 4.3% of LGD cases progressed to invasive carcinoma (median follow-up: 2.6 years). In 59% of patients with HGD, GC was diagnosed histologically within 12 months of the initial diagnosis, but the natural history of HGD remained undetermined. Of the 16 patients who did not develop adenocarcinoma in 1 year, 11 had been treated with endoscopic resection and 2 progressed to GC {27185078}.

It is important to note that these results did not take into account the subtyping of dysplasia. For example, they stand in contrast with the prognosis of sporadic foveolar-type dysplastic lesions arising in H. pylori–naïve stomachs, which purportedly have low malignant potential {35968980;38837872}.

However, foveolar-type dysplasia arising in H. pylori–positive stomachs and serrated dysplasia with a foveolar phenotype appear to have a higher risk of malignant progression {25146833;18300795;23678381;23404616}. Dysplastic lesions with a hybrid phenotype may carry a higher risk of progression {18300795;25146833;25886985}.

Finally, in the syndromic setting, progression of FAP-associated foveolar-type adenomas from low-grade to high-grade or to adenocarcinoma is low overall {24525509;24548295;35584332}, with an estimated rate of 4% in patients with FAP (over a mean follow-up period of 6 years) {24548295;18830123}. However, the risk increases with mucosal carpeting by multiple polyps and when the polyps are > 10 mm in size {35584332}.
