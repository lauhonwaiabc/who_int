6.1.2.2 Appendiceal adenocarcinoma

Definition

Appendiceal adenocarcinoma is a malignant neoplasm with glandular differentiation or mucin production characterized by infiltrative invasion, originating in the appendix.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B81.0 Adenocarcinomas of appendix

Related terminology

Not recommended: mucinous cystadenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma (84900/3); mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (82520/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3)

Localization

Appendiceal adenocarcinoma can occur anywhere along the appendix.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with appendicitis or abdominal pain, but they can also present with a palpable mass, obstruction, gastrointestinal bleeding, or symptoms attributable to metastases.

Epidemiology

A primary carcinoma of the appendix is rare. Reported incidence lies between 0.9 and 5.4 cases per 100 000 person-years {37956190;36029031}. Appendiceal adenocarcinomas typically affect patients in their fifth to seventh decades of life. In the USA, in both the SEER Program database and the National Cancer Database (NCDB), there is a slight female predominance (53–57%) for mucinous adenocarcinoma but a slight male predominance (55%) for non-mucinous adenocarcinoma. Signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma has a female predominance (62–64%), which was statistically significant in both studies {23001080;26506400}.

Etiology

For most patients, the etiology is unknown. However, a minority (11.5%) of patients have a genetic predisposition to develop appendiceal adenocarcinoma due to pathogenic germline (constitutional) variants in cancer-predisposing genes, which include MUTYH, DNA mismatch repair genes associated with Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2),APC, CHEK2, SMAD4, and TP53 {36368039}.

Pathogenesis

Mucinous adenocarcinomas exhibit KRAS mutations in 55–80% of cases {24633196;26475379;24821835;28263231} and GNAS mutations in approximately 30–70% of cases {28263231;26475379;24821835;24925222}. NRAS mutations can be identified but are less common (< 5%) {36493333;37509254}. TP53 mutations can be identified in all histological subtypes but are more common in non-mucinous adenocarcinoma (54%) {36493333}. RAS gene and GNAS co-mutation is frequent (65–85%) in mucinous adenocarcinomas {28263231;24925222;36493333;36723696}, while GNAS and TP53 mutations are nearly mutually exclusive {36493333}. Additional low-frequency mutations can be identified in SMAD4 (16–19%), APC (10%), ARID1A (8%), RNF43 (7%), BRAF (2–5%), PIK3CA (4%), ATM (4%), and PTEN (2%) {30692096;37314541}. Microsatellite instability is rarely identified in mucinous adenocarcinoma {24633196;26475379;24821835;25274248;30692096;37314541;39435876}. Non-mucinous adenocarcinoma can demonstrate high levels of microsatellite instability {23648460}. The pathogenesis of signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma is not known; they less frequently harbour KRAS and GNAS mutations {24633196}.

Macroscopic appearance

The appendix may be dilated and filled with mucin. The tumour may be polypoid, ulcerative, or infiltrative, and appendiceal perforation may be evident. In advanced disease, the appendix can be destroyed.

Histopathology

Mucinous adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype of appendiceal adenocarcinoma and is defined as having extracellular mucin composing > 50% of the tumour {26506400}. Histological patterns of infiltrative invasion in mucinous adenocarcinoma include infiltrative glands that may exhibit cribriform architecture, incomplete glands, or single infiltrative tumour cells with extracellular mucin, associated with a desmoplastic stromal response and a small cellular mucin pool pattern of invasion characterized by small dissecting pools of mucin containing floating nests, glands, or single neoplastic cells. Mucinous adenocarcinoma can arise from several precursor lesions within the appendix, including lesions resembling low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN), high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (HAMN), and sessile serrated lesion. Appendiceal mucinous neoplasms (LAMN and HAMN) can be distinguished from mucinous adenocarcinoma by the pattern of invasion and invade along a broad pushing front, with hyalinization and fibrosis of the underlying tissue. Examining the entire appendix is recommended when the differential diagnosis includes appendiceal mucinous neoplasm, to exclude infiltrative invasion that would warrant classification as adenocarcinoma.

Signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma of the appendix requires > 50% of the tumour consisting of signet-ring cells, which may be present in large numbers in mucin pools or may invade tissue. High-grade goblet cell adenocarcinomas can resemble signet-ring cell adenocarcinomas or other poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas. High-grade goblet cell adenocarcinomas show at least rudimentary clustering of tumour cells, and most have at least focal areas that resemble low-grade goblet cell adenocarcinoma.

Non-mucinous adenocarcinoma shows irregular or jagged glands infiltrating the wall of the appendix with a desmoplastic stromal response. Most cases have glands lined with columnar cells and some degree of extracellular mucin. The morphology may resemble colorectal adenocarcinoma, with glands lined with columnar cells containing hyperchromatic nuclei with intraluminal necrosis.

Undifferentiated carcinomas of the appendix are rare. Their histology is analogous to that of undifferentiated carcinomas of the colorectum.

In line with the grading of appendiceal mucinous tumours (see #49321Table 6.03), mucinous adenocarcinomas qualify as grade 2. Mucinous adenocarcinomas with signet-ring cells have a worse prognosis and are classified as grade 3 if ≥ 10% of the cells show signet-ring morphology {24165252;24633196;33272738}. Non-mucinous adenocarcinomas are graded using a two-tiered grading system, similar to that for colorectal adenocarcinoma (see section Colorectal carcinoma).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

KRAS, GNAS, and TP53 are frequently mutated in appendiceal adenocarcinoma and have shown prognostic significance in some studies {30458197;36493333;37314541;39435876}. Mismatch repair immunohistochemistry and/or microsatellite instability testing should be performed, although mismatch repair deficiency in appendiceal adenocarcinoma is rare {23648460;36493333}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Mucinous adenocarcinoma

Essential: a malignant glandular neoplasm with infiltrative invasion and extracellular mucin making up > 50% of the tumour.

Signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma

Essential: a malignant glandular neoplasm comprising > 50% signet-ring cells, which may either be present within extracellular mucin pools or invade tissue without associated mucin.

Non-mucinous adenocarcinoma

Essential: a malignant glandular neoplasm with infiltrative invasion that resembles colorectal adenocarcinoma, or other less common patterns.

 

Staging

Staging is performed according to version 9 of the TNM classification of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]]. Tumour deposits are only relevant for non-mucinous adenocarcinoma and categorize a tumour as N1c in the absence of nodal metastases. For mucinous adenocarcinoma, the distinction between tumour deposits and involvement of the pericolonic fat by pseudomyxoma peritonei is arbitrary. Given the propensity of appendiceal mucinous adenocarcinoma for peritoneal metastasis, involvement of the pericolonic fat most likely represents peritoneal metastasis with invasion into the underlying pericolonic fat. The presence of distant metastases categorizes a tumour as M1a (intraperitoneal acellular mucin only), M1b (intraperitoneal metastases only, including mucinous epithelium), or M1c (non-peritoneal metastases). Tumours with ovarian involvement, omental infiltration, and involvement of the surface of abdominal organs (e.g. liver, spleen, and intestines) are classified as M1b. Involvement of the serosa of abdominal viscera with invasion into the underlying tissue represents transperitoneal spread and also classifies a tumour as M1b.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year survival rate for patients with appendiceal adenocarcinoma ranges from 25% to 54% and is affected by stage, grade, and histological type {8297177;26506400}. Patients with mucinous adenocarcinomas have a better outcome than those with non-mucinous adenocarcinomas {7634979;8297177;26506400}. Histological grade has repeatedly been shown to be an independent prognostic factor in mucinous adenocarcinoma: grade 2 and grade 3 tumours have overall 5-year survival rates of 30–60% and 20–30%, respectively {24633196;24165252;26506400}.

One study {36493333} identified four prognostic molecular subtypes of appendiceal adenocarcinoma, with peritoneal metastasis with RAS-mutated/GNAS-wildtype/TP53-wildtype tumours having the most favourable outcomes and with TP53-mutated tumours having a worse prognosis {30458197;36493333;37314541}. Adenocarcinomas lacking RAS, GNAS, and TP53mutations (triple-negative) had a worse prognosis {36493333}.
