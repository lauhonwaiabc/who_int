4.1.1.1 Non-ampullary adenoma

Definition

Non-ampullary adenoma is a non-invasive gland-forming epithelial neoplasm of the duodenum arising outside the ampulla.

ICD-O coding

82101/0 Non-ampullary adenoma, low-grade

82101/2 Non-ampullary adenoma, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.2 & XH3DV3 Adenoma, NOS, of duodenum

Related terminology

Not recommended: Brunner gland adenoma.

Subtype(s)

Intestinal-type adenoma, low-grade (81441/0); intestinal-type adenoma, high-grade (81441/2); sessile serrated lesion with dysplasia (82130/2); traditional serrated adenoma, low-grade dysplasia (82132/0); traditional serrated adenoma, high-grade dysplasia (82132/2); pyloric gland adenoma, low-grade (81404/0); pyloric gland adenoma, high-grade (81404/2)

Localization

The duodenum is the most common localization of small intestinal adenomas {2167140}. Each histological subtype exhibits distinct distributions: intestinal-type adenomas develop anywhere in the small intestine, and pyloric gland adenomas (PGAs) are mostly located in the proximal duodenum {12715167;25092673;31529725}.

Clinical features

Duodenal adenomas are usually small and solitary; most are asymptomatic and endoscopically accessible. Large adenomas can cause obstructive symptoms {23809242}. Duodenal adenomas can be removed endoscopically and/or using surgical approaches {39545885}.

Epidemiology

In general, non-ampullary duodenal adenomas are rarer than their colorectal counterparts. Several series have reported duodenal adenomas to occur in as many as 4.6% of patients presenting for endoscopy, with the prevalence of sporadic non-ampullary duodenal adenomas being 0.3–0.5% {28842048;23809242}. The mean age of patients with duodenal adenomas is 62 years, and there is a slight male predominance {28467793}. The intestinal-type adenomas are nine times as common as PGAs {28376132}.

Etiology

The majority (75%) of the duodenal adenomas are sporadic cases, and one quarter of the cases are associated with syndromes {33814312}. Of the syndromic duodenal adenomas, the majority occur in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) {28467793;33814312}, predominantly intestinal-type adenomas with low-grade dysplasia {14960520;33814312}. Indeed, FAP often presents as duodenal polyposis, and the severity of polyposis has been linked to the risk of adenocarcinoma {2571019}. MUTYH-associated polyposis is also associated with an increased risk of duodenal adenomas and adenocarcinomas; increased adenoma size and villous architecture are associated with tumour progression {29702101;26905905}. PGAs are associated with ectopic gastric mucosa, gastric foveolar hyperplasia, or Brunner gland metaplasia {29998424}. Larger tumour size and tubulovillous architecture of PGAs are associated with high-grade dysplasia (HGD) and invasive adenocarcinoma {31529725}.

Pathogenesis

The model of the adenoma–carcinoma sequence is predicted for intestinal-type adenoma {15951555}. The intestinal-type adenomas show frequent APC mutation and WNT/β-catenin activation {28467793;15951555;34514550}. In contrast, GNAS and KRASmutations are more commonly observed in PGA {32770675}. There is a clear correlation of PGAs with gastric-type dysplasia, gastric foveolar metaplasia, and Brunner gland hyperplasia, which are associated with gastric-type adenocarcinomas {25310836}. In addition, a common presence of GNAS and KRAS mutations in gastric foveolar metaplasia, gastric heterotopia, and adenocarcinoma of the duodenum {25867268;23208952} has been established. Together, these facts strongly suggest that gastric foveolar metaplasia and gastric heterotopia may be the precursor lesions of PGAs and adenocarcinomas with gastric phenotype.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopy of non-ampullary duodenal adenomas is similar to that of the counterpart adenomas in the colon and rectum. They are smooth, sometimes tan, sessile or pedunculated growths in the mucosa (for further characterization, see section Conventional colorectal adenoma). The number and size of the duodenal adenomas may carry a certain prognostic value (see Prognosis and prediction, below). Duodenal adenomas in the setting of FAP are usually multiple, sessile, and predominantly located in the mucosal folds {14652558}.

Histopathology

Intestinal-type adenomas are the most common subtype in the duodenum, where most cases show a tubular or tubulovillous growth pattern and intestinal epithelial differentiation. Most intestinal-type adenomas have low-grade dysplasia and consist of tall columnar cells with elongated nuclei and inconspicuous nucleoli. Paneth, goblet, or endocrine cells may be prominent in low-grade lesions {8543313}. Adenomas with HGD are characterized by increased architectural complexity, nuclear stratification, and nuclear atypia. FAP-associated adenomas have the same histological features as sporadic lesions. Immunohistochemically, intestinal-type adenomas express markers of the intestinal epithelium, including CDX2 and MUC2 {28321513;33794536}. Coexpression of CK7 and CK20 is common {18670349}.

Serrated lesions and polyps, including sessile serrated lesions and traditional serrated adenomas, similar to their colonic counterparts (see section Colorectal sessile serrated lesions and section Colorectal traditional serrated adenoma), are identifiable in the duodenum, frequently associated with dysplasia or adenocarcinoma {24894811;26468393;32721915}.

PGAs consist of closely packed pyloric-type glands lined by cuboidal to low columnar cells. Foamy, ground-glass cytoplasm is characteristic of this type of adenoma. The tumour cells have round and basally located nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli. These lesions can be associated with gastric-type foveolar metaplasia and gastric heterotopia {31529725}. Immunohistochemically, PGAs show MUC6 expression (usually extensive) and a variable extent of MUC5AC expression {18830123}.

Cytology

The cytopathological diagnostic features of duodenal intestinal-type adenomas have been described {22943018}, showing small sheets and clusters of elongated columnar cells with granular chromatin and one or more nucleoli, and/or features of HGD with nuclear overlapping, loss of polarity, and coarse clumped chromatin. However, cytology cannot always differentiate between HGD and adenocarcinoma, providing no substantial improvement over biopsy diagnosis {9576571;11335971}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: sessile or pedunculated mucosal growth(s) in a non-ampullary duodenal location; a closely packed non-invasive tubular, glandular, or tubulopapillary growth pattern with epithelial dysplasia.

Desirable: intestinal epithelial differentiation, pyloric gland differentiation, or a serrated growth pattern (for subtyping).

Staging

Adenomatous polyps with HGD are regarded as pTis.

Prognosis and prediction

The risk of developing invasive disease is related to the size of the adenoma, the villous component, and the presence of HGD {7248908}. In sporadic cases, large size (> 20 mm) in combination with the presence of HGD at first biopsy is an indicator of malignant progression {21139577}. This natural history also dictates treatment: duodenal adenomas should be endoscopically removed if possible, and larger lesions should be considered for surgical removal. The Spigelman classification, which is based on the number, size, histological type, and degree of dysplasia of the adenomas (see #44925Table 4.01), grades the burden of duodenal adenomas and estimates the risk of developing duodenal adenocarcinoma in patients with FAP, but not in patients with MUTYH-associated polyposis {2571019;14752072;33130102}.
