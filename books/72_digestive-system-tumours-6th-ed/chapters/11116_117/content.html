11.1.1.6 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas

Definition

Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm (ITPN) of the pancreas is a non-invasive intraluminal epithelial neoplasm composed of back-to-back glands with homogeneous high-grade dysplasia and without overt production of mucin.

ICD-O coding

85037/2 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm

ICD-11 coding

2E61.Y & XA3QC5 & XH64S7 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Approximately half of all ITPNs occur in the head of the pancreas, and approximately one fifth involve the pancreas diffusely {27984235;35583805}.

Clinical features

Patients may present with nonspecific symptoms, with abdominal pain being the most common (in 40% of cases). Obstructive jaundice is uncommon. Approximately one third of ITPNs are detected incidentally {27984235;35583805}.

Epidemiology

ITPNs account for < 1% of all pancreatic exocrine neoplasms and 3% of intraductal neoplasms of the pancreas {19440145}. The exact prevalence is unclear. They are slightly more common in women {27984235;35583805}. Patient age ranges from 25 to 84 years (mean: 60 years) {27984235;35583805}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The genetic features of ITPNs differ from those of ductal adenocarcinomas and other intraductal neoplasms of the pancreas {28776573;35583805}. Most of the reported alterations associated with ductal adenocarcinoma and intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), including somatic KRAS mutations, are rare in ITPNs {19440145;21945955;23893889;36056133}. Instead, most ITPNs harbour fusion genes that may act as genetic drivers, including fusions involving the BRAF, FGFR2, MAML2, MAML3, NRG1, and RET genes {28776573;36056133;36801389;38378317}. Some of these gene fusions create actionable targets for therapy {38378317}. Chromatin-remodelling genes (ARID2, ASXL1, and PBRM1) may also be somatically mutated in a subset of cases {36056133}. Transcriptome analysis has suggested that most ITPN-associated carcinomas display some squamous features {36056133}.

Macroscopic appearance

ITPNs form solid, fleshy to rubbery, nodular masses within dilated pancreatic ducts, sometimes with polypoid appearance {8677955;19440145;27984235}. The intraductal growth may be difficult to recognize as the neoplasm can completely fill the involved ducts. Cyst formation is less evident than in IPMNs, and the vast majority of ITPNs are identified as solid or solid/cystic masses on imaging {35583805;36056133}. Mucinous secretions are not present. The average diameter of an ITPN is 40 mm (range: 5–150 mm) {35583805}.

Histopathology

Microscopically, ITPNs form nodules of back-to-back glands, resulting in large cribriform structures {27984235;19440145}. The intraductal location of at least some of the nodules is supported by continuity of the neoplastic epithelium with non-neoplastic ductal epithelium. However, most tumours obliterate the ductal lumen, appearing as sharply circumscribed nests of neoplastic cells surrounded by fibrotic stroma. Although most ITPNs are predominantly tubular (cribriform), papillae may also be observed {19440145;35583805}. ITPNs are architecturally complex and typically have high-grade dysplasia. The tumour cells are predominantly cuboidal, with modest amounts of eosinophilic to amphophilic and rarely clear cytoplasm {27984235}. In some cases, intraluminal secretions may be seen; however, intracellular mucin is typically not detectable or is at most minimal {27984235;19440145}. The nuclei are round to oval and atypical but uniform. Mitotic figures are often readily identifiable {19440145}. Most cases have focal necrosis, often with a comedo-like pattern.

ITPNs typically have a relatively homogeneous appearance; transitions to areas displaying less-marked cytoarchitectural atypia, IPMNs, and pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia are not seen {15257103;19440145}. An associated invasive carcinoma is found in almost 60% of cases, but the majority (75%) do not have associated nodal metastases {35583805}. Invasive carcinoma is represented by individual cells or small, angulated non-mucinous glands irregularly extending from the periphery of the tumour into surrounding desmoplastic stroma. It is often difficult to determine whether an invasive carcinoma is present or not, because many of the individual neoplastic nodules lack a peripheral rim of non-neoplastic ductal epithelium. Neoplastic cells outside the branching pattern of the normal duct system, in vessels, or in nerves also support the diagnosis of invasion. Extensive sampling is warranted as invasion may be focal and subtle.

The neoplastic cells consistently express pancytokeratins and commonly label for CK7, CK8/18, and CK19. Labelling for acinar markers and neuroendocrine markers is negative. MUC1 and MUC6 are commonly expressed (~90% and 70% of cases, respectively). MUC2 is consistently negative, and MUC5AC, a marker of all subtypes of IPMNs, is completely lacking or very rarely and focally expressed in ITPNs {26586167;27984235;35583805}.

Pancreatobiliary-type IPMNs can be difficult to distinguish from ITPNs. A mucinous nature in parallel with MUC5AC expression, a more pronounced papillary architecture, and the presence of a spectrum of dysplasia, all favour pancreatobiliary-type IPMN {27984235;35583805}.

ITPN is also difficult to distinguish from acinar cell carcinomas, particularly when the latter grows within the ducts of the pancreas {32111396;17325477}. Unlike ITPNs, acinar cell carcinomas often have apical acidophilic granules and occasionally have intraluminal crystals (enzymatic concretions). Immunohistochemical labelling for markers of pancreatic exocrine enzymes, such as BCL10, trypsin, and chymotrypsin, is essential in this differential diagnosis {23026929}.

Cytology

Although no systematic analyses have been conducted, case reports describe highly cellular, relatively cohesive clusters with cribriform and tubular patterns, along with tumour cells exhibiting homogeneous high-grade nuclear atypia and absence of intracytoplasmic mucin in cytological smears of ITPNs {24339429;22807417;27459312}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraluminal nodules composed of non-invasive back-to-back glands, resulting in large cribriform structures; absence of detectable intracytoplasmic mucin.

Desirable: absence of MUC5AC expression; lack of expression of markers for acinar cell and markers of neuroendocrine differentiation.

Staging

As all ITPNs have high-grade dysplasia, they are staged as pTis. Staging of an invasive carcinoma arising in association with an ITPN is based on the invasive component and follows the protocol for pancreatic carcinomas [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

Despite the challenges in determining the extent of invasive carcinoma in many cases, the overall outcome for patients with an ITPN appears to be significantly better than that for patients with ductal adenocarcinoma {27984235;8677955;19440145;35583805}. Even when considering only ITPNs with an associated invasive carcinoma, the 5-year survival rate is 70% {27984235}. ITPN with a “pure” branch duct involvement shows the lowest risk of recurrence {35583805}. TP53 mutation status has been reported as a marker of adverse prognosis {36056133}.
