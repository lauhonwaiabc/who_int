15.0.2.2.2 Peritoneal metastatic appendiceal mucinous neoplasms

Definition

Appendiceal mucinous neoplasms metastatic to the peritoneum may cause the distinctive clinical syndrome of pseudomyxoma peritonei, the histological counterpart of which is known as mucinous carcinoma peritonei (MCP).

ICD-O coding

84800/6 Mucinous carcinoma peritonei

ICD-11 coding

2D91 & XH83J5 Pseudomyxoma peritonei

Related terminology

Acceptable: mucinous carcinoma peritonei (preferred term); pseudomyxoma peritonei.

Not recommended: disseminated peritoneal adenomucinosis; peritoneal mucinous carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

All sites of the peritoneal cavity can be affected, including the omentum and ovary {21159590}. Tumour distribution is partly dependent on movement of the peritoneal fluid, which accumulates in dependent areas in the pelvic cavity due to gravity, or in sites at which ascitic fluid is resorbed, such as the greater and lesser omentum and the undersurface of the right hemidiaphragm, a pattern known as the redistribution phenomenon {21159590}. Mobile parts of the intestine such as the small bowel may be spared early in the disease process.

Clinical features

MCP is often characterized by voluminous mucinous ascites, a feature that can distinguish it from non-appendiceal gastrointestinal mucinous adenocarcinomas. MCP can present with symptoms of appendicitis, increasing abdominal girth, abdominal pain, or mucin within a hernia sac. In women, presentation may be with rapidly enlarging ovarian masses due to involvement of the ovaries. Lymphatic and/or hematogenous metastasis is very rare in low-grade MCP. Even in high-grade MCP, metastasis beyond the peritoneal cavity is unusual {28540829;21159590}.

Epidemiology

MCP is described to affect women somewhat more often than men in case reports, usually in the fourth to seventh decades of life {28746986}.

Etiology

The overwhelming majority of cases of MCP arise from peritoneal dissemination of an appendiceal mucinous neoplasm. However, mucinous tumours from other sites, such as the urachus and ovarian teratomas, may produce the same phenomenon {28449606;39308901}. Mucinous adenocarcinomas of other organs such as the pancreas and biliary tract may metastasize to the peritoneum, and (rarely) the clinical presentation is indistinguishable from mucinous carcinoma peritonei of appendiceal origin {30531244;22406366}.

Pathogenesis

Most studies have evaluated genomic alterations in MCP / pseudomyxoma peritonei of appendiceal origin {25274248;24633196;26475379;24821835;28263231;36493333;37314541;39435876}. The majority of cases of MCP exhibit KRAS mutations (42–84% of cases), and GNAS mutations are found in approximately 30–70% of cases {24633196;24821835;26475379;28263231;24925222;36493333;37314541;39435876;24944587;25274248}. NRAS mutations are less common (< 5%) {36493333;37509254}. MCP can harbour TP53 mutations (8–26%) and SMAD4 mutations (4–19%), and these mutations are more commonly identified in high-grade MCP (both grade 2 and grade 3) but are rare in low-grade disease {36493333;37314541;39435876}. RAS and GNAS co-mutation is frequent (65–85%) {24944587;28263231;24925222;36493333;36723696;25274248}, while GNAS and TP53 mutations are nearly mutually exclusive {36493333;39435876}. Approximately 10% of mucinous carcinoma peritonei cases will be wildtype for RAS, GNAS, and TP53 mutations {36493333}. Additional low-frequency mutations can be identified in PIK3CA (4%), ATM (4%), BRAF (2%), PTEN (2%), and CDKN2A (1%) {37314541;25274248}. Mismatch repair deficiency / microsatellite instability has not been identified in mucinous carcinoma peritonei {30692096;36493333;25274248}.

Macroscopic appearance

Mucinous globules of tumour usually range in consistency from watery mucus to more-solid mucinous masses, depending on the degree of fibrosis and tumour cellularity. As the mucinous tumour progresses, it tends to encase abdominal viscera and is often associated with large amounts of mucinous ascites. Extensive omental infiltration (omental cake) is common, and the ovaries can be transformed into mucinous masses.

Histopathology

To be considered MCP, tumours must consist of abundant mucin, with varying degrees of tumour cellularity. Mucin without visible neoplastic cells (i.e. acellular mucin) should not be classified as MCP, although a ruptured appendiceal mucinous neoplasm may be the source of acellular mucin in the peritoneal cavity. The diagnosis of MCP is most relevant when the primary tumour is an appendiceal mucinous tumour or, rarely, another tumour known to cause the syndrome of pseudomyxoma peritonei, such as a urachal mucinous neoplasm or ovarian teratoma. If a peritoneal mucinous tumour arises from another organ, such as colon or stomach, then nomenclature and grading appropriate for that organ should be utilized (e.g. metastatic mucinous adenocarcinoma of the stomach, moderately differentiated). If the tumour is of unknown primary (for example, if only a biopsy is received) or morphologically compatible with an appendiceal primary, histological grading of the tumour using the criteria for MCP is appropriate. Tumour characteristics and expression pattern of immunohistochemical markers (CK7, CK20, CDX2, PAX8, SATB2, uroplakin-2, and SMAD4) can be used in distinguishing the primary site of MCP, although overlap in expression patterns commonly occur (see #50097Table 15.01) {28711650;31239549;26551622;3083879;30281367;31771640;30362546;30398914;37134268;28746986;25827135;25025366;31054158;24618609;30205948;27785422;25572681}.The grading of MCP is based on tumour cellularity, cytological and architectural atypia, and the presence of signet-ring cells {24633196;26492181}. Most cases of MCP are grade 1, and to avoid overgrading, pathologists should exercise restraint when features of a higher-grade tumour are limited and focal. To be classified as grade 2 MCP, high-grade cytological and architectural features should be present in ≥ 10% of the neoplastic epithelium. A diagnosis of grade 3 relies on the presence of signet-ring cells; however, true signet-ring cells must be distinguished from pseudo–signet-ring cells, which are degenerated tumour cells floating in mucin (usually a focal finding). For this reason, to be classified as grade 3 MCP, signet-ring cells should make up ≥ 10% of the neoplastic epithelium {24814804}. A peritoneal regression grading score (PRGS) should be given when there has been previous therapy (#49522Table 15.02) {30911613}.

Cytology

Peritoneal material contains mucin with varying numbers of atypical cells, singly and in 3D clusters. There are limited data on grading of MCP on cytological specimens. For this reason, grading on cytological specimens is not recommended.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intra-abdominal mucinous deposits composed of > 50% extracellular mucin with presence of neoplastic glandular epithelial cells.

Desirable: identification of the primary site of origin – usually the vermiform appendix; very occasionally the urachus, an ovarian teratoma, or unknown.

Staging

Staging is according to the primary site of the neoplasm. For appendiceal primaries, the stage of MCP depends on the presence or absence of mucinous epithelium within the mucinous deposits. Acellular mucin is considered M1a. Involvement of the peritoneal surface of abdominal organs such as the liver, spleen, and intestines with mucinous tumour deposits containing mucinous epithelial cells is classified as M1b. Ovarian involvement, omental infiltration, and infiltration of liver beneath serosally based tumour deposits are also classified as M1b. Stage M1c is reserved for non-peritoneal metastases, such as parenchymal liver not arising from serosally based deposits, pulmonary metastases, or pleural metastases.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognostic factors include the presence of tumour cells in the peritoneal mucin, histological tumour grade, and whether complete surgical cytoreduction can be achieved. Patients with low-grade tumours and complete cytoreduction have a median survival time of 59–170 months, whereas patients with grade 2 and 3 tumours have median survival times of 45–59 months and 18.9–26 months, respectively {30017331;31090036;26597367;30523470}. Although the grade of the primary lesion and the grade of MCP are usually the same, occasionally the grades differ. In these circumstances, it is the grade of the MCP that is more closely associated with prognosis {34731401;39327362}.

One study {36493333} identified four prognostic molecular subtypes of appendiceal mucinous adenocarcinoma, with peritoneal metastasis with RAS-mutated / GNAS wildtype / TP53 wildtype tumours having the most favourable outcomes and with TP53-mutated tumours having a worse prognosis {30458197;36493333;37314541}. However, not all studies have demonstrated the association of KRAS mutation with improved prognosis in MCP {39435876}. Adenocarcinomas lacking RAS, GNAS, and TP53 mutations (triple-negative) had a worse prognosis {36493333}.
