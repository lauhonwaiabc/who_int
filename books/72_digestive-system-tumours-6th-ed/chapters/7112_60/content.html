7.1.1.2 Colorectal sessile serrated lesions

Definition

Sessile serrated lesion (SSL) is a benign epithelial neoplasm originating in the colorectum, characterized by a proliferation of serrated crypts with abnormal architecture, which may or may not present with cytological features of dysplasia.

Related terminology

Not recommended: sessile serrated polyp; sessile serrated adenoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

SSLs have a predilection for the proximal colon (70–80%), particularly SSLs with dysplasia (SSLDs) {25724036;25350766;24418851}.

Clinical features

Most SSLs are asymptomatic and detected during screening or surveillance colonoscopy. Because bleeding from an SSL is rare, faecal blood–based screening and virtual colonoscopy are not effective screening methods {27021193}. SSLs produce subtle macroscopic changes that pose challenges for endoscopic detection {27110696}. There are data to suggest that interval colon cancers are likely to be the result of missed or incompletely excised precursors {30455417}. Technological advances in colonoscopy and increasing adherence to quality metrics, such as bowel preparation and withdrawal times, have improved detection rates and facilitate complete removal, thereby reducing the risk of colonic adenocarcinoma {28268235;36712908;37417792;35550250}.

SSLs vary in size. Large lesions (> 10 mm) are more likely to progress to dysplasia and are considered advanced lesions in most colonoscopic surveillance guidelines.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of SSLs in average-risk populations is variable, ranging between 1% and 18% owing to differences in detection rates and criteria used for their histological classification {27110696;24418851;20717107;23022496;20459568;17101316}. Because of the improvements in colonoscopy technology, clinician education on polyp detection, and the lower threshold recommended for diagnosis, the prevalence of SSL is likely to continue increasing {36712908}. Patients are commonly in their fifties when diagnosed, and a slight male predominance has been observed {38857619;32192628}.

Etiology

Described risk factors for SSL include White ethnic background, family history of colorectal carcinoma, smoking, red meat intake, and alcohol intake {27852795;31323292} (see also section Colorectal carcinoma)

Pathogenesis

As many as 30% of all colorectal carcinomas arise via the serrated neoplasia pathway {31323292;31028362}. There is strong evidence to suggest that SSL is a neoplasm that may develop dysplasia (i.e. SSLD) and progress to colonic adenocarcinoma. The origin of SSL is not known; it is possible that SSL may develop de novo or may be preceded by a microvesicular hyperplastic polyp. The morphological progression of SSLs is accompanied by a sequence of genetic and epigenetic alterations that culminate in a sporadic carcinoma with hypermethylation and frequent microsatellite instability. Activating BRAF mutations initiate the development of most SSLs {25216220;17101316;25127095;15087661}. The reported prevalence of BRAF mutations is lower in SSLs from patients in eastern Asian countries than those from the USA, Canada, Australia, Germany, Austria, and Switzerland {32580537}. Rare KRAS mutations have been reported in SSLs from eastern Asian populations {29920740}. A small subset of SSLs without BRAF or KRAS mutations are associated with NTRK gene rearrangements {34402529}. Extensive methylation of CpG islands leads to a CpG island methylator phenotype and accompanies the development of dysplasia {26475632}. Extensive methylation ultimately leads to silencing of MLH1 via promoter methylation and is detected in as many as 75% of SSLDs; this alteration results in mismatch repair deficiency {26475632;28752838}. The reported rate of MLH1 deficiency is lower (30–40%) in SSLDs from the Republic of Korea and Japan {26713412;28614199}. The remaining mismatch repair–proficient SSLDs tend to harbour TP53 mutations {26475632}. Alterations in the WNT signalling pathway and p16 loss secondary to CDKN2A methylation occur in the transition from SSL to SSLD and carcinoma {29148535;28614199;26475632}.

Macroscopic appearance

SSLs are pale, slightly raised sessile (i.e. Paris type Is) to flat (i.e. Paris type 0-IIa and type 0-IIb) lesions covered with a mucus cap and a rim of debris or bubbles that impart a cloud-like surface {22018553;23433877;14652541}. They tend to flatten during bowel inflation, making their detection challenging. The dysplastic areas of SSLD are often elevated compared with the rest of the lesion. As a result, these neoplasms may be incompletely removed if the elevated part alone is recognized. Depressed areas and/or reddish discolouration are more typical of dysplasia or carcinoma associated with SSL {37219625;27663716;23022496}.

Histopathology

Sessile serrated lesion

The distinction between SSL and other serrated polyps is based mainly on architecture, although cytological features also play an important role {18162766;12502929}. Like MVHPs, SSLs lack overt cytological dysplasia and contain crypts with prominent serration. SSLs contain a mixture of goblet cells and non-goblet mucin-containing cells with microvesicular mucin droplets that fill the cytoplasm. The presence of cells with microvesicular mucin is a helpful feature that aids distinction from potential mimics, such as mucosal prolapse polyps and inflammatory-type polyps. Crypt architectural changes in SSL result from alterations in the proliferative zone and include horizontal dilation parallel to the muscularis mucosae, dilation of the basal one third of the crypt bases, serrated epithelium extending into the crypt bases (in contrast to superficial serrations in HP), and asymmetrical proliferation along the lateral aspects of the crypts {24418851;12502929}. These changes result in a type II open-shaped endoscopic Kudo pit pattern (type II-O) {22233696}. Branched crypts are frequently seen but are not specific to SSL and can be found in other polyp types.

The presence of ≥ 1 unequivocal architecturally distorted serrated crypt, as described above, is sufficient for a diagnosis of SSL {24418851;22710576;24399034}. Importantly, the size, location, and endoscopic appearance alone should not be used to make the diagnosis of SSL. HPs can also occur in the right colon, and location alone should not prompt an SSL diagnosis when characteristic features described above are lacking. Adjunctive features may be used to favour an SSL diagnosis in ambiguous cases or poorly oriented sections. Furthermore, endoscopic size assessment may not be accurate given the difficulty in delineating the borders of these polyps {23022496}. SSLs of the proximal colon often show herniation of mucosal elements through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa, particularly in lipomatous areas or lymphoid aggregates. The presence of mucosal herniation should not be confused with invasion, particularly when dysplasia is present.

Because the diagnosis of SSL is largely dependent on crypt architecture, well-oriented sections evaluating crypt bases are often necessary to establish a diagnosis. Deeper levels may be helpful when samples are not well oriented {24399034}. SSLs have been associated with an unusual stromal proliferation of pericryptal fibroblasts that have been considered to represent perineuriomas of the colon. This type of stromal proliferation is probably a reactive phenomenon related to the adjacent serrated epithelium rather than a true stromal neoplasm. While this phenomenon has no clinical significance, such polyps are best classified according to the epithelial component {33313671;21836484;29747061}.

Sessile serrated lesion with dysplasia

Dysplasia may develop in some SSLs as a transient step during progression to carcinoma {26475632}. The dysplastic component is sharply demarcated from the SSL. Architectural changes include villous architecture, crypt elongation, crowding of crypts with complex branching, cribriform glands, and excessive or reduced luminal serration compared with the background SSL. The dysplastic cells can show intestinal differentiation resembling dysplasia in conventional adenomas; serrated dysplasia with round atypical nuclei, prominent nucleoli, numerous mitotic figures, and eosinophilic cytoplasm; or minimal deviation dysplasia. Minimal deviation dysplasia is characterized by subtle cytological atypia with hypermucinous changes and concurrent MLH1 loss {29271414;28752838}. Multiple morphological patterns of dysplasia often occur in a single polyp, thereby precluding further classification {28752838}. Loss of MLH1 expression may facilitate a diagnosis when the dysplastic changes are subtle, but not all dysplastic patterns are MLH1-deficient {26475632;28752838}. Stratification of dysplasia into low-grade and high-grade is not recommended, since the presence of any kind of dysplasia is associated with significant risk of progression and the heterogeneity of morphological changes makes it difficult to reproduce criteria for dysplasia classification {25263173;28752838}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for SSL: a proliferation of serrated crypts with abnormal distorted architecture; absence of cytological dysplasia; for SSLD: a proliferation of serrated crypts with increased architectural complexity; cytological dysplasia of varied morphology.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Index SSLs are associated with an increased risk of large metachronous serrated polyps {28927878}. Similar to large (> 10 mm) serrated polyps of any histological type, SSL also increases the risk of synchronous and metachronous colorectal carcinoma {39104049;22710576;25399542;22763141;26677986}, and this can be reduced by adequate serrated polyp detection rate and complete removal {37417792;35550250}. Recommended follow-up intervals are based on low-level evidence and vary by country {32044092;32572858;28450390}. The presence of synchronous conventional adenomas may affect surveillance intervals {26855297}. Serrated polyps increasing in number and size may eventually fulfil the diagnostic criteria of serrated polyposis that requires regular surveillance.
