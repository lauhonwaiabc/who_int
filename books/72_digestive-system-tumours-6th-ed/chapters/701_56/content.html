7.0.1 Tumours of the colon and rectum: Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a significant source of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Global incidence of the disease will continue to rise over the next decade due to population growth and ageing, and an increasing trend is recently seen in young adults. It is, therefore, not surprising that in this sixth edition the chapter on colorectal cancer has been significantly expanded to provide an extensive overview of the morphological spectrum and its genetic underpinnings. The distinctive low-grade tubuloglandular carcinoma, typically seen in inflammatory bowel disease, is now included among the morphological subtypes. The tumour previously described as dome carcinoma or gut-associated lymphoid tissue carcinoma in literature is now included as lymphoglandular complex–like adenocarcinoma. The term “carcinoma with mesenchymal differentiation” replaces “carcinoma with sarcomatoid components” from the fifth edition, a change that has been harmonized across all sections in this edition. Grading of colorectal carcinoma is now described with some clarity. Specific features have been laid out for distinction of low-grade adenocarcinomas from high-grade adenocarcinomas.

Prevention of colorectal cancer relies heavily on colonoscopic screening and surveillance guidelines that in turn depend on generation of robust longitudinal outcome data at a population level. Although this has been achieved to a significant degree for conventional adenomas, the guidelines for patients with serrated polyps are still based on low quality of evidence, largely the result of changing diagnostic criteria and challenges with interobserver reproducibility. The current edition leaves the diagnostic criteria for serrated polyps unchanged from the prior edition and recognizes the slight variations in terminology used in clinical practice and published literature (#44383Table 7.01). Each of the three serrated polyp categories are now discussed in a separate chapter, enabling expanded text and better illustrations, while at the same time recognizing the relatedness and evolution between the groups. The occurrence of conventional adenomatous-type dysplasia in traditional serrated adenomas is clearly illustrated, and guidance is provided that these lesions are best classified as a traditional serrated adenoma, a proposed change that may alter the reported prevalence of such adenomas in future studies. A small section is included that describes juvenile polyps in the sporadic setting. Serrated polyposis syndrome that in the previous edition was under the chapter on hereditary syndromes is now included here in recognition of the fact that the majority of these patients do not harbour any currently detectable inherited germline alteration. Nonetheless, the sections on colon polyps included in this chapter are best read together with those on hereditary polyposis syndromes that involve the colorectum and are described separately.

The morphological spectrum of inflammatory bowel disease–associated dysplasia is described and illustrated in greater detail than in the last edition. It is also emphasized that diagnosis of dysplasia and dysplasia grading is more critical to case management than making a clear distinction between the various morphological patterns, as more than one pattern can often be seen in the same biopsy in these patients and the diagnostic criteria and clinical significance of some patterns remain to be validated in future studies.
