14.1.1.1 Oesophageal neuroendocrine neoplasms

Definition

Oesophageal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are malignant epithelial neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation that arise in the oesophagus.

Related terminology

Acceptable: well-differentiated endocrine tumour/neoplasm; poorly differentiated endocrine carcinoma; mixed exocrine–endocrine carcinoma.

Not recommended: carcinoid; composite carcinoid-adenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80130/3); small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80410/3); mixed large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–squamous cell carcinoma (80133/3); mixed large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80132/3); mixed small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–squamous cell carcinoma (80453/3); mixed small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80451/3); amphicrine-like carcinoma (85743/3); amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma (85744/3); amphicrine-like squamous cell carcinoma (85745/3)

Localization

NENs most commonly occur in the distal oesophagus {18670347;23426118}. This distribution is related to their pathogenesis. Endocrine cells are most numerous in the submucosal glands of the distal oesophagus, and as expected, well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) show a predilection for this region. Most mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) contain an adenocarcinoma component, so they show a distribution like that of oesophageal adenocarcinoma and may occur in association with metaplastic Barrett mucosa or (rarely) ectopic gastric mucosa. Poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) can occur at any level in the oesophagus, affecting both squamous and glandular mucosa {18670347;23426118}.

Clinical features

NETs usually appear as polypoid or nodular submucosal masses, and they tend to be small, < 20 mm in diameter {11914632;16437611}. For this reason, many of these tumours are discovered incidentally or produce only mild symptoms {11914632;16437611}. NETs may be functional, and thus, metastatic oesophageal tumours that produce serotonin can manifest with carcinoid syndrome {15599742}. NECs and MiNENs are often large, infiltrative, and ulcerated tumours {28656561;31852479}, that present with dysphagia, pain, weight loss, asthenia, and/or melaena {15599742;22012036;28656561}. Poorly differentiated NECs may be associated with paraneoplastic syndromes similar to those occurring in the lung and other sites {21576835}.

Epidemiology

Oesophageal NENs are exceedingly rare, accounting for only 0.04–1% of all gastroenteropancreatic NEN cases reported {15599742;22012036;28656561;28079231}. NETs represent < 1% of all oesophageal NEN cases reported, although their epidemiology is difficult to ascertain because most studies group them together with poorly differentiated NECs and MiNENs, both of which are much more common. Poorly differentiated NECs and MiNENs show a male predilection (M:F ratio: 6:1), with a predominance in the sixth and seventh decades of life (mean patient age: 56 years) {15599742;28656561;9024720}.

Etiology

The etiology of most cases is unknown. MiNENs and NECs likely show a predilection for the distal oesophagus due to the premalignant potential of Barrett mucosa with which they are often associated {4134908;11914632;8511743}. NECs and MiNENs with a squamous component may be associated with alcohol consumption and/or heavy smoking, similarly to oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma {28079231;8511743}.

Pathogenesis

Like other poorly differentiated NECs, those arising in the oesophagus have a high rate of inactivating mutations in TP53 and RB1 {29728688}. Mutations in NOTCH1, FAT1, FBXW7, PDE3A, PTPRM, and CBLN2 have also been reported {29728688}.

Macroscopic appearance

NETs are usually small polypoid or nodular submucosal masses, and they tend to be small, < 20 mm in diameter {11914632;30302162}. NECs and MiNENs are often large, bulky, deeply infiltrative tumours. They may be exophytic or ulcerated {23426118}.

Histopathology

NET

Oesophageal NETs display the classic well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. These tumours are morphologically like NETs occurring at other gastrointestinal sites. They display a nested or insular growth pattern and contain medium-sized cells with abundant cytoplasm and a small ovoid nucleus with dispersed chromatin and small nucleoli. Variable amounts of well-vascularized stroma are present. Tumours with a high Ki-67 labelling index and/or readily identifiable mitotic figures may show single cell necrosis, but extensive necrosis is not seen. Neuroendocrine differentiation is confirmed by the expression of chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and/or INSM1. Various hormones have been detected by immunohistochemistry, including serotonin, (entero)glucagon, pancreatic polypeptide, gastrin, and calcitonin {11914632;28944148;9077732}. VMAT2 expression, suggestive of an enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cell–like phenotype, has been described in some cases {8511743;17684761}. Grading information is rarely available, especially for cases reported in the older literature, but most cases can be retrospectively graded as G1–2.

NEC

Oesophageal NECs display the classic poorly differentiated, neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage, either small or large cell type [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. NECs account for > 90% of all oesophageal NENs {22012036}. They are subtyped as small cell NEC and large cell NEC; the relative proportion of large cell NEC varies across studies, from 12% to 62% {18670347;23426118;18360283}. Like other NECs, those of the oesophagus usually show solid, rosette-like, or palisading patterns; necrosis is frequent and often extensive. Mitotic activity is consistently brisk. Small cell NECs are characterized by small to medium-sized cells with a high N:C ratio, scant basophilic cytoplasm, and elongated nuclei containing finely dispersed chromatin without well-formed nucleoli. Large cell NECs are characterized by medium-sized to large cells with basophilic cytoplasm and large ovoid nuclei containing large nucleoli. Neuroendocrine differentiation should be confirmed by immunohistochemistry. Synaptophysin expression is a consistent feature, whereas chromogranin A expression is present in about 60% of cases and often less conspicuous {23426118}. Although the literature is limited, INSM1 appears to be highly sensitive, staining 100% of NECs and MiNENs in one study {34555651}. TTF1 is positive in about 70% of cases, so this marker cannot be used to determine the site of origin of these tumours {23426118}. The main differential diagnosis is basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, which usually shows immunohistochemical evidence of squamous cell differentiation, including expression of p63, p40, and CK5/6 {10759947}. Of note, p63 staining may be detected in some oesophageal NECs {23426118}. Undifferentiated carcinomas with SMARCA4 inactivation may look like oesophageal NEC and may express synaptophysin though in absence of chromogranin A and INSM1 {37143312}.

MiNEN

Oesophageal MiNEN display the classic morphological features of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine neoplasia [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]] and usually consists of poorly differentiated NEC and either squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma {28079231}.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Oesophageal amphicrine-like carcinoma may occur as amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma also, with goblet cell features {2413979;38178706}, and amphicrine-like squamous cell carcinoma {25747609}.

Grading

Oesophageal NENs are graded using the same system used for other gastroenteropancreatic NENs (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

NET

Essential: well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: SSTR2 positive immunohistochemistry.

NEC

Essential: small cell carcinoma or large cell carcinoma pattern; sheets or trabeculae of poorly differentiated cells; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: reduced/absent SSTR2 expression; loss of RB1 expression or abnormal p53 expression.

MiNEN

Essential: a mixed tumour composed of two morphologically recognizable components; either type ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine marker expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count to grade the NET component or to prognostically stratify the NEC component; grading of the non-neuroendocrine component.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Essential: a mixed tumour with no morphologically distinct zones of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation; ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine marker expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index.

Staging

There is no TNM staging system specific for oesophageal NET. The staging is in line with the UICC/AJCC systems for oesophageal carcinoma, similar to NEC, MiNEN, and amphicrine-like carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Based on newer literature, the prognosis for NETs appears to be favourable, mainly depending on tumour size and extent of dissemination. Although grading criteria for NETs are similar in the oesophagus compared with those in other sites, the influence of tumour grade on behaviour cannot be assessed because very limited data are available. Metastatic dissemination is rare {16437611}. In contrast, the prognosis for NECs is poor. Many patients present with metastases at diagnosis. The median overall survival time ranges from 8 to 15 months; most patients die within 2 years of diagnosis. The presence of distant metastases seems to be the strongest adverse prognostic factor {18670347}. There is no statistically significant difference in survival between large cell and small cell NEC {18670347}. The prognosis apparently depends on the Ki-67 proliferation index: poorly differentiated NECs with a Ki-67 labelling index < 55% are associated with a median survival of 24.5 months compared with 5.3 months for tumours with a Ki-67 labelling index > 55% {26943788}. MiNENs might have a better prognosis than pure NECs; the median survival time is about 20 months {18670347}, and prognosis is based on the Ki-67 proliferation index of the NEC component {29592868}.

There is no consensus regarding the optimal treatment for oesophageal NENs {28656561}. Small NETs (< 20 mm) without evidence of locoregional metastasis can be successfully treated by endoscopic resection. Larger tumours require surgery; adjuvant chemotherapy has been used in metastatic cases. The treatment of NECs and MiNENs is commonly based on platinum-based chemotherapy. Tumours with only locoregional extension can be treated by a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, and even radiotherapy {18670347}. Emerging data suggest a potential role for checkpoint inhibitor therapy among patients with poorly differentiated NECs {34973511}.
