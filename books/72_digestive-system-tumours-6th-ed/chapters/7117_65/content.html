Definition

Colorectal inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)–associated dysplasia is the neoplastic alteration of the intestinal epithelium confined within the basement membrane and arising in colorectal mucosa damaged by long-term IBD.

ICD-O coding

81480/0 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade

81480/2 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.4Y & XA4ZR7 & XH6AF9 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade, of colorectum

2E61.Y & XA4ZR7 & XH28N7 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade, of colorectum

Related terminology

Not recommended: dysplasia-associated lesion or mass; adenoma-like mass; flat dysplasia (as used to refer to grossly or endoscopically invisible dysplasia).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In ulcerative colitis, dysplasia mostly occurs in the rectosigmoid colon: 44–68% of all neoplasias and 47–70% of advanced neoplasias (defined as high-grade dysplasia [HGD] and colorectal cancer [CRC]) {1634062;8425717;8976012;16618396;21739534}. There is some evidence to suggest that low-grade dysplasia (LGD) located in the left colon (distal to the splenic flexure) is more likely to progress to advanced neoplasia than proximal LGD {21907984;23916526;27916678}.

Dysplasia and CRC in Crohn disease develop in areas of the colon affected by inflammation of Crohn disease {7828978;34041848}.

Clinical features

There are no signs or symptoms specifically linked to IBD-associated dysplasia, which is largely detected during surveillance colonoscopies. Polypoid or ulcerated lesions may cause bleeding. Obstruction is unusual until CRC has developed.

Epidemiology

Prevalence of IBD is estimated to be as high as 1 in 125 (0.8%) cases in some countries {31300515;34043730} and includes Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis. The overall risk of colorectal dysplasia is higher in patients with ulcerative colitis but may be similar in ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease when adjusted for the length of intestine involved with inflammatory disease {31175827;32592468}. Recent analyses suggest that the incidence ratio of CRC in patients with IBD is 1.7 and cumulative risk is 1%, 3%, and 7% after 10, 20, and 30 years of disease duration, respectively {23448792;23707778}, which is lower than previously reported. This is likely due to advances in endoscopic visualization and resection techniques and adherence to recommended biopsy protocols, since inadequate colonoscopy, insufficient surveillance, and improper management of dysplasia have been shown to be associated with a higher risk of interval CRC in patients with IBD {25956835}. In addition, improved management of the underlying inflammatory process also reduces the subsequent cancer risk.

Etiology

Neoplasia risk is consistently higher in patients with IBD with early disease onset, longer disease duration, more extensive involvement, concomitant primary sclerosing cholangitis, family history of CRC, and severe inflammation seen on endoscopy and histology {12085034;14762782;17919486;22289873;23872237;28750838;29378311;30267862;33385426}.

Pathogenesis

IBD-associated tumorigenesis is characterized by sustained inflammatory processes that drive the early genetic and epigenetic alterations. Persistent inflammation leads to oxidative DNA damage and genomic instability, which is exacerbated by microbial dysbiosis involving species like Escherichia coli and Fusobacterium nucleatum {28174420;34757143}. Common genetic alterations include TP53 mutations and MYC amplifications, whereas KRAS and APCmutations occur less frequently than in sporadic CRCs {26764183;27063727;29788391}. TP53 mutations are often initiating mutations that may occur before the development of histologically detectable dysplasia {8039614;22178590;28174420;29087436;34757143}. Some CRCs in patients with IBD show hypermutation linked to defects in mismatch repair (e.g. MLH1) or proofreading mechanisms (e.g. POLE) {26764183;29950348}, and the possibility of an underlying hereditary syndrome should be excluded in these patients. Key signalling pathways, including NF-κB, WNT/β-catenin, and PI3K/AKT, promote the development of dysplasia and CRC {26764183;27063727;36611031}. The frequent multifocal nature of dysplasia and CRC indicates a field effect, where large areas of the mucosa are populated by epithelial clones carrying aneuploidy or deleterious mutations, enabling them to expand and progress {8039614;22178590;28174420;29087436;34757143}. While copy-number alterations start to accumulate in non-dysplastic epithelium, the transition from LGD to HGD is often marked by a dramatic increase in these alterations {29991641}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of IBD-associated dysplasia has been classified according to the modified Paris classification put forth by the SCENIC consensus statement {25702852}. This has recently been updated {34416977} and divides dysplastic lesions into visible or invisible on endoscopy and further subclassifies visible lesions as polypoid (pedunculated or sessile), if protruding ≥ 2.5 mm into the lumen, or non-polypoid (flat elevated, flat, or flat depressed), if they do not protrude into the lumen. This system also requires reporting the lesion size, surface morphology (Kudo pit pattern), border clarity (distinct or indistinct), ulceration (present or absent), location (whether in an area of colitis), completeness of resection, techniques used for visualization, and a description of the surrounding mucosa (disease activity, other lesions). As a result of this new classification, the terms “dysplasia-associated lesion or mass”, “adenoma-like mass”, and “flat dysplasia” (to refer to invisible dysplasia identified on non-targeted, random biopsies) are no longer recommended.

Because of this variability in macroscopic appearance, in a colonic resection specimen, multiple areas should be sampled, usually every 100 mm, including both normal-appearing and abnormal inflamed mucosa, to detect any foci of macroscopically invisible dysplasia. Additional samples should also be taken from any visible raised or polypoid areas, mass lesions, and flat mucosa with velvety, granular, or villiform appearances, as these may represent dysplasia or CRC.

Histopathology

Most dysplasia in IBD resembles sporadic adenomas (with conventional dysplasia) or serrated lesions (with serrated dysplasia) and is graded accordingly. Several morphological patterns of dysplasia (hypermucinous, goblet cell–deficient, crypt cell, and Paneth cell-–rich) have also been described. However, these are less common and may be difficult to recognize, particularly in the setting of active inflammation. No validated grading systems exist for these unique patterns, but it is important to recognize them as neoplastic lesions so they can be managed accordingly. It is not unusual for several patterns to exist in the same lesion. There is significant variability in the diagnostic reproducibility of IBD-associated dysplasia among studies {37247824;37985140;38158125}.

Conventional (adenomatous) dysplasia

Conventional dysplasia is also referred to as intestinal- or adenomatous-type dysplasia because of its morphological resemblance to the dysplasia seen in sporadic colorectal adenomas. Its architecture may be tubular, tubulovillous, or villous. Dysplasia in intestinal-type lesions is typically graded according to either the Vienna {10896917} or the Riddell {6629368} system and categorized into LGD or HGD on the basis of a combination of cytological and architectural abnormalities. Dysplasia in its most typical and easily recognizable form uniformly involves the crypts and extends to the surface epithelium. LGD is characterized by mildly enlarged, elongated, hyperchromatic, stratified, and crowded nuclei that are mostly confined to the basal half of the cell cytoplasm. Surface involvement of the mucosa, as mentioned above, is key to the diagnosis. In HGD, the dysplastic cells show markedly enlarged, often pleomorphic, round, hyperchromatic nuclei, often with prominent nucleoli. There is usually a loss of nuclear polarity and full-thickness stratification of nuclei. HGD is often accompanied by architectural atypia, such as glandular crowding and cribriform formation. However, these features are not necessary, and a diagnosis of HGD can be rendered if the cytological criteria mentioned above are present in the dysplastic focus.

In rare instances, intestinal- or adenomatous-type atypia, suspicious for dysplasia, may be confined to the basal portions of the crypts without surface involvement, which has been termed “basal crypt dysplasia” {31111543;33155325;34232295;35771958;35942654;37012656;37055929;37985140;38907614;39956269}. Aberrant p53 staining is observed in 57–63% of cases {31111543;34232295}, and the rate of aneuploidy can reach 100% {31111543}. The use of an interim diagnosis of “indefinite for dysplasia” is also appropriate in this setting given interobserver variability and the need for additional follow-up in these patients. The indefinite category is also used when distinction between reactive and dysplastic changes cannot be made with certainty due to active inflammation or ulceration.

Intramucosal carcinoma is defined by the presence of cells or glands that invade the lamina propria or extend into, but not beyond, the muscularis mucosae. Intraluminal necrosis may be identified. Desmoplasia is absent when the carcinoma is confined to the mucosa, and its presence is typically diagnostic of submucosal invasion.

Serrated dysplasia

Serrated lesions in IBD encompass a spectrum similar to that seen in the sporadic setting. They may be morphologically identical to sporadic hyperplastic polyps (HP) and sessile serrated lesions and lack cytological dysplasia. The latter are characterized by dilatation and/or lateral spread of the crypt bases, resulting in the formation of dilated L-shaped or inverted T-shaped crypts at the interface with the muscularis mucosae. Like its sporadic counterpart, sessile serrated lesion–like lesions in IBD may develop serrated dysplasia with progression {26403785;31822800;33155325;37247824;40104985}. Serrated dysplasia in IBD may also resemble traditional serrated adenoma, which typically shows tubulovillous or villous fronds lined by low-grade columnar cells with elongated nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm, slit-like serrations, and ectopic crypts {26403785;31822800;31917154;33155325;37247824;40104985}. Traditional serrated adenoma–like and adenomatous-type dysplasias occurring within the same lesion are not uncommon in IBD. Serrated dysplasia NOS is defined by a complex serrated profile with varying degrees of atypia {31822800;37247824;40104985}. Endoscopically invisible serrated proliferations in IBD resembling sporadic HP have been termed “serrated epithelial changes”. These foci may harbour molecular alterations associated with neoplastic progression {38907614;33727167}, but there is no evidence to suggest any benefit from increased surveillance in these cases. HP, sessile serrated lesions, and traditional serrated adenoma that occur in patients with IBD in areas not involved by colitis are considered sporadic in origin. The short-term risk of neoplastic progression of these lesions appears to be low {26297256;26403785}.

Hypermucinous dysplasia

Hypermucinous dysplasia (also referred to as gastric dysplasia in some studies, particularly when accompanied by a foveolar phenotype) is often visible macroscopically as a plaque-like, glistening lesion. It is characterized histologically by a tubulovillous or villous growth pattern with tall columnar cells with multilayered mucin vacuoles that may contain acidic or neutral/gastric mucin, the latter resembling a foveolar phenotype {10517904;31822800;33155325;34232295;37055929;37247824;37985140}. Nuclear atypia is typically mild and often more pronounced in the lower portions of the crypts, making diagnosis challenging. Approximately 33–50% of cases demonstrate aberrant p53 expression on immunohistochemistry, either as null or strong expression phenotype {31801800;34232295}, and as many as 80% show aneuploidy {33155325}. Hypermucinous dysplasia also shows a high prevalence (61%) of KRAS mutations {10517904} and is often found adjacent to mucinous adenocarcinoma {37055929}. Reactive epithelial change and HP in IBD may sometimes exhibit a mucin-rich epithelium {38584461;38907614}, mimicking hypermucinous dysplasia. However, they lack prominent villous architecture and instead show the typical saw-tooth epithelium of serrated lesions.

Goblet cell–deficient dysplasia

The key histological feature of this pattern is tubular architecture without architectural complexity, typically low-grade dysplastic nuclei, and prominent enterocyte-like eosinophilic cytoplasm with a complete or near-complete absence of goblet cells {31822800;33155325;34232295;37247824;37985140;38546105}. It is important to emphasize that the most common conventional or adenomatous-type dysplasia described above is also mucin-depleted with few or no goblet cells and should not be mistaken for this unique pattern. The enterocyte-like eosinophilic cytoplasmic appearance is key to making this diagnosis. Approximately 31–40% of cases are reported to show high-grade features {33155325;34232295}, but the diagnosis becomes more challenging and less reproducible with an increasing N:C ratio and loss of the typical cytoplasmic appearance. Aberrant p53 staining is found in 29–44% of cases {31801800;34232295}. PIK3CA (56%) and KRAS (22%) mutations also commonly occur in this pattern {31801800}.

Crypt cell dysplasia

This pattern (which has also been termed “with terminal differentiation” and is not to be confused with basal crypt dysplasia mentioned above) is characterized by mild enlargement and hyperchromasia of mostly round to oval or slightly elongated, non-stratified nuclei without significant architectural crypt changes {37247824}. Some cases may show prominent Paneth cells, but most cells appear like normal crypt cells with enterocyte and goblet cell differentiation. Scattered cells may show more than mild nuclear enlargement and/or focal loss of nuclear polarity, but features of high-grade lesions are not well defined in terms of prognosis.

Paneth cell–rich foci may be present in any of the above patterns. This finding has been described as Paneth cell–rich dysplasia in the literature {31822800;33155325}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Immunohistochemistry for p53 is often used in clinical practice to assess the presence of TP53 alterations. However, not all dysplastic lesions are p53-mutant, and a wildtype pattern of p53 staining does not preclude a diagnosis of dysplasia if the characteristic morphological features of dysplasia described above are present. TP53 alterations are early events in IBD-associated neoplasia, particularly conventional (adenomatous) type, and it is not unusual to find mutant patterns of staining in what morphologically appears to be LGD {34232295;31375911}. However, p53 aberrations can rarely occur in non-dysplastic epithelium {37884500}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: unequivocal neoplastic intestinal epithelial changes confined within the basement membrane; setting of IBD.

Desirable: aberrant p53 staining (diffuse strong or complete loss of staining).

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The natural history of dysplasia generally varies according to its endoscopic appearance {25702852}. However, prior published data in many cases did not distinguish patterns of dysplasia and have mostly referred to conventional (adenomatous) dysplasia. Endoscopically occult adenomatous LGD has demonstrated a progression rate of > 50% to advanced neoplasia within 5 years {7926483;14598247;28843957}. Occult HGD has been linked to a higher likelihood of detecting CRC at immediate colectomy (40–67%) {7903776;7926483;20141808}. In contrast, polypoid adenomatous dysplasia has a low rate of progression to CRC, regardless of its histological grade {10579969;23567776;23920032}. A systematic analysis of 10 studies, including 376 patients with IBD who had resected polypoid dysplasia and were followed for an average of 54 months, found an annualized CRC incidence of 0.5% {23920032}. Recent studies have also indicated that hypermucinous and goblet cell–deficient dysplasias may represent high-risk lesions biologically despite the absence of traditional features of HGD and may be associated with a high rate of advanced neoplasia during follow-up (49–59%) {10517904;33155325;34232295;35486500;35771958;37055929;37985140;38546105}. These findings remain to be validated in future studies.
