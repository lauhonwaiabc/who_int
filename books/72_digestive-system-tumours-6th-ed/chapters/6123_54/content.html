6.1.2.3 Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma

Definition

Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma (GCA) is an amphicrine-like carcinoma with goblet cell–like mucinous features and variable neuroendocrine differentiation, typically arranged as tubules/nests resembling intestinal crypts.

ICD-O coding

82430/3 Goblet cell adenocarcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2B81.Y & XH4262 Goblet cell adenocarcinoma of appendix

Related terminology

Acceptable: goblet cell carcinoma.

Not recommended: goblet cell carcinoid; crypt cell carcinoma; microglandular carcinoma; adenocarcinoid.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Appendiceal GCA can involve any part of the appendix with a predilection for the distal portion {35903705}.

Clinical features

The most common presentation of GCA is acute appendicitis followed by nonspecific abdominal pain or an abdominal mass, but it can also be found incidentally in appendices removed for other indications {35903705}. The most common sites of metastasis are the peritoneum, omentum, abdominal wall, and ovaries {26030247;27338636;18685490}.

Epidemiology

For this uncommon tumour, a recent systematic review demonstrated an incidence of 0.05–0.3 cases per 100 000 person-years among North American registry studies {35903705}. Men and women are affected equally, although in some series women outnumber men, perhaps due to frequent ovarian involvement in the advanced stage. Age at diagnosis ranges from 23 to 90 years (mean: 56 years) {26030247;18685490;29579011;33360118}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Mutations in genes typical of colorectal adenocarcinoma (KRAS, APC, BRAF, and SMAD4) are rare in GCAs {29634977;29327707;29422640;14681318;35903705;36454563}. TP53 mutations may be found in high-grade GCAs {10391558;18685490;29422640}. In one study, genetic profiling of GCAs revealed mutations in genes in the WNT signalling pathway (USP9X, NOTCH1, CTNNA1, CTNNB1, and TRRAP) {29327707}. Other studies have shown mutations in chromatin-remodelling genes, including ARID1A, ARID2, KDM6A, and KMT2D (MLL2) {29634977;29422640}. A subset of cases have mutations in genes associated with gastric signet-ring cell adenocarcinomas, such as CDH1 and RHOA {29422640;29634977}.

Macroscopic appearance

The appendix may be grossly normal or thickened, and early-stage tumours can be overlooked macroscopically. GCAs of advanced stage may form an indurated large mass in the right lower quadrant, occasionally, without recognizable appendix.

Histopathology

The tumour usually involves the wall of the appendix circumferentially. To be classified as GCA, the tumour must display characteristics of an amphicrine-like carcinoma (as defined in section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification). This includes a mixture of goblet cell–like mucinous features, along with varying degrees of neuroendocrine and Paneth cell–like differentiation, all found as intimately admixed tumour cells. The presence of a component of classic low-grade GCA is helpful for diagnosis, but this may be challenging in an exclusively high-grade and advanced tumour. In addition, in contrast to conventional adenocarcinoma and mucinous adenocarcinoma of the appendix, GCA lacks precursor dysplasia on the surface epithelium and may provide a clue for the diagnosis of GCA.

Low-grade tumours grow as cohesive tubules, nests, or clusters with smooth contours composed of cells with small and peripherally located nuclei, goblet cell–like intracellular mucin, and granular eosinophilic cytoplasm. When invading into the muscularis propria, the tubules can be compressed, creating a linear appearing pattern, but the tumour cells maintain a cohesive architecture with low-grade cytological features. Architectural disarray and cytological atypia are mild, and mitotic activity is null. Tumour invasion is not associated with significant stromal reaction. However, in appendectomy specimens removed for acute appendicitis, GCAs identified may display tumour cells admixed with neutrophils, mild architectural distortion, increased nuclear atypia, and extracellular mucin, which may result in false upgrading of the tumour.

Histological features of high-grade GCA include infiltrating single tumour cells; complex anastomosing and irregular tubules/nests; and cribriform, single-file, and confluent sheets/aggregates of tumour cells. The tumour can present with patterns similar to signet-ring carcinoma with or without extracellular mucin, conventional adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), or undifferentiated carcinoma. Invasion of high-grade GCA is associated with stromal desmoplasia. Marked nuclear atypia is characteristic of high-grade GCA with nuclear pleomorphism and frequent mitoses. Necrosis may be present in solid areas.

Perineural invasion is common among all grades and is not prognostically significant. Lymphovascular invasion is more common in high-grade tumours.

The pattern of metastatic spread of GCA is predominantly to peritoneum (with regional lymph node metastasis being a significant factor) {35913635;32557796}, particularly for low-grade tumours. In contrast to mucinous neoplasms of the appendix, GCA rarely produces abundant mucinous ascites (pseudomyxoma peritonei). Thus, metastatic GCA in the peritoneum without abundant mucin should be classified and graded according to the criteria for GCA rather than for mucinous carcinoma peritonei.

Immunostaining for chromogranin and synaptophysin highlights variable numbers of tumour cells, which may be helpful{34804955}, but is not required for diagnosis.

GCA is graded using a three-tiered system based on the proportion of low-grade and high-grade components within the tumour (#43537Table 6.04) {2163192;26030247;29579011;32557796}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Low-grade goblet cell adenocarcinoma

Essential: cohesive and smooth-contoured tubules/nests of tumour cells with goblet-like mucin and with small, peripherally located nuclei without frequent mitoses or nuclear atypia; tumour invasion without stromal desmoplasia; mild architectural disarray and nuclear atypia is acceptable.

High-grade histological features

Essential: loss of cohesive tubule/nest architecture (e.g. single-cell infiltration and single-file growth with cytological atypia; complex anastomosing tubules/nests with irregular contours; or cribriform, confluent sheets or aggregates of tumour cells); stromal desmoplasia; infiltrative patterns similar to signet-ring cell carcinoma or poorly differentiated / undifferentiated carcinoma; marked nuclear atypia and pleomorphism with brisk mitotic activity.

Staging

The staging of appendiceal GCA is identical to that of appendiceal adenocarcinomas (see section Appendiceal adenocarcinoma).

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis depends on age, stage, and grade of the tumour. The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates are 95.5%, 85.9–87.6%, and 76.0–80.6%, respectively {35903705}. Most patients with low-grade GCA present with stage I or stage II disease {29579011;26030247}, although in one series, one third of patients with low-grade GCA presented with metastasis {18685490}. In contrast, 50–70% of patients with high-grade GCA present with stage IV disease {29579011;26030247}. Low-grade tumours have an overall survival time of 84–204 months. Intermediate-grade tumours have an overall survival time of 60–86 months. High-grade and disseminated tumours have an overall survival time of 29–45 months {26030247;29579011;18685490}. In addition, the presence of > 50% extracellular mucin in metastases of high-grade GCAs is associated with reduced overall survival {32557796}. Adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended for patients with high-grade GCA and lymph node metastasis {33231767;33360118}. Studies evaluating survival after cytoreductive surgery and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) in patients with high-grade GCA have reported mixed results {27401448;31646450;32892267}.
