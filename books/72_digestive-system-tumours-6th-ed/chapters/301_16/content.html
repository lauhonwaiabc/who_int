3.0.1 Tumours of the stomach and precursor lesions: Introduction

Epithelial malignancies can occur in any gastric compartment (cardia, corpus, or antrum) {26884394;35725291;32606208}. There has been a significant shift in gastric carcinoma (GC) topography over time, from the distal to the proximal stomach {38383142;32606208}, showing an ongoing transition in epidemiological patterns, with an increase in proximal carcinomas in Europe and the USA, and reduced distal carcinoma particularly in eastern Asia. This is probably related to the global efforts to eradicate Helicobacter pylori, as well as lifestyle factors, particularly obesity {27183032}. The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification specifies that any cancer whose epicentre is > 20 mm distal from the oesophagogastric junction (i.e. into the proximal stomach) should be staged using the system for stomach cancer, even if the oesophagogastric junction is involved {15895452;38841851}.

Gastric epithelial malignancies are correlated with a spectrum of genetic and epigenetic abnormalities that are heterogeneous within the tumour {28513632;35449086;38135758}. Syndromic GCs account for < 5% of all gastric epithelial malignancies worldwide and exhibit peculiar molecular profiles associated with heritable tumour syndromes (see section Genetic tumour syndromes of the digestive system). Somatic mutations may occur in sporadic GCs and in their adjacent non-invasive lesions {18830123;24272205;27514024;32415517;35092716}.

Molecular profiling has provided a framework for GC classification {27032689}. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Research Network and the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) have described four GC subtypes associated with key (epi)genetic molecular abnormalities: (1) EBV-positive, (2) microsatellite-unstable, (3) genomic stable, and (4) chromosome unstable (see #49674Table 3.03 in section Gastric adenocarcinoma) {22761333;25079317;25894828;34367379}. Specific somatic molecular alterations have been linked to rare subtypes of GC. However, despite advances in targeted therapy and other novel treatments, at its core, the clinical management of GC still relies on pTNM staging based on traditional histology.

In addition to adenocarcinoma, in this volume, we also discuss gastroblastoma and other GLI1-altered neoplasms with mesenchymal neoplasms. Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are included in the discussion of NENs throughout the GI tract, and lymphomas are included in the appropriate section.

Gastric mucosa at risk for development of carcinoma

Longstanding gastric mucosal inflammation promotes genotypic, phenotypic, and functional changes resulting in the cancerization field, laying the foundation for GC promotion, i.e. inflammation-related cancer {18650914;23809241;36108874}. The biological and clinical underpinnings of cancer development remain incompletely understood; therefore, expanding the understanding of these abnormalities is crucial in order to optimize strategies for primary and secondary GC prevention.

In the natural history of sporadic GC, the classic Correa cascade distinguishes early precancerous lesions from advanced precancerous lesions {15306570;23809241;36108874}.

Early precancerous gastric lesions

Gastric mucosal atrophy, defined as the loss of native glandular units, is consistently recognized as a precancerous condition. The histological phenotypes of gastric mucosal atrophy can be classified into two patterns: non-metaplastic and metaplastic patterns {12144574;33785282;35944925;21459343}.

Non-metaplastic atrophy is characterized by lamina propria fibrosis, which replaces the native glandular units. Metaplastic changes include two key distinct histological phenotypes: (1) pseudopyloric metaplasia (pPM), which affects only the oxyntic glands, and (2) intestinal metaplasia (IM), which occurs in native antral glands or metaplastic antralized oxyntic glandular units. All these modifications are potentially involved in decreasing the population of native glandular structures {39236896}.

pPM, also referred to as oxyntic antralization or pyloric metaplasia, results from long-term inflammation, primarily caused by H. pylori–associated or autoimmune gastritis {34728185;29508389;32356024}. The “clear cells” in pPM, as shown through H&E staining and TFF2 immunohistochemistry, effectively support the histological identification of this condition. Pepsinogen I expression in pPM mirrors that in spasmolytic polypeptide-expressing metaplasia, suggesting a similarity between these two metaplastic transformations {29508389;32394165}. Experimental evidence demonstrates a precancerous profile in pPM. In humans, this risk is supported by the association of pPM with pyloric gland adenomas, particularly in the setting of autoimmune gastritis {27767239;25092673;35287143}, but the risk of development of carcinoma is not definitively established {37196797}.

Intestinalized glands exhibit different H&E, histochemical, and immunohistochemical properties. Two major subtypes of IM are distinguished: small intestinal and colonic. However, the terms “complete type” and “incomplete type” are also used; the former recapitulates small intestinal mucosa, and the latter shows gastric foveolar–type cells interspersed between goblet cells on H&E. The small intestinal phenotype (synonyms: type I IM, mature IM) recapitulates small intestinal mucosa with enterocytes, goblet cells, and Paneth cells with an unequivocal brush border. Histochemistry typically demonstrates sialomucin-positive vacuoles; immunohistochemistry features low expression of native gastric mucins (EMA [MUC1], MUC5AC, MUC6) and replacement with intestinal-type mucins {6759388;39031976}. Colonic-type IM (synonyms: immature IM, type II–III IM, incomplete IM) includes goblet and columnar cells associated with sulfomucin secretion and immunohistochemical expression of MUC2 {11889603}. Colonic-type (incomplete) IM is consistently associated with increased GC risk {34454714;39718517}.

The two IM subtypes usually coexist in association with non-metaplastic atrophy, and the subtype prevalence correlates significantly with the topographical extension of the metaplastic atrophy (as gastric mucosa intestinalization increases, the colonic phenotype becomes more prominent) {21481917}. Consequently, histological quantification of the prevalent IM subtype is affected by sampling adequacy.

All atrophic changes result in the failure of gastric functions and modification of the gastric microenvironment, including remodulation of the gastric microbiota {31974133;32449430;39645287}. The precise role of microbiota in gastric carcinogenesis remains unclear but is a promising variable in studies of metaplasia progression and regression {30138135}.

Gastric functional serology, which includes the measurement of pepsinogen I, pepsinogen II, and gastrin, can serve as a biomarker for assessing the status and progression or regression of gastric atrophy {22696910;26342014}.

Globally, H. pylori infection is the primary cause of metaplastic IM–type gastric atrophy and is strongly associated with precancerous lesions and carcinoma {32184184;37598157}. This bacterium also plays a significant role in the development of primary gastric B-cell lymphomas by triggering the activation of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue {1682595;26773441}.

Current evidence unequivocally supports the notion that autoimmune oxyntic atrophy triggers the development of primary neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), particularly type I NETs of enterochromaffin-like–cell (ECL-cell) type {36913909}. The risk of NET is consistently associated with hyperplastic lesions of ECL cells, resulting from impaired gastric acid secretion due to the loss of oxyntic glands {28569272;35294740}. Autoimmune gastritis accounts for a small fraction of gastritis, ranging from 1% to nearly 5% depending on the population {20975338;39350751;39533765}. Progression from early lesions to complete loss of oxyntic glands can be rapid (~2 years) {26291507;34732311;38050966}.

From a semantic perspective, the term “atrophic gastritis” refers to cases characterized by mucosal atrophy that occurs alongside an overt inflammatory component. However, in daily clinical practice, this definition is often applied without regard for the presence of coexisting inflammation. In patients with H. pylori–associated gastritis, post-eradication resolution of the polymorphonuclear leukocyte–rich bacterial-related inflammation significantly reduces the genotoxic and cancer-promoting effects of inflammatory cytokines {37798959}, but patients with atrophic lesions that remain after H. pylori eradication are still at higher risk of cancer than H. pylori–naïve patients. The term “naïve” is used to differentiate H. pylori–negative patients who have never tested positive from those who are H. pylori–negative as a result of successful eradication therapy.

Non-syndromic environmental/sporadic GCs account for > 90% of gastric malignancies {24618998;37931640;35988963}. Worldwide, virtually all of these “epidemic” cancers are promoted by communicable agents (H. pylori, in particular), resulting in atrophic gastritis. The key exception is host-related gastric autoimmunity; H. pylori–naïve patients with autoimmune gastritis are unlikely to develop GC {38050966;35772926;37598157}.

The Operative Link on Gastritis Assessment (OLGA) staging system (see #44879Table 3.01) is based on the biological rationale of the gastric cancerization field {18424244;17142647;22198778}. The OLGA system classifies gastric atrophy into five stages, with the risk of cancer increasing from stage 0 to stage IV. This staging differentiates between low-risk (0, I, and II) and high-risk (III and IV) stages for gastric cancer. This distinction provides a biological basis for follow-up strategies aimed at the secondary prevention of GC. Based on the same biological assumption, the Operative Link on Intestinal Metaplasia (OLGIM) system evaluates the atrophy stages solely on the histological score of intestinalized mucosa and has been shown to be more reproducible than OLGA {20381801;33975867}. Gastritis staging promotes tailored surveillance programmes for patients, ensures that appropriate endoscopic procedures are performed, and reduces the number of endoscopic procedures in low-risk patients {35089403;30333540;37989553;38207154;36475275;29306868}.

Evolving data indicate that multimodal endoscopy, biopsy, and functional serology could be applied to improve the performance of GC secondary prevention strategies {38517462}.

Advanced precancerous lesions: gastric dysplasia

In 1980, an international group including pathologists from the Eastern and Western hemispheres defined gastric dysplasia as “the most advanced” precancerous lesion. In the Correa cascade of gastric carcinogenesis, dysplasia was regarded as the lesion closest to intestinal-type invasive gastric adenocarcinoma. At the beginning of the current century, the term “dysplasia” was replaced by “intraepithelial neoplasia” (IEN), and accepted synonyms included “intraglandular neoplasia” and “non-invasive neoplasia”. All the proposed terms highlight the neoplastic, genetically transformed biological profile of phenotypical changes that still lack the capacity for stromal invasion {14476362}. Most dysplasias show intestinal differentiation, but over time, other (less prevalent) dysplastic phenotypes have been described, as detailed in the dedicated sections that follow. From a morphological viewpoint, diagnosis of dysplasia/IEN requires the variable concurrence of cytological and architectural changes, varying according to the dysplastic histological subtypes. The clinicobiological profile of dysplasia/IEN is best documented in intestinalized epithelia. As expected, non-invasive neoplasia mostly associates with high-risk (OLGA III–IV) gastritis stages. Dysplastic phenotypes include two grades: low-grade dysplasia and high-grade dysplasia. Any grade of dysplasia/IEN requires H. pylori eradication (when needed) and endoscopic resection, followed by dedicated endoscopic surveillance using a biopsy sampling protocol. A prospective case series with long-term follow-up correlated high-grade dysplasia with a high risk of progression to invasive adenocarcinoma {12865267}. Invasion of the lamina propria changes the pT0 stage of IEN to that assigned to intramucosal GC (i.e. pT1a).

Raised dysplastic foci have been traditionally classified as adenomas. But such lesions are nearly always analogous to visible colitis-associated colorectal dysplasia as they arise in damaged mucosa, differing from colorectal adenomas, which almost always arise in normal mucosa. Therefore, the classification of gastric dysplasia has been modified here to mirror the philosophy of the management of epithelial precancerous conditions and lesions in the stomach (MAPS III) guidelines {40112834}. Sections previously dedicated to gastric dysplasia, intestinal-type adenomas, and foveolar-type adenomas have been combined into one section concerning gastric dysplasia.

Non-neoplastic gastric polyps

Benign inflammatory polyps are mostly encountered in patients with a background of cycles of gastric injury and repair due to communicable (e.g. H. pylori) or non-communicable (e.g. gastric autoimmunity) etiologies. These raised lesions commonly arise in the setting of longstanding inflammation involving distal mucosecreting or proximal oxyntic mucosa {11257625}. Their potential for malignancy is related to the occasional occurrence of dysplastic foci.

Syndromic polyps (juvenile type, Peutz–Jeghers type, familial adenomatous polyposis, and gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of the stomach [GAPPS]) are rare {27573780}. Their potential for malignancy involves the development of dysplasia. The biological profile of syndromic polyps is discussed in section Genetic tumour syndromes of the digestive system.
