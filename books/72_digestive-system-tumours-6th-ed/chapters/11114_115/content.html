11.1.1.4 Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm of the pancreas

Definition

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) of the pancreas is a grossly visible (typically > 10 mm) non-invasive neoplasm composed of mucin-producing epithelial cells typically forming papillae, arising in the main pancreatic duct and/or its branches.

ICD-O coding

84531/0 Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm with low-grade dysplasia

84531/2 Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia

ICD-11 coding

2E92.8 & XH8MD2 Intraductal papillary-mucinous neoplasm with low-grade dysplasia of the pancreas

2E61.Y & XH3MB3 Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia of the pancreas

Related terminology

High-grade IPMN

Acceptable: carcinoma in situ (used parenthetically in some parts of the world).

Subtype(s)

Gastric intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (low-grade, 84532/0; high-grade, 84532/2); intestinal intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (low-grade, 84533/0; high-grade, 84533/2); pancreatobiliary intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (low-grade, 84534/0; high-grade, 84534/2)

Localization

IPMNs arise within the main pancreatic duct and/or its branches. IPMNs mostly occur in the pancreatic head and uncinate process, but they can arise anywhere in the pancreas {34009233;37406887;38195084}. About 40% of patients have multifocal disease, and some diffuse involvement of the entire gland {17631133;22850621;28872701;31473224;35871123}.

Clinical features

With frequent use and advancements in abdominal cross-sectional imaging, IPMNs are increasingly identified as incidental pancreatic lesions {22245846}. It is estimated that their prevalence on CT is 11% for patients aged ≥ 50 years, and the prevalence increases with age {22245846;37847503}. Patients with an IPMN-associated invasive carcinoma are 3–5 years older than those without, suggesting that the progression of an IPMN to invasive carcinoma occurs over years {28002329;28416122;28877981;39191596}. Patients are usually asymptomatic upon presentation. If clinical manifestations are present, they include epigastric pain, chronic pancreatitis, weight loss, diabetes mellitus, and, rarely, jaundice, which is a strong predictor of high-grade dysplasia and/or an associated invasive carcinoma {28416122;34353996;38161091}.

The designations “main duct–type intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm”, “branch duct–type intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm”, and “mixed duct–type intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm” are imaging terms rather than distinct pathological subtypes. By radiographic imaging, these three distinct types of IPMNs can be discerned {31478800;33409798;34353996;38888840}. Main duct–type IPMNs are characterized by primary involvement of the main pancreatic duct with segmental or diffuse ductal dilatation. Branch duct–type IPMNs are the most common type of IPMN and involve the smaller, secondary ducts without affecting the main pancreatic duct {38744705;38182527}. Mixed duct–type IPMN is a combination of the other two types {38182527;25775066}. Enhancing mural nodules ≥ 5 mm or solid components on imaging correspond to high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma {38161091;38182527;39612583}.

Serum tumour markers such as CEA and CA19-9 are generally not of value, but preoperative pancreatic cyst fluid analyses showing elevated CEA and decreased glucose levels {37230894;38195219;33964311;33731599} and molecular testing for somatic mutations {36209796;37212422;37230894;23743931} may serve as useful adjunct to other findings in the diagnosis and risk assessment of IPMNs {26253305;28700444;28970292}.

Epidemiology

Eighty per cent of pancreatic cystic lesions are IPMNs {38423346}. The true incidence of IPMNs is unknown as most of them are small and asymptomatic, but studies of cross-sectional images of patients without a history of pancreatic lesions have shown that approximately 2.6–13.5% of adults have pancreatic cysts and that its incidence correlates with increasing age. Also, population-based studies have estimated the age and sex-adjusted cumulative incidence of IPMN of 2.04 cases per 100 000 person-years {21160836}, and the incidence has increased over the past several decades {22210438}. IPMNs occur predominantly in men aged > 60 years {39499841}, and although large geographical differences in the sex distribution exist (Japan and the Republic of Korea have an M:F ratio of 3:1 for the main duct–type IPMN) {28002329;28877981;38423346}, the age at diagnosis remains similar across regions (62–67 years) {21673537;18645105;38423346}.

Etiology

There are no well-established environmental etiological factors for IPMNs. Patients with Peutz–Jeghers syndrome or McCune–Albright syndrome are at an increased risk for developing IPMNs {10362809;12171972;28188442}. Individuals with a family history of pancreatic cancer or deleterious germline variant in a familial pancreatic cancer gene (e.g. ATM) have an increased prevalence of branch duct–type IPMNs as compared with the general population {22245846;30716324;31285527}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of IPMNs is unknown, although they often arise in a background of smaller intraductal neoplastic lesions indistinguishable from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) {24525507}. In theory, a macroscopic intraductal neoplasm must have evolved from a prior microscopic lesion, and small gastric-type IPMNs are essentially indistinguishable from larger low-grade PanIN lesions {24525507}. Comprehensive methylome and gene expression analysis has shown a great similarity between PanIN and gastric IPMN, thus providing further support to a common origin / pathogenesis of these two neoplasms {32796935;35944927}.

Low-grade IPMNs frequently harbour somatic mutations in KRAS {24604757;25423558}, GNAS {22158988;23846778;24604757;25423558}, and, less often, BRAF {17097223;26253305;36209796;37212422}. KRAS mutations are the most prevalent, occurring in 60–80% of IPMNs {17097223;22158988;25840541}. Activating GNAS mutations at codon 201 are found in 50–70% of IPMNs, particularly in the intestinal subtype {22355676;21775669;23846778;25840541}. BRAF alterations occur in approximately 5% of IPMNs and, in contrast to colorectal adenocarcinoma, are predominantly non–p.Val600Glu (V600E) mutations {36209796}.

RNF43, which encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase, is also somatically mutated in about 50% of IPMNs {22158988;22355676;24604757;25081753}. TP53 mutations can be found in 10–40% of IPMNs with high-grade dysplasia and 40–60% of invasive carcinomas arising in association with an IPMN {17198188;25423558;28930868;29301828}. A similar pattern is observed with CDKN2A alterations {26253305;32796935}. SMAD4 alterations are typically seen only in associated invasive carcinomas {26253305;36209796;37212422}. The tumour microenvironment may play a role in the progression of IPMNs {40001347}.

Macroscopic appearance

Similar to their appearance in radiographic imaging, IPMNs can be broadly subdivided into three subtypes on the basis of their involvement of the pancreatic duct system. Main duct–type IPMNs are commonly present in the pancreatic head but may involve the entire main pancreatic duct, including extending out into the ampulla of Vater, leading to mucin extrusion from a patulous ampullary orifice that imparts a fish-mouth appearance to the orifice {22658387;24714290;31430199}. The main duct is dilated (> 5 mm in diameter) {38182527}, often filled with thick, tenacious mucin, and typically lined by soft, tan, and friable papillae {25775066;26559377}. It is important to note that the size of a normal main pancreatic duct varies based on anatomical location with a mean diameter in the head, body, and tail of 3.4 mm, 2.4 mm, and 1.6 mm, respectively {38351473}. While abnormal enlargement of the main pancreatic duct is often set at a threshold of > 5 mm, thresholds of 6.5 mm in the head–neck and 7.7 mm in the body–tail regions are associated with the presence of an IPMN {36402960}.

Branch duct–type IPMNs are peripheral multilocular cystic lesions embedded in the pancreatic parenchyma. The cyst contents are commonly mucinous, and papillae may not be grossly obvious. Mixed duct–type IPMNs involve the main duct and its peripheral branches {25081232}.

IPMNs > 30 mm and/or with an associated nodule are likely to harbour high-grade dysplasia or an associated invasive carcinoma. Invasive carcinomas are identified as solid or mucinous, firm tumours, usually adjacent to dilated ducts. It is important to note that in some cases, PDACs and IPMNs occur in the same pancreas but are just concomitant, not genetically related neoplasms {28739282;30308608}.

Histopathology

IPMNs are characterized by intraductal growths of columnar mucin-producing neoplastic epithelial cells that form papillae ranging from microscopic folds to grossly visible projections {25775066;37604731}. Based on the highest degree of cytoarchitectural atypia, IPMNs are classified as low-grade or high-grade. IPMNs with low-grade dysplasia are characterized by mild to moderate atypia, at least focal papillary projections and only occasional mitoses. High-grade dysplasia in IPMNs is characterized by a constellation of findings that include severe architectural abnormalities, such as papillae with irregular branching and budding, nuclear atypia consisting of nuclear stratification with loss of polarity, significant pleomorphism, and prominent nucleoli; mitoses may be numerous.

IPMNs can be subclassified into three histological subtypes on the basis of the predominant differentiation of the neoplastic epithelium: gastric, intestinal, and pancreatobiliary {15223952;16088402;21193453}. Two or, rarely, even all three histological differentiations can be found in a single IPMN.

Gastric-subtype IPMN: This is the most common histological subtype of IPMN (~70% of the cases) and is often present in branch duct–type IPMNs. The epithelium is composed of columnar cells with basally oriented nuclei and pale mucin-producing cytoplasm, reminiscent of the gastric foveolar epithelium {16088402}. Scattered goblet cells can be seen. IPMNs with gastric differentiation express MUC5AC but are negative for MUC1 and MUC2 {16088402}. Most gastric-type IPMNs are classified as having low-grade dysplasia; however, some cases have focal high-grade dysplasia and even an associated invasive carcinoma {16088402;21193453;39597904}.

Intestinal-subtype IPMN: This is the second most common subtype (~20% of IPMNs) and is typically found in main duct–type IPMNs. The epithelium is characterized by long, villous papillae and lined by columnar epithelial cells with cigar-shaped enlarged nuclei and basophilic cytoplasm with variable amount of apical mucin {15223952;12015744;16088402}. Intestinal-type IPMNs express MUC2, CDX2, and MUC5AC, and are negative for MUC1 {16088402}. Compared with gastric-type IPMNs, intestinal-type IPMNs are more often classified as having high-grade dysplasia {35944927}.

Pancreatobiliary-subtype IPMN: The pancreatobiliary-subtype IPMN is the least common. It is typically present in main duct–type IPMNs. Pancreatobiliary IPMNs have complex arborizing and interconnecting papillae composed of cuboidal cells with amphophilic cytoplasm, enlarged nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. Pancreatobiliary-type IPMNs express MUC1 and MUC5AC and are negative for MUC2 {16088402}. Due to the architectural complexity and degree of nuclear atypia, most pancreatobiliary-type IPMNs have high-grade dysplasia {12379756}.

Ductal markers including CK7, CK19, and CEA are strongly expressed in most IPMNs {8910043;9448015}. The expression pattern of mucins, as well as CDX2 (a marker of intestinal differentiation), is useful for distinguishing the morphological subtypes, although overlapping expression patterns may occur (see #44665Table 11.01) {12379756;15223952;16088402;20139757;27198568}. Large numbers of differentially expressed proteins have been identified through mass spectrometry {39005476}.

The differential diagnosis of an IPMN includes other pancreatic intraductal neoplasms, PanIN, mucinous cystic neoplasms (MCNs), simple mucinous cysts, and retention cysts. Intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms (IOPNs) are characteristically complex, with florid proliferation of monotonous cells that have abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, round enlarged nuclei with a prominent nucleolus, and intraepithelial lumina {8712298}. The distinctive nature of IOPNs is further supported by the identification of PRKACA and PRKACB fusion genes, absent in IPMNs {31678302;31676785}. Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasms (ITPNs) may be difficult to distinguish from pancreatobiliary-type IPMNs {19440145}. Features favouring an ITPN include predominantly cribriform architecture, diffuse high-grade dysplasia, minimal intracellular mucin, and lack of MUC5AC expression {19440145;27984235;36056133;36801389}. PanIN is a microscopic (< 5 mm) non-invasive, flat or papillary epithelial neoplasm arising in the smaller pancreatic ducts {26559377}. The epithelial cells in almost all PanIN lesions have gastric foveolar differentiation. In contrast, IPMNs are typically > 10 mm in greatest dimension and can have varying directions of differentiation {26559377;25775066}. Therefore, although small gastric-type IPMNs are difficult to distinguish from large PanIN lesions, lesions with intestinal differentiation are most likely IPMNs {26559377;25775066}. The term “incipient IPMN” can be applied to lesions 5–10 mm in diameter with long finger-like papillae, intestinal differentiation, or a somatic GNAS mutation {26559377;25775066;24525507}. MCNs, unlike IPMNs, typically occur in women, are located in the tail or body of the pancreas, and do not communicate with the duct system. A defining feature of MCNs is the presence of cellular ovarian-type stroma that expresses PR by immunohistochemistry {25517958}. Simple mucinous cysts are > 10 mm in size and are composed of gastric-type mucinous epithelium. The single layer of cuboidal to columnar mucinous epithelium of a simple mucinous cyst contrasts the gastric foveolar morphology of a gastric-type IPMN. In addition, simple mucinous cysts have a smooth and flat lining that lacks papillary architecture {26559377;25775066;27740966}.

Retention cysts (dilatation of the pancreatic ducts due to an obstructive process) are usually unilocular and lined by a flat single layer of ductal epithelium without nuclear atypia. They may mimic IPMNs, when involved by PanIN. The location of the lesions (commonly in the periphery of a mass lesion) and lack of florid papillae may help differentiate these from true IPMNs {25775066;27740966}.

Associated invasive carcinoma: IPMN and invasive carcinoma can occur in the same pancreas. If the invasive carcinoma arises in the vicinity of the IPMN, it is designated as an IPMN with an associated invasive carcinoma. If the carcinoma is not contiguous with the IPMN, it is designated as IPMN with a concomitant invasive carcinoma {21499212;26559377;25775066}. Therefore, if an invasive carcinoma is identified, thorough sampling of intervening pancreatic tissue to determine the relationship of the IPMN to the invasive carcinoma is recommended {26559377}. When all IPMNs are considered, the overall incidence of an associated invasive carcinoma is relatively low {29560572}; however, invasive carcinoma is identified in association with as many as 60% of main duct–type IPMNs {23508017;21193453;18645105}.

The invasive carcinoma associated with an IPMN is typically one of two distinct types: colloid carcinoma or tubular (ductal) adenocarcinoma. Colloid carcinomas arise in association with intestinal-type IPMNs {11145249;12717243;25840541;21193453}.

Cytology

FNA specimens range from hypocellular to hypercellular, with isolated single cells or loosely cohesive groups of mucin-producing columnar cells. Papillary structures or sheets may also be seen. There is often abundant, thick, viscous mucus in the background {18497541;18089490;38343764}. The WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology is recommended for reporting results of cytopathology findings {36516741} [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Because the stroma is often not sampled, IPMNs cannot be distinguished from MCNs on FNA. In such cases, the nonspecific diagnosis of mucin-producing neoplastic cyst is preferred, with a comment, depending on the clinical findings, indicating that the differential diagnosis includes both IPMN and MCN {25775066}. Once a cyst is determined to be a mucin-producing neoplastic cyst, it is important to determine the grade of cytological dysplasia (low-grade vs high-grade), but it may be impossible to distinguish high-grade dysplasia from invasive carcinoma {25775066;26559377}.

In addition to FNA, fine core needle biopsies after aspiration of pancreatic cyst fluid or micro forceps biopsies of the pancreatic cyst wall may be diagnostically useful {31076375;29660844}. Both FNABs and microforceps biopsies can capture fragments of cyst epithelium and, in some cases, aid in the diagnosis of a mucin-producing cyst, and, in the setting of an IPMN, have the potential for grading of dysplasia. Accurate grading of these biopsies is limited because high-grade dysplasia, and even invasive carcinoma, can be focal and can therefore be missed due to sampling. Nevertheless, micro forceps can be helpful in the distinction between an IPMN and MCN, as in many cases, the underlying ovarian-type stroma may be sampled with sufficient material for immunohistochemical and molecular confirmation {29660844;35964683}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in postoperative specimens. In the preoperative setting, molecular testing of pancreatic cyst fluid may be helpful in establishing the differential diagnosis of a mucin-producing cyst, and specifically an IPMN. Molecular testing of preoperative pancreatic cyst fluid for alterations in KRAS, GNAS, and BRAF are associated with a sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 100%, respectively, for a mucin-producing tumour {36209796}. Mutations in CDKN2A, PIK3CA, SMAD4, and TP53 are also associated with high specificities of 97%, 97%, 98%, and 95%, respectively, in the detection of high-grade dysplasia and/or associated invasive carcinoma {37230894;37212422}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a grossly visible non-invasive mucin-producing epithelial neoplasm within the pancreatic ductal system, with papillary formations.

Desirable: histological subtyping.

Staging

IPMNs with high-grade dysplasia are categorized as Tis (carcinoma in situ). Staging of an invasive carcinoma arising in association with an IPMN is based on the invasive component and follows the protocol for pancreatic carcinomas [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]]} [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The classification of IPMNs as main duct–type versus branch duct–type is of utmost importance for the clinical management of IPMN cases, because main duct–type IPMNs are associated with an elevated risk of high-grade dysplasia and associated invasive carcinoma {17631133;19835989;28079542;30413822}. However, once the neoplasm is resected, this classification is superseded by the absence or presence of an associated invasive carcinoma {38182527;25775066;28416122;34353996;39666193}.

IPMNs without an associated invasive carcinoma are often curable. The 5-year survival rates for patients with resected IPMNs with low-grade dysplasia and high-grade dysplasia are reported as 100% and 85–95%, respectively {34353996;28416122;38918108;24378847;30413822;31463655;36906508}. While the mortality in the latter group includes deaths due to other causes and the presence of residual neoplasia at a margin, these data suggest that metachronous multifocal disease remains a substantial risk for patients with an IPMN, and careful clinical follow-up is warranted after the resection {17481526;31175866;39225424}.

The prognosis for IPMNs with an associated invasive carcinoma is significantly worse than it is for those without. The 5-year survival rate is 36–90%, depending on the histological type and stage of the invasive carcinoma {21508421;34920899;39283575;39666193}. Patients with a colloid carcinoma arising from an IPMN have a better prognosis than those with tubular adenocarcinoma {39425752;39086101}. Those with invasion of < 5 mm have an excellent prognosis {18223327}, whereas the prognosis for larger invasive carcinomas and/or associated poor prognostic features (e.g. perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, lymph node involvement, etc.) have a similarly poor patient outcome as conventional PDAC {26135696;39086101;39283575;39425752;39666193}. The histological subtype of the precursor IPMN is also predictive in patients without an associated invasive carcinoma; non-invasive gastric-type IPMNs have improved outcomes as compared with non-gastric–type IPMNs {21193453;21508421}.
