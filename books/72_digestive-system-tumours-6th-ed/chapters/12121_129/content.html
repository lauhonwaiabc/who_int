12.1.2.1 EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer

Definition

EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer (EBVMCU) is a lymphoproliferative disorder (LPD) with a polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate including EBV-positive atypical large B cells and/or Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg–like (HRS-like) cells, which typically involves mucosal and cutaneous sites in patients with immune deficiency and dysregulation.

ICD-O coding

96800/1 EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer

ICD-11 coding

2A85.Y & XH3SG2 EBV-positive mucocutaneous ulcer

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

EBVMCU involves mucosal sites and skin, including the oral mucosa, tonsils, palate, and the entire GI tract {19889052;20154586;29314151;30615240}.

Clinical features

EBVMCU typically presents as a well-circumscribed, often painful ulcer in mucosal or cutaneous sites, without systemic symptoms, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, or bone marrow involvement {20154586;22769419;25007145;30418184;32561847}. Multifocal lesions within one anatomical region may be rarely seen {20154586;30418184}.

Epidemiology

No reliable data are available on the incidence of EBVMCU. Presentation is generally in elderly patients (aged > 70 years). EBVMCU occurs in the setting of inborn or acquired immune dysregulation, including autoimmunity, therapy-related immune modulation, immunosenescence, HIV infection, and post–organ transplantation {20154586;22769419;25007145;29314151;32561847}.

Etiology

EBV is present in all cases and is the presumed etiological agent.

Pathogenesis

Available data indicate that pathogenesis is related to alterations in the cytotoxic T-cell repertoire and functionality in response to EBV in immunosuppressed patients. IG gene rearrangements are detected in 50% of cases and are associated with clonal expansions of EBV-infected B cells; rare clonal T-cell expansions may also lead to the detection of TR gene rearrangements {20154586;28395107}.

Macroscopic appearance

Shallow ulcer without an associated mass

Histopathology

Mucosal and cutaneous lesions show well-circumscribed ulcers with a polymorphous infiltrate of lymphoid cells ranging in size from small to large, variable numbers of immunoblasts, and HRS-like cells. There are admixed plasma cells, histiocytes, and eosinophils. Angioinvasion, thrombosis, and necrosis with neutrophils are often prominent; apoptotic cells may be seen. The overlying epithelium may show pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia. The base of the lesion is sharply demarcated by a dense rim of small lymphocytes. Small lymphocytic infiltrates predominate in some cases {33739792}.

The polymorphic infiltrate consists of a mixture of CD20-positive and CD3-positive lymphoid cells. CD8-positive T cells may predominate in some cases. The atypical large B cells and HRS-like cells are EBV-positive, express B-cell markers, and are frequently CD30-positive, but they only rarely express CD15 {20154586;25007145;28395107;30418184}. Small CD3-positive T cells that lack EBV typically form a boundary with the uninvolved adjacent tissue.

The differential diagnosis of EBVMCU includes polymorphic LPDs, EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and classic Hodgkin lymphoma, especially when there are increased large immunoblasts and HRS-like cells. EBV is typically detected in cells with a range of sizes in EBVMCU and polymorphic LPDs. The circumscription and localization to cutaneous and mucosal sites of EBVMCU are unlike polymorphic LPDs, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, or classic Hodgkin lymphoma; and attention to tissue architecture is important. The diagnosis may be challenging in superficial, small, or core needle biopsies. The presence of necrosis and angiocentric infiltrates containing EBV in nasal or oropharyngeal locations may morphologically mimic extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma; immunohistochemistry showing an NK/T-cell phenotype excludes EBVMCU.

Cytology

FNA samples may show a polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate with necrosis. Given the overlap of EBVMCU with polymorphic LPDs and neoplastic lymphoid lesions, histological assessment is required for a definitive diagnosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Monoclonal B-cell receptor or TR gene rearrangements are found in > 30–35% of cases and therefore do not separate EBVMCU from polymorphic LPD and overt lymphomas {20154586}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • Setting confirmed or highly suspicious for immune deficiency and dysregulation
  • Well-circumscribed shallow ulcer in mucosal or cutaneous sites with a polymorphous lymphoid infiltrate
  • Atypical large cells positive for CD20 (variable), CD30 (variable), and PAX5
  • EBV positivity in tissue (EBV viral load measurements in blood are not sufficient for diagnosis)

Desirable:

  • Band of CD3-positive small T cells lacking EBV at the periphery and at the base of the lesion

Staging

Formal staging procedures for EBVMCU are not indicated; however, when there is clinical suspicion of more extensive LPDs in a patient with known or suspected immune deficiency and dysregulation, including a suspected inborn error of immunity, staging evaluation should be performed to exclude additional and/or more aggressive LPDs at other sites. Imaging should also be undertaken to exclude mass lesions or deep-seated lesions.

Prognosis and prediction

The majority of EBVMCUs regress spontaneously or respond to withdrawal of immunosuppression when immune reconstitution is possible. Localized therapy may be needed in some cases. Occasional cases may have a relapsing and remitting course, without progression {19889052;20154586;22769419;24456137;25007145;28395107}.
