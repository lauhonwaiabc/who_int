10.1.1.3 Mass-forming intracholecystic neoplasm

Definition

Mass-forming intracholecystic neoplasms (ICNs) are grossly visible, polypoid/papillary non-invasive neoplasms composed of dysplastic epithelium that typically projects into the lumen of the gallbladder.

ICD-O coding

85037/0 Mass-forming intracholecystic neoplasm with low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia

85037/2 Mass-forming intracholecystic neoplasm with high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia

ICD-11 coding

2E92.6 & XA8KL9 & XH6RX1 Intracystic papillary neoplasm with low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of gallbladder

2E61.3 & XA8KL9 & XH6AH7 Intracystic papillary neoplasm with high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of gallbladder

Related terminology

Not recommended: biliary adenoma; intestinal adenoma; papillary (villous) adenoma; tubulopapillary (tubulovillous) adenoma; pyloric gland adenoma; non-invasive papillary neoplasm/carcinoma; intracystic papillary neoplasm; papillomatosis; papillary carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Intracholecystic papillary neoplasm (low-grade, 85033/0; high-grade, 85033/2); intracholecystic tubular neoplasm (low-grade, 85038/0; high-grade, 85038/2)

Localization

ICNs can involve any part of the gallbladder mucosa and may be unifocal, multifocal, or diffuse. Some examples arise in an adenomyoma and as such appear as mural nodules on the wall.

Clinical features

Almost half of all patients with ICNs present with right upper quadrant pain. The other half are detected incidentally {22895264}. Close to half of the cases are designated radiologically as gallbladder cancer {22895264}.

Epidemiology

ICNs are uncommon. ICNs are found in 0.4% of cholecystectomies, and about 6% of gallbladder carcinomas arise in association with ICN. ICNs show a female predilection. Patient age ranges from 20 to 94 years (mean: 61 years) {22895264;8416136;9923922;22386521}; very rare examples in children are on record.

Etiology

Many ICNs arise in patients with gallstones {22895264}. Additionally, ICNs can be seen in patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis or pancreaticobiliary maljunction {33201123}. Intracholecystic tubular neoplasms (ICTNs) are commonly associated with cholesterolosis {32691142}. ICNs can rarely be found in adenomyomas without any overt alterations in the rest of the gallbladder mucosa or the bile ducts {33060404;37134225}.

Pathogenesis

For ICNs that develop in primary sclerosing cholangitis or because of gallstones, various inflammatory pathways and cytokines involved in gallbladder cancer are likely factors and an inflammation/injury–ICN–carcinoma sequence is likely. Pancreaticobiliary maljunction–associated carcinogenesis in the gallbladder appears to be preceded by a distinctive mucosal hyperplasia, termed reflux cholecystopathy, caused by reflux of pancreatic exocrine enzymes into the gallbladder {28622182}.

Data on the molecular and genetic background of ICNs are inconsistent so far, partly because ICNs have been analysed together with invasive adenocarcinomas or with intraductal neoplasms of the bile ducts. ICTNs appear to be driven by alterations in the WNT signalling pathway {12023580;11196159}. The common drivers of pancreatic and bile duct intraductal neoplasms such as KRAS mutations {9923922;21307665} or GNAS mutation at codon 201 are relatively rare in ICNs {23335286;22954004}, with the exception that ICNs that develop in consequence to pancreaticobiliary maljunction have higher KRAS mutation rates. p53 alterations have been reported in intracholecystic papillary neoplasms (ICPNs) {34643317}.

Macroscopic appearance

ICNs typically form grossly visible granular, friable excrescences or prominent exophytic (polypoid/papillary mucosal) lesions {22895264}. The median size is 22 mm {22895264}. ICPNs form broad-based granular lesions. They are often extensive, and approximately one third are multifocal. In contrast, ICTNs are typically solitary, pedunculated, lobulated polypoid masses and with stalks so thin that the lesions often detach from the surface and may be mistaken as sludge in the lumen {32691142}. ICNs arising in adenomyomas form demarcated mural nodules filled with granular material, and often spare the surface mucosa {33060404;37134225}.

Histopathology

There are several similarities between ICNs and their more clearly defined counterparts in the pancreas and extrahepatic biliary tree. However, these parallels are incomplete. For instance, gallbladder tumours are rarely mucinous. As ICNs are uncommon with limited available evidence, the classification of subtypes remains quite preliminary. Rarely, ICNs may show oncocytic cytology, but lack other key characteristics such as arborizing papillae and intraepithelial lumina of IOPNs. This makes a definitive classification challenging for now.

Intracholecystic papillary neoplasm (ICPN)

The neoplastic epithelial cells of ICPNs form broad-based exophytic masses with predominantly papillary architecture and commonly focal tubular pattern. ICPNs can extend into Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses, mimicking invasive carcinoma. They are morphologically heterogeneous with several intermingled directions of differentiation. Biliary, gastric, and (rarely) intestinal differentiation can be seen in ICPNs, similar to the pancreas and extrahepatic biliary tree {22386521;22895264} with MUC expression following the differentiation. ICPNs with gastric differentiation are to be distinguished from polypoid pyloric gland metaplasia (previously designated as pyloric gland adenoma and fairly common in injured gallbladders). Currently, these do not qualify as ICPN unless they form a distinct nodule > 10 mm or have high-grade dysplasia. The neoplastic epithelium of ICPNs is often contiguous with flat dysplasia in the adjacent mucosa (biliary intraepithelial neoplasia). ICPNs are classified as low-grade or high-grade on the basis of their highest degree of cytoarchitectural dysplasia. High-grade lesions are characterized by architectural complexity as well as loss of cellular polarity and nuclear pleomorphism {22895264}. Rarely, ICPNs arise in adenomyomas, forming partly solid and partly cystic mural nodules in the fundic region. ICPNs arising in adenomyomas are often low-grade lesions, but invasive carcinoma is found in about 15% of these cases {37134225}. When ICPNs arise in adenomyomas, the rest of the gallbladder mucosa lacks dysplasia that is commonly seen accompanying ICPNs.

At the time of diagnosis, more than half of all ICPNs have progressed to an invasive carcinoma. This is more common in ICPNs that have a predominantly papillary architecture, biliary morphology, or extensive high-grade dysplasia {7127263;22386521;22895264}. The resulting invasive carcinoma is often a tubular adenocarcinoma, although other types, such as mucinous, adenosquamous, or neuroendocrine carcinoma, have also been described {12481004;31981075}. The carcinoma may be inapparent macroscopically and may also occur eccentrically in the ICPN lesions. For this reason, carcinomas arising in association with an ICN should not be classified as non-invasive or minimally invasive (T1 or early T2) unless extensive (preferably total) sampling and thorough examination have been conducted.

Intracholecystic tubular neoplasm (ICTN)

ICTNs, previously been reported as a part of the intracholecystic papillary-tubular neoplasm spectrum {22895264;32691142;36821179;27984235}, typically form solitary polyps with a thin stalk. They are characterized by a complex tubular architecture with back-to-back small acinar-like units formed by mucin-poor cells with round to ovoid nuclei, which distinguish them from the tubular-prominent examples of ICPNs. The lesion commonly contains corpuscles (morules) with nuclear labelling for β-catenin. Although the degree of cellularity and atypia often formally qualify these tumours as high-grade, to date, there is no evidence of transformation of ICTNs into an invasive carcinoma. The majority of ICTNs are diffusely positive for MUC6, and they often express MUC1. They typically do not express MUC2, MUC5AC, or CDX2 {32691142}. Dysplasia is not typically present in the background gallbladder.

Cytology

Cytological diagnoses of these entities are limited.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a non-invasive mass-forming polypoid epithelial neoplasm arising in the gallbladder mucosa.

Desirable: intraluminal growth of non-invasive back-to-back epithelial units composed of intraepithelial neoplastic cells, in a tubular, tubulopapillary, or papillary configuration.

Staging

ICNs with high-grade dysplasia are staged as pTis. The staging of invasive carcinoma arising in association with ICNs follows that of gallbladder carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with an ICN without an invasive carcinoma have an excellent prognosis after cholecystectomy. Some patients with ICPN develop carcinoma in the biliary tract many years after the diagnosis, which is attributed to the field risk phenomenon that is especially valid in patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis and pancreaticobiliary maljunction. This field risk phenomenon does not appear to be applicable to ICPN arising in adenomyomas or ICTNs. For invasive carcinomas with an ICPN component, a 5-year survival rate of about 60% has been reported {22895264}.
