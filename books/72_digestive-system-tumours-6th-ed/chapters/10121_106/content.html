10.1.2.1 Carcinoma of the gallbladder

Definition

Carcinoma of the gallbladder is an invasive epithelial neoplasm arising in the gallbladder.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C13.0 Carcinoma of the gallbladder

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); poorly cohesive carcinoma (84901/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3)

Localization

The most common site is the fundus (60%) followed by the body (30%) and neck (10%), including the cystic duct. Most carcinomas exhibit mucosal spread to the remainder of the gallbladder, extensively involving the organ beyond the main lesion.

Clinical features

The signs and symptoms are nonspecific and indistinguishable from those produced by gallstones. Right upper quadrant pain is common. Approximately 50% of cases are clinically inapparent (incidental), resected with the preoperative diagnosis of gallstones or cholecystitis with the carcinoma disclosed only after pathological examination {22570074}. Imaging predominantly recognizes advanced lesions or those arising from intracholecystic neoplasms {22895264;32691142;33060404;39191539;37125405}.

Epidemiology

Gallbladder cancers (GBCs) are the most common biliary tract carcinomas, and a recent meta-analysis estimates the prevalence of GBC at 20 ± 5% among populations at risk, such as individuals with gallstones or other predisposing conditions {40148974}. The incidence of GBC varies geographically and ethnically. The highest incidence is reported in the Indigenous Mapuche people of Chile, with an incidence among females of 27.3 cases per 100 000 person-years {39580808}. High incidences are reported for parts of India, eastern Asia, and some central and eastern European countries. In Chile and India, GBC occurs predominantly in female patients with gallstones. In eastern Asia, it is almost as common in men, and the association with gallstones is much weaker {39580808}. The reason for these differences is unclear.

Etiology

Among the established risk factors for GBC, gallstones are the most common; in some high-risk regions (e.g. Chile), they are found in > 80% of gallbladders harbouring carcinoma {10430291;39580808}. However, the overall incidence of GBC in patients with cholelithiasis is < 0.2% {18000509}. The weight, volume, and size of the gallstones have been found to correlate with GBC incidence, more likely associated with prolonged exposure to inflammation related to stone formation than its composition {26980625;16724353}. The association of GBC with primary sclerosing cholangitis may also be attributable to inflammation. Another established risk factor for GBC is pancreaticobiliary maljunction {33201123}, which is the suprasphincteric confluence of the common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct. Rare cases have been associated with familial cancer predisposition syndromes such as Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis {29238914;36302789;35207722}. Metachromatic leukodystrophy is a very rare condition that has been linked to GBC, especially in younger patients {27261095}.

Pathogenesis

Chronic inflammation with mucosal damage and regeneration appears to be the main starting point for gallbladder carcinogenesis. Aflatoxin B1, found in food staples with fungal infestation due to improper storage, and Salmonella typhi have been implicated as triggers of the inflammatory cascade. In metabolic syndrome, which predisposes individuals to the development of gallstones, growth factors, adipokines, and cytokines are believed to contribute to the risk {26599607}. A distinct type of gallbladder injury called hyalinizing cholecystitis (also known as incomplete porcelain gallbladder) shows a close association to GBC {21716080}. It has been shown that selective mucosal calcification rather than the diffuse intramural calcification detected radiologically is the herald of the type of hyalinizing cholecystitis that carries a substantial cancer risk {21716080;11391368}. Pancreaticobiliary maljunction allows pancreatic enzymes to reach the gallbladder and leads to a distinctive type of mucosal hyperplasia referred to as reflux-cholecystopathy, followed by the development of adenomatous lesions (intracholecystic neoplasms) and carcinomas {28622182;33201123}. Reflux of pancreatic enzymes into the gallbladder is believed to be causative in this condition {33201123}. Rarely, carcinomas may overtly arise in adenomyoma, a malformative lesion {33060404}.

GBC is a product of the accumulation of multiple genetic alterations resulting from genetic predisposition and exposure to environmental risk factors {25639632;35207722}. More than 50% of GBCs harbour TP53 alterations {26980625}. Other more common mutations include alterations in CDKN2A or CDKN2B (19%), ARID1A (13%), PIK3CA (10%), and CTNNB1 (10%). Amplifications of ERBB2 (16%) have been reported {24185509;27622582}. Microsatellite instability and CDKN2A inactivation by promoter methylation have been reported in preinvasive and invasive lesions {15692793}. The dysplasia–carcinoma sequence appears to employ BilIN and BilIN-independent pathways with paucity of mutations in TP53 and CDKN2A and a higher frequency of mutations in CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin). A higher KRAS mutation rate in lesions related to the pancreaticobiliary maljunction but not in flat precursor lesions has been reported {9923922;11760569}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most GBCs (70%) arise in the fundus of the gallbladder. They are usually flat, firm, white, gritty, granular, and poorly defined tumours that typically grow diffusely. Distinguishing carcinoma from chronic cholecystitis preoperatively and intraoperatively can be challenging, and GBC may be missed even with thorough macroscopic examination {24022828}. For this reason, it is essential to thoroughly sample in cases of incidental GBC to reliably determine invasion extent and to identify the cystic duct margin separately, even in routine cholecystectomy specimens {33629395}. GBCs arising from intracholecystic neoplasms by definition have an exophytic component that can fill the lumen of the gallbladder {22895264}, and rare cases arising in adenomyomas have a fundic, predominantly submucosal pattern {37134225}. Mucinous tumours have a more gelatinous appearance {23106580}, and sarcomatoid and undifferentiated tumours might have a polypoid contour with a fleshy appearance {31177317}.

Histopathology

Adenocarcinoma of biliary type is the most common carcinoma type in the gallbladder.

Most GBCs are of tubular differentiation, similar to cholangiocarcinomas, both biologically and morphologically. Glandular elements can display a variety of patterns including clear cell, foamy gland, syringoid, microglandular {38972607} among others. Rare tubular-type GBCs are composed of columnar cells with elongated pseudostratified nuclei, resembling colonic adenocarcinomas; they have been designated as intestinal type and require ruling out gallbladder involvement by a colonic adenocarcinoma {3953931}. Poorly differentiated, biliary-type adenocarcinomas form single cells, cords, micropapillae, as well as nests with sheet-like arrangement {20460736;7802141;8946610}.

Specific subtypes
Mucinous adenocarcinoma

In about 7% of GBC, extracellular mucin deposition is present, and a third of these cases fulfil the criteria for mucinous adenocarcinoma of this organ (> 50% of the tumour) {23106580}. Their overall survival is significantly shorter than that of ordinary GBC, but some of this may be stage-dependent as mucinous carcinomas are typically more advanced at the time of diagnosis {23106580;35422386}. Unlike gastrointestinal mucinous carcinomas, they are often microsatellite-stable {23106580}.

Poorly cohesive carcinoma

These carcinomas are defined by the presence of diffusely infiltrating single cells, nests, cords, and sheets constituting > 50% of the tumour and may result in a linitis plastica growth pattern {27666767}. Plasmacytoid (as described in the urothelium) and signet-ring tumour cells may occur in some cases but are not a requirement. These are more frequent in women and are more aggressive than ordinary GBCs {27666767}.

Adenosquamous and squamous carcinoma

Focal squamous differentiation is found in approximately 5% of gallbladder carcinomas {21532545}. If squamous elements constitute a substantial part of the tumour (> 25%), the neoplasm is classified as adenosquamous carcinoma. Cases with < 25% squamous elements are reported as adenocarcinoma with focal squamous differentiation. Glandular and squamous components of the tumours have corresponding immunophenotypes. Pure squamous carcinoma and squamous carcinoma in situ are exceedingly rare; additional sampling typically reveals at least focal glandular in situ or invasive components qualifying the case as adenosquamous {21532545}. They often show substantial keratinization. Squamous differentiation confers a more aggressive behaviour on GBCs, with more advanced presentation and worse prognosis {21532545;26458491}.

Undifferentiated carcinoma

Sarcomatoid carcinoma with spindle cell morphology can also occur in the gallbladder and is often diagnosed at an advanced stage (pT3/4) {31177317}. Sarcomatoid components may be subtle and fibroblast-like, but they are more commonly pleomorphic (including giant cells) or may show evidence of heterologous differentiation (i.e. skeletal muscle, bone, and cartilage) {8276377}. In contrast, osteoclast-like giant cell carcinoma is rarely observed {31177317}.

Rare examples of rhabdoid undifferentiated carcinomas characterized by loss of SMARCB1 (INI1; SWI/SNF-deficient) have also been reported in the gallbladder.

Other subtypes

Some adenocarcinomas have an undifferentiated (non-glandular, nondescript) or lymphoepithelioma-like morphology {2451557}. Rarely, GBCs may display a hepatoid pattern, showing also immunohistochemical features of hepatocellular differentiation as well as elevated serum levels of AFP {21935757}, and thus must be distinguished from hepatocellular carcinoma invading the gallbladder [[Gerber TS, Goeppert B, Hausen A, et al. N-Cadherin distinguishes intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma from liver metastases of ductal adenocarcinoma of the pancreas. Cancers (Basel). 2022 Jun;14(13):3091. PMID 35804866.]].

Differential diagnosis

Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses, pseudoinfiltrative metaplastic glandular differentiation, and adenomyomas need be excluded, especially when high-grade dysplastic changes involve these units in a pagetoid manner {37134225}. Similarly, subhepatic Luschka ducts as well as peribiliary accessory glands of the neck and cystic duct may have a proliferative atypical and pseudoinfiltrative appearance {21566517}. The preservation of the organoid architecture is the most helpful criterion to correctly classify these conditions. No molecular or immunohistochemical markers are reliable to make these distinctions.

Cytology

Because many cases are undiagnosed before cholecystectomy and many of the unresectable overt carcinomas are diagnosed by imaging, FNA of the gallbladder is seldom used. However, in some regions where GBC is common and often present in advanced stages, FNA is performed to confirm the diagnosis. These cases display the corresponding cytology established in other sites of the pancreaticobiliary tract {23858322}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

None

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an invasive epithelial neoplasm arising within the gallbladder.

Staging

GBC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

Stage represents a relevant prognostic factor in GBC. Most GBCs present as T2 (perimuscular penetrating) carcinoma. Five-year overall survival rate for T2 GBC is 45% {33411027}. For T2 carcinomas, the location of the carcinoma as towards the serosal or hepatic surface was found prognostic in one study, and this parameter was therefore added to the TNM staging system as T2a and T2b [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]] {24854451}; however, some studies have failed to confirm its prognostic correlation {33053857}. T3 GBC, characterized by involvement of the serosa or hepatic tissue, has a poor prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate below 15%. Early gallbladder cancers (T1a/T1b) and those with an invasive component smaller than a few millimetres in size have a 5-year disease-specific survival rate > 90% {36580138;30562839;24022828}. Previous impression regarding the aggressive nature of T1b cancers has not withstood detailed reinvestigation {36580138}. For early cancers, the extensive mucosal carcinoma, involvement of Rokitansky–Aschoff sinus, and positive cystic duct margin status are associated with shorter survival {24022828;24713738}. For the long-term outcome of early cancers, the issues of multifocality and field risk phenomenon should be considered, especially if there is a condition, like pancreaticobiliary maljunction {33201123} or PSC, in the background. Early clinical data support the potential benefit of ERBB2 (HER2) immunohistochemistry to guide targeted therapy {27517322;36335173}. Microsatellite instability has been demonstrated in a small fraction of cases and may have therapeutic implications {27829276}.
