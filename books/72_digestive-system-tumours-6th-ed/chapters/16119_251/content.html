Definition

Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in STK11 and characterized by mucocutaneous melanin pigmentation, gastrointestinal polyposis, and cancer predisposition.

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

About 95% of patients with PJS have polyps in the small intestine. Up to 25% of patients have polyps in the colon and stomach {26721304}, but this may be an underestimate, as polyps in these sites are less likely to come to clinical prominence {15480979}.

Clinical features

The diagnostic criteria are summarized in #43508Box 16.04. Presenting symptoms include abdominal pain, intestinal bleeding, anaemia, and intussusception, which typically manifest in the first two decades of life {17311531;36970589}. If present, the characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation facilitates diagnosis of asymptomatic patients in familial cases, but the characteristic Peutz–Jeghers polyps are the main clinical hallmark {35487791}.

PJS is associated with a moderate or high risk of a range of malignancies, with an overall risk of any cancer by age of 40 years of approximately 21% and 71–85% by the age of 70 years (see #43509Table 16.16) {37017260;16707622;11113065;36517957}. This equals a 6-fold increased risk of any cancer in patients with PJS (hazard ratio: 6.2; 95% CI: 5.6–10.7). Mean age at cancer diagnosis for patients with PJS has been reported as 44 years (median: 40 years) {37659672}. In one recent unselected series, luminal gastrointestinal cancer accounted for 36% of cancer diagnoses, and the remainder were extraintestinal cancer diagnoses {37659672}. Breast cancer is the second most common malignancy after gastrointestinal tumours, with a cumulative breast cancer risk of up to 54% at age 64 years and a mean age at diagnosis of 37 years {11113065;36468211}. Other well-documented extraintestinal tumours include carcinomas of the pancreas, as well as otherwise rare gonadal lesions, including sex cord tumour with annular tubules of the ovary and Sertoli cell tumour of the testis {36717037}.

Epidemiology

PJS has an estimated incidence of 1 case per 50 000–200 000 births.

Etiology

A germline pathogenic variant (GPV) in the tumour suppressor gene STK11 (formerly called LKB1), which encodes a serine/threonine kinase, can be found in > 90% of patients with PJS {37017260}. Rare patients with somatic mosaicism have been described {32504210}. Most germline defects are point mutations and small intragenic deletions, but some larger deletions of one or more exons have also been described {17924967}.

Pathogenesis

The exact drivers of both polyp formation and carcinogenesis in PJS remain largely unknown. Mouse models have suggested that loss of a single murine Stk11 allele in the mesenchyme suffices to provoke hamartomatous polyp development {29202476}. However, dysplasia in hamartomatous Peutz–Jeghers polyps is exceedingly rare {35988959}, and these polyps are likely an epiphenomenon to the cancer-prone condition and not obligate malignant precursors {16344569}. Research focusing on the normal unaffected bowel has shown protracted clonal evolution in normal colonic crypts from patients with PJS, which potentially explains the increased risk of colorectal cancer {16344569;21940722}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance is not distinctive.

Histopathology

Full-blown colorectal Peutz–Jeghers polyps have a distinctive histology, with villous architecture and arborizing smooth muscle cores, covered by mucosal tissue of near-normal appearance. Epithelial misplacement due to prolapse and peristaltic kneading is relatively common in Peutz–Jeghers polyps and may extend into the serosa, in particular in the small intestine, mimicking a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma, a well-known pitfall {16344569}. Many of the smaller endoscopically resected polyps, both in the lower as well the upper GI tract, however, lack distinctive prolapse-type features and are challenging to classify histologically {20924281;26721304}. Without genetic confirmation of Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, it is often preferable to avoid reporting such lesions as Peutz–Jeghers hamartomas; instead defer to descriptive diagnoses such as prolapse-type polyp and recommend additional endoscopic examination with sampling of intestinal polyps and genetic testing in equivocal cases {36717037}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

GPVs in STK11 can be demonstrated using next-generation sequencing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria for PJS are presented in #43508Box 16.04. See summary in #59818Table 16.17.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours

Prognosis and prediction

Small bowel intussusception can be a major source of morbidity and mortality in PJS, but this can be prevented with push enteroscopy with clean-sweep polypectomy and treated by surgery {36970589;10217080}. The prognosis for patients with PJS is now mainly determined by the risk of malignancy. Patients with PJS should be surveilled to prevent gastrointestinal complications and cancer {25645574}. Recent studies suggest that a majority of incident cancer diagnoses in patients with PJS on surveillance programmes occurred between scheduled examinations {37017260}. Overall, patients with PJS demonstrate a 3-fold increased risk of death (hazard ratio: 3.4; 95% CI: 1.5–7.9) {37659672;21205875}.
