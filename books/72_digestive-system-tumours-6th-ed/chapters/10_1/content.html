1.0 Introduction to tumours of the digestive system

The Digestive system tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours is an essential diagnostic reference for the gastrointestinal system, with significant ethical and scientific responsibilities. Like previous editions, this sixth edition aims to meet the expectations of pathologists, oncologists, geneticists, epidemiologists, scientists, and others worldwide. However, the book’s end users differ in terms of the epidemiological context of professional practice, patients’ access to health services, and the availability of diagnostic techniques; these differences have made the authors’ task very challenging {36626866}.

The sixth edition can be considered a substantial advancement from the previous edition. We have refined the classification, improved the content, and incorporated new research findings and new illustrations. Within the digestive system, most neoplastic processes – both premalignant and malignant – are epithelial. Descriptions of these entities, with the associated clinical features and consequences for patients, form the largest part of the book. Like in the fifth edition, this volume categorizes neoplastic pathology by its primary anatomical site, arranged from the cranial to the caudal axis (section Tumours of the oesophagus to section Tumours of the pancreas). The non-epithelial tumours, including section haematolymphoid tumours and soft tissue tumours (i.e. section mesenchymal tumours), are described in separate chapters, each including a discussion of key anatomical sites. section Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are similarly separated. Other tumours are described in section Other tumours of the digestive system, and hereditary genetic tumour syndromes with digestive system involvement appear in section Genetic tumour syndromes of the digestive system.

Throughout this volume, our focus was on offering unequivocal and universally acceptable terminology for neoplastic diseases {36400101;39232164}, which is essential as the basis for future research (including artificial intelligence and molecular studies) and evidence-based treatment {33279516;34740486}. This volume also provides ICD-O coding and updated clinical and pathological staging.

In addition to the tumour definitions and the essential and desirable diagnostic criteria, we increasingly incorporate guidelines for reporting. In collaboration with the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR), the provider of datasets for cancer reporting following the WHO classification, we have included a number of disease-essential reporting criteria summarized in boxes. When citing evidence, we gave consideration to the levels defined for tumour pathology research evidence {37866639}. We sourced new high-quality images to illustrate the diagnoses and concepts; images from the earlier editions remain available online, along with the new figures. This approach optimizes the balance between making multiple diagnostically useful histopathological images available and illustrating key features for diagnosis. Additionally, a large number of whole slide scans are available online.

For both epithelial and non-epithelial neoplastic growths, reporting mitotic counts as “mitoses/mm2” instead of “mitoses/HPF” is advocated {34079071}. This approach is also recommended in real-world clinical practice, to ensure greater diagnostic applicability, promote interobserver consistency, and improve comparability in assessments.

In the chapter on section oesophageal tumours, a notable addition in this edition is oesophageal epidermoid metaplasia, with compelling data suggesting that it is a precursor to squamous cell carcinoma. Several chapters have been updated to reflect epidemiological trends and evolving clinical practices, featuring expanded histopathology descriptions. As endoluminal therapy for early oesophageal neoplasms gains prominence, the endoscopic Paris classification has received greater emphasis. Updates reflect our enhanced understanding of molecular carcinogenesis in oesophageal adenocarcinoma and its precursors, particularly highlighting the practical application of p53 immunohistochemistry. The text elaborates on various biomarkers used in case management, recognizing their increased role in guiding targeted therapies for advanced cases. The undifferentiated carcinoma section has been expanded to discuss a distinct subset characterized by SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex deficiencies. The organization of adenosquamous, mucoepidermoid, and adenoid cystic carcinomas has been refined, and they are now grouped together, on the basis of their mixed morphology.

The section stomach tumours chapter reflects updates and advances on the precursor neoplastic lesions and prognostic and therapeutic markers in various types of gastric neoplasms {38383142}. Criteria for oxyntic gland neoplasia have been refined. A key modification concerns the approach to dysplasia. Raised gastric dysplastic lesions have been traditionally classified as adenomas, but they are nearly always analogous to visible colitis-associated colorectal dysplasia. Namely, they arise in damaged mucosa, differing from colorectal adenomas, which almost always arise in normal mucosa. For this reason, the classification in this sixth edition has been modified to mirror the philosophy of the guidelines for managing epithelial precancerous conditions and lesions in the stomach {40112834;30841008;40104323}. Sections previously dedicated to gastric dysplasia, intestinal-type adenomas, and gastric-type adenomas have been combined into a single section concerning gastric dysplasia. Lesions arising beneath the gastric foveolar surface (oxyntic gland neoplasms and pyloric gland adenomas) are separated.

Based on differences in epidemiological, anatomical, and biological features, this sixth edition splits tumours of the small bowel into those of the section duodenum and ampulla and those of the section jejunum and ileum. The duodenum is the most common site for small intestinal neoplasms; duodenal and ampullary neoplasms have overlapping surgical treatments. Ampullary adenomas include two groups, namely ampullary duodenal adenomas and intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasms. These are distinguished from flat dysplasia, which gives rise to ampullary ductal adenocarcinoma, the most aggressive cancer subtype in this region.

Tumours in the jejunum and the ileum share many features with their colorectal counterparts, although there is considerably less knowledge about small bowel tumours. They are much less frequent and less accessible to endoscopy for biopsy or endoscopic resection, thus resulting in fewer early diagnoses. Small bowel malignancies with the highest incidence are metastatic tumours, covered in section Other tumours of the digestive system.

Three appendiceal malignancies include low/high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, appendiceal adenocarcinomas, and appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinomas. Appendiceal colorectal-type adenocarcinomas are relatively rare and resemble their colorectal counterparts. In this volume, goblet cell adenocarcinoma is categorized as an amphicrine-like carcinoma composed predominantly of goblet-like mucin-producing cells with a variable component of intimately admixed neuroendocrine cells. It is staged like appendiceal adenocarcinomas rather than section appendiceal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), because of their more aggressive course.

In the section colorectal tumours chapter, increased recognition of serrated polyps necessitated their subdivision into three types – hyperplastic polyps, sessile serrated lesions, and traditional serrated adenomas – and the spectrum of conventional and serrated precursors is better defined and illustrated. Furthermore, serrated polyposis is recognized as a largely sporadic (rather than inherited) syndrome. In larger polyps, where conventional dysplasia may be combined with either traditional serrated adenomas or sessile serrated lesions, the classification has been refined to add traditional serrated adenomas with conventional dysplasia. Sessile serrated lesions with conventional dysplasia remain part of the classification. Hamartomatous juvenile polyps are included here for the first time. This chapter includes the various patterns of dysplasia occurring in patients with inflammatory bowel disease, to enable their recognition and guide appropriate patient care. The uncommon and rare morphological subtypes and patterns of colorectal carcinoma, including intramucosal carcinoma, are better defined and illustrated, along with an improved definition of determination of high-grade carcinoma, with more-detailed molecular subtyping based on transcriptomic data.

In the section anal canal tumours chapter in this sixth edition, we have aligned the terminology with that of the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) project, which reflects our understanding of HPV biology, facilitating clear communication across specialties. We have also clarified that giant condyloma (Buschke–Löwenstein tumour) is linked to low-oncogenic-risk HPV infection {35562936} and is unrelated to verrucous carcinoma. Although most cases of anal intraepithelial neoplasia are related to HPV, this sixth edition recognizes that subsets arise independent of HPV that are analogous to either TP53-mutant or TP53-wildtype vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia {26402383;35437330;39863109}. The section on anal squamous cell carcinoma has been updated to incorporate evolving genetic and treatment data. Lastly, we have incorporated available information on high-risk HPV–associated glandular neoplasms {31857682;39308041}.

The section liver and intrahepatic bile ducts chapter includes two main innovations: further morphomolecular subtyping of hepatocellular adenoma (adding sonic hedgehog–related hepatocellular adenoma) and separation of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma into two etiological, morphological, and molecular subtypes, namely small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and large duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

There are many parallels among non-invasive epithelial neoplasms of the gallbladder, the large intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, and the pancreas. They may share patterns of differentiation and genetic alterations and can progress to invasive cancer. Therefore, in the sixth edition, harmonization of the nomenclature across these anatomical sites has been emphasized where possible. In the gallbladder, mass-forming intraepithelial neoplasms have been provisionally designated as intracholecystic neoplasms, which are further subcategorized. In the extrahepatic bile ducts, non-invasive mass-forming epithelial tumours include intraductal papillary neoplasms of the bile ducts, intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasms, and intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms. Microscopic intraepithelial neoplasia in the biliary tract continues to be designated as biliary intraepithelial neoplasia.

Although the overall classification of tumours of the pancreas remains largely unchanged, this chapter has been updated to emphasize newly recognized somatic genetic alterations characteristic of some tumour types, such as rearrangements involving PRKACA or PRKACB in intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms.

The section haematolymphoid tumours chapter in the sixth edition reflects an effort to harmonize content across the volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours by following a systematic approach to the selection of entities included in the volume. This approach begins with the recognition of the fifth-edition Haematolymphoid tumours volume as the primary source from which tumour types are selected based on the following criteria: (1) entities that arise specifically in the digestive system; (2) entities with a particular tropism for the digestive system; and (3) entities that involve the digestive system with relatively high frequency. Organ-specific considerations have been incorporated. It is acknowledged that these haematolymphoid entities do not, and arguably should not, encompass the entire spectrum of haematolymphoid neoplasms that may involve the GI tract, liver, and pancreaticobiliary system. For a comprehensive list, readers are referred to the latest Haematolymphoid tumours volume of this series and are encouraged to consult haematolymphoid subspecialty expertise wherever possible.

The section mesenchymal tumours chapter focuses on those soft tissue tumours most relevant to the digestive system, without descriptions of several sarcomas rarely detected in the digestive system. The full breadth of deep soft tissue tumours is covered in the Soft tissue and bone tumours volume. We include additional information on previously recognized lesions, furthering the understanding of GLI1-rearranged tumours (including gastroblastoma), as well as incorporating data that demonstrate that tumours previously regarded as “giant fibroepithelial polyp of the oesophagus and hypopharynx” are in fact liposarcomas. We have also added a section on NUT-rearranged sarcoma. Grading, staging, and prognostic factors are covered where appropriate.

NENs are covered in section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system, under the sections on section NENs of the luminal GI tract and section NENs of the liver, gallbladder, and biliary tract. The chapter also includes a section on the non-epithelial NEN paraganglioma and the description of composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour (CoGNET). The category of amphicrine-like neoplasia (or amphicrine-like carcinoma) has been added to describe a category of tumours with mixed neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation. These tumours are distinct from mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN), with intermingling rather than clear separation of the two patterns of differentiation. Although the grading scheme for NETs remains largely unchanged from the fifth edition, there is now a recommendation that the presence or absence of tumour necrosis should be explicitly reported in parallel with the grade (e.g. “G2 NET positive for tumour necrosis” or “G3 NET negative for tumour necrosis”).

The chapter covering section other GI tract neoplasms has been expanded to include a separate section on liver metastases, including a discussion of carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP) presenting in tumour deposits in this organ, peritoneal metastases, and other digestive system metastases. The peritoneal metastases include both metastatic non-appendiceal tumours and metastatic appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, many of which may be termed mucinous carcinoma peritonei, the pathological correlate of the clinical syndrome of pseudomyxoma peritonei. The discussion on germ cell neoplasia has been expanded to include differential diagnostic considerations, namely hepatoid adenocarcinoma, SMARC-deficient (SWI/SNF-deficient) malignancies, hepatocellular carcinoma, and hepatoblastoma. CUP may present as metastasis anywhere in the body and can be broadly divided into favourable and unfavourable subtypes. The only favourable subtype of CUP with a gastrointestinal-like profile is the colon-like subtype (which is defined as being CDX2-positive, CK7-negative, and CK20-positive by immunohistochemistry or showing a similar molecular profile to colorectal carcinoma). CUP and those with a gastrointestinal-like profile are described in the new section section Carcinoma of unknown primary.

The genetic tumour syndromes chapter has been harmonized with the recently completed fifth-edition WHO classification volume on these syndromes, with classification by abnormal gene and pathway involved for each syndrome, reflecting and illustrating the mechanisms contributing to tumour development. This approach focuses on identifying germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in patients who develop precursor lesions and cancers, providing insights into disease pathogenesis, potential treatments, and screening and surveillance approaches. Some genetic tumour syndromes affect a single organ (e.g. gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of the stomach [GAPPS]), whereas others involve several organs of the digestive system (e.g. Lynch syndrome), as well as other organ systems, but their description here focuses on the digestive system manifestations.

As with the previous editions, the sixth edition is open to criticism and further refinement. We welcome all feedback, which will help improve the series for the seventh edition, and we remain committed to updating the scientific content while aligning with the diagnostic expectations for cancer patients around the globe.
