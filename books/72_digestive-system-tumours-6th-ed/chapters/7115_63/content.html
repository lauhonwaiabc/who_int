Definition

Peutz–Jeghers polyp is a non-neoplastic polypoid lesion characterized by an arborizing core of smooth muscle surrounding discrete lobules of normal or near-normal mucosal epithelium.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

DA98.0 Hamartomatous polyp of small intestine

DB35.3 Hamartomatous polyp of large intestine

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyp; so-called sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyp

Localization

Syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps have been described throughout the GI tract. Most patients have polyps in the small intestine, and intussusception is a well-known clinical hallmark in children with Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (PJS) {37054692;38825635}. The occurrence of polyps in the colon and stomach is likely underreported {15480979}. The so-called sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps display a particular predilection for sites of mechanical force and peristalsis, including the proximal duodenum, sigmoid, and rectum {31582972}.

Clinical features

In patients without a documented germline (constitutional) pathogenic STK11 variant, affected family members, or clinical stigmata (including characteristic, prominent mucocutaneous pigmentation), a formal clinical diagnosis of PJS requires a minimum of three unequivocal Peutz–Jeghers polyps. On occasion, solitary polyps bearing the histological hallmarks of syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps are encountered in individuals who do not meet WHO criteria to fulfil a clinical diagnosis of PJS. Thus, sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps are solitary lesions that arise, by definition, in patients without associated features of PJS {12458378;37776958}.

One large study compared patients with documented syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps with patients with sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps {37776958}. In this study, patients with syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps were younger and more likely to develop neoplasia upon follow-up than patients with sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps. Moreover, syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps were more likely to occur in the small intestine and less likely to harbour dysplasia than were sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps {37776958}.

Recently, a small number of patients with PJS have been described with somatic mosaicism for pathogenic STK11variants leaving open the possibility that in very rare cases, bona fide syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps may develop in patients without associated clinical stigmata. These cases would require detailed molecular genetic confirmation before this diagnosis can be rendered {26386697;29948449;32504210}.

Epidemiology

PJS is uncommon and mainly documented in case reports or small case series. A recent study from Japan estimated a prevalence of 0.6 in 100 000 over a 3-year period, and an incidence in 2021 of 0.07 per 100 000 population {39623880}. However, the incidence of sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps has not been definitively established, and an older case series (121 polyps from 38 patients) concluded that sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps are vanishingly rare {17667545}. In that study, most solitary polyps arose in patients who were found to meet the WHO criteria for PJS (see section Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (STK11)). A small number of solitary polyps in the small intestine arose in patients with clinical histories suggestive of PJS, although these patients did not meet the criteria. Lastly, five solitary colonic polyps in patients without a formal diagnosis of PJS showed histopathological features suggestive of Peutz–Jeghers polyps, although mucosal prolapse could not be excluded {17667545}.These data show that solitary Peutz–Jeghers polyps are rare, and most suspected cases occur in patients who fulfil WHO criteria for PJS; a small minority of cases are likely due to mucosal prolapse {16344569}. Cases describe occurrence in middle-aged to older patients (average age at diagnosis: 56 ± 13 years), without clear evidence of a sex-specific distribution {12458378;31428497}.

Etiology

Germline pathogenic variants in the tumour suppressor gene STK11 (formerly LKB1) can be found in > 90% of patients with PJS {37017260} (see section Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (STK11)). Rare patients with somatic mosaicism have been described {26386697;29948449;32504210}. Sporadic solitary Peutz–Jeghers polyps, by definition, lack germline or mosaic pathogenic STK11 variants. This diagnosis can therefore only be entertained after adequate genetic workup.

Pathogenesis

The exact mechanism of polyp formation in PJS remains largely unknown. Mouse models have suggested that loss of a single murine Stk11 allele in the mesenchyme suffices to provoke hamartomatous polyp development {29202476}, suggesting that STK11 is haploinsufficient with regard to the suppression of polyp development.

Mucosal prolapse is an exceedingly common secondary feature of protruding lesions in the tubular GI tract, including benign adenomas, inflammatory polyps, and healing erosions or ulcers. Mucosal prolapse as a primary driver of mucosal injury can occur at different sites and lead to mucosal prolapse polyps and solitary rectal ulcer syndrome. The unifying feature of these lesions – and syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps – is a central area of proliferating muscle fibres surrounded by normal or reactive mucosa, believed to be secondary to prolapse or protrusion of the mucosa into the lumen of the GI tract. Sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps are likely a similar manifestation of mucosal prolapse {16344569}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance is that of a non-distinctive polyp.

Histopathology

Intestinal syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps have a distinctive histology, with arborizing smooth muscle cores that divide the epithelial compartment into discrete lobules composed of normal or near-normal mucosa. Epithelial misplacement due to prolapse and peristaltic kneading is relatively common in Peutz–Jeghers polyps and may extend into the serosa, mimicking a well-differentiated invasive lesion {16344569}. Small lesions in syndromic patients may lack the characteristic appearance described above.

The mean size of sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps has been reported as 15–20 mm (range: 5–33 mm) {31582972}. The central features of sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps resemble syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps. When related to mucosal prolapse, the fibromuscular proliferation is more haphazard and wraps around individual glands, and the neat sectoral division of mucosal epithelium into lobules is lacking. Adenomatous dysplasia or epithelial serration suggests a diagnosis of prolapse occurring in an adenoma or serrated polyp.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline pathogenic STK11 variants can be demonstrated using next-generation sequencing in patients with PJS.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: arborizing core of smooth muscle within the polyp mesenchyme; as a rule, there is a lack of extensive epithelial dysplasia.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with syndromic Peutz–Jeghers polyps have approximately a 6-fold increased risk of any malignancy and a 3-fold increased risk of death (see section Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (STK11)) {37659672;21205875;37017260;16707622;11113065;36517957}. Several studies have documented that solitary sporadic Peutz–Jeghers polyps do not portend an increased risk of death or malignancy {31582972;37745183;12458378}. Recurrences have not been found in longitudinal follow-up studies {31582972;12458378}. To exclude a clinical diagnosis of PJS in patients with solitary Peutz–Jeghers polyps, panenteric endoscopy may be considered, and patients may be referred for genetic counselling to assess family history and, depending on clinical index of suspicion, to initiate genetic testing for germline pathogenic variants.
