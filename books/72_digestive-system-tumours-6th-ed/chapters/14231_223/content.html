14.2.3.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the extrahepatic bile ducts

Definition

Extrahepatic bile duct (EHBD) neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are malignant epithelial neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation that arise in the extrahepatic bile ducts.

Related terminology

Acceptable: well-differentiated endocrine tumour; poorly differentiated endocrine carcinoma; mixed exocrine–endocrine carcinoma.

Not recommended: carcinoid; composite carcinoid-adenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80130/3); small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80410/3); mixed large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80132/3); mixed small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80451/3); amphicrine-like carcinoma (85743/3); amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma (85744/3)

Localization

EHBD NENs can arise throughout the biliary tract; however, the common bile duct is the most common anatomical site, accounting for approximately 60% of reported cases {15599742;15362737;11075851}. Other sites include the perihilar region and common hepatic duct {15599742;15362737;19491075}. These neoplasms show a predilection for junctional/transitional areas of EHBD {16633759;19669289;12850675}.

Clinical features

EHBD NENs can cause bile duct obstruction, leading to abdominal pain or painless obstructive jaundice and pruritis. Other symptoms include weight loss and nausea {1497121;23901240;14629756;24917351}. Rare cases become evident with symptoms of hormone hypersecretion such as primary gastrin-producing NET (gastrinoma) with Zollinger–Ellison syndrome or peptic ulcer disease {10095161;19491075;24917351;8835905;7665823}.

Epidemiology

EHBD NETs are extremely rare, and account for about 0.2% of all described gastrointestinal NETs in case series {19917473;15599742}. Neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) are more common than NETs at this site and account for about 1.3% of all reported gastrointestinal NECs and 0.19% of all EHBD neoplasms {19917473;33686308}.

Patients with EHBD NETs usually present in middle age, with the mean age at diagnosis reported to be around late fourth to late fifth decade of life, while NECs at this site present around late sixth or seventh decade of life in older patients {24917351;19917473;20586641;31752927}. However, all ages can be affected, and rare paediatric cases have also been reported for these neoplasms {19524713;20920731}.

NETs at this site seem to be more common in women, with an M:F ratio varying from 1:1.5 to 1:2.2 in different reports {24917351;20586641;15599742;19917473}. NECs appear to show no sex predilection {19917473;31752927}.

Etiology

The cause of EHBD NENs remains unknown. An association with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome has been proposed as some cases have been reported in these patients {18300362;2379881}. Some NETs at this site are associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 {19491075}.

Pathogenesis

The biliary tract has very small numbers of neuroendocrine cells, which explains the rarity of NENs at this site {20586641}. It has been hypothesized that NENs of EHBD may originate from multipotent stem cells of the biliary mucosa as also supported by mixed phenotypes observed in NENs {24917351;17227590}. Some propose that these neoplasms may be derived from pre-existing neuroendocrine cells dispersed throughout the biliary mucosa {18274439}, or ectopic pancreatic tissue within biliary ducts distributed during embryogenesis {24917351;19491075}. Others have suggested that these may arise from increased endocrine cells in postinflammatory metaplastic mucosa {24917351;39267205;20586641}.

Not much is known about the molecular pathways underlying the development of NETs at this site. However, mutations and alterations in tumour-related genes associated with adenocarcinomas such as TP53, KRAS, and SMAD4 seem not to play a major role in the pathogenesis of NETs {11075851;33686308}.

Similarly, there are minimal data about the molecular pathogenesis of NECs at this site. Frequent association of NECs of the biliary tree with non-neuroendocrine components (i.e. adenocarcinoma or its precursors) suggests that NEC at this site may also share common pathogenetic pathways with adenocarcinomas, analogous to other digestive system locations {33686308;33433884}.

Macroscopic appearance

EHBD NETs are usually small, submucosal nodules, and the mean size is about 20 mm (range: 2–55 mm) {24917351;11075851;15362737;17660084;17227590}. They can be grossly circumscribed or ill-defined poorly demarcated nodules associated with thickening of bile duct walls {11075851}. Some can be polypoid, mimicking papillary bile duct neoplasms {16001626;17227590}. The overlying mucosa may appear intact or ulcerated. The cut surface is homogeneous and yellow/tan to grey/tan. These tumours can be very firm because of striking desmoplasia {20586641}.

EHBD NECs and MiNENs appear as nodular or polypoid masses diffusely infiltrating the bile duct wall. Although the average size is about 30 mm, they can be much larger and usually show extensive invasion of the liver and adjacent tissue {14629756;11020854;23901240;18580314;34567573;19917473}.

Histopathology

NET

EHBD NETs display the classic well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. These tumours show well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology, like that seen with NETs at other anatomical sites {33686308;11075851}. Typically, a submucosal proliferation of cells is seen arranged in nests, clusters, or trabeculae, and occasionally forming tubules. The cells exhibit abundant granular eosinophilic to amphophilic or clear cytoplasm, with round to ovoid nuclei, small inconspicuous nucleoli, granular (salt-and-pepper) chromatin, and minimal pleomorphism {11075851;20586641}. The neoplastic cell nests can compress or infiltrate the intramural glands and sometimes replace these glands. The intervening stroma can range from dense sclerotic collagen to loose vascularized connective tissue. Focal calcification and stromal amyloid deposits can be seen, as can perineural and lymphovascular invasion. These tumours often infiltrate the bile duct wall and can also extend into the periductal adipose tissue {11075851}. Tumour necrosis is rare. If present, it should be included in the report {35294740}.

NEC

EHBD NECs display the classic poorly differentiated, neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage, either small or large cell type [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. Small cell NECs are composed of sheets, nests, cords, or festoons of round or fusiform cells with very high N:C ratio, round or ovoid hyperchromatic nuclei with characteristic nuclear moulding, homogeneously disposed chromatin, and inconspicuous nucleoli with indistinct cell borders {35294740}. These tumours typically show extensive necrosis and a subepithelial growth pattern. Intense basophilic staining of blood vessels can be seen in necrotic tissue {34567573;19669289;11075851}. Large cell NECs display an organoid growth pattern often with rosette formation and is composed of large cells with vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli. The cytoplasm is moderately abundant and is clear to eosinophilic, with distinct cell borders {16845291;35294740}. Single-cell and confluent necrosis are seen. Both small cell and large cell NECs consistently show a high proliferation index, documented by brisk mitotic activity and high Ki-67 proliferation index (usually > 55%). Numerous apoptotic bodies are seen. An NEC component can be also seen in association with intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct {12481004;23716999}.

MiNEN

EHBD MiNENs display the classic morphological features of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine neoplasia [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. This category encompasses mixed neoplasms with a neuroendocrine component combined with a non-neuroendocrine component. Each component should be histologically and immunophenotypically recognizable and make up ≥ 30% of the neoplasm, by arbitrary definition. In the biliary tract, more than a third of NECs are accompanied by an adenocarcinoma component {9697272}. The neuroendocrine component can show small cell or large cell cytomorphology, and the presence and extent of the neuroendocrine component should be acknowledged, as this component dictates treatment and outcome {27684986;32233993}.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

EHBD amphicrine-like carcinoma may occur as amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma often with mucinous cell features {35317101;36646615;27684986}.

Immunohistochemistry

EHBD NENs express cytokeratins, synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1 {35294740}. Some NETs express hormones to a variable degree {1877530;11075851;10095161;24917351;20586641}; however, clinical symptoms due to hormone production are extremely rare. Chromogranin A staining is often patchy and may show dot-like perinuclear positivity in NECs {31857137;35294740}. Aberrant p53 expression and loss of RB1 expression are commonly seen in NECs. TTF1 staining may be seen, particularly in small cell NEC {30024526;33433884;35294740}.

Grading

EHBD NENs are graded using the same system used for other gastroenteropancreatic NENs (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification).

Cytology

EHBD NENs are seldom aspirated, because of the rarity of these tumours and relative inaccessibility of these sites. The incidence of false negative results for NETs in brush cytology may be high due to submucosal location {24917351}. In NECs, smudge cells with crush artefact, nuclear moulding, brisk mitosis, extensive necrosis, apoptosis, and significant nuclear pleomorphism are common {20586641}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

NET

Essential: well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: SSTR2 positive immunohistochemistry.

NEC

Essential: small cell carcinoma or large cell carcinoma pattern; sheets or trabeculae of poorly differentiated cells; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: reduced/absent SSTR2 expression; loss of RB1 expression or abnormal p53 expression.

MiNEN

Essential: a mixed tumour composed of two morphologically recognizable components; either type ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine markers expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count to grade the NET component or to prognostically stratify the NEC component; grading of the non-neuroendocrine component.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Essential: a mixed tumour with no morphologically distinct zones of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation; ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; two neuroendocrine markers expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index.

Staging

Due to their rarity, EHBD NETs do not have their own staging systems. For NETs, NECs, MiNENs, and amphicrine-like carcinomas, the staging follows the criteria of EHBD adenocarcinoma in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for this site.

Prognosis and prediction

The data on prognosis for bile duct NETs are limited because of the rarity of these tumours. Metastatic disease is seen in one third of cases, either to local lymph nodes or to the liver {24917351}. Local invasion to the surrounding structures such as pancreas or portal vein can also be found in 18% of patients {15362737;24917351}. Even very small NETs (< 10 mm) can present with metastatic disease {9187551;16001626;15362737;24917351}. Complete surgical resection with negative histological margins is curative and affords the best chance of long-term disease-free survival, even in the setting of metastatic disease {15244130;20586641;15362737;18274439;24917351}. The 5-year survival ranges from 60% to 100% in different series; the 10-year survival rate is reported to be around 60–80% and is significantly better than that of biliary adenocarcinoma {15599742;19917473;11075851;33686308}.

NECs have a poor prognosis {16845291}, with more than half of all patients having disseminated disease at diagnosis {19917473}. Small cell NEC appears to be responsive to platinum-based chemotherapy as well as radiotherapy, and the presence of even a minor component of small cell NEC should be mentioned in the diagnosis {32233993}. The 5-year and 10-year survival rates for small cell NECs have been reported to be 8% and 0%, respectively {19917473}. Similarly, the prognosis for large cell NEC appears poor with 83% mortality within 5 years, and a median survival of 11 months after initial diagnosis, despite the treatment modalities used {31752927}. The stage of the disease influences prognosis {33686308;31752927}. The survival time of patients with EHBD MiNENs appears to be similar to that of patients with pure NECs {33327249}. When seen in association with an invasive adenocarcinoma, the NEC component often determines the prognosis {12481004}.
