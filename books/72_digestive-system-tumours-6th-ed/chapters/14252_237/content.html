14.2.5.2 Pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma

Definition

Pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma (PanNEC) is a poorly differentiated, high-grade malignant epithelial neoplasm characterized by neuroendocrine differentiation.

ICD-O coding

82460/3 Neuroendocrine carcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C10.1 & XH0U20 Neuroendocrine carcinoma, NOS, of pancreas

Related terminology

Acceptable: high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC); poorly differentiated neuroendocrine neoplasm (NEN); large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC); small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC); small cell carcinoma.

Not recommended: high-grade neuroendocrine tumour.

Subtype(s)

Large cell pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma (80130/3); small cell pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma (80410/3)

Localization

The majority of PanNECs occur in the head of the pancreas {24503751}.

Clinical features

The presentation of PanNECs is similar to that of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDAC) {8789136;24503751;25723112;26113608}, and includes back pain, jaundice, and/or nonspecific abdominal symptoms {17383419;22967994}. In contrast to pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), PanNECs are typically non-functioning {4350960}. Serum calcitonin levels may be elevated in some patients {29063495}. SSTRs (in particular SSTR2) are not usually expressed {28059098}, which may help in distinguishing PanNECs from PanNETs. Functional imaging studies, such as SSTR scintigraphy (111In-pentetreotide [Octreoscan]) and 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT {25786743;33433884;33459926} are often negative in PanNECs. However, 18F-FDG PET/CT is usually positive {25786743}.

Epidemiology

PanNECs are uncommon, accounting for < 1% of all pancreatic neoplasms and no more than 2–3% of all pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNENs) in published case series {6268272;18508996;20702725;25723112}. Most of the reported patients are in their late fifties, with a slight male predilection (M:F ratio: 1.4:1) {22967994;24503751;25723112;26113608}.

Etiology

The genetic syndromes associated with PanNETs (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, von Hippel–Lindau syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, etc.) are not associated with PanNECs.

Pathogenesis

Genetically PanNECs are characterized by mutations in TP53, followed by RB1, which distinguish PanNECs from PanNETs. Additionally, PanNECs may exhibit, to a lesser extent, mutations in KRAS, BRAF, APC, SMAD4, and other genes {22251937;24292676;27482646;28059098;29596894;33433884;35294740;36788102}. KRAS mutations, which play a driver role in PDAC, have been observed in approximately 40–50% of PanNECs, and their presence supports a pathogenetic relationship between PanNECs and PDAC {29596894;36788102}. The genetic alterations commonly associated with PanNETs, such as those in MEN1, DNA damage repair genes (e.g. MUTYH, CHEK2, BRCA2), chromatin-remodelling genes (e.g. ARID1A, SMARCA4), genes associated with telomere alteration (e.g. DAXX/ATRX), PI3K/mTOR signalling, PTEN, and HIF2A/EPAS1 as well as EWSR1 fusions are not features of PanNECs {22251937;28199314;33459926} (see also section Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction).

A recent study reported that up to one third of G3 PanNETs harbour TP53 alterations {36788102}. In addition, in a small number of patients with G3 PanNETs, transitions to LCNEC-like changes were observed in liver metastases after long-term multimodal treatment. These changes were found to be associated with TP53 (and occasionally RB1) mutations and a significant increase in Ki-67 proliferation index during metastatic progression. However, despite the NEC-like histology, the tumours retained the gene alterations characteristic for PanNETs, suggesting that they still genetically differ from true PanNECs {39360347;39382626;39556303;39666243}.

Macroscopic appearance

PanNECs present as large (mean size: 40 mm, range: 20–180 mm), relatively circumscribed, solid tumours that are typically fleshier than PDAC. They tend to be white-grey or yellow in appearance and often reveal vague nodularity. Haemorrhage is common, and necrosis may also be seen {24503751}.

Histopathology

PanNECs display the classic poorly differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. The histology of PanNECs is like that of their counterparts in other organs with large cell and small cell subtypes {20664477;24430597;24503751;25723112}. The large cell PanNEC (LCNEC) is more common (60%) and is characterized by relatively uniform round to polygonal cells with amphophilic cytoplasm, large nuclei, vesicular chromatin, and typically prominent nucleoli. Diffuse large irregular cell nests, with trabeculae and peripheral palisading, are frequently intermingled in various proportions {24503751;25723112;32298138}. Small cell PanNECs (SCNEC) exhibit diffuse sheets of small to intermediate-sized cells with nuclei containing finely granular chromatin, high N:C ratios, inconspicuous nucleoli, prominent nuclear moulding, and crush artefacts. In both subtypes, mitotic activity is brisk (often > 20 mitoses/2 mm2) and extensive tumour necrosis is present. The Ki-67 proliferation index is by definition > 20% and commonly ranging 60–80%, particularly in SCNEC {22967994;24503751;25723112}. The Ki-67 index tends to be lower in LCNEC.

PanNECs can be associated with a non-NEC component {24503751} (see section Pancreatic mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms).

Immunohistochemistry

PanNECs are cytokeratin-positive and are characterized by neuroendocrine differentiation {20664477;24430597;24503751;25723112;27529763;33999752} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. General neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1) are expressed in the tumour {35294740}. Synaptophysin is the most sensitive marker. In contrast, chromogranin A, although highly specific, has a relatively low sensitivity, as its expression can be limited to few cells or be weak in most, especially in SCNEC, where the cytoplasm contains few secretory granules {9865821}. Typically, chromogranin A exhibits a dot-like staining pattern {24503751;27529763}. INSM1 may be expressed in neoplasms with variable synaptophysin and/or focal chromogranin A expression {28849340;31786484;31857137;32813226;33786701}. Labelling for nonspecific biomarkers (CD56, CD57, NSE) does not constitute diagnostic evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation and is discouraged. Abnormal immunohistochemical expression of p53 and/or RB1 suggests PanNEC rather than PanNET {34499237;36788102;37345605}.

Differential diagnosis

PanNEC can be separated from PanNET G3 by applying the practical algorithm entailing the use of immunohistochemistry for p53, RB1, DAXX, and ATRX {27259015}. SSTR2 immunohistochemistry as well as Ki-67 proliferation index may also be of help {27259015;28059098;29451738}. An important mimic of PanNEC, particularly LCNEC, is acinar cell carcinoma (or mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma), due to its monotonous histology, high proliferative rate, and common expression of neuroendocrine markers (at least focally). Therefore, the diagnosis of large cell PanNEC should not be made without immunohistochemical evaluation for acinar differentiation markers (e.g. trypsin, chymotrypsin, BCL10) {33786701}. In one study, 15% of cases originally classified as PanNEC were reclassified as mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas upon further analysis {24503751}. Metastatic NEC to the pancreas should also be excluded and requires clinical correlation {15057558}. Immunohistochemical analysis may not be helpful in this context, as PanNECs commonly express organ-specific transcription factors, such as thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), CDX2, insulin gene enhancer protein ISL1, or PDX1 {22046491;23503646}. The crushed and moulded tumour cells of the small cell subtype may resemble lymphoma, and immunohistochemistry is critical for distinguishing them. Other small round cell tumours, such as the Ewing family of tumours, may also involve the pancreas and need to be distinguished {34350470}, and immunolabelling for CD99 can be positive {12170091;15166674}.

Cytology

The features of PanNEC are like those of other organs {26837492}. Smears of small cell carcinoma are hypercellular and show sheets of small cells with very high N:C ratios, poorly defined cytoplasm, hyperchromatic angulated and elongated nuclei with uniformly distributed fine salt-and-pepper chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, nuclear moulding, numerous mitotic figures, and apoptotic bodies. Nuclear crush artefacts and necrotic debris in the background are prominent, along with stripped nuclei and a variable number of intact single tumour cells. Large cell subtype demonstrates tumour necrosis, mitoses, and anisonucleosis, and may also have prominent nucleoli {26837492}. PanNECs are diagnosed as category 7 (malignant) using the WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology (WHO System) {38709670}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

PanNECs show abnormal p53 expression and/or complete loss of RB1 expression in 95% and 74% of cases, respectively, corresponding to intragenic mutated TP53 and RB1 genes. PanNECs lack mutations in DAXX, ATRX, and MEN1 genes {22251937;29596894}. Therefore, genomic profiling can be useful in distinguishing PanNECs from PanNETs in selected cases.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: small cell carcinoma or large cell carcinoma pattern; sheets or trabeculae of poorly differentiated cells; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: reduced/absent SSTR2 expression; loss of RB1 expression or abnormal p53 expression.

Staging

The pathological staging of PanNEC is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for pancreatic carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

PanNECs are highly aggressive malignant neoplasms {22967994;24430597;24503751;25723112}. Advanced stage at presentation is common {17383419;22967994;26113608}, and chemotherapy is often the primary therapeutic approach {20664477;27759713;33999752}. The aggressive behaviour of PanNECs also warrants consideration of adjuvant therapy after surgery, even for locoregional disease. Although definite data are lacking, platinum and etoposide are recommended {22967994;25723112;33999752}. However, even with resection and platinum-based therapy, the median survival time remains very short (< 1 year), with < 25% of patients surviving beyond 2 years {22967994;24503751;33565789;33999752}. Furthermore, PanNECs with other carcinoma components (e.g. ductal adenocarcinoma) appear to be similarly aggressive, emphasizing the importance of recognizing the PanNEC component in such cases.

There are no data to support the use of somatostatin analogue therapy in PanNECs. Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors is currently under investigation for extrapulmonary NECs {33999752}.
