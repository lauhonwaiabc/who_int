16.1.1.7 Serrated polyposis

Definition

Serrated polyposis is a condition of unknown etiology characterized by multiple serrated polyps in the colon and rectum and associated with an increased risk of colorectal carcinoma.

ICD-O coding

82201/0 Serrated polyposis

ICD-11 coding

2E92.40 Serrated polyposis syndrome

Related terminology

Not recommended: hyperplastic polyposis (a historical term used before the recognition of different histological subtypes of serrated polyps).

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Serrated polyposis affects the large intestine but not the upper GI tract or the small intestine {22490521}.

Clinical features

The clinical criteria for the diagnosis of serrated polyposis are presented in #44995Box 7.01. The first clinical presentation can be at the time of colorectal carcinoma diagnosis, during screening colonoscopy in symptomatic patients or in patients with a family history of colorectal cancer, or by population screening in asymptomatic patients. Faecal blood tests do not perform well in detecting serrated polyps, because serrated polyps are less likely to bleed than conventional adenomas. The phenotype of serrated polyposis is heterogeneous, representing a continuum between patients who barely meet the clinical definition and patients with high polyp burden and multiple large polyps fulfilling both criteria {26603485;28400194}. About 25% of patients present with a type 1 phenotype (i.e. fulfilling only clinical criterion 1), 45% present with a type 2 phenotype (fulfilling only clinical criterion 2), and 30% have both phenotypes {26264224;26603485}. The risk of colorectal cancer may also be increased in patients (and first-degree relatives) with multiple serrated polyps who fall short of the criteria for serrated polyposis syndrome {28400194}. The clinical phenotype in any given patient may evolve over time, depending on the findings from surveillance colonoscopies.

The median cumulative polyp number is most commonly 30–40, with a range as wide as 6–240 polyps and frequent pancolonic distribution {22490521;26264224;26603485;22510757}. Extracolonic manifestations have not been reported.

Multiple serrated polyps can also be a component of other well-defined genetic syndromes: MUTYH-associated polyposis {19013464} and hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome caused by a duplication upstream of GREM1 {22561515}. Although patients with PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome / Cowden syndrome and juvenile polyposis may in rare cases strictly fulfil the criteria of serrated polyposis, they almost always have other polyp types or extraintestinal features, and expert pathology review is extremely important in the diagnostic workup of these rare patients with polyposis for a correct clinicopathological classification.

Epidemiology

Serrated polyposis is now the most commonly diagnosed colorectal polyposis. This is largely because of the increasing awareness among endoscopists and pathologists about the role of serrated polyps in colorectal carcinogenesis. The highest reported prevalence in primary screening colonoscopies is 0.1% {26911398}. In faecal occult blood test–based screening cohorts, the rate of serrated polyposis ranged from 0.31% to 0.66% in initial colonoscopies {27741536;26126164}. The rate of serrated polyposis after follow-up colonoscopy was reported as 0.4–0.8% {26911398}. The true prevalence of serrated polyposis is probably even higher as it remains underdiagnosed by community endoscopists {37810899}.

Most patients are diagnosed at 50–60 years of age, but the age range at diagnosis is wide, with some patients diagnosed in early adulthood {22490521;26264224;26603485;22525305}. Both sexes are almost equally affected. About one third of patients with serrated polyposis have at least one first-degree relative with colorectal carcinoma; 5% have a first-degree relative with serrated polyposis {26264224;26603485}. The first-degree relatives of patients with serrated polyposis have 5 times the incidence of colorectal carcinoma as is seen in the general population {22525305;20584785}.

Etiology

Environmental factors associated with serrated polyposis include cigarette smoking and high body mass index {36435745}. Paradoxically, patients with a history of smoking have a lower risk of colorectal carcinoma {26603485;20661287}. Familial clustering of serrated polyposis has been described in rare cases, but the disease etiology is essentially unknown.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is largely unknown; however, involvement of the RNF43 gene at 17q22 suggests perturbation of the WNT signalling pathway {24512911;24308963}. Pathogenic germline (constitutional) variants in RNF43 have been reported in a few families, accounting for < 2% of patients with serrated polyposis {27582512;29330307;27329244;39546056}. However, the presence of RNF43 variants in families with no history of serrated polyps or colorectal cancer suggests the possibility of incomplete penetrance {39546056}. In one study, no germline mutations in any of the well-known polyposis genes were found in 65 patients with serrated polyposis {23805267}. Results from large-scale genetic studies have failed to identify high-penetrance candidate genes {31663907;36270769}.

In a systematic review and meta-analysis, BRAF mutation was found in 73% of serrated polyps and 0% of conventional adenomas, while KRAS mutation was found in 8% of serrated polyps and 3% of conventional adenomas {27499922}. In patients with serrated polyposis with colorectal carcinoma, approximately 50% have a BRAF mutation, 40% are MLH1-deficient, and < 5% have KRAS mutation {21641392;23211288}.

Macroscopic appearance

Multiple serrated polyps are seen, similar to the sporadic lesions associated with this entity.

Histopathology

The histopathological features are similar to those of the sporadic polyps associated with this entity. Any subtype of serrated lesions/polyps (hyperplastic polyps, sessile serrated lesions with or without dysplasia, traditional serrated adenomas, and unclassified serrated adenomas; for definitions and descriptions, see section Colorectal hyperplastic polyps, section Colorectal sessile serrated lesions, and section Colorectal traditional serrated adenoma) is included in the final count. The diagnosis may require more than one colonoscopy, and the polyp count is cumulative across multiple procedures.

In a series of 100 patients with serrated polyposis syndrome, histology review of the polyps using the 2010 WHO classification revealed that 45% of all lesions were hyperplastic polyps, 34% were sessile serrated lesions (including 7% with dysplasia), 17% were conventional adenomas, and 4% were traditional serrated adenomas {22510757}. In two large retrospective studies without central histology review, 25–30% of patients presented with at least one serrated polyp with dysplasia, 76% with at least one serrated polyp ≥ 10 mm, 75–80% with at least one conventional adenoma, and 35–45% with at least one advanced conventional adenoma {26264224;26603485}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Serrated polyps in the setting of serrated polyposis have molecular characteristics similar to those of sporadic polyps.

Referral to clinical genetic services is recommended to test all possible underlying genetic defects in patients with any undiagnosed polyposis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical criteria for the diagnosis of serrated polyposis.

Desirable: other syndromes associated with multiple serrated polyps should be excluded.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Once dysplastic, sessile serrated lesions may develop relatively rapidly into cancer {26475632}. The cancer risk differs depending on the phenotype, first clinical presentation, and polyp histology. In two large retrospective multicentre cohort studies, colorectal carcinoma was diagnosed in 16% and 29% of patients, most of them before or at the time of serrated polyposis diagnosis {26264224;26603485}. Rectosigmoid was the site of involvement in about 50% of patients, and synchronous or metachronous colorectal carcinomas occurred in 4% of patients. Reported risk factors for colorectal carcinoma included the fulfilment of both clinical criteria for serrated polyposis, more than two sessile serrated lesions proximal to splenic flexure, at least one serrated polyp with dysplasia, and at least one advanced conventional adenoma. Cases can be successfully managed by serial colonoscopies in tertiary centres with low rate of surgical referral {28253245;28450390}. In a prospective study of 41 patients under annual surveillance after clearing colonoscopy, polyps were detected in 80% of patients at each surveillance colonoscopy, but none developed colorectal carcinoma during the 5 years of follow-up {24657624}. In a large retrospective Spanish study, the cumulative incidence of colorectal carcinoma detected during surveillance was 3.1% and 6.4% at 3 years and 5 years, respectively {30068004}.

Criterion 1:

≥ 5 serrated lesions/polyps proximal to the rectum, all being ≥ 5 mm in size, with ≥ 2 being ≥ 10 mm in size

Criterion 2:

> 20 serrated lesions/polyps of any size distributed throughout the large bowel, with ≥ 5 being proximal to the rectum

Any histological subtype of serrated lesion/polyp (hyperplastic polyp, sessile serrated lesion without or with dysplasia, traditional serrated adenoma, and unclassified serrated adenoma) is included in the final polyp count. The polyp count is cumulative over multiple colonoscopies.
