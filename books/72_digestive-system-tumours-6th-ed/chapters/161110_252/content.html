16.1.1.10 PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PTEN)

Definition

PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in PTEN and characterized by diverse types of gastrointestinal polyps and a wide variety of cancers.

MIM numbering

601728 Phosphatase and tensin homolog; PTEN

158350 Cowden syndrome 1; CWS1

ICD-11 coding

Related terminology

Acceptable: Cowden disease/syndrome; multiple hamartoma syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba syndrome; Lhermitte–Duclos disease

Localization

Gastrointestinal polyps are present in virtually all individuals with PHTS, occurring throughout the GI tract with a predominance in the colorectum {20600018}. Diffuse oesophageal glycogenic acanthosis is present in > 80% of PHTS cases.

Clinical features

Macrocephaly, mucocutaneous lesions (facial trichilemmomas, acral keratoses, papillomatous papules, and mucosal lesions are considered pathognomonic), benign overgrowths of tissues (including gastrointestinal polyposis; see under Histopathology), and an increased cancer risk (especially breast, endometrial, thyroid) collectively characterize PHTS (see #57078Box 16.05 and #49778Table 16.18) {24136893}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is estimated to be approximately 1 in 200 000, although this is likely an underestimate, as many individuals with a PTEN germline pathogenic variant (GPV) go undiagnosed {10234502;32461654}. Overall, evidently increased risks and earlier onset of breast, endometrial, and thyroid cancer are observed in patients with PHTS; however, cancer risks have previously been overestimated likely because of ascertainment bias {33140411}. More recently, lower risks have been reported in a large European cohort study of adults with PHTS {36171661}. PHTS-related cancer risk is higher in female patients (cumulative risk by 60 years of age: 68.4–86.3%; median age at diagnosis: 43 years) than male patients (cumulative risk by 60 years of age: 16.4–20.8%; median age at diagnosis: 51 years), mainly due to significantly increased risks of breast and endometrial cancer. Both sexes appear to be at equally increased risk of thyroid cancer (cumulative risk by 60 years of age: 8.9–20.5%, median age at diagnosis: 42 years), which is strongly increased compared with the general population (standardized incidence ratio: 42.9, 95% CI: 23.4–72.0). Other cancers, such as colorectal cancer, renal cancer, and melanoma, have been reported in patients with PHTS, but their associated risks appear less elevated for either sex (cumulative risk by 60 years of age: < 10% for each cancer) in larger case series {36171661}.

Etiology

PHTS is an autosomal dominant disorder with age-related penetrance and variable expression. A germline pathogenic variant in PTEN (10q23.3) is found in about 85% of PHTS cases. Previous syndromic disorders such as Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba, Lhermitte–Duclos disease, and Cowden syndrome are all thought to be part of PHTS {15121767;39256443}, whereas for less well-defined conditions, such as Proteus-like syndrome, it is not clear that PTEN GPVs play a significant role {36270489;14518070;15372511}. Individualized risk calculation may aid in the assessment of a patient’s risk of carrying a germline PTEN pathogenic variant based on clinical features {21194675}. GPVs in succinate dehydrogenase have been found in about 5% of PTEN pathogenic variant–negative PHTS / PHTS-like cases and are associated with increased frequencies of breast, thyroid, and renal cancers beyond those conferred by pathogenic variants in PTEN {18678321}.

Pathogenesis

PTEN is a critical tumour suppressor gene, second only to TP53 in variant frequency across various cancers. PTEN’s multifaceted role in tumorigenesis, focusing on its canonical function in PI3K signalling and its emerging PI3K-independent activities, is summarized below.

PTEN and PI3K signalling

PTEN’s primary tumour-suppressive function lies in its ability to antagonize the PI3K pathway, a key regulator of cell growth, metabolism, and survival {10200246;22473468}. As a lipid phosphatase, PTEN dephosphorylates PIP3 to PIP2, directly opposing PI3K activity {29858604}. This action prevents the activation of AKT, a central effector of the PI3K pathway, thereby inhibiting downstream signalling cascades that promote cell proliferation and survival {18767981}.

The loss or pathogenic variant of PTEN leads to hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, a hallmark of many cancers {30999672}. This dysregulation results in increased cell proliferation, reduced apoptosis, and enhanced cellular metabolism, all of which contribute to tumorigenesis. Moreover, PTEN loss can synergize with other oncogenic events, such as RAS activation, to accelerate tumour formation and progression.

Beyond PI3K signalling

PTEN’s tumour-suppressive functions extend beyond its canonical lipid phosphatase activity regulating PI3K signalling, encompassing roles in protein dephosphorylation that affect cell migration, maintaining genomic stability through interactions with proteins like CENPC and RAD51, modulating nuclear events via p53 stabilization and chromatin remodelling, and influencing the tumour immune microenvironment {12620407;25199838;17218262}. These diverse functions highlight PTEN’s complexity in cancer prevention and suggest new avenues for targeted therapies in PTEN-deficient cancers.

Macroscopic appearance

As for sporadic counterparts, see the descriptions of the polyps within the relevant sections.

Histopathology

Gastrointestinal polyps and polypoid lesions in patients with PHTS exhibit a variety of histological types {20600018;31273317}. These are most commonly hamartomatous polyps in the colon with mixed components of juvenile-type polyps, ganglioneuromas, lymphoid aggregates, and lipomas. Conventional adenomas and serrated polyps have also been reported {26721304;31273317}. Many of the endoscopically resected polyps, in particular in patients undergoing surveillance, are small or lack full-blown features, making them challenging to classify histologically. As a result, they may be (mis)classified as near-normal or inflammatory polyps. The presence of a mixture of polyp types, particularly those including ganglioneuromas, can serve as a diagnostic clue for PHTS, as ganglioneuromas are rare in other syndromes and in the general population. Polyps typically range in size from 3 to 10 mm but can occasionally be larger.

Most individuals with PHTS develop gastric polyps {21437855;23238744}, which can be numerous, measuring between 1 and 20 mm and can show a variety of juvenile or juvenile-like features, although definitive classification can be challenging in smaller lesions. Importantly, dysplasia has not been reported in gastric polyps associated with PHTS. While cases of gastric cancer in individuals with PHTS have been documented, an increased risk of gastric cancer has not been substantiated {20600018;22252256;26721304}.

Diffuse oesophageal glycogenic acanthosis, in conjunction with colonic polyposis, may serve as a diagnostic indicator of PHTS {21437855;23238744}.

No distinctive histopathological characteristics have been identified in the various cancer subtypes associated with PHTS {38861330}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Patients with PHTS all have GPVs in PTEN.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria are listed in #57078Box 16.05. See summary in #59819Table 16.19.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Individuals with PHTS are at increased risk of several types of cancer, and interval screening is recommended (see #49778Table 16.18) {22252256;23613428;23335809;37093598;36171661}. It remains uncertain whether the prognosis for individuals with PHTS who develop cancer differs from that for the general population.
