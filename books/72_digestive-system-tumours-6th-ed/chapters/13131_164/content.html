13.1.3.1 Smooth muscle tumours

Definition

Leiomyomas, leiomyosarcomas, and EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours are neoplasms showing pure smooth muscle differentiation with varying malignant potential. The latter usually occur in the setting of immunosuppression.

Related terminology

Not recommended: AIDS-associated EBV-positive smooth muscle tumour; EBV-associated posttransplant smooth muscle tumour; smooth muscle tumour in immunocompromised patient.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Leiomyomas predominantly occur in the oesophagus and colorectum; they rarely arise in the stomach and small intestine. In the colon, leiomyomas are typically small, arise from the muscularis mucosae, and usually involve the sigmoid colon and rectum. In the oesophagus, they are more deeply located, larger, and most often arise in the oesophagogastric junction or the mid oesophagus {15797036}.

Leiomyosarcoma of the GI tract most often arises in the colon (more often right side) followed closely by the small intestine, accounting for ≥ 80% of cases. Origin in the stomach and oesophagus is less common {11023095;23763337;26786155}.

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours can arise at any anatomical location; but within the GI tract, the liver is the most common site (often in liver transplant recipients) followed by the small intestine and colon {25027306}. Gallbladder examples are known {16330945;22420456}.

Clinical features

Leiomyomas of the oesophagus typically present with dysphagia. Virtually all colorectal leiomyomas are subcentimetre polypoid lesions and are therefore asymptomatic and discovered incidentally at endoscopy or surgery. Smaller (< 7 mm) incidentally recognized leiomyomas have been reported in oesophagogastric resections (seedling leiomyomas) {7319487}. Oesophageal leiomyomatosis is regarded as a hamartomatous disorder in children, but case reports in adults have been published {12784310;18299739}.

For leiomyosarcoma and EBV-associated smooth muscle tumour, presenting symptoms are usually secondary to a mass lesion and depend on the anatomical site of involvement, and therefore include upper or lower gastrointestinal bleeding, dysphagia, pain, or obstruction {11023095}.

Epidemiology

The overall prevalence is unknown, with case reports describing that approximately 10% of all leiomyomas of the GI tract occur in the oesophagus {12717247;15797036;18059218}, and leiomyoma, although relatively rare, is the most common benign mesenchymal tumour of the oesophagus. In the oesophagus, leiomyomas are most common in young adults, with a median patient age of 35 years. A slight male predilection is described {10680889}. Patients with colorectal leiomyomas are usually older, with a median age of 62 years, and there is a marked male predominance {11598163}.

Leiomyosarcomas of the GI tract are extremely rare. Patients are usually middle-aged to elderly adults, with a median age of 60 years. No apparent sex predilection is seen in reported series, at least for small intestine and colorectal cases {11023095}. There are some studies that suggest that oesophageal and gastric cases are more common in male patients and arise at a younger age {26786155}.

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours are very rare and occur over a wide age range (1–66 years) and with a slight female predominance being described. Most cases occur in one of three settings: immunodeficiency due to HIV/AIDS, immunodeficiency after transplantation of a solid organ or haematopoietic stem cells, and (least commonly) congenital or primary immunodeficiency {39136236;35949429}.

Etiology

The causation of somatic leiomyomas is unknown. Some cases of leiomyomatosis may be familial, associated with Alport syndrome and deletions of the COL4A5 and COL4A6 genes {12784310}.

Predisposing factors for leiomyosarcoma include Li–Fraumeni syndrome, hereditary retinoblastoma, and radiation exposure {12610106;21837677;29165710;29220301}, but the etiology in most cases is unknown.

The etiology of EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours involves EBV infection in the setting of T-lymphocyte immunosuppression.

Pathogenesis

Leiomyosarcoma typically displays extensive genomic instability, including chromothripsis and whole-genome duplication in subsets of cases {29321523}. Recurrent DNA copy-number alterations include losses involving tumour suppressors PTEN (10q23.31), RB1 (13q14.2), and TP53 (17p13.1). Gene expression studies suggest that there are multiple subgroups of leiomyosarcoma {25896974}.

MYC overexpression and AKT/mTOR pathway activation appear to be the main mediators of EBV-induced smooth muscle tumours {19706821;23682851}.

Macroscopic appearance

Leiomyomas are well circumscribed and have a firm, white, often lobulated, cut surface. In the oesophagus, the median tumour size is 50 mm (range: < 10 to 180 mm) {10680889}. In the colon and rectum, leiomyomas are significantly smaller, with a median size of 4 mm, and a range of 1–20 mm {11598163}.

Leiomyosarcomas are usually relatively large, ranging from 30 to 190 mm, with an average size of 60 mm. They may present as polypoid intraluminal tumours or as ulcerating, sessile, solid, or cystic masses {26786155;18971781;11688571;11023095}. They are predominantly centred in the muscularis propria. Extension through serosa and into mucosa may occur. The cut surface is variably tan/white and firm, and necrosis may be present.

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours range from subcentimetre to > 200 mm {25027306;22420456}, with white to grey cut surfaces, a firm to rubbery consistency, and well-circumscribed to infiltrative borders.

Histopathology

Leiomyomas are composed of bundles or fascicles of spindled cells with brightly eosinophilic cytoplasm and blunt-ended, cigar-shaped nuclei with fine chromatin. There is no significant cytological atypia, and mitotic activity is absent or low; the tumours usually resemble normal smooth muscle. Tumour necrosis is not seen. Oesophageal tumours may show nuclear atypia, and without any other worrisome histological features that are considered analogous to so-called symplastic leiomyoma, lacking potential for an adverse outcome. Scattered admixed eosinophils and mast cells are frequently present. By immunohistochemistry, tumour cells are typically positive for desmin, SMA, and caldesmon. Care must be taken to avoid overinterpretation of KIT positivity in admixed interstitial cells of Cajal and/or mast cells to avoid an erroneous diagnosis of gastrointestinal stromal tumour, which is extremely rare in the oesophagus. The tumour cells are negative for KIT (CD117), DOG1, and CD34.

Leiomyosarcomas are composed of fascicles of spindle cells showing varying degrees of smooth muscle differentiation, with brightly eosinophilic cytoplasm and blunt-ended nuclei. In contrast to leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma shows varying degrees of nuclear atypia (hyperchromatic enlarged nuclei), cytological pleomorphism, high cellularity, tumour necrosis, and high mitotic activity {26786155;18971781}. Ulceration of overlying mucosa is common. The tumour cells of leiomyosarcoma show expression of SMA, usually in a multifocal or diffuse pattern. Desmin expression is variable and present in approximately 80% of cases. Caldesmon shows variable expression in leiomyosarcoma, being present in ≥ 50% of cases {23763337}. CD34, KIT, DOG1, and S100 protein are usually negative, but focal CD34 expression is occasionally present {11688571;11023095}. Keratin expression is found in 30–40% of leiomyosarcomas {10692172}.

EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours can show a range of histological appearances, from lesions mimicking leiomyomas to tumours composed entirely of primitive small round to ovoid cells. In most cases, there is a mix of both conventional fascicular spindled cells with bright eosinophilic cytoplasm typical of smooth muscle differentiation, along with an admixture of rounded more primitive-appearing smooth muscle cells. Cytological atypia is usually mild to moderate but can be marked, especially in patients who are HIV-positive, whose tumours also show higher mitotic activity and/or necrosis. An intratumoural T-lymphocytic infiltrate is common and usually sparse but can be pronounced {16330945}. The tumour cells express SMA and caldesmon and show variable expression of desmin. All show diffuse, strong staining for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER), which is not seen in leiomyosarcoma. This diagnosis should be considered in immunosuppressed patients and patients with multiple synchronous or metachronous tumours {16330945}.

Cytology

Large mass-forming oesophageal leiomyomas may be sampled by EUS-guided FNA. Fragments of spindle cells with bland cytology are noted in adequate samples. Confirmatory immunohistochemical staining can be performed on cell block material {18208801}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

For EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours, demonstration of EBV is usually by in situ hybridization for EBER.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • For leiomyoma: fascicles of spindle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm; lack of conspicuous mitotic activity and necrosis
  • For leiomyosarcoma: cellular tumours with long intersecting fascicles of spindle cells; pleomorphic, enlarged nuclei; increased mitoses, sometimes with tumour necrosis
  • For EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours: neoplasm with smooth muscle differentiation; positivity for EBER transcripts by in situ hybridization

Desirable:

  • Expression of SMA, desmin, and/or h-caldesmon; negativity for KIT (CD117) and DOG1
  • For EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours: clinical history of immunosuppression

Staging

Staging is not clinically relevant for leiomyoma or EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours.

Leiomyosarcoma should be staged using the sarcoma staging information from the latest edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification.

Prognosis and prediction

Leiomyomas are benign tumours, and excision is curative.

Due to the rarity of primary leiomyosarcoma of the GI tract, data regarding prognosis are limited, but most patients have an aggressive clinical course, with death from disease occurring in the majority of patients {10680889;11023095}. However, accurate prognostication on the basis of histological features such as mitotic count and degree of atypia alone have not correlated well {23763337;18971781;26786155}. Metastatic disease usually involves liver, lungs, and peritoneal cavity {32051556}.

A recent large study attempted to risk stratify patients with smooth muscle tumours of the GI tract (other than leiomyomas of the muscularis mucosae). The findings showed that non-oesophageal gastrointestinal smooth muscle tumours measuring > 100 mm and/or showing ≥ 3 mitoses/5 mm2 may behave aggressively {32051556}.

The prognosis for EBV-associated smooth muscle tumours is mainly dependent on the condition of the individual patient’s immune system. Most of these tumours do not metastasize. Some posttransplant cases respond to reduced immunosuppression.
