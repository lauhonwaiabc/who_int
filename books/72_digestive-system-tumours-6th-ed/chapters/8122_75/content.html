8.1.2.2 Anal adenocarcinoma

Definition

Anal adenocarcinoma (AA) is an invasive neoplasm of the glandular epithelium of the anal canal.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C00.0 Adenocarcinoma of anus or anal canal

Related terminology

Primary perianal adenocarcinoma

Acceptable: non–anal gland adenocarcinoma; non–fistula-associated adenocarcinoma.

Localization

AA arises in the anal canal, including the perianal/ischiorectal region. Two types can be distinguished: mucosal and extramucosal. The former arises in the luminal mucosa and may be of intestinal or transitional type; the latter may be associated with anal glands, a pre-existing anal fistula, or other glandular structures of the anal canal (congenital or acquired malformations or embryological remnants).

Clinical features

The mucosal type typically shares clinical features with distal rectal adenocarcinoma and anal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). To date, our understanding of the characteristics of extramucosal-type tumours is more limited. According to the reported cases of pathologically confirmed anal gland (also termed “anal duct”) adenocarcinoma, patients typically present with bleeding or an anorectal lesion {21820151;39308041}.

Most fistula-associated adenocarcinomas develop in the setting of Crohn disease {19826882;21240063;23393368}. Fistula-associated adenocarcinomas unrelated to Crohn disease occur almost exclusively in men {19205706;16969607}. In most cases of fistula-associated carcinomas, the duration of the fistula before the diagnosis of carcinoma is ≥ 10 years. New-onset mucous discharge from the fistula is a common symptom. A high index of suspicion is key to early detection of this malignancy.

Clinical data on primary perianal adenocarcinoma are scarce, with available information limited to case reports {29464744}describing occurrences in men and women in their sixth decade of life.

The endoscopic and radiological investigation of AA is similar to that of anal SCC (see section Anal squamous cell carcinoma).

Epidemiology

Primary AA is a rare entity, and some studies have reported that its occurrence is increasing together with that of the rest of anal cancer cases. In 2025, 10 930 new cases of anal cancer were projected to occur in the USA alone [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program (Internet). Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2020. Cancer Stat Facts: anal cancer. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/anus.html. Bookref]], and it is estimated that adenocarcinomas account for 5–10% of all anal cancers; most are mucosal {38583905;26362848}. Based on SEER data obtained from 2004 to 2019, 48% of patients with AA were women, and the median age was 67 years {38583905}.

Etiology

Risk factors include smoking, HIV infection, and anoreceptive intercourse. Predisposing lesions include anal Crohn disease and chronic anal fistula (accounting for 3–11% of all anal canal malignancies) {22379406}. HPV infection has been implicated in a subset of anal canal adenocarcinomas {1850518;19189402;29700411;31857682}, primarily in tumours arising from the anal transitional zone {29700411;31857682}. The most commonly infecting genotype is HPV16, followed (distantly) by HPV18 {29700411}.

Pathogenesis

While mucosal-type adenocarcinoma may share carcinogenesis pathways with rectal carcinoma, recent data indicate that high-risk HPV is a driver in a subset of neoplasms {31857682;39308041}. Fistula-associated carcinomas in the setting of Crohn disease are probably triggered by specific cytokines and transcriptional factors upregulated in an inflammatory microenvironment (see section Inflammatory bowel disease–associated dysplasia of the colorectum and section Colorectal carcinoma).

Molecular data are limited, and microsatellite instability is very rare. One study of AA identified variants frequently in KRAS (47% of cases) but less commonly in PIK3CA (7.6%), NRAS (5.9%), and BRAF (1.5%) {29700411}.

Macroscopic appearance

AAs may present as mucosal or submucosal lesions, sometimes associated with fistulas. Most are macroscopically indistinguishable from rectal adenocarcinomas.

Anal gland adenocarcinomas have their origin in the anal gland or duct and form firm, infiltrative masses, typically centred in the wall of the anorectal area, 20–50 mm in greatest dimension {17683193}.

Histopathology

Mucosal-type AAs arise within the luminal mucosa. While the great majority of intestinal-type neoplasms are unassociated with HPV, approximately 40% of tumours with non-intestinal phenotypes (those resembling anal transitional zone or cervical neoplasms) may be HPV-driven {29700411}. Thus far, two distinct histological patterns have been documented in HPV-driven examples. Some cases exhibit features similar to cervical stratified mucin-producing carcinoma {39308041}. These include infiltrative nests of cells with varying levels of intracytoplasmic mucin, peripheral nuclear palisading, abundant apoptosis, and neutrophilic infiltrates. These cells typically express CK7 and p16 but lack CK20 and p40 expression. Other cases resemble usual-type high-risk HPV-related endocervical adenocarcinoma {31857682}, characterized by papillary fronds of mucinous or mucin-poor cells or a hybrid of both. These feature glandular cells with overlapping, cigar-shaped to ovoid irregular nuclei, abundant mitoses, and apically arranged apoptotic bodies. This pattern often shows expression of CK7 and p16, variable CDX2, and minimal CK20 immunoreactivity. Both patterns demonstrate strong reactivity to high-risk HPV in situ hybridization. Adenosquamous carcinoma of the anus is exceedingly rare, and there are little to no data on this type of tumour at this site. Available data suggest behaviour similar to or worse than that of colorectal adenocarcinoma {10211505}.

Extramucosal-type anal canal adenocarcinomas, by definition, lack a luminal in situ component, although they may involve the surface mucosa by erosion, colonization, or pagetoid extension. Based on their relatedness to either an anal gland–like immunophenotype or anal fistula, these tumours are currently classified into three subtypes: anal gland adenocarcinoma; fistula-associated adenocarcinoma; and primary perianal adenocarcinoma. Some fistula-associated carcinomas may have an anal gland–like immunophenotype.

Anal gland adenocarcinomas have their origin in the anal gland or duct and are HPV-independent {21820151}. The histological spectrum of this subtype remains to be sharply defined. Typical cases exhibit haphazardly dispersed glands with cuboidal cells and scant mucin {11596018}. Unusual subtypes may manifest tumour cell stratification, solid growth, or prominent mucin production. The tumour cells characteristically express CK7 and MUC5AC and lack CK20 and CDX2 {11596018;21820151}. CK5/6 and p63, markers constitutively positive in benign anal glands, are negative in anal gland carcinomas {17683193;18834205;22707467}. The main differential diagnoses include direct spread from the genital tract (both female and male) and breast metastases.

Fistula-associated adenocarcinomas may be histogenetically related to anal gland epithelium {7338481;7881949} or to transitional-type or rectal-type epithelium (via congenital or acquired mucosal duplication or malformations) {7881949;6327491}. The majority of these tumours are mucinous, grossly and morphologically resembling ordinary colorectal-type mucinous adenocarcinoma. An adenoma or adenoma-like component may be seen. Non-mucinous subtypes have been reported (as has SCC) {19826882}. The available immunohistochemical studies support the presumed variability in histogenesis, with some cases showing an intestinal phenotype (positive for CK20 and MUC2) {21240063;36757514} and others exhibiting an anal gland–like mucin composition (no immunoreactivity for O-acetyl sialic acid groups) that is distinct from that of rectal epithelium {7338481}. Diagnosis hinges on the presence of a pre-existing, often longstanding fistula and the exclusion of subsequent involvement by a carcinoma from elsewhere, particularly the rectum or colon.

Primary perianal adenocarcinomas may arise in association with developmental cysts and have a phenotype akin to that of colorectal carcinomas.

All types of anal canal adenocarcinoma may show pagetoid spread to anal squamous mucosa or perianal skin, resulting in secondary anal Paget disease. Rarely, adenomas (without documented invasive disease) can give rise to secondary anal Paget disease {28058078;32705713}. The secondary Paget cells may be contiguous with the underlying neoplasm or manifest at sites distinctly away from it (with skip lesions). In some cases, the secondary anal Paget disease presents months to years before or after the underlying adenocarcinoma becomes clinically detectable. It has been observed that Paget disease subsequent to rectal-type adenocarcinoma tends to express not only CK20 but also CK7 in both the Paget cells and the associated adenocarcinoma {9500217}. Unlike secondary anal Paget disease, primary anal Paget disease is believed to originate in the perianal skin and constitutes an intraepithelial carcinoma with skin appendage differentiation. Typically presenting as erythematous eczematous plaque lesions, primary anal Paget disease can extend to the anal canal mucosa. It can also become invasive and involve the perianal or ischiorectal region, mimicking primary anal canal adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry can facilitate the distinction: primary Paget disease cells typically coexpress CK7 and GCDFP-15 but not CK20 or CDX2 {9500217;11473461}.

The main characteristics of the various primary and secondary anal canal adenocarcinomas are summarized in #49452Table 8.03. Various other neoplasms associated with anogenital mammary-like glands and skin appendage neoplasms can be encountered; hidradenoma papilliferum, encountered nearly exclusively in women, is the most common {28291131}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an invasive neoplasm of glandular origin arising from the anal canal.

Staging

AAs are currently staged according to the TNM classification for anal cancer, which is primarily designed for SCC and on the basis of tumour size. However, analysis of SEER data has not shown TNM tumour sizing to have prognostic value for AA {31361759}. Further, a recent study indicated that staging AA on the basis of depth of invasion (as for rectal adenocarcinoma) better discriminates between T stages to reflect patient survival {38583905}. The 2023 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines propose that AA is managed as for rectal adenocarcinoma {37308125} but do not specify the optimal staging system for AA.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for anal canal adenocarcinoma is generally worse than that of anal SCC {9264365;17492635;36757514}. From analyses of SEER data on AA, younger age, mucinous features, and localized disease predict for better outcome, but it is controversial whether tumour grade has prognostic value {31361759;38934176;32455011}. There are insufficient data to comment on the impact of HPV on outcomes.
