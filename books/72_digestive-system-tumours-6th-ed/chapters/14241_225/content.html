14.2.4.1 Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction

Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNENs), like other neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs), express synaptophysin, INSM1, and usually chromogranin A. They include small (< 5 mm) well-differentiated NENs, designated as pancreatic neuroendocrine microtumours; malignant well-differentiated NENs, designated as pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), which can be low-grade (G1), intermediate-grade (G2), or high-grade (G3); and poorly differentiated high-grade NENs, designated as pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (PanNECs). Neoplasms with significant (≥ 30%) components of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation are conceptually categorized as mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs).

Clinical features

PanNETs are generally slow-growing, with a median overall survival time of 60–70 months, and overall survival rates of 60–80% at 3 years and 50–75% at 5 years {28448665;34554237;35348774;30300897}. The 5-year survival rate for patients with localized disease is 80–90%, for patients with regional disease it is 60–70%, and for patients with distant spread it is 30% {34554237;35348774;30300897}. Surgical resection is associated with markedly improved survival rates (see section Non-functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours). In contrast, patients with fast-growing PanNECs rarely survive 1 year {35174558;35616186}. PanNETs are divided into functioning and non-functioning neoplasms. Tumours associated with clinical syndromes caused by abnormal secretion of hormones by the neoplastic cells are considered functioning (syndromic) PanNETs and include gastrinomas, glucagonomas, insulinomas, and VIPomas, along with other less common tumours producing ACTH, CCK, GHRH, PTHrP, and serotonin. Non-functioning (non-syndromic) tumours are not associated with a clinical hormone secretion syndrome, but they may secrete peptide hormones and biogenic substances (such as PP, somatostatin, and chromogranins), which either are secreted at levels insufficient to cause symptoms or by themselves do not give rise to a clinical syndrome. Non-functioning tumours are incidentally discovered or become clinically symptomatic because of their large size, invasion of adjacent organs, or metastasis. In the past, functioning PanNETs accounted for 60–85% of all PanNENs, with insulinomas being the most frequent type (accounting for as many as 70% of cases), followed by gastrinomas, glucagonomas, VIPomas, and other functioning PanNETs (e.g. PanNETs producing serotonin, ACTH, GHRH, PTHrP, and CCK {22442262;24905065;23514309}). Recent data show that non-functioning PanNETs outnumber functioning neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) and now account for > 60% of all PanNENs, a change likely reflecting enhanced detection of non-functioning PanNETs with advanced imaging modalities {39243580}. Tumours < 5 mm in greatest dimension (the minimum size for detection by imaging) are typically non-functioning and are designated as neuroendocrine microtumours.

Epidemiology

PanNETs account for 2–5% of all pancreatic tumours, with an age-adjusted incidence of < 1 case per 100 000 person-years {34554237;35348774}. Autopsy studies that include tumours measuring ≤ 5 mm give a higher prevalence {2070707} (see section Non-functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours). PanNETs show no substantial sex difference and occur over a wide age range, with the highest incidence in patients aged 30–60 years. The incidence of PanNETs has steadily increased over the past 40 years, probably because of advances in imaging {28448665;39243580;34554237;35348774}. Risk factors include a family history of NETs, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, and diabetes mellitus {36630937}. With the increased use of germline sequencing, a deleterious germline variant in a familial cancer gene such as MEN1, VHL, TSC2, MUTYH, CHEK2, and BRCA2 is found in 16–21% of patients with an apparently sporadic PanNET, suggesting a role for routine germline assessment in all patients {28199314;30687805;38662083}. PanNECs are very rare, accounting for < 1% of pancreatic neoplasms {39243580}. Their demographic and risk features parallel those of conventional pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (see section Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma).

WHO classification

The division of PanNENs into two distinct groups, well-differentiated PanNETs and poorly differentiated PanNECs, on the basis of differences in morphology, somatic mutations, responses to therapy, and patient survival is now well accepted {35174558;34499237;35616186;35683561;24503751} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. Morphologically, as the name suggests, PanNETs are well-differentiated epithelial neoplasms composed of cells that histologically resemble the non-neoplastic cells of the islets of Langerhans. Neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), by contrast, are poorly differentiated epithelial neoplasms that resemble small cell or large cell NECs of other organs.

As is true for NETs throughout the gastroenteropancreatic system, many studies support the grading of PanNETs on the basis of proliferation rate as measured by counting mitotic figures and determining the Ki-67 labelling index {22525418;16967267}. G1 PanNETs have a Ki-67 labelling index of < 3% and < 2 mitoses/2 mm2, G2 PanNETs have a Ki-67 labelling index of 3–20% or 2–20 mitoses/2 mm2, and G3 PanNETs have a Ki-67 labelling index of > 20% or > 20 mitoses/2 mm2 {22525418;16967267}. Studies of large patient cohorts have been used to suggest refinements to this grading system, such as the division of G2 tumours into G2a, with a Ki-67 index of 3% to < 10%, and G2b, with a Ki-67 index of 10–20% {38955993;22525418;30300897}. However, the evidence of differences in clinical management based on these additional cut-off points is insufficient to justify modifying the pre-existing grading parameters. Similarly, tumour necrosis has been reported in some studies to be an adverse prognostic factor, but there is currently insufficient evidence to include necrosis in grading schemes {35934531}. PanNETs can undergo progression from lower grade (G1 or G2) to higher grade (G2 or G3) over time, with the higher-grade component being found focally within the primary neoplasm or upon disease progression at a metastatic site {25723112;26482044;27259015;31658459}. In most cases, the higher-grade component retains a well-differentiated appearance along with the mutations characteristic of well-differentiated PanNETs (e.g. MEN1, DAXX, or ATRX) {27259015;36788102;34499237}. However, in some rare instances, NEC-like histological features (diffuse growth, high-grade cytological atypia, necrosis) have been reported {39382626;39556303}. Usually, TP53 and RB1 mutations are absent in the higher-grade component, although some cases acquire TP53 mutations, and a few cases with RB1 mutations have been described {39382626;39556303}. However, although the possibility that well-differentiated PanNETs can transform into poorly differentiated PanNECs has been suggested, definitive cases have not yet been demonstrated [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]].

Before the 2017 WHO classification of NENs of the pancreas, the term used for mixed NENs was “mixed adenoneuroendocrine carcinoma” (MANEC). In 2017, the conceptual category of “mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm” (MiNEN) was introduced to encompass better all neoplasms with both neuroendocrine and another significant direction of differentiation [[Lloyd RV, Osamura RY, Klöppel G, et al., editors. WHO classification of tumours of endocrine organs. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (WHO classification of tumours series, 4th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/554. Bookref]]. There were two reasons for this change. First, although both components of these mixed neoplasms are usually poorly differentiated, the neuroendocrine component may occasionally be well differentiated; and second, they may contain a non-neuroendocrine component other than adenocarcinoma, such as an acinar cell carcinoma.

Hyperplastic and preneoplastic lesions occur in the settings of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), von Hippel–Lindau syndrome (VHL), familial insulinomatosis, and glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia and are described in the relevant sections {22461457;26365060;25695890;24718881;29339498}.

Definition of PanNET

PanNET is a well-differentiated NEN composed of cells with minimal to moderate atypia, displaying organoid patterns, and expressing general markers of neuroendocrine differentiation (diffuse/intense synaptophysin, INSM1, and usually also chromogranin A immunolabelling) and hormones (usually intense but not necessarily diffuse), either orthotopic or ectopic to the pancreas. Based on their proliferative activity, PanNETs, as described above, are graded as G1, G2, or G3). In cases with an associated clinical hormonal syndrome, PanNETs are further categorized as ACTH-producing tumour, gastrinoma, glucagonoma, insulinoma, serotonin-producing tumour (with or without carcinoid syndrome), somatostatinoma, VIPoma, etc. However, immunolabelling for hormones (with exception of serotonin-producing tumour) in the absence of a clinical syndrome does not warrant these functional diagnostic designations.

Definition of PanNEC

PanNEC is a poorly differentiated high-grade NEN, composed of markedly atypical small cells or intermediate to large cells expressing markers of neuroendocrine differentiation (diffuse/faint synaptophysin and INSM1 immunolabelling as well as faint/focal chromogranin A immunolabelling) and rarely hormones. PanNECs do not express markers of acinar differentiation such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, or carboxyl ester hydrolase (detected by antibodies to BCL10). PanNECs may also express ductal lineage markers such as MUC1 and CEA {29596894}. Based on their proliferative activity (> 20 mitoses/2 mm2 or a Ki-67 proliferation index > 20%), PanNECs are considered high-grade neoplasms, and usually their proliferative indices far exceed these thresholds.

Definition of MiNEN

MiNEN is a conceptual category intended to encompass a range of neoplasms with mixed differentiation. It is not intended to be a specific diagnostic term; neoplasms within the MiNEN category should be diagnosed on the basis of the specific components they contain. MiNEN has a significant neuroendocrine component as well as a significant non-neuroendocrine component (typically ductal adenocarcinoma). Both components are usually high-grade, but occasionally the neuroendocrine component may be a well-differentiated NET. Since the non-neuroendocrine component is invasive by definition, they fall within the category of carcinoma. The prognosis appears to be driven by the highest-grade component; therefore when the components are morphologically distinguishable, each component should be graded on the basis of each component’s grading system. For a tumour to qualify as MiNEN, each component should account for ≥ 30% of the tumour cell population. Non-neuroendocrine carcinomas with scattered neuroendocrine cells by immunohistochemistry do not fulfil this criterion; the presence of focal (< 30%) neuroendocrine differentiation can be mentioned (especially if the minor component is small cell NEC) but does not affect the diagnostic categorization. Pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma and pancreatoblastoma commonly demonstrate expression of neuroendocrine markers by immunohistochemistry but should not be considered as MiNENs unless there are morphologically distinctive areas of classic NEN that lack acinar cell differentiation. Cases without morphologically separate components fit the novel definition of amphicrine-like carcinoma when the neuroendocrine component is estimated to be ≥ 30% (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification). Diagnoses indicating each specific cellular component should be given (e.g. mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma, mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma, etc.) for cases in the MiNEN and amphicrine-like carcinoma categories (see section Pancreatic mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms).

Reporting of PanNENs

Type (PanNET, PanNEC, or specific components of a MiNEN [e.g. mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma, mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma, etc.]), stage, and grade should be reported for all PanNENs. Clinical function, if present, should also be noted. See the sections that follow for the tumour-type–specific requirements.

Genetic syndromes

Approximately 15–20% of patients with a PanNET harbour a deleterious germline variant that predisposes to a hereditary syndrome such as MEN1, VHL, neurofibromatosis type 1, tuberous sclerosis, glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia, and familial insulinomatosis {29339498;38662083;34036514;35612824;26365060;25695890;29849115}. PanNECs do not show these associations {26365060}. Deleterious germline variant in DNA repair genes (MUTYH, CHEK2, and BRCA2) and other hereditary cancer genes have been described in patients with apparently sporadic PanNENs {38662083}.

Molecular genetics

Somatic loss of heterozygosity is particularly prevalent in PanNETs {30687805;35125451}. The chromatin-remodelling gene MEN1 is somatically inactivated in approximately 40% of sporadic PanNETs {28199314;21252315;20566584;30687805}. This gene codes for menin, a tumour suppressor protein with diverse functions. Other chromatin-remodelling genes such as SETD2, ARID1A, and MLL3 are also inactivated in PanNETs, but at a significantly lower rate {28199314;30687805}. In addition, approximately 40% of PanNETs show mutation in either DAXX or ATRX {28199314;21252315}. The function of DAXX and ATRX proteins is to maintain chromatin remodelling at telomeric and pericentromeric regions. DAXX and ATRX mutations are strongly associated with the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres pathway for telomere maintenance, as well as with chromosomal instability {21719641}. About 15% of PanNETs have mutually exclusive mutations in genes that code for members of the mTOR pathway, such as TSC1, TSC2, and PTEN, and additional cases have activation of this pathway due to other types of genomic alterations {28199314;21252315;30687805}. These aberrations in the mTOR pathway are important because they are a potential therapeutic target. PanNETs may also harbour HIF1A and VHL alterations {28199314;21252315}. PanNETs may accumulate somatic alterations with tumour progression, and chemotherapy-associated mutagenesis has also been noted {30687805}. TP53 is wildtype in most PanNETs, although up to one third of G3 PanNETs harbour TP53 alterations {36788102}.

Deleterious germline variants affecting MEN1, VHL, NF1, GCGR, and MAFA are found in patients with MEN1 syndrome, VHL, neurofibromatosis type 1, glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia, and insulinomatosis, respectively {35577612;35612824;18063061;38706378;29849115;29339498;25695890}. Some PanNETs arising in these settings have distinctive morphologies. Mutations in VHL result in dysregulation of the HIF pathway, and PanNETs that arise in patients with VHL often, but not always, have clear cell morphology {11342771}. Mutations in GCGR alter glucagon receptor signalling and lead to glucagon cell hyperplasia and subsequent neoplasia, but the mechanism through which these alterations occur is not yet clear {25695890}. Missense mutations of the MAFA gene in familial insulinomatosis cause the development of familial multifocal trabecular insulin-producing PanNETs {29339498}.

The somatic mutations reported in PanNECs include mutations in TP53, RB1, and CDKN2A, whereas chromatin-remodelling genes such as MEN1, ATRX, and DAXX are typically not involved {34880079;35174558;28199314;21252315}. TP53 and RB1 are targeted in PanNECs. In addition, PanNECs may harbour somatic KRAS mutations identical to those that occur in conventional ductal adenocarcinomas, and other NECs have microsatellite instability {22251937;29596894;28455360;35174558}. PanNECs are highly aggressive neoplasms, and the treatment of PanNECs often includes etoposide and platinum-based chemotherapies {35980649;27759713;25275070}.

Staging (TNM)

The staging of PanNETs is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for NETs of the pancreas. These staging systems rely on the macroscopic assessment of tumour size, invasion into adjacent organs (stomach, spleen, colon, adrenal glands), and metastases. This approach largely corresponds to that of the European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) classification {22525418;30300897}. For NECs and MiNENs, the staging follows the criteria of pancreatic carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for PanNENs is strongly related to the distinction of PanNET versus PanNEC, with the NECs having a uniformly poor prognosis, whereas PanNETs range from small, low-grade cases that are very indolent to large or high-grade (G3) cases that are often quite aggressive {27759713}. The criteria that predict the risk of tumour progression of PanNETs are summarized in the TNM staging table. The listed criteria have a high probability and statistical significance (in particular, stage and grade {28448665;34554237;35348774;30300897}) for predicting the behaviour of a given PanNEN. Insulinomas, as well as cystic and duct-centric sclerotic PanNETs, are associated with an improved prognosis among the PanNETs, while some have suggested that the oncocytic NET has a poorer prognosis {27216830;23073325;35784630;20713615;32488621}.

Several studies have established that patients with a PanNET with DAXX or ATRX mutations have a worse clinical outcome than those with tumours that are wildtype DAXX and ATRX {30081149;33849943;39522643;34269919;35872157}. As noted above, loss of DAXX or ATRX is associated with ALT, and ALT can now be detected in clinical samples using an in situ hybridization approach {21719641;21888887;39522643}. Loss of expression of DAXX or ATRX can also be assessed by immunolabelling and can be helpful to distinguish G3 PanNETs from PanNECs, as the latter consistently retain expression of these proteins {27259015;30747827;36788102}.
