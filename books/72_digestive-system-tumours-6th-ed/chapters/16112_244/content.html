16.1.1.2 Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)

Definition

Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome (CMMRD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by germline (constitutional) biallelic pathogenic variants affecting one of the four mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2) and characterized by a broad spectrum of malignancies usually with childhood onset.

MIM numbering

276300 Mismatch repair cancer syndrome (MMRCS) 1–4

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mismatch repair cancer syndrome 1 (MLH1); mismatch repair cancer syndrome 2 (MSH2); mismatch repair cancer syndrome 3 (MSH6); mismatch repair cancer syndrome 4 (PMS2)

Localization

Cancers can arise throughout the body, including in colon, rectum, stomach, small bowel, and liver (hepatic adenoma) {24737826;33945292;26318770;29785566;37664053;38699071;39031223;39420201}. Multiple, mainly adenomatous, polyps can arise in the large bowel and also in small intestine and stomach {24737826;25307252;26729549;21376568;21182953}.

Clinical features

CMMRD predisposes individuals to a broad spectrum of malignancies with an age of onset usually from childhood until young adult age (0.5–38 years; mean age: < 10 years) {24737826;38552658;39420201}. These include predominantly malignant brain tumours, with high-grade gliomas being the most prevalent, haematological malignancies among which T-cell lymphoblastic lymphomas are the most common, colorectal and other Lynch syndrome–associated cancers, and also embryonal malignancies, sarcomas, and other cancers. Unlike in Lynch syndrome, individuals with CMMRD develop multiple colorectal and small intestinal adenomas, often with high-grade dysplasia {24737826;25307252;26729549;21376568;33945292} (see section Lynch syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)). Most patients with CMMRD also have neurofibromatosis type 1–like features (mainly café-au-lait macules) and/or other hyperpigmented and hypopigmented skin areas {9927033;9927034;18709565;27779754} and/or multiple developmental venous anomalies (also known as cerebral venous angiomas) in separate regions of the brain {30166433;38981890;32354708}. Other less frequent non-malignant features include non–therapy-induced cavernoma, agenesis of the corpus callosum, one or more pilomatrixomas, and paediatric systemic lupus erythematosus {24737826;23629955;22692065;31501241;30013564;21182953;22837778}.

A 3-point scoring system has been proposed for the suspected diagnosis of CMMRD in paediatric or young adult patients with cancer on the basis of the type of tumour and additional features, including non-malignant features, consanguinity of parents, and family history {24737826}. These Care for CMMRD criteria have been recently revised (#49394Box 16.01), and additional indication criteria have been formulated {39420201} (see Fig. #49393). Mainly brain tumours, but also other CMMRD-associated tumours, frequently have somatic POLE and POLD1 exonuclease domain proofreading mutations and are ultramutated tumours (tumour mutation burden: > 100 mutations/Mb) {25642631;27001570;29056344;33355208}. Hence, a tumour mutation burden that is unusually high for a paediatric tumour should raise the suspicion of CMMRD, in particular if the mutation signature indicates mismatch repair deficiency (Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer [COSMIC] signatures SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26, SBS44, ID1, ID2, or ID7). Furthermore, a paediatric tumour in a Lynch syndrome family, with a heterozygous germline pathogenic variant (GPV) in a mismatch repair (MMR) gene, or MMR protein expression loss in neoplastic and non-neoplastic cells as detectable by immunohistochemistry should raise the suspicion of CMMRD.

Epidemiology

Based on the prevalence of heterozygous MMR gene pathogenic variants {27799157}, the estimated birth incidence of CMMRD is 1 in a million in children of unrelated parents, and it may be as much as 110 times as high if the parents are consanguineous {30415209;32773772}. CMMRD is reported to account for 1.0–2.7% of adolescents and young adults (aged ≤ 25 years) with colorectal cancer {32585361;26195711}. The prevalence of CMMRD in paediatric high-grade gliomas may be up to 23% and even higher (39%) in countries where parental consanguinity is high {26293621}. Up to 9% of paediatric patients with T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma may have CMMRD {34964038}.

Etiology

CMMRD is an autosomal recessive syndrome caused by biallelic GPVs of one of the four MMR genes MSH2, MLH1, MSH6, or PMS2; this can be the result of either two different GPVs being present in trans (compound heterozygous) or the same pathogenic variant being present on both alleles (homozygous). In > 60% of cases, CMMRD is caused by germline biallelic PMS2 pathogenic variants, in > 20% by MSH6 biallelic pathogenic variants, and in < 20% by either MLH1 or MSH2 biallelic pathogenic variants {24737826;38552658}. CMMRD pathogenic variants are listed by the International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours (InSiGHT) database (https://www.insight-database.org) {33622763;39420201}. It appears that hypomorphic MMR variants may be considered pathogenic for CMMRD, but the risk for heterozygous carriers is not high enough for the variant to be considered pathogenic for Lynch syndrome {38789506}.

CMMRD is closely related to Lynch syndrome (see section Lynch syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)), and attenuated forms of CMMRD may clinically overlap with Lynch syndrome {38789506}. A CMMRD-like phenotype may result also from Polε proofreading deficiency due to a GPV in POLE {34816535}; digenic inheritance of a heterozygous GPV of PMS2 in combination with a heterozygous GPV either of POLE or of POLD1 may also result in a CMMRD-like phenotype {34967012;35260767;36291559;26659639}.

Pathogenesis

Biallelic GPVs affecting both alleles of an MMR gene result in loss of MMR function in all body cells of the affected individual. Like what happens in neoplastic mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) cells in Lynch syndrome, the loss of MMR capacity in all body cells in CMMRD leads to an overall increased mutation rate due to accumulation of unrepaired base mismatches and insertion/deletion mutations (indels). The latter occur particularly in microsatellite sequences (i.e. sequence motifs consisting of 1–5 nucleotides that are repeated 5–50 times or more) and alter the length of the microsatellites {25707793}, which is detectable as microsatellite instability (MSI), a long-known mutation signature specific for dMMR. The increased mutation rate renders the cells susceptible to tumour development as it increases the likelihood of mutations in tumour-associated genes. Additionally, dMMR also leads to abnormal regulation of apoptosis of genetically unstable cells, which confers a relative growth advantage that contributes to tumour formation {10097137;20102395}. Unlike Lynch syndrome where dMMR is restricted to neoplastic cells (and dMMR crypts) with a somatic mutation inactivating the wildtype MMR gene allele, the dMMR in CMMRD affects all body cells, and consequently malignancies develop much earlier and span a broader spectrum {25707793;24737826;39420201} (Fig. #49708). Tumours in CMMRD may not only be hypermutated with a mutation signature indicative of dMMR, they may also be ultramutated with mutation signatures indicative of combined Polε/Polδ proofreading inactivation and dMMR. The occurrence of POLE/POLD1 variants are presumed to be potential causes for why conventional MSI testing methods developed for Lynch syndrome may fail to reveal MSI in CMMRD-associated tumours (see Diagnostic molecular pathology).

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearances of intestinal adenomas and carcinomas are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

The colorectal polyps are mostly adenomas, often with high-grade dysplasia. The colorectal cancers in CMMRD are similar to those in Lynch syndrome with increased tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, Crohn-like peritumoural lymphocytic aggregates, and sometimes with poor differentiation or mucinous pattern with a pushing edge {11159189;29906313;18283560;23435936}. The histological findings of other CMMRD-associated carcinomas are generally similar to those in Lynch syndrome.

Cytology

The neoplasms are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts by cytology.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Definitive diagnosis of CMMRD requires the detection of two GPVs in an MMR gene and confirmation that the two pathogenic variants are in trans {33622763;39420201}. However, due to the limitations inherent to genetic testing, a significant subset of CMMRD cases cannot be definitively diagnosed via germline testing of the MMR genes alone, and ancillary tests become necessary. Several of these ancillary tests have been developed to assess germline dMMR in DNA from (non-neoplastic) blood leukocytes, mainly by testing for germline MSI {23483711;26116798;30740824;31494577;33355208;36586540;38531023}. Some of them have limitations {23483711}, but most of them are highly reliable and confirm or exclude CMMRD with (nearly) 100% sensitivity and specificity {26116798;30740824;31494577;33355208;36586540;38531023}. However, currently there is limited availability and approval of these tests outside specialized laboratories. Immunohistochemical staining of the MMR proteins has been proposed as an ancillary diagnostic test {33622763}. But due to the risk of false abnormal results, recent guidelines for the diagnosis of CMMRD no longer include immunohistochemistry as an ancillary test but suggest its use in any cancer suspected to be CMMRD associated {39420201}. To confirm CMMRD by immunohistochemistry, loss of MMR expression in all cells of the investigated tissue (neoplastic and non-neoplastic cells) should be observed (Fig. #49683). Consequently, there is no internal positive immunohistochemical staining control. Hence, it is important that appropriate internal quality control be performed by including sections from tissues of other patients of known status, and, ideally, the laboratory participates in external quality assurance. It is noteworthy that PMS2 may be expressed at very low levels in some tissues, which may lead to incorrect interpretation in tissues that are not related to CMMRD. On the other hand, weak MMR protein immunohistochemical staining can be observed in both the neoplastic and non-neoplastic epithelial cells (particularly proliferative zone epithelial cells) in some MSH6 and PMS2 CMMRD cases {33622763}. In such cases, the stromal cells tend to be totally devoid of staining if the case is truly CMMRD-related {39387980}. In the case of MSH6 CMMRD with GPVs involving the MSH6 exon 5 C8 coding microsatellite (e.g. NM_000179.3:c.3261dup), discrete MSH6 positively staining glands or crypt foci (on MSH6 immunohistochemistry) have been described in colonic adenomas and in non-neoplastic intestinal mucosa (Fig. #49392) and are shown to reflect a reversal of the c.3261dupC to the wildtype sequence, in keeping with the high plasticity of this coding microsatellite {38956208}. Awareness of these MMR-proficient crypt foci can help avoid misinterpreting MMR immunohistochemistry in this scenario {39387980}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: identification of two causative GPVs in trans (homozygous or compound heterozygous) affecting one of the four relevant MMR genes (MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, MLH1) confirms a diagnosis of CMMRD; in cases with an inconclusive result, a validated ancillary test assessing germline dMMR can be used to confirm CMMRD {39420201} (see Fig. #49393 and #49395Table 16.06).

Desirable: evaluation of immunohistochemical staining of all four MMR proteins in tumour tissue in any suspected CMMRD-related tumour to determine the expression in neoplastic and non-neoplastic cells, including tumour-infiltrating leukocytes {39420201}.

Staging

Staging is the same as that for sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

CMMRD-associated tumours are frequently hypermutated or ultramutated, resulting in high neoantigen (frameshift peptides) formation {27001570}. There is growing evidence of the clinical efficacy of immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, for treatment of patients with CMMRD-associated tumours {34992263;35235414;27001570;38773265}. Survivors of a CMMRD-associated tumour have a high risk of further malignancies and need intensive surveillance mainly for intestinal tract cancers and brain tumours {24556086;28572265;28363489;39420201} (#49396Table 16.08), which improves clinical outcome of the patients {33945292;36411031}.
