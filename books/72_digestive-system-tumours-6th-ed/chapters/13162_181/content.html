13.1.6.2 Mesenchymal hamartoma of the liver

Definition

Mesenchymal hamartoma (MH) is a benign liver tumour composed of abundant hypocellular fibromyxoid stroma containing stellate mesenchymal cells, bile duct–like structures, and numerous blood vessels of variable calibre.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

2E89.10 Mesenchymal hamartoma of liver

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

MH is exclusively hepatic, occurring in the right liver lobe in 75% of cases, the left lobe in 22%, or both lobes in 3%.

Clinical features

MH typically presents with abdominal distension and an upper abdominal mass, although some cases are found incidentally. Pain is rarely a dominant feature, and only a few patients show anorexia, vomiting, or failure to thrive {2331217}. Abdominal distention may rapidly progress and cause respiratory distress {363258}. Large MHs in neonates and infants may compromise blood circulation and become life-threatening. MH may be associated with placental mesenchymal dysplasia and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome {18260692;17535089;24044629}. A subset of MHs are detectable prenatally (fetal MH), usually in the last trimester of pregnancy. Liver function is usually normal, but serum levels of AFP may be slightly elevated {15024683}; in exceptional cases, MH is associated with high levels of serum AFP {18850482}. Radiologically, MH appears as a multicystic or a complex cystic tumour with internal septations.

Epidemiology

MH is the third most common paediatric liver tumour, after hepatoblastoma and infantile haemangioma {6687279;16291152}, accounting for 12% and 8% of all liver tumours by 2 and 21 years of age, respectively. About 85% of affected children present before the age of 3 years, and < 5% of MHs are diagnosed after the age of 5 years {2331217}. About 15% of cases have been observed in the neonatal period {11464991}. MH is slightly more common in boys than in girls. In contrast, the rare MHs occurring in adults are more frequent in women than in men {16365828}.

Etiology

Most cases of MH are sporadic with no clearly demonstrable etiological risk factor. Androgenetic/biparental mosaicism, seen in Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome and placental mesenchymal dysplasia, is a genetic risk factor for development of MH {18260692;24044629;17535089}. Occult ABM has been reported in a patient with sporadic MH without placental mesenchymal dysplasia {21585278}. Association of MH with DICER1 mutations {31067372} is debated, because the cystic liver tumours described in 2 patients with DICER1 mutations did not demonstrate microscopic features characteristic of MH {31390509}; these tumours probably represent distinct cystic neoplasms similar to pleuropulmonary blastomas {34599283}.

Pathogenesis

Activation of the chromosome 19q microRNA cluster (C19MC), located at 19q13.3-q13.4, has been demonstrated in both sporadic tumours as well as those associated with ABM {24555441}. Sporadic tumours are associated with chromosomal rearrangements involving 19q13.4, also known as mesenchymal hamartoma of the liver breakpoint 1 (MHLB1), which interpose regulatory elements of other genes with C19MC, resulting in its activation. The most common translocation is with 11q13 t(11;19)(q13;q13.4), which harbours the MALAT1 gene {15325096;16544232;24120702}.

Activation of C19MC in ABM is thought to result from abnormal gene imprinting {24555441;25751191} of the gene. C19MC is an imprinted gene, with only the paternal allele normally expressed in the placenta. ABM leads to abnormal imprinting in androgenetic cells with 2 paternal alleles and no maternal allele, causing dysregulation and activation of C19MC.

The mesenchymal stroma appears to represent the primary developmentally abnormal component, with subsequent disordered epithelial–mesenchymal interactions. FISH analysis has shown that stromal cells harbour the genetic alterations in sporadic MHs, and that androgenetic, bipaternal cells are enriched in the stroma of MHs in ABM-associated tumours {24555441}. Levels of C19MC miRNAs are increased several-fold in MH as compared with those in the adjacent non-tumorous liver {24555441}. MicroRNAs of C19MC may have a role in hepatic differentiation and maturation, and MH is thought to originate as a developmental abnormality in late embryogenesis.

Macroscopic appearance

MH appears as an expansile, well-delineated but unencapsulated mass in a non-cirrhotic liver. The cut surface is solid or solid-cystic. The cysts are often multiloculated, range in size from a few millimetres to a few centimetres, and contain clear or straw-coloured fluid, or gelatinous material. The cysts do not communicate with the biliary tree. The solid areas have a soft, glistening appearance. In one series, 41% of MHs were solid and 59% were cystic {16479067}.

Histopathology

Microscopically, MH is a lobulated mass of abundant mesenchymal stroma containing bile ducts–like structures and numerous blood vessels. The mesenchyme is typically hypocellular and myxoid, rich in glycosaminoglycans, and contains bland, stellate, or spindle-shaped mesenchymal cells. Embedded within the stroma are anastomosing, tortuous, linear, or branching bile duct–like structures, which may be cystically dilated and reminiscent of ductal plate malformation. The ductal structures usually have a circumferential collar of dense collagen; at times, the collagenous stroma may be prominent throughout the tumour. Blood vessels are abundant within the stroma, forming a combination of thick-walled arterioles and thin-walled ectatic capillaries. Islets of hepatocytes without acinar architecture may be present. Foci of extramedullary haematopoiesis are observed in most (> 85%) cases. Larger and older lesions may show fluid-filled areas of cystic degeneration without an epithelial lining. In older children, atypical features such as scarcity of the ductal component and stromal calcification may occur {22935827;18648751}.

By immunohistochemistry, the spindle cells express vimentin, actin, and desmin (focal) while the ductal structures express CK7 and CK19. Nuclear β-catenin is absent. The intervening hepatocytes as well as the tumour components may express glypican-3 (GPC3), which may cause confusion with hepatoblastoma {21937079}.

Principal differential diagnoses include mixed epithelial–mesenchymal hepatoblastoma, infantile haemangioma, and embryonal sarcoma. The cystic form of MH may be confused with other hepatic cystic lesions, especially in adults {12378513;16365828}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A proportion of MHs demonstrate a characteristic molecular signature with chromosomal rearrangements involving the breakpoint 19q13.4, termed MHLB1 {24120702}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: abundant hypocellular, fibromyxoid stroma containing stellate or spindle-shaped mesenchymal cells, bile duct–like structures surrounded by a collagen collar, and numerous blood vessels of variable calibre.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

MH is benign, with an excellent prognosis if the mass is completely resected {14629534}. Exceptions are cases with severe cardiopulmonary complications and rare instances of malignant transformation into undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma {10591570;18751987}. Karyotype analyses of undifferentiated embryonal sarcoma have shown chromosomal rearrangements involving 19q13.4, similar to those in MH {17311249}.
