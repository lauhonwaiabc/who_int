3.1.2.1 Gastric adenocarcinoma

Definition

Gastric adenocarcinoma (GA) is a malignant neoplasm originating from the glandular epithelium of the stomach.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B72.0 Adenocarcinoma of stomach

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Tubular adenocarcinoma (82112/3); papillary adenocarcinoma (82600/3); poorly cohesive carcinoma (84901/3); signet-ring cell carcinoma (84900/3); mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); mucinous adenocarcinoma, differentiated type (84805/3); mucinous adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated type (84806/3); adenocarcinoma, mixed, NOS (82550/3); carcinoma with lymphoid stroma (85120/3); AFP-producing carcinoma (85761/3); micropapillary adenocarcinoma (82651/3); gastric adenocarcinoma of fundic gland type (81402/3); sarcomatoid carcinoma (80330/3)

Localization

Adenocarcinomas occur throughout the stomach. In regions with a high incidence, distal stomach is predominantly involved, whereas in countries with a low incidence, involvement of the proximal stomach and the oesophagogastric junction (OGJ) is more common {16404738}.

Clinical features

Early-stage GA is asymptomatic. Symptomatic presentation with indigestion or upper abdominal pain may not occur until the cancer has progressed. In advanced-stage disease, symptoms can include severe fatigue, unintentional weight loss, and haematemesis with melena, which can be interpreted as signs and symptoms of an ulcer. Bilateral ovarian involvement (Krukenberg tumour) can result from transperitoneal or haematogenous spread. Pulmonary lymphangitic carcinomatosis and pulmonary tumour thrombotic microangiopathy may rarely develop in patients with GA {23536282;25740390}.

Endoscopic examination with forceps biopsies remains the gold standard for diagnosing GA. Narrow-band imaging or chromoendoscopy, often in conjunction with magnifying endoscopy, enables detailed evaluation of the mucosal surface. Suspicion of submucosal invasion may arise from the presence of mucosal abnormalities such as deep depressions or irregular nodules {27734273}.

Epidemiology

GA is the fifth most common cancer globally and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide {38572751}. GA incidence exhibits significant geographical variation. While eastern Asia, the Andean regions of South America, and eastern Europe have the highest incidence rates, North America, northern Europe, and most countries in Africa and south-eastern Asia have lower incidence rates {38572751;32606208}. The global incidence of GA has been steadily decreasing over the past decades. This decline is likely due to improvements in Helicobacter pylori infection treatment, dietary habits, and environmental conditions. Laurén’s classification {14320675} divided GA into two main histological types: intestinal type and diffuse type. While the incidence of intestinal-type GA has paralleled the global decline in overall GA incidence rates, the decrease in incidence of diffuse-type GA has been more gradual {7640228;26185368;30870726}. Patients are usually aged 60–80 years and male. A worse survival rate is also reported for men {40080286}.

Etiology

GA is a multifactorial disease; 90% of GAs are sporadic and the other 10% develop in a familial/hereditary setting [[KÅ‘vári B, Carneiro F, Lauwers GY. Epithelial tumours of the stomach. In: Bateman AC, Greenson JK, Lauwers GY, et al., editors. Morson and Dawson's gastrointestinal pathology. 6th ed. Hoboken (NJ): Wiley-Blackwell; 2025. pp. 180–222. Bookref]]. Established environmental factors, including H. pylori infection, tobacco smoking, rubber manufacturing, and X- and γ-radiation, have sufficient human evidence for stomach carcinogenicity. Emerging evidence also indicates that EBV infections, dietary factors such as processed meat, and occupational factors such as asbestos are risk factors, although there is limited evidence in humans for stomach cancer carcinogenicity {23189750;23193840;23189753;23189752;29949327;23189751;35963539} (see #49572Box 3.01). Microbial and mycobiotic patterns have been observed in patients with GA, suggesting a potential role of dysbiosis in the development and progression of GA. However, their exact contribution to the pathogenesis remains unclear {37020297;33754037}.

Several subtypes of GA have been associated with specific etiologies and molecular mechanisms. Germline mutations have been identified as the underlying cause of specific GA subtypes. CDH1 mutations are associated with hereditary diffuse GA, while APC exon 1B mutations are linked to (intestinal-type) GA and proximal polyposis of the stomach (GAPPS) {9537325;27087319} (see section Hereditary gastric and breast cancer syndrome (CDH1, CTNNA1)).

Pathogenesis

H. pylori infection is thought to be responsible for most GAs, particularly non-cardia GAs {35944925;28771894;29562147}. Infection with CagA-positive H. pylori strains is the strongest risk factor for the development of GA {11743164;28322182}. Infection with the most virulent vacAs1, m1, and i1 strains is associated with an increased risk of GA {19726606;28322182}. Chronic H. pylori infection has been assumed to cause DNA mutations and epigenetic changes in the epithelial cells of the stomach {24786892;29358395;28513632}. Some GAs arise via the Correa cascade {22188910}, whereas some arise directly from the native gastric mucosa without passing through the intestinal metaplasia step {7903667;30138135}. Although H. pylori eradication reduces the risk of GA {26836587}, the degree of risk reduction depends on the presence, severity, and extent of the atrophic damage at the time of eradication {26187502;26995689;29306868;26836587}. In patients with advanced high-stage gastritis as per the Operative Link on Gastritis Assessment (OLGA) {17142647} and Operative Link on Intestinal Metaplasia (OLGIM) staging systems {20381801}, H. pylori eradication may no longer reduce the risk of neoplastic progression {29306868}. Even 10 years after eradication, GA can still develop, with an increasing proportion of cancers with poorly differentiated components {38434145}. Furthermore, genetic factors, such as SNPs in inflammation-associated genes (IL1B, TNF), seem to increase the risk of developing GA {39563199}. Although autoimmune gastritis increases the risk of developing type 1 neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), it contributes less to the risk of developing GA unless associated with H. pylori {35772926;35301012;36849227;28532700}. Bile reflux has been reported as an independent risk factor for developing GA, especially in the cardia and in gastric remnants after previous surgery {26029176;33793080}. Alterations in microbiota also play a role in gastric carcinogenesis {36704105;29102920;39858849;26385073}.

Multiple genetic and epigenetic alterations occur during gastric carcinogenesis and progression {22005013;39164671;31067475;33482911;36344677}. These include abnormalities in oncogenes, tumour suppressor genes, growth factors, receptor tyrosine kinase, DNA repair genes, matrix degradation enzymes, cell-cycle regulators, and cell adhesion molecules. Genetic polymorphisms are predisposing endogenous factors and appear to alter GA susceptibility as well as disease progression {25731870;31516756;27789275}. DNA methylation, histone modification, chromatin remodelling, and regulation by non-coding RNAs are important epigenetic alterations underlying GA carcinogenesis {26823082;27718135;28513632}. Advanced multiplatform genomic technology has recently identified potential molecular mechanisms underlying GA carcinogenesis {32861308;26861606;33790428}.

Using high-throughput sequencing technology, several groups, including the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Research Network and the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG), proposed different molecular classifications of GA {23684942;25079317;25894828}. TCGA proposed a classification of GA into four molecular subtypes: EBV-positive, microsatellite-unstable, genomically stable, and chromosomally unstable {24816255;25079317;28004449;30361512;31836926;32514646;34292431;36914835;38621923} (see #49674Table 3.03). ACRG proposed a classification of GA into four subtypes: microsatellite-unstable, microsatellite-stable with epithelial–mesenchymal transition gene signature, microsatellite-stable and TP53-active, and microsatellite-stable and TP53-inactive {25894828}.

Considering the histogenesis and using immunohistochemical markers of cell differentiation, GAs can be of gastric phenotype (MUC5AC/TFF1+ and/or MUC6/TFF2+), intestinal phenotype (MUC2+, CD10+, CDX2+), mixed/hybrid phenotype (expressing gastric and intestinal markers), and null phenotype (negative for all markers) {10699992;11668493;15086835}. The mixed/hybrid phenotype may be further subdivided into gastric-predominant phenotype and intestinal-predominant phenotype.

Macroscopic appearance

Early GA is defined as a carcinoma that has invaded the mucosa or submucosa, regardless of the presence or absence of lymph node metastasis {21573743}. Early GA can be endoscopically or macroscopically classified into three main types: type 0-I (protruded); type 0-IIa (slightly elevated), type 0-IIb (flat), type 0-IIc (depressed), and type 0-III (excavated) {21573743;14652541}. GA that has infiltrated the muscularis propria and beyond is classified as advanced. Advanced GA can be classified into four types, according to the Borrmann classification [[Borrmann R. Geschwulste des magens und duodenums. In: Henke F, Lubarch O, editors. Handbuch der speziellen pathologischen anatomie und histologie. Volume 4/1. Berlin (Germany): Springer Verlag; 1926. pp. 812–1054. German. Bookref]]: type 1 (polypoid), type 2 (ulcerated with sharp raised margin), type 3 (ulcerated with poorly defined infiltrative margin), type 4 (diffusely infiltrative), or type 5 (unclassifiable) {21573743}.

Histopathology

Despite epidemiological differences, the adenocarcinomas of the proximal and distal stomach have similar microscopic features. The histological classification of GA is challenging because of intratumoural variations in architecture and/or differentiation. Over the years, several histological classifications have been proposed. The WHO classification, which does not take into account histogenesis and differentiation, recognizes five main subtypes of adenocarcinoma (tubular, papillary, mucinous, poorly cohesive, and mixed) and other rarer histological subtypes. #52977Table 3.04 shows the histological GA subtypes according to the WHO classification in comparison to the classifications of Laurén {14320675}, Nakamura {5726267}, and the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association (JGCA) [[Japanese Gastric Cancer Association. Japanese classification of gastric carcinoma. 15th ed. Tokyo (Japan): Kanehara Shuppan; 2017. Japanese. Bookref]].

Tubular adenocarcinoma: This is the most common GA subtype, with frequency ranging from 45% in Europe {27522550} to 72% in elderly Japanese patients {23274922}. This subtype is composed of dilated or slit-like branching tubules of variable diameter. Acinar structures may also be present. Individual neoplastic cells can be columnar or cuboidal, and nuclei can be flattened by prominent intraluminal mucin or cell debris. A clear cell pattern has been recognized, mainly in the proximal stomach {17886094}. The distinction between very well differentiated adenocarcinoma with low-grade nuclear atypia, intestinal metaplasia, and foveolar hyperplasia can be challenging especially in biopsy specimens {10414502;16688795;23723017;39362133}. Crawling-type adenocarcinoma, a highly differentiated pattern of tubular adenocarcinoma, is characterized by an anastomosing/fused growth pattern of glands that can be distended, abortive, or spiky in appearance {16952035;22865191;23723017;22987215;38755445}. Crawling-type adenocarcinoma may exhibit poorly cohesive carcinoma components. RHOA mutations and CLDN18::ARHGAP fusions have been identified in crawling-type adenocarcinoma {30425335}. Tumours with solid structures and barely recognizable tubules are included in this category and classified as poorly differentiated tubular (solid) adenocarcinoma, which corresponds to the designation “poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, solid-type (por1)” in the JGCA classification (see #52977Table 3.04). When encountering a solid tumour, it may be necessary to use appropriate immunohistochemical studies to distinguish between neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), hepatoid adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and poorly differentiated tubular (solid) adenocarcinoma.

Papillary adenocarcinoma: Papillary adenocarcinoma accounts for 3–10% of all GAs {11013353;28830689;37341602}. It usually shows an exophytic growth pattern and is histologically well differentiated, with elongated finger-like processes lined by columnar or cuboidal cells supported by fibrovascular connective tissue cores. Some papillary adenocarcinomas contain tubules also and are then called tubulopapillary adenocarcinoma. Despite being a well-differentiated tumour with a pushing invading edge and frequent infiltration by inflammatory cells, papillary adenocarcinoma is associated with a higher frequency of liver metastasis and poor survival {23090003;28123605;34115672}.

Poorly cohesive carcinoma (PCC), including signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC): PCCs account for 16.8–28.4% of all GAs {32545410;33811588;35938363}. PCCs are composed of individual neoplastic cells or neoplastic cells arranged in small groups without forming glands or tubules. PCC can be subclassified as signet-ring cell PCC or non–signet-ring cell PCC {30167905}. Signet-ring cell PCCs are composed predominantly of signet-ring cells characterized by large amounts of intracytoplasmic mucin, eccentric nuclei, and minimal pleomorphism. The cytoplasm can be clear or eosinophilic. Signet-ring cell can also have slightly eccentric nuclei or intracytoplasmic cysts [[Oota K, Sobin LH. Histological typing of gastric and oesophageal tumours. Geneva (Switzerland): World Health Organization; 1977. Bookref]] {3037769}. Non–signet-ring cell PCC includes tumours composed of neoplastic cells characterized by scant intracytoplasmic mucin, centrally located nuclei, and high cellular atypia/pleomorphism. PCCs can be accompanied by marked desmoplasia in the deeper levels of the gastric wall. Pure SRCC (defined as > 90% of tumour cells with signet-ring cells) {30167905} is relatively rare, typically presents at an early disease stage, and may show a layered structure within the mucosa. The typical SRCC morphology is often lost as the tumour progresses and transforms into non–signet-ring cell PCC {3037769;30963318}.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma: Mucinous adenocarcinoma accounts for 3–9% of all GAs. It is often aggressive, with more advanced-stage disease and poorer survival outcomes, compared with non-mucinous GAs {11706765;16604474;20208134;35534656}. It is composed of malignant epithelium and extracellular mucin pools, the latter accounting for > 50% of the tumour area. Two histologically and genetically distinct subtypes have been proposed: differentiated-type mucinous adenocarcinoma, characterized by glandular structures, tubules, and strips of columnar epithelium surrounded by abundant extracellular mucin; and undifferentiated-type mucinous adenocarcinoma, composed of small clusters or single tumour cells (often including signet-ring cells) in a similar mucinous background {27313181}.

Mixed adenocarcinoma: Mixed adenocarcinoma accounts for 6–22% of all GAs {25761964;27298411;18266006}. These adenocarcinomas display two or more distinct histological components; for example, a glandular (tubular/papillary) component and an SRCC / non–signet-ring cell PCC component. Ideally, any distinct histological component should be mentioned in the pathology report. The components of mixed adenocarcinomas appear to be the same clone {17167181;18266006}. E-cadherin inactivation, frequently observed in SRCC / non–signet-ring cell PCC, is also commonly seen in the SRCC / non–signet-ring cell PCC component of mixed adenocarcinomas {10211998}. Available data suggest that patients with mixed adenocarcinomas have a poorer prognosis than those with only one component {9269907;18266006}.

Other rare histological subtypes

Gastric carcinoma with lymphoid stroma (GCLS): This tumour accounts for 1–7% of all GAs {947518;29438172}. It is also referred to as lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma or medullary carcinoma. GCLS is characterized by irregular sheets, trabeculae, poorly defined tubules, or syncytia of polygonal cells surrounded by a very prominent lymphocytic infiltrate including intraepithelial lymphocytes {21654677;19445939;29779068}. In early-stage diseases, GCLS exhibits a characteristic lace-like pattern with anastomosing or branching glandular structures {11745439}. GCLS is frequently localized in the proximal stomach or in the gastric stump after previous gastric surgery and shows a male predilection. The frequency of EBV infection in GCLS varies from 22.5% to 100% {29438172;11432666;10370694}, and the highest frequency (> 80%) is detected when performing EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization {27342912;29536167;19445939;24771002;25912839}. EBV may also be present in other histological patterns such as tubular adenocarcinoma {25633561}. EBV-positive GAs invading the submucosa have a lower risk of lymph node metastasis than other (histologically EBV-negative_ subtypes {26573601;31062195;32852875}. GA with microsatellite instability and/or mismatch repair deficiency can have a similar histological phenotype but have a different transcriptomic profile {30018250}.

AFP-producing carcinomas including hepatoid adenocarcinoma: Hepatoid adenocarcinoma is an AFP-producing adenocarcinoma, which accounts for 0.3–2% of all GAs {28505005;19243386}. Other AFP-producing adenocarcinomas account for 2.6–5.4% {28505005;1371637} of all GAs. Hepatoid adenocarcinoma is predominantly composed of large polygonal eosinophilic hepatocyte-like neoplastic cells. AFP can be detected by immunohistochemistry in the tumour cells and also in the serum. Bile and PASD-resistant intracytoplasmic eosinophilic globules can be observed. Other AFP-producing adenocarcinomas include well-differentiated papillary or tubular adenocarcinoma with clear cytoplasm, adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation, and yolk sac tumour–like carcinoma {11706627;29034239;29751042;21592404;26622718}. These histological types often coexist {22173117}. Adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation shows a tubulopapillary architecture composed of columnar neoplastic cells with clear cytoplasm, resembling early fetal gut epithelium. These types of tumours may express marker proteins of fetal gut, such as SALL4, claudin-6, and glypican-3 (GPC3) {20182341;22803571;19243386;23837142}. Tubular or papillary adenocarcinomas expressing fetal gut markers, even without clear cytoplasm, may exhibit an aggressive phenotype akin to those with enteroblastic differentiation {37581693}. SALL4 and claudin-6 immunohistochemistry are diagnostically useful in distinguishing primary gastric hepatoid adenocarcinoma from metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma in the stomach {20182341}.

Micropapillary adenocarcinoma: Micropapillary adenocarcinoma is characterized by the presence of small clusters of tumour cells without fibrovascular cores protruding into clear spaces {20661012}. This growth pattern can account for 10–90% of the entire tumour and often accompanies tubular or papillary GAs. The micropapillary subtype has an unfavourable prognosis, and patients often already have lymph node metastasis at the time of diagnosis {21164291;22175888;24562421}.

Gastric adenocarcinoma of fundic gland type (GA-FG): Tumours with histology similar to that of oxyntic gland adenoma with submucosal invasion are classified as GA-FG (see section Oxyntic gland adenoma) {20410811;22472957;31375767;34268625}. Such lesions have also been referred to as parietal cell carcinomas {23575104}. GA-FG accounts for 1% of early GAs treated by endoscopic submucosal dissection {26185396}. However, the actual incidence is likely to be lower, as many of those categorized as GA-FG are considered to represent oxyntic gland adenoma (OGA) with prolapse-like misplacements rather than true submucosal invasion {22472957;36792200}.

Gastric sarcomatoid carcinoma (carcinosarcoma): Gastric sarcomatoid carcinoma consists of both adenocarcinoma and sarcomatoid components, often presenting as a large polypoid tumour. The sarcomatoid component may or may not show a specific line of mesenchymal differentiation {9293537;17334721;33761637}.

Grading

Grading applies to tubular and papillary adenocarcinomas and not to any of the other GA subtypes. Thus, well-differentiated adenocarcinomas are composed of well-formed glands, whereas poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas have poorly formed glands and may display solid areas. Grading is preferably performed using a two-tiered system: low-grade (formerly well or moderately differentiated) versus high-grade (formerly poorly differentiated).

Response to therapy

Neoadjuvant therapy may modify the histopathological appearance. Tumour response, which can vary from no detectable response to pathological complete response, can be assessed by tumour regression grade (TRG). Various methods have been proposed, including the Becker and Mandard systems {14508841;28918544;24109590;8194005}, but there is no international consensus on which TRG system should be used. Pathological complete response is defined by some as the absence of adenocarcinoma and dysplasia in the resected specimen.

Cytology

Peritoneal lavage cytology provides information for GA staging, predicting patient prognosis and determining treatment strategies {28779261;28256061}. Abnormal cytology findings include cells with irregularly shaped nuclei, a high N:C ratio, increased chromatin, and anisonucleosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of GA is based on histopathological characteristics. Molecular analyses of GA have not yet transitioned into clinical practice {38473896}. In routine pathology, immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6), p53, and E-cadherin combined with in situ hybridization for EBER might allow molecular GA subtyping similar to the classification proposed by TCGA {27342907;27819872;27032689}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant epithelial tumour showing glandular differentiation (adenocarcinoma); invasion into the lamina propria or deeper layers of the stomach wall.

Staging

According to the ninth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]] and version 9 of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging manual [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]], tumours involving the OGJ with an epicentre ≤ 20 mm into the proximal stomach are staged as oesophageal cancers; tumours with an epicentre located > 20 mm into the proximal stomach are staged as GAs, even if the OGJ is involved. All tumours in the stomach that do not cross the OGJ are staged as GA.

Endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection are utilized for the staging and treatment of superficial lesions, including dysplasia and intramucosal carcinoma {26234303;26317585}. For more advanced cancers, staging is typically performed using EUS, contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, or 18F-FDG PET/CT {21837458;22237654;24782607;27130061}. Diagnostic laparoscopy and peritoneal washings for cytology are performed for patients with resectable GA who are also candidates for perioperative chemotherapy {35130500;35914639}. GAs spread by direct extension to adjacent organs, by invading lymphatic vessels and/or blood vessels, and/or by serosal dissemination.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognostic factors

pTNM stage is currently the single most important factor for prognosis prediction in patients with GA. The 5-year survival rate in patients with pT1 (tumour invades the lamina propria, muscularis mucosae, or submucosa) and pN0 (no regional lymph node metastasis) GA is > 90% {26897166}. Survival decreases in a stepwise fashion with increasing pT and pN {26897166}.

Histological type may affect prognosis {16714395;19453074}. The Japanese recommendation for endoscopic treatment of GA, adopted in practice in the USA and Europe, is based on the size and histopathological characteristics of the lesion {27704225;33161444} [[Japanese Gastric Cancer Association. Japanese gastric cancer treatment guidelines. 6th ed. Tokyo (Japan): Kanehara Shuppan; 2021. Japanese. Bookref]] (see #44484Table 3.05). Detailed histopathological examination of the resected material is mandatory, to identify even minute foci of submucosal invasion or other features that indicate the need for further treatment {21573743}. Patients with PCC with abundant fibrous stroma (linitis plastica or scirrhous carcinoma) have the poorest prognosis {21717092;28679451;29299363}.

Tubular/papillary adenocarcinomas are more likely to give rise to liver metastases by hematogenous spread, whereas PCCs are more likely to involve the serosa. Mixed adenocarcinomas typically show the metastatic patterns of both subtypes. Widespread tumour extension is particularly common in PCCs, which frequently show extensive spread on the serosal surface, well beyond the macroscopically visible tumour. Consequently, frozen section examination of longitudinal margins is desirable, particularly if the clearance is < 40 mm, to assess the completeness of resection.

Lymphovascular invasion is an indicator of biological aggressiveness and may be a prognostic factor for GA without lymph node metastasis {25376868;19878963}.

Established predictive biomarkers

ERBB2 (HER2): When used as first-line therapy, trastuzumab, an anti-HER2 antibody, significantly improved overall survival in patients with HER2-positive, advanced, unresectable GA or OGJ cancer (OGJC) {25038874}. Trastuzumab deruxtecan, an anti-HER2 antibody–drug conjugate, significantly improved survival outcomes in patients with HER2-positive advanced GA/OGJC receiving third-line or later treatments {32469182}. These studies underscore the importance of confirming HER2 protein overexpression or gene amplification before initiating therapy {28129524}.

Claudin-18.2: Claudin-18.2, a protein crucial for the formation of tight junctions, is expressed in normal gastric epithelium. Two phase III trials have demonstrated that the addition of zolbetuximab, an antibody targeting claudin-18.2, to a chemotherapy regimen significantly improved survival outcomes in patients with HER2-negative, advanced, unresectable GA or OGJC expressing claudin-18.2 {37068504;37524953}. In these studies, claudin-18.2–positive tumours are defined as ≥ 75% of tumour cells showing moderate to strong membranous claudin-18 staining.

Mismatch repair: ICIs can be used for patients with mismatch repair protein–deficient advanced unresectable GA/OGJC {33792646;34102137;38382001;37875143}. Immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins and PCR-based microsatellite instability testing have shown nearly identical results {28241187}.

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PDL1): For HER2-negative, unresectable, advanced, or recurrent GA/OGJC, chemotherapy combined with ICIs (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) has demonstrated improved survival outcomes compared with chemotherapy alone {35030335;37875143;38382001}. While scoring guidelines are under development, the combined positive score (CPS) for PDL1 is defined as the number of PDL1-expressing cells (tumour cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of tumour cells, multiplied by 100 {34102137}. The CPS suggests varying degrees of benefit from the addition of ICIs.

Markers under development include FGFR2, VEGF and its receptor (VEGFR), EGFR, and c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase {25682441;26049023;29329598;30355453;30366938;30718072;30770350;33633310;36244398;37147645;38308771;38861192;39383487}.
