8.1.2.1 Anal squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Anal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a malignant epithelial tumour originating from the squamous anal mucosa.

ICD-O coding

80700/3 Squamous cell carcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C00.3 Squamous cell carcinoma of anus or anal canal

Related terminology

Not recommended: cloacogenic carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Verrucous squamous cell carcinoma (80510/3)

Localization

Anal SCC is localized in the anal canal and margin.

Clinical features

The symptoms of anal SCC generally relate to the presence of a mass. The common symptoms are bleeding and pain. Other symptoms include discomfort, discharge, perianal itch, change in bowel habits, and tenesmus {32693779}. Clinical examination may reveal a mass, indurated ulcer with friability, or discolouration. Colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy is used for the evaluation and biopsy of anal SCC. MRI and ultrasound (transanal/endoanal ultrasound) {25354914;29134349;28152518} are used to assess local disease. MRI is also useful in assessing treatment response {25354914;29089827} after an appropriate interval {28209296}. PET improves the assessment of regional nodal status and distant spread {28972796;28280225}. FNABs are sometimes used to assess regional lymph node involvement.

Epidemiology

SCC is the most common malignancy among anal cancers and has been becoming more prevalent throughout the general population worldwide {27789668;26224796}. The age-standardized incidence rate of invasive SCC ranges from 0.2 cases per 100 000 person-years in Asia to 1.7 cases per 100 000 person-years in North America and northern Europe {26224796;24410872;27789668;26651444;25973771}. Rising incidence rates are believed to be related to evolving sexual behaviour and increased duration of HPV infection in HIV-positive patients {23042932;27774311;18427149}, and have experienced a sharp increase of 2.7% per year from 2001 to 2015. In most nations, women experience higher incidence rates of anal SCC, but unlike in men, this is not related to HIV infection {26224796;24127500;26651444;25973771}. Most patients are diagnosed in the sixth decade of their life or later {26224796}, but immunodeficiency contributes to a younger age at presentation {23042932;27774311}.

Etiology

The etiological relationship between HPV and anal SCC is well established {15241823;25510243}. HPV (most often HPV16) infection underlies 90% of cases {15241823;22958581}. Multigenotypic infection occurs {19838162}. Additional risk factors include immunodeficiency {23042932;29093575}, anoreceptive intercourse {14682441;25867258}, coinfection with other sexually transmitted infections {22427679;29179876}, and cigarette smoking {25867258}. Longstanding perianal Crohn disease has been suggested as a potential risk factor for anal cancer {26938479;26044314}, but a large study revealed that only 2 of > 2900 such patients developed anal SCC {29199142}.

Pathogenesis

HPV infection is the main oncogenic driver, and its key oncoproteins are E6 and E7. E6 physically inactivates p53 DNA binding, facilitating survival of cells with genotoxic damage {8207801}. E7 leads to degradation and sequestering of RB1, allowing mutation-bearing cells to continue to proliferate {8840974}. Loss of RB1 function leads to overexpression of p16, which is used diagnostically {12861066}. Additional factors such as immunosuppression {23042932;29093575;19960431;20730855} are required for tumorigenesis, but the specific mechanisms/pathways by which they contribute to tumour development are unknown.

Frequent PIK3CA/AKT/mTOR pathway variants have been identified, most commonly in PIK3CA {27052656;29416628}. After PIK3CA, variants are most commonly found in FBXW7 and KMT2D, and this genomic profile shows no obvious differences between Japanese and White patients {36869079}. Overall, the tumour mutation burden is low {29416628}. HPV E6–mediated degradation inactivates p53, so TP53 mutations are typically a feature of HPV-negative SCC {27052656}.

Macroscopic appearance

Thickening of the mucosa or a mass may be present, with or without ulceration.

Histopathology

Anal SCC is histologically heterogeneous. Histomorphology ranges from large eosinophilic cells with keratinization in non–HPV-driven tumours {31091204} to examples with basaloid appearance, mainly in HPV-associated cases, characterized by marked nuclear hyperchromasia, scant cytoplasm, and a peripheral palisade of tumour cells around the infiltrating trabeculae. This was previously termed “cloacogenic carcinoma”. Rarely, basaloid tumour cell nests may be accompanied by hyalinization, imparting an adenoid cystic or cylindroma-like appearance {26866355;16327441;32461623}. Some tumours may have urothelial carcinoma–like morphology {26866355}. Recognition of these various histological patterns is helpful to avoid misdiagnosis, but frequent admixture, subjectivity, lack of biological significance, and the predominance of biopsy-only sampling have contributed to the abandonment of histological subtyping for clinical purposes {10782881;18724100}. There are no convincing studies on the role of grading of anal SCC in prognosis or therapeutic prediction.

Pseudoepitheliomatous squamous hyperplasia and basal cell carcinomas of the perianal skin may mimic SCC. Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia shows limited cytological atypia and no invasion. An underlying granular cell tumour or syphilis may be identified. Retraction artefacts, lack of atypical mitoses, and absence of an in situ component distinguish basal cell carcinoma from SCC {23599161}. A broad range of neoplasms may mimic poorly differentiated SCC (see #49328Table 8.02). Identifying an in situ component is helpful in diagnosing SCC, but it should be interpreted cautiously because mimics may involve the surface mucosa, including in a pagetoid manner.

The subtype verrucous carcinoma (VC) is rare and not associated with HPV infection. VC displays exophytic and endophytic growth of broad bulbous fronds of thickened epithelium with a pushing interface. VC lacks HPV cytopathic effect and severe cytological atypia. Mitoses are rare and basal. Definitive diagnosis on a small biopsy is often impossible and may only be suggested on the basis of clinical information. VC is locally destructive but does not metastasize. In some cases, areas of unequivocal conventional SCC arise in VC. These cases can metastasize and should be diagnosed as conventional SCC. Thorough sampling is prudent before diagnosing VC. VC is different from giant condyloma of Buschke–Löwenstein, which is HPV-driven and discussed in section Anal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia.

Anal SCC is positive for pancytokeratin, CK5/6, p40, and p16. Interpretation of p16 immunoreactivity should consider the morphological context and the presence of other markers. HPV-driven and non–HPV-driven mimics of SCC, such as melanoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC), and others, may also show positivity. Useful ancillary diagnostic markers for anal SCC and its mimics are presented in #49328Table 8.02.

Cytology

The cytology of SCC is described in section Anal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: invasive squamous neoplasia.

Staging

Anal SCC is staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system. In view of the ninth-edition TNM definition of anal canal carcinoma, the same system is also used for tumours arising at the anal verge and perianal tumours arising within 50 mm of the anal verge.

The ninth-edition TNM system subclassifies N1 disease according to the anatomical location of the metastatic nodes. Lymph drainage from proximal anal SCC is usually to mesorectal, inferior mesenteric, and some internal iliac nodes, whereas that from distal/perianal SCC is usually to superficial inguinal nodes {37308125}.

Prognosis and prediction

Male sex, skin ulceration, nodal metastasis, and tumour size > 50 mm are reported to independently predict poorer outcomes {18430910} [[PDQ Adult Treatment Editorial Board. Anal Cancer Treatment (PDQ®): Health Professional Version. In: PDQ Cancer Information Summaries. Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute (US); February 12, 2025. Bookref]]. The prognostic value of grade appears to be confounded by stage, and morphological heterogeneity in anal SCC has also questioned the predictive value of grading {34175386}. Tumour response by imaging correlates with outcome {25354914;28972796;27423677}. HIV status does not alter disease-specific outcome, but HIV infection portends more treatment-related toxicity {27774311;25976897}.

Recent data indicate that p16, p53, and HPV testing in anal SCC may have prognostic value, with better survival in patients with p16-positive, HPV-positive, and p53 non-aberrant/wildtype tumours {33483624;32728225;32051192;25871546;29466950}. These findings support the potential use of a dualistic classification system akin to that employed for head and neck tumours, although further research is needed to validate this approach.

Neoplastic cell PDL1 expression is identified in approximately half of anal SCC cases {28334399}, with limited responses to PD1 inhibition {38272561;35114169;28453692;37308125;34175386}. Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy is an option for patients with metastatic disease who have experienced progression on first-line therapy {37308125}.
