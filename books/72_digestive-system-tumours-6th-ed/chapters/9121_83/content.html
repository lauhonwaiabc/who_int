9.1.2.1 Hepatocellular carcinoma

Definition

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a primary malignancy of the liver composed of epithelial cells showing hepatocytic differentiation.

ICD-O coding

81700/3 Hepatocellular carcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C12.02 Hepatocellular carcinoma of liver

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Clear cell–type hepatocellular carcinoma (81740/3); chromophobe-type hepatocellular carcinoma (81707/3); fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma (81710/3); lymphocyte-rich hepatocellular carcinoma (81703/3); macrotrabecular massive hepatocellular carcinoma (81708/3); neutrophil-rich hepatocellular carcinoma (81706/3); sarcomatoid hepatocellular carcinoma (81730/3); scirrhous hepatocellular carcinoma (81720/3); steatohepatitic hepatocellular carcinoma (81709/3)

Localization

HCC shows no preferential location within the liver.

Clinical features

Patients can present with clinical signs and symptoms related to the tumour or to underlying chronic liver disease. Symptoms may include right upper quadrant abdominal pain, weight loss, and rapid deterioration in the setting of liver cirrhosis. Clinical signs may include hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, jaundice, and rapid increase of ascites. Symptomatic HCCs are typical of advanced stage and have a poor prognosis. Serological biomarkers can support a clinical diagnosis of HCC or can be used to follow patients undergoing therapy. Surveillance programmes are mostly based on ultrasonography and are important for detecting HCC at an earlier, potentially curable stage.

Contrast-enhanced dynamic CT and MRI can be used to diagnose HCC in individuals at high risk {28620797;29628281;37199193}. Typical enhancement patterns on dynamic CT and/or MRI are hyperenhancement on the arterial phase (wash-in) and hypoenhancement compared with the surrounding liver in the later phases (washout). A particularly problematic area is accurately diagnosing HCC of < 20 mm diameter (small HCC). Early HCCs (eHCCs; see below) usually exhibit isoenhancement or hypoenhancement, whereas small progressed HCCs (pHCCs) mostly demonstrate hyperenhancement on the arterial phase and hypoenhancement on the later phases. Gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI has proved particularly effective for eHCC diagnosis {21998047;29437912;32064560}.

Imaging features are linked to molecular pathological characteristics and biological behaviour of HCCs {32064560;35124821;37014244}. Certain subtypes of HCC may show atypical imaging findings. HCCs with macrotrabecular pattern, sinusoidal vascular pattern, CK19 expression, and belonging to the proliferative class are reported to exhibit rim arterial phase hyperenhancement on dynamic CT / MRI {30815393;34227881;35124821;38507054}. HCCs with prominent fibrous stroma, such as scirrhous and fibrolamellar HCC, may not exhibit the typical washout pattern and often show delayed enhancement due to the presence of fibrotic tissue {29948061;34712626}. A histopathological diagnosis of HCC should be undertaken in curative situations when it is suspected in an individual who is not considered high-risk or when imaging findings are inconclusive {28620797;29628281;37199193}, and it is recommended in palliative care situations to improve diagnostic accuracy.

Multifocal HCCs are common in cirrhotic livers. They may represent either independent HCCs arising simultaneously (about 36% of resected HCCs) or intrahepatic metastases from a primary tumour {15664248;12579536}. HCC can spread by haematogenous and less commonly lymphatic routes. Intrahepatic spread via the portal vein branches is the most common route, resulting in venous invasion foci and satellite tumour formation, and the frequency increases with tumour size. HCC invasion into the major bile ducts is uncommon and found in 0.5–12.9% of cases {36675352}. Extrahepatic spread is common in advanced disease, including metastases to the lungs, bones, and adrenal glands.

Epidemiology

HCC is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for 75–85% of cases, with the remainder primarily consisting of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and rare tumour types (see the sections on section malignant biliary tumours). In 2022, 865 269 new cases of primary liver cancer and 757 948 deaths were reported globally {38572751}. The incidence of HCC varies significantly across regions, and it is highest in eastern Asia (age-standardized incidence rate [ASR]: 14.8), northern Africa (ASR: 13.2), and south-eastern Asia (ASR: 9.5) {34942552}. There are two to four times as many male patients as female patients. Most cases are diagnosed over the age of 60 years; however, factors such as sex, ethnicity, and underlying liver disease influence the age at diagnosis {39659901}.

Etiology

In the vast majority of patients (estimated to be > 90%), HCCs are associated with chronic liver diseases and chronic toxin exposure. These include chronic hepatitis B and C, chronic alcohol consumption, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, and several inherited diseases (e.g. genetic haemochromatosis, glycogen storage diseases, and hereditary tyrosinaemia type 1) {32319693;33479224;36932227}. Predisposing diseases and conditions vary in geographical prevalence and in their specific oncogenic potency (e.g. higher for chronic hepatitis B than for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease). In addition, exposure to exogenous substances such as fungal toxins (in particular aflatoxin B1) due to contamination of nutrients in tropical and subtropical regions can contribute to HCC {29533866}. Aristolochic acid, a highly mutagenic compound found in plants and used in traditional herbal medicines in Asia, is another toxin related to HCC {31439937}. Other hazards include tobacco smoking and exposure to vinyl chloride {31543680;20232717}.

The relationship between genetic syndromes and HCC is weak and remains unclear. The BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome has been reported to cause several tumours, including HCC and cholangiocarcinoma, while the incidence of germline BAP1 pathogenic variants in patients with HCC is very low (~0.5%) {27748099}. The link between Carney complex and fibrolamellar carcinoma has been reported in a few cases {29222914}.

Because chronic liver disease is a major driver for HCC risk, most patients (~80%) have advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis at the time of diagnosis, but HCC can less commonly develop in a normal or near-normal liver {30705715}. Risk factors for HCC in livers without significant fibrosis include metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, HBV infection {19115377;34239263}; rarely, malignant transformation may occur in a hepatocellular adenoma (see section Hepatocellular adenoma) {27939373}.

Pathogenesis

In most cases, HCC appears to follow an evolution starting from chronic liver disease providing the basis for premalignant lesions (dysplastic foci, dysplastic nodules [DNs]), which develop into highly differentiated eHCC, progress to small pHCC, and subsequently evolve into increasingly less differentiated HCC with intrahepatic and finally extrahepatic metastases. This development is accompanied by an increasing accumulation of clonal molecular alterations. The molecular features of high-grade DN are closer to those of HCC than those of low-grade DN, including telomere shortening, TERT activation, and inactivation of cell-cycle checkpoint regulators {19465904}. TERT promoter mutations are an early event, seen in 14–19% of high-grade DNs and in increasing frequency from eHCC to pHCC {25123086;32170761;33479224;38344447} (see #48781Table 9.03).

Mechanisms of hepatocarcinogenesis can be loosely divided into etiology-specific and nonspecific mechanisms. The nonspecific mechanisms result from chronic liver disease and include the sequence of hepatocellular death, regeneration, and stochastic acquisition of mutations, as well as oncogenic factors derived from the inflammatory milieu and effects due to fibrosis and vascular rearrangement. Specific oncogenic mechanisms have been shown for HBV (transactivating viral factors, viral integration with constant cis- and trans-activation of oncogenic factors) {15826727} and aflatoxin (direct genotoxic effects leading to for example, codon 249 mutation of TP53) {33479224}, and proposed for HCV (oncogenic effects of core antigen and NS5A) {12101418;12217602}.

Numerous large-scale genomic analyses have clarified the mutation landscape and identified key cell-signalling and metabolic pathways altered in hepatocarcinogenesis {28622513}. The molecular alterations in HCC are quite variable {28622513;38355797}, with the number of protein-altering mutations ranging from 5 to 121 per tumour {29505843}. The diverse etiologies of HCCs also influence their genetic profiles {21822264;29672926}. Despite the many known mutations in HCC (see #48842Table 9.04), developing targeted therapy remains a challenge.

In many cases, HCC is a sequela of chronic liver inflammation, leading to the formation of a complex tumour microenvironment composed of immune and stromal cells. The tumour microenvironment participates to build an immunosuppressive and tumour-promoting niche in HCC {34140495}, resulting in the formation and progression of the tumours.

Macroscopic appearance

HCCs vary in colour from green to yellow to light tan, depending in part on their fat and bile content. The tumours often have a capsule composed of inflamed and fibrotic tissue, especially in cirrhotic livers. There are four main macroscopic patterns of pHCC that are important for clinical staging purposes {6091861}. First, there can be a single distinct nodule of HCC. Second, there can be a large dominant nodule of HCC with multiple smaller satellite nodules, usually present within 20 mm of the dominant nodule. Most satellite tumours in untreated HCC represent intrahepatic spread of the HCC, usually through the portal venules. Third, there can be many small nodules of HCC (dozens to hundreds) that are approximately the same size and shape as cirrhotic nodules – a growth pattern called diffuse or cirrhotomimetic. In these cases, the microscopic tumour burden is essentially always greater than that recognized by imaging or gross examination. Some of the small nodules can coalesce into a larger nodule and become more prominent and visible by imaging and on gross examination. Lastly, there can be multiple distinct nodules of HCC. In these cases, the tumours are clearly separate from each other and do not fit a dominant tumour / satellite tumour pattern or a diffuse pattern. This pattern is more likely to represent multiple independent primaries, especially when the tumours are small (≤ 20 mm).

Histopathological features suggestive of independent primaries include well-differentiated HCC / eHCC, HCC arising from a DN, or HCC with a nodule-in-nodule pattern with an outer nodule of well-differentiated HCC. The occurrence of two or more HCCs with these features supports multicentric occurrence {21459342}. Pedunculated HCCs protrude from the surface of the liver but otherwise do not represent a unique macroscopic growth pattern and do not have distinct histological correlates {3036680}. In all cases, the size of the tumour and whether there is tumour rupture should be reported.

Histopathology

The tumour cells in HCC show hepatocytic differentiation by morphology and/or immunohistochemistry (see #48783Table 9.05). There is loss of normal hepatic architecture, such as loss of portal tracts and reduction or loss of the normal reticulin framework. Cytological atypia varies from minimal to marked, and tumour cells commonly show increased proliferation. HCCs have four principal histological growth patterns {8625150}: trabecular, solid (synonym: compact), pseudoglandular (synonym: pseudoacinar), and macrotrabecular (defined as trabeculae ≥ 6 cells thick in some studies and ≥ 10 cells thick in others). About 50% of resected HCCs have mixed patterns, usually trabecular plus one or two others. The macrotrabecular pattern, when present in > 30–50% of the tumour, has been associated with a worse prognosis {28532995;31135484}. HCCs typically show increased arterialization, with aberrant arterioles in the parenchyma and sinusoidal capillarization, which are more effectively detected by immunostaining for CD34 {9630172}. Stromal fibrosis and lymphocytic infiltration, forming tertiary lymphoid structures, as well as granulomas, can be observed in some HCCs {33442539;30213589;34757621}. Subsets of HCC show characteristic cellular changes, including bile production, lipofuscin deposits, glycogen accumulation leading to clear cell change, and fatty change. The tumour cells can develop inclusions: hyaline bodies, Mallory–Denk bodies, or pale bodies. Pale bodies are common in fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma but are not specific for it {24278737}. Changes of the intratumoural sinusoids include peliosis-like areas and small aggregates of macrophages. Some HCCs have two or more distinct morphologies, which can include differences in architectural pattern, morphological subtype, and/or tumour grade. These cases represent tumour progression, with a nodule of poorer differentiation arising within an existing HCC – a pattern called nodule-in-nodule growth or morphological clonal progression.

As many as 35% of HCCs can be further subclassified into morphological subtypes; these subtypes were initially defined by histological features, but many have broad genetic correlates (see #48841Table 9.06). Outside of fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma, however, the subtypes currently do not have defining genetic changes, but they are clearly distinct at the morphological level. The morphological definitions continue to undergo refinement with one of the principal challenges being that HCC morphology can change dramatically over the course of morphological clonal progression. For example, steatohepatitic HCC can lose its characteristic histological features (e.g. reduction of steatosis) due to tumour progression {38876199}.

In addition, approximately 60% of HCCs with CTNNB1 mutations have a characteristic histopathological pattern composed of well-differentiated tumour cells growing in thin trabecular/solid growth with pseudoglands and often bile production. It is associated with a better prognosis {39261716;34592239}. The vessels encapsulating tumour clusters pattern is defined by a continuous lining of sinusoid-like vessels encapsulating tumour clusters, which is better detected by immunostaining for CD34. This pattern has a prevalence of 30–40% and often, but not always, overlaps with the macrotrabecular pattern. The vessels encapsulating tumour clusters pattern is associated with high serum AFP levels and a worse prognosis {31206715}. It is also characterized by high angiopoietin 2 and an invasion-independent pathway of metastasis {37456680}. All subtypes except fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma are described in cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic livers; fibrolamellar hepatocellular carcinoma occurs in non-cirrhotic livers.

Small HCC: eHCC and pHCC

Small HCCs are defined as being ≤ 20 mm in diameter and are further divided into eHCC and small pHCC (see #48784Table 9.07). On gross examination, eHCC has a vaguely nodular appearance with indistinct margins and no tumour capsule. Microscopically, eHCCs are typically well-differentiated with frequent fatty change (40%) and may exhibit a few portal tracts with portal veins. They commonly show subtle stromal invasion but lack vascular invasion {19177576}. In contrast, small pHCCs have distinct margins grossly, frequently have a tumour capsule, and lack portal tracts; not uncommonly, they show microscopic vascular invasion (25%), and even expansive/infiltrative growth patterns on microscopy {21459342;25260310}. Overall, small pHCCs are more likely to have histological features similar to those of larger pHCCs. Another difference between eHCC and small pHCC is the vascular supply characterized by fewer unpaired (non-triadal) arteries and focal areas of increased CD34 expression in eHCC, compared with a higher number of unpaired arteries and diffuse CD34 expression in small pHCC {26343958}.

Distinguishing between high-grade DN and eHCC is challenging, especially in biopsy material, but there are several useful discriminating features (see #48786Table 9.08). A nodule-in-nodule growth pattern essentially always indicates HCC, with at least the inner nodule being HCC. Stromal invasion is a helpful feature for the diagnosis of HCC, and it is defined by the lack of a CK7/CK19-positive ductular reaction around/at the border of a nodule. In contrast, a ductular reaction is present around benign nodules {17279586}. Stromal invasion as a diagnostic feature is most useful in resected specimens but can be hard to reliably detect in biopsy specimens. In biopsy diagnostics, some immunohistochemical markers can be particularly useful. There is variation regarding preferred stains in different regions of the world, but one established marker panel includes the use of HSP70, glypican-3 (GPC3), and glutamine synthetase (GS) {29628281;30285213;37199193}. Positivity for at least two of these markers is diagnostic for HCC, with 100% specificity and 72% sensitivity in surgical specimens {17326147}. Despite a decrease in sensitivity, the panel retains a 100% specificity in liver biopsy specimens {19231003}.

Histological grading

HCC tumour grade identifies the degree of differentiation on the basis of H&E staining as compared with the morphology of a mature benign hepatocyte and is categorized as well differentiated, moderately differentiated, or poorly differentiated. Undifferentiated carcinomas primary to the liver by definition have no compelling evidence of either hepatocellular or biliary differentiation, and they are not a grade of HCC (see section Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma and undifferentiated primary liver carcinoma). Some HCCs can display more than one grade, in which case the worst grade (even if in the minority) and the predominant grade should both be reported, as the worst grade tends to drive prognosis {23517197}. Needle biopsy grade correlates well but not perfectly with the grade of the respective resection specimen {21056498}.

A universal HCC grading system remains to be adopted into routine diagnostic practice, but even with the current heterogeneous approaches, tumour grade predicts patients’ survival and disease-free survival after resection with curative intent in cirrhotic livers and non-cirrhotic livers, as well as after liver transplantation {34721508}. The four-tiered modified Edmondson–Steiner system is primarily used in research but can also be applied to clinical care {8625150}. Recent expert recommendations have favoured a three-tiered grading system {29573451}. A pragmatic grading approach (see #48785Table 9.09) represents a synthesis of current published systems and experience.

Premalignant lesions

Premalignant lesions for HCC, found almost exclusively in cirrhotic livers, include dysplastic foci and DNs {19177576}. Dysplastic foci are microscopic lesions, whereas DNs are most commonly identified (although not specified) by imaging that is performed in screening programmes for eHCC detection {28620797;29628281;37199193}, or in gross examination of resected or explanted specimens.

Dysplastic foci are < 1 mm in diameter and are incidental lesions discovered on histological examination of livers with advanced fibrosis. The foci are composed of groups of hepatocytes with small-cell change or as iron-free foci in the context of iron storage disease.

Small-cell change is defined as hepatocytes showing decreased cell volume, cytoplasmic basophilia, increased N:C ratio, mild nuclear pleomorphism, and hyperchromasia, giving the low-power impression of nuclear crowding / increased cell density. Hepatocytes with small-cell change have higher proliferative activity than surrounding hepatocytes, chromosomal gains and losses, telomere shortening, and p21-checkpoint inactivation – together indicating the hepatocytes’ premalignant nature {17393506;11477144}.

Large-cell change may coexist in chronically diseased livers and is defined as hepatocytes with both nuclear and cellular enlargement (therefore, a preserved N:C ratio), nuclear pleomorphism, frequent nuclear hyperchromasia, and often multinucleation. Large-cell change is a heterogeneous phenomenon {16982109}, and the evidence of its premalignant nature is less convincing. It can be a risk indicator for HCC in chronic viral hepatitis B {19585549}, but it can also result from cellular senescence in prolonged cholestasis {9060601}.

Iron-free foci that arise in the setting of iron storage disease are precancerous lesions {7902316}.

There is no consensus on the need for reporting incidentally identified dysplastic foci in liver biopsy specimens, although reporting iron-free foci in iron storage disease and small-cell change in chronic viral hepatitis may have clinical value, indicating a higher risk for HCC development.

DNs are usually 5–15 mm in diameter and are detected either macroscopically or by imaging in cirrhotic livers, as single or multiple lesions. Their prevalence in cirrhotic livers ranges from 11% to 40% {12873817}. DNs are further classified as low-grade or high-grade, depending on the degree of cytological and architectural atypia (see #48786Table 9.08). The vascular remodelling of DNs leads to a progressive shift from a venous to an arterial blood supply. Portal tracts are commonly retained in DNs, but they may be reduced in number as newly formed unpaired (non-triadal) lobular arteries gradually increase from low-grade DN over high-grade DN to HCC {26343958}. Therefore, in the arterial phase of contrast-enhanced hepatic imaging, DNs are usually isovascular or hypovascular compared with the surrounding parenchyma.

The histological distinction between a low-grade DN and a large regenerative nodule (synonym: macroregenerative nodule) can be difficult. Reproducible and widely accepted histological criteria have not been developed, but the presence of unpaired arteries or cytological atypia greater than the background liver favours a DN.

DNs have a relative risk of developing HCC that ranges from 9% to 32%, mostly within 24–36 months, with the greatest risk found in high-grade DNs {12873817;23022425}. However, a substantial proportion (20–50%) of DNs do not progress to HCC, and may even disappear during follow-up {12873817;10999730}. Therefore, imaging-based surveillance and pathological evaluation by liver biopsy are crucial for case management {28620797;29628281;37199193}.

Differential diagnosis

Histologically, HCC must be differentiated from other primary liver cancers (intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma) and metastases of other malignant tumours (e.g. neuroendocrine tumours [NETs], urothelial carcinoma, non-keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma, and amelanotic melanoma), potentially supported, when necessary, by lineage-defining immunohistological criteria {20661013;12645337}. The differential diagnosis of well-differentiated HCC can be challenging, especially in biopsy specimens; it must be distinguished from premalignant liver tumours (DN) and benign liver tumours (hepatocellular adenoma, focal nodular hyperplasia, regenerative nodule). Commonly used stains to help distinguish benign liver tumours from well-differentiated HCC vary by region of the world but include the following: reticulin (loss), Ki-67 (increased above the background liver parenchyma, especially when the background liver shows no or minimal inflammation), glypican-3 (GPC3), EZH2, HSP70, and GS (strong and diffuse staining). The use of a single marker to support the diagnosis of HCC requires caution. In addition, some rare tumour entities primary to the liver may pose a differential diagnostic challenge, such as epithelioid angiomyolipoma or angiosarcoma.

 

Cytology

On smears, findings that suggest hepatocytic differentiation include abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, fatty change, and bile production. Findings that favour malignancy over a benign hepatic lesion include high cellularity, hyaline bodies, high N:C ratio, nuclear pleomorphism, multinucleated cells, naked nuclei, and mitotic figures. Architectural changes that suggest malignancy include pseudoacinar structures and thick trabeculae. The trabeculae can also demonstrate endothelial wrapping or transgressing vessels {10432883;10572267}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of HCC is based on histopathological characteristics. Molecular analyses can aid in the diagnosis of difficult cases and in the identification of specific subtypes (see #48841Table 9.06); for example, testing for the DNAJB1::PRKACA translocation to confirm a diagnosis of fibrolamellar carcinoma {28862261}. This same fusion can also be seen in other oncocytic non-hepatocellular tumours, such as intraductal oncocytic neoplasms of the bile duct {31678302}, so diagnostic specificity for fibrolamellar carcinoma is achieved by combining morphology with molecular characteristics.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: hepatocellular differentiation as demonstrated by histology and potentially supported by corresponding immunohistological markers; evidence of malignancy using histological criteria, in critical cases supported by appropriate immunohistological markers; absence of definite heterotypic, non-hepatocellular (e.g. cholangiocytic) differentiation (see also section Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma and undifferentiated primary liver carcinoma).

Staging

Staging of HCC follows the TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. Reflecting the peculiar situation of HCC regarding the frequent coexistence of severe chronic liver disease and specific therapeutic options (e.g. transplantation), staging systems and decision algorithms partly based on clinical criteria have been developed and implemented internationally to guide treatment decisions in HCC. These include the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC), Cancer of the Liver Italian Program (CLIP), Hong Kong Liver Cancer (HKLC), and Japan Integrated Staging (JIS) staging systems {10518312} [[Takanishi D, Severino R, Wong L. A new prognostic system for hepatocellular carcinoma: a retrospective study of 435 patients: the Cancer of the Liver Italian Program (CLIP) investigators. Hepatology. 1998 Sep;28(3):751-5. Bookref]] {24583061;19092276}, as well as, for example the Milan criteria and the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) criteria for transplantation {8594428;17868066}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for patients with HCC is generally poor, particularly those with advanced-stage HCC. Globally, the 5-year survival rate of HCC is 18% {30970190}, but it is increased to 40–50% in Asian countries like Japan and the Republic of Korea, largely due to the implementation of nationwide surveillance systems for HCC {35934831;38098744}. Long-term survival is likely only in patients with small, asymptomatic HCCs that can be treated by complete resection, by liver transplantation, or by adequate locoregional treatment including percutaneous radiofrequency ablation or transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), and the 5-year survival rate after resection is 80–90% for very early-stage HCC (BCLC stage 0) {29628281}. Late HCC recurrence (> 2 years after initial treatment) is most probably de novo disease and not metastasis from the original tumour {36925456}.

CK19 expression in HCC is a poor prognostic factor. Its expression is usually patchy rather than diffuse. If it involves > 5% of the tumour area in resected HCCs and > 1% of the tumour area in biopsied HCCs, it correlates with more infiltrative growth, poorer differentiation, more frequent vascular invasion, more intratumoural fibrous stroma, a worse prognosis, and higher rates of lymph node metastasis than CK19-negative HCCs {22045674;33442539}. The prevalence of this pattern is 4–28% and is associated with high serum AFP, HBV infection, and higher rates of resistance to locoregional therapy such as TACE and percutaneous radiofrequency ablation {27312064;21734420}. It is characterized by upregulated signatures related to the TGF-β pathway and epithelial–mesenchymal transition {33442539}. Clinical, morphological, and molecular factors are used to predict patient prognosis (see #48787Box 9.01), but the only histological parameters included in routine clinical care are tumour grade and stage.

Several purely molecular HCC classifications {26099527} and HCC classification systems using combined histology and molecular findings have been proposed {28532995;37300379} but have not reached clinical application so far. The two main molecular classes of HCC are proliferative (~50%) and non-proliferative {33479224}. The proliferative class, which is more common in HBV-associated HCC, is characterized by an enrichment of TP53 mutations and amplifications of FGF19 or CCND1. It is associated with a poor prognosis. The non-proliferative class, more prevalent in alcohol-associated and HCV-related HCC, has a better prognosis. It is characterized by dominant canonical WNT signalling associated with CTNNB1 mutations or the activation of interferon-α signalling. Recently, an immune classification of HCC has been reported, and the immune-excluded class, enriched in HCCs with CTNNB1 mutations, is proposed to be resistant to ICIs {34764464;35197323}. Although different molecular markers are employed in clinical trials, the current status does not necessitate regular predictive molecular testing in HCC.

Microsatellite instability is very rare in HCC. With immune cell identification strategies, various immune cell subsets have been reported to have roles in responses to ICI treatment {37726418;35236211}. An example includes CD8+ T cells, which are increased in the HCC cases exhibiting major pathological necrosis after ICI treatment {38344450}. Integrated morphological-molecular classifications of HCC are likely to be more robust and clinically useful (see also #48841Table 9.06).
