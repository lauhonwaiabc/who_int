15.0.1.1 Other tumours of the digestive system: Introduction

In this chapter, a variety of neoplasms that do not fit neatly into other chapters of the classification of digestive tumours are discussed. Like in the fifth edition, mucosal melanoma and germ cell tumours of the digestive system are included here. Although primary germ cell tumours of the digestive system are very rare, it is frequently a pathological differential diagnosis of a range of primitive and high-grade malignancies presenting in the digestive system. As a result, this chapter has been expanded to include a discussion of entities in the differential diagnosis of germ cell neoplasia comprising germ cell–like differentiation in other digestive malignancies (including hepatoid adenocarcinoma), SMARC-deficient (SWI/SNF-deficient) malignancies, hepatocellular carcinoma, and hepatoblastoma.

The fifth edition included a section describing digestive system metastases. In this sixth edition, it has been separated into three sections: liver metastases, peritoneal metastases, and metastases to other digestive system sites. The peritoneal metastasis section has been further subdivided into metastatic non-appendiceal tumours and metastatic appendiceal mucinous neoplasms. This subdivision acknowledges that, although appendiceal tumours are a relatively uncommon cause of peritoneal metastasis, appendiceal mucinous neoplasms are the most frequent cause of the distinctive clinical syndrome of pseudomyxoma peritonei. In addition to peritoneal metastasis from low-grade and high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, this section covers peritoneal metastases from appendiceal adenocarcinomas and also discusses the differential diagnosis with other entities (e.g. metastasis to the peritoneum from mucinous carcinomas and other mucinous neoplasms arising from other sites).

A section on carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP) has also been added. CUP refers to a metastatic malignancy where the primary site of origin cannot be identified despite comprehensive clinical, radiological, and pathological investigation. CUP can be broadly divided into those where the clinical, radiological, immunohistochemical, and molecular profile suggests origin from within the GI tract (which is further divided into favourable and unfavourable groups); and those where the features suggest GI tract origin is unlikely (again further divided into favourable and unfavourable groups). Therefore, the new section on CUP emphasizes those with a gastrointestinal profile which represent the majority of cases.
