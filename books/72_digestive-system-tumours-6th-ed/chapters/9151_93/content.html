9.1.5.1 Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma and undifferentiated primary liver carcinoma

Definition

Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA) is a primary liver carcinoma (PLC) defined by the unequivocal presence of both hepatocytic and cholangiocytic differentiation within the same tumour.

Undifferentiated PLC lacks evidence of any differentiation beyond epithelial nature.

ICD-O coding

81800/3 Combined hepatocellular carcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma

80200/3 Carcinoma, undifferentiated, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C12.00 Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma

2C12.0Y & XH1YY4 Carcinoma, undifferentiated, NOS, of liver

Related terminology

Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma

Acceptable: mixed hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma; mixed hepatobiliary carcinoma; hepatocholangiocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

No preferential location within the liver

Clinical features

The clinical features of cHCC-CCA (including the presence of underlying liver disease and cirrhosis) are similar to those of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The imaging features overlap with those of HCC and small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and they are often not specific {23883213;29360137}.

Established treatments for HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma do not formally apply to cHCC-CCA. Therefore, the diagnosis of cHCC-CCA must be made cautiously to ensure patients with HCC and iCCA are not deprived of appropriate treatment. In general, patients with cHCC-CCA are treated according to the predominant component of the tumour {38344449;39289102}, and this information should be included in the pathology report.

Epidemiology

cHCC-CCA is a rare tumour, accounting for < 1% of PLCs {39375615;24777610}. Undifferentiated PLC is even rarer, with no definite incidence rate available. The age- and sex-specific incidence rates and variations in geographical distribution are similar to those of HCC.

Etiology

The etiologies established for HCC and iCCA apply to cHCC-CCA {29360137}. Many undifferentiated carcinomas also arise in the setting of chronic liver disease. Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) of PLC has been associated with a higher frequency of cHCC-CCA {21491582}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of cHCC-CCA is still debated. Plasticity or transdifferentiation of HCC has been proposed, demonstrated in several mouse models {29248454} and supported by the occurrence of cHCC-CCA after TACE {21491582}. A potential liver progenitor/stem cell origin has also been considered {22696594;28126467}. The pathogenesis of undifferentiated carcinoma remains unclear.

Molecular studies of cHCC-CCA support a shared clonal origin between both the HCC and iCCA components. Variability of the mutation spectrum in different studies probably reflects differences in collective composition included in the sequencing analyses. While the data are not entirely consistent, the mutation profile of cHCC-CCA is closer to that of HCC than to that of iCCA in most cases, with mutations typical of HCC (e.g. TERT promoter, CTNNB1) being more common than those typical of iCCA (e.g. KRAS, IDH1) {30690729;37903726;25636086;29408647}. Molecular data of undifferentiated carcinoma are limited, but many tumours show mutations commonly found in HCC {34949765}.

Macroscopic appearance

cHCC-CCA and undifferentiated PLC show no specific macroscopic features. In cHCC-CCA, the macroscopic appearance depends on the major component, and detailed sampling of any macroscopic heterogeneity should be performed for microscopic examination.

Histopathology

The cHCC-CCA diagnosis requires (1) two different and unequivocal H&E morphologies, one of HCC and one of CCA according to respective established criteria, and (2) in most cases, immunohistochemical confirmation of the respective morphologies. When the histology is absolutely classic for each component, immunostaining might not be needed, but given the clinical importance of the diagnosis for treatment, it can be beneficial. Morphologically, the hepatocytic and cholangiocytic tumour areas may show all the architectural and cytological differentiation described for HCC and CCA. These components should also demonstrate their characteristic immunohistochemical staining patterns. Immunohistochemistry alone is not sufficient for a diagnosis of cHCC-CCA without supportive histomorphology; this considers both aberrant expression of biliary markers in a tumour that is morphologically HCC and of hepatocellular markers in a tumour that is morphologically CCA. Likewise, if stains are performed and the morphological patterns of apparent HCC or CCA cannot be confirmed by immunohistochemical findings, then the tumour does not qualify as a cHCC-CCA {29360137;28256363;31931021}. The HCC and CCA components in cHCC-CCA may be either close to each other or deeply intermingled, with either poorly defined or sharp transition. There are definite data to support the inclusion of minimum cut-off amounts of each component, so the diagnosis of cHCC-CCA is made regardless of the percentage of each component. However, it is useful to provide an estimate in the pathology report, and both components should be graded separately. Distant metastases can show either cHCC-CCA features or can be purely HCC or CCA {28707055}. Small uniform tumour cells, displaying stem cell features with scant cytoplasm and inconspicuous nucleoli (so-called cancer stem cells), can be identified in various proportions in the transitional zone between the two components, at the periphery of HCC trabeculae, or as small nests without specific location. A wide variety of immunohistochemical markers, such as CK19, SALL4, EpCAM, CD56, KIT (CD117), and CD133, have been used to identify these stem cell features. However, these markers are not specific for hepatocellular or biliary cancer stem cells and can be present in both conventional HCC and iCCA. In fact, CK19-positive HCC does not meet the diagnostic criteria for cHCC-CCA. Therefore, the use of the category “cHCC-CCA with stem cell features” is no longer recommended {29360137}. Likewise, a stem cell component is not needed for the diagnosis of cHCC-CCA, and its presence or absence does not need to be included in the pathology report. Rare cHCC-CCA can also have a separate neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) component {36312494}.

Intermediate cell carcinoma of the liver are rare PLCs showing monotonous morphological features that are intermediate between those of hepatocytes and cholangiocytes at the cytological level {29360137;14739102}. At lower magnification, the tumour is homogeneous. At higher magnification, the tumour cells are small with scant cytoplasm, and they are arranged in cords, strands, trabeculae, and occasional gland-like structures in an abundant fibrous stroma. The dual expression of hepatocytic and cholangiocytic markers in the tumour cells supports the intermediate hepatobiliary cell nature {39101773}, but these do not qualify as cHCC-CCA and for now should be classified separately. The diagnosis should be reserved for PLCs in which intermediate features involve the entire tumour {24712771}, and focal presence of intermediate (hepatobiliary) tumour cells in a cHCC-CCA does not qualify for the diagnosis of intermediate cell carcinoma.

Several other mixed tumours can be primary to the liver. As with cHCC-CCA, in many but not all cases, the HCC component is of lower grade than the mixed component, suggesting dedifferentiation/transdifferentiation of the HCC. Rare tumours can show a neuroendocrine component as well as HCC or iCCA. Each component should be morphologically recognizable and confirmed by immunohistochemical markers {27169712}. Carcinosarcoma is a very rare PLC showing both carcinomatous (HCC and CCA) and sarcomatous components. The sarcomatous component must lack any sign of epithelial differentiation on morphology and is even better identified when there is morphological and/or immunohistochemical evidence of specific sarcomatous differentiation {28506370}.

Undifferentiated carcinoma lacks definitive morphological and immunohistochemical features of any differentiation beyond epithelial nature, including HCC and CCA. In these cases, sequencing is an important tool for identifying mutations that may allow specific therapy or at least favour HCC or CCA therapy; many cases have mutations suggesting an origin as HCC {34949765}. A subset of undifferentiated carcinomas will be SMARC-deficient {34949765}. However, SMARC mutations can also be found in conventional HCC and are not a defining mutation.

Undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells is an analogue of the respective tumour subtype found in the pancreas; it can be associated with biliary intraepithelial neoplasia, and molecular studies suggest that it is derived from the biliary tree {9777987}.

Cytology

Cytological diagnosis of these entities is at best limited. The cytology of cHCC-CCA is variable and reflects the cytomorphology of the respective components {10097699}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses are not relevant for the diagnosis of cHCC-CCA.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma

Essential: a biphenotypic PLC showing unequivocal features of both hepatocytic and cholangiocytic differentiation based on H&E morphology; expression of immunohistochemical markers alone, without histomorphological features, is not sufficient for diagnosis.

Desirable: in most cases, best practice is to confirm the morphological differentiation by immunohistochemical stains.

Undifferentiated carcinoma

Essential: a PLC showing no specific differentiation, except for an epithelial nature based on H&E morphology and immunohistochemistry.

Staging

According to the ninth edition (2025) of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]], the staging of cHCC-CCA is based on that of iCCA, and the staging of undifferentiated carcinoma is based on that of HCC. This approach to staging is broadly based on the clinical behaviour of cHCC-CCA and undifferentiated PLC but remains to be independently validated for both tumours. In addition, the features of the staging system for cholangiocarcinoma staging system do not fit completely for cHCC-CCA. For example, there is no pTis in cHCC-CCA.

Prognosis and prediction

cHCC-CCA has a worse prognosis than HCC {12170085;22113592}. Resectability is a positive prognostic factor. Overexpression of a variety of genes has been linked to a worse prognosis (e.g. nestin {35076959;35987274}), but none have entered clinical decision-making. The prognosis for undifferentiated carcinoma is worse than that of HCC.
