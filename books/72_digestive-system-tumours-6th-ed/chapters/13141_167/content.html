13.1.4.1 Benign vascular anomalies and vascular tumours

Definition

Benign vascular anomalies are lesions composed of blood vessels lined by non-atypical endothelial cells. Some of them are mass-forming and neoplastic (haemangiomas), while the others are considered mass-forming malformative lesions (vascular malformations).

Localization

Published data are difficult to interpret because most GI tract lesions traditionally classified as haemangiomas are more likely vascular malformations, using newer criteria from the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification {26055853;36309042}, included in the fifth-edition Paediatric tumours volume of the WHO Classification of Tumours series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Paediatric tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 7). https://publications.iarc.who.int/608. Bookref]]. Haemangiomas and vascular malformations can occur as single lesions, or be multiple, involving the oesophagus to the colon {22942801}; using current criteria, most cases affecting adults are vascular malformations. The small intestine is the most common site, with haemangiomas and vascular malformations accounting for 10% of tumours arising in this location {9672985}. Vascular tumours and vascular malformations of the colorectum are less frequent with 50% located in the rectum. Gastric lesions are usually vascular malformations {6740459;21738815}.

Clinical features

Most patients with GI tract vascular malformations and haemangiomas present with occult or acute gastrointestinal bleeding {11641173}, followed by abdominal pain, bowel obstruction, perforation, or intussusception. Small polypoid lesions may be detected incidentally during screening endoscopy. Grossly, GI tract vascular malformations and haemangiomas are either polypoid and intraluminal or poorly defined diffusely infiltrating submucosal lesions. They are purplish-red to blue, soft and compressible unless containing a thrombus or phleboliths {11641173;16538013}.

Vascular malformations can manifest as ulcerating lesions with haemorrhage and thrombosis, and they can involve surrounding structures, particularly in the rectosigmoid region {22942801}. Diagnosis is usually made endoscopically and/or by angiography or other imaging modalities. Biopsy is performed in some cases {21738815}. Multiple lesions may arise in specific settings (e.g. Maffucci syndrome, Klippel–Trénaunay syndrome, blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome, and hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia), and they may be associated with similar lesions in other organs, such as the skin and liver.

Most hepatic infantile haemangiomas (HIHs) are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging. However, some patients present with hepatomegaly, abdominal enlargement, abdominal compartment syndrome, failure to thrive, and cardiac failure {8804322;17208542;19159729;22152857}. Some patients develop hypothyroidism {10900278}. Approximately 70% of infants with HIH have one or more cutaneous lesions {19159729}. HIH is usually multifocal or diffuse, and rarely single; the diffuse pattern is usually seen as innumerable, often coalescent nodules that virtually replace the hepatic parenchyma {17208542}.

Hepatic congenital haemangiomas (HCHs) are primarily asymptomatic and incidentally found on imaging in infancy or on prenatal ultrasound {19159729;17208542;26223454}. Most present as single lesions. Some newborns may present with hepatomegaly, thrombocytopenia, and mild hypofibrinogenaemia, and rarely with cardiac failure from arteriovenous or portohepatic venous shunting {8804322;19159729;23025620}, which may also result in hydrops fetalis {2644601}.

Hepatic cavernous haemangiomas are usually < 50 mm but can vary markedly in size, from a few millimetres to very large (giant) tumours measuring 150–310 mm {1911479}. Those > 40 mm are often symptomatic, producing pain or mass effect.

Rupture is very rare, as is consumptive coagulopathy. They may enlarge or rupture in pregnancy, and they can also enlarge or recur in patients taking estrogens. Most examples are solitary, but they can be multiple.

Epidemiology

The age range for GI tract haemangiomas and vascular malformations is from congenital to advanced age. Sex distribution varies from equal to an overall male preponderance for haemangiomas; gastrointestinal vascular malformations appear more frequent in women {18636349;21738815}.

HIHs are the most common mesenchymal hepatic tumours in infants and children {25241093}. HIH is associated with female sex, multigestational pregnancy, low birth weight, and prematurity {8804322;10403856;12472344;17307549;17891692;18429772;24641194}.

The true incidence of HCH is unknown. HCH and HIH combined account for 12% of all paediatric liver tumours. HCH is more common in girls and is rarely associated with rapidly involuting cutaneous haemangioma {19159729;26223454}.

Hepatic cavernous haemangiomas are the most common benign hepatic tumours in adults {18814083}, with a prevalence in the general population as high as 20% in autopsy studies {3950039}. They can occur at all ages but are most found in women in the third to fifth decades of life.

Etiology

In most of these lesions, the etiology is unknown.

Pathogenesis

Many lesions harbour somatic mutations affecting PI3K/mTOR or RAS/MAPK signalling pathways. Endogenous and exogenous estrogen exposure is a potential pathophysiological mechanism for development and progression of hepatic cavernous haemangiomas {15306599}.

Venous malformations: Most are sporadic and associated with somatic mutations in TEK (TIE2) or PIK3CA {29217067}. Venous malformations in blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome is characterized by dual (cis) mutations in TEK {27519652}.

Arteriovenous malformations: The majority harbour somatic mutations in MAP2K1; less frequently, mutations in KRAS, HRAS, or BRAF have been reported {28190454;32799314;31637524;29461977}.

Infantile haemangiomas: No specific recurrent genetic alterations have been reported.

Hepatic congenital haemangiomas: These are associated with somatic missense mutations in GNAQ and GNA11, with variant allele frequencies ranging from 3% to 33% {27058448}. A subset also harbours PIK3CA mutations {32115227}.

Anastomosing haemangiomas and hepatic small vessel neoplasms: Most show somatic activating mutations in GNAQ, GNA11, and GNA14 {28084343;29574926;29975248}.

Hepatic cavernous haemangiomas: Some cases have been associated with recurrent somatic mutations in GJA4 {33912852}.

Diffuse hepatic and splenic haemangiomatosis (in adults): A CTC1 mutation has been reported in a single case {36761951}.

Macroscopic appearance

Haemangiomas and vascular malformations of the GI tract and liver have different macroscopic appearances depending on anatomical location and type. GI tract lesions present as polypoid or intraluminal lesions, or as poorly defined, infiltrative lesions that sometimes involve the muscularis propria and surrounding structures. Hepatic examples most often form localized masses, with exceptions. They are usually purplish-red and can have a spongy consistency, or they may be firmer when containing thrombi or phleboliths.

Histopathology

The variety of names for GI tract haemangiomas reflects the morphological heterogeneity of overlapping entities {21738815}. Haemangiomas and vascular malformations comprise proliferations of malformed vessels within the mucosa or submucosa. Reactive congestion of normal mucosal capillaries away from the lesion is typical {21738815}.

Histopathological features of mass-forming venous and arteriovenous malformations are similar to those of their soft tissue counterparts. Mucosal ulceration can be present. Dieulafoy lesion is a type of gastrointestinal vascular anomaly characterized by a tortuous enlarged vessel that protrudes through the lining of the GI tract, typically the stomach.

Anastomosing haemangioma is a rare benign vascular neoplasm that was initially described in the genitourinary tract {19606014}; it can also arise in the GI tract and liver. It is composed of anastomosing small capillary-like vessels with mild endothelial atypia and endothelial cell hob nailing. Vascular thrombi are relatively frequent, and hyaline globules and extramedullary haematopoiesis are characteristic. The anastomosing growth pattern can resemble angiosarcoma, but the lesions are typically relatively circumscribed and lack severe atypia {23887160}; hepatic examples can be infiltrative (see below).

GI tract haemangiomas and vascular malformations show expression of immunohistochemical endothelial markers CD31 and ERG in the lining endothelium, and podoplanin can be expressed in venous malformations of patients with blue rubber bleb naevus syndrome {38512910}.

HIH (formerly often designated as infantile haemangioendothelioma) comprises well-defined proliferations of numerous small, capillary-like vascular channels that are particularly prominent at the periphery. The channels are lined by plump endothelial cells, which are usually present as single layers. The vessels are surrounded by small amounts of compact or loose fibrous stroma. More centrally, particularly in larger lesions, there are frequent larger cavernous vessels lined by flattened endothelium with fibrosis of the surrounding stroma, corresponding to tumour regression. Characteristic findings include entrapment of hepatocytes and small bile ducts peripherally, as well as areas of extramedullary haematopoiesis. The lining endothelial cells express CD31, CD34, and von Willebrand factor (factor VIII–related antigen). GLUT1 is typically diffusely and strongly positive and helps exclude vascular malformations, as well as most other benign vascular neoplasms that do not express this marker {14991538;28552538}. Expression of GLUT1 indicates that a lesion is likely to involute over time.

HCH is composed of variably sized aggregates or lobules of capillary-type vessels lined by plump to hobnailed endothelial cells and surrounded by pericytes. As the tumour involutes, the lobules are replaced by fibrous stroma. The intervascular stroma is fibrotic with entrapped hepatocytes that are occasionally immunoreactive for glypican-3 (GPC3), dispersed bile ducts, foci of extramedullary haematopoiesis, and occasional dilated sinusoids. Central necrosis, macrophages (occasionally haemosiderin-laden), dystrophic calcification, chronic inflammatory cells, and dysplastic veins with thrombi are frequently observed. Lesional endothelial cells are immunoreactive for CD31, CD34, and WT1, and negative for GLUT1 {15018449}. The non-involuting cases lack necrosis, haemorrhage, fibrosis, and haemosiderin.

Hepatic cavernous haemangiomas consist of proliferations of blood-filled vascular channels of varying sizes, lined by single layers of flattened endothelial cells and separated by fibrous septa of varying thicknesses. The surrounding fibrous stroma may contain small, arborizing vessels. Although they are macroscopically well defined, they may have an irregular interface with the liver parenchyma, and dilated, blood-filled haemangioma-like vessels can be present 1–20 mm beyond the main lesion {16584396}. The vessel walls are predominantly composed of collagen, with some elastic fibres and smooth muscle. Associated organizing thrombi and infarction, with variable fibrosis and calcification, may be seen.

In sclerosed hepatic haemangiomas, most or all lesional vessels are occluded, accompanied by surrounding fibrosis, increased elastic fibres, and calcifications. The vessels may only be discernible with elastic stains.

Diffuse hepatic haemangiomatosis is extremely rare, with extensive replacement of liver parenchyma by diffuse and multiple vascular nodules, and some cases involving multiple organs {36761951}. Diffuse or multiple lesions must be distinguished from peliosis hepatis and hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.

Hepatic small vessel neoplasm is a hepatic variant of anastomosing haemangioma, exhibiting benign behaviour, but it differs by its infiltrative growth pattern. These neoplasms occur in adults and are relatively small (average: 21 mm), infiltrative proliferations of small, thin-walled vessels lined by flattened to plump, ovoid, hobnail endothelial cells, with occasional extramedullary haematopoiesis. The vessels can infiltrate around portal tracts and between hepatic plates. Papillary growth or multilayering, cellular atypia, mitotic activity, and necrosis are absent {27090685}, as are features of hepatic cavernous haemangioma. These lesions can mimic angiosarcoma, and Ki-67 proliferation index is a helpful tool in discriminating them. A Ki-67 labelling index > 10% suggests angiosarcoma (mean Ki-67 proliferation index for hepatic anastomosing haemangioma and hepatic angiosarcoma: 3.7% and 42.8%, respectively) {27090685}. Recurrence or metastasis has not been reported, although complete resection and clinical follow-up are recommended.

 

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genetic testing is not routinely required for the diagnosis of vascular anomalies of the liver and GI tract, but it can be valuable in characterizing indeterminate cases (see Pathogenesis).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphology based on the mass-forming neoplasm / mass-forming vascular malformation; low to absent endothelial cell proliferation; endothelial cells lacking cellular atypia; GLUT1 expression by immunohistochemistry confirms the diagnosis of infantile haemangioma.

Desirable: endothelial differentiation by immunohistochemistry; genetic testing in selected cases.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis is related to the specific type of lesion and extension of the disease.

The typical course of HIH is of postnatal proliferation followed by maturation and childhood involution, but these lesions can be life-threatening when associated with congestive heart failure and/or consumptive coagulopathy {18022426;24689756}. The overall survival rate is approximately 70%, with deaths tending to occur during the initial presenting episode. Adverse risk factors include associated congestive heart failure, jaundice, multiple lesional nodules, and the absence of cavernous differentiation {8020903}. If the proliferative phase continues beyond 2 years of age, there is a greater risk of progression to angiosarcoma {24740626;28521077}.

Most HCHs undergo spontaneous involution (90% reduction in size in 13 months), leaving only a small remnant. Most lesions do not require any treatment {19159729}, but a small subset of large lesions may result in morbidity and even mortality due to intratumoural haemorrhage, hypofibrinogenaemia, and thrombocytopenia, or high-output cardiac failure from arteriovenous or portohepatic venous shunting. Some cases undergo only partial involution or show no involution {30244993}.

Hepatic cavernous haemangiomas do not undergo malignant transformation.
