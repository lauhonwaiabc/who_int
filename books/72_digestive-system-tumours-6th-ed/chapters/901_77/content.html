9.0.1 Tumours of the liver and intrahepatic bile ducts: Introduction

This chapter concerns epithelial neoplasms of the liver. There are separate chapters and sections on section neuroendocrine, section mesenchymal, section germ cell, and section haematolymphoid neoplasias for all sites of the digestive tract, including also several tumour entities that predominantly arise in the liver. Hepatic metastases and carcinoma of unknown primary are also described separately (see the sections on section metastases). The many tumour-forming non-neoplastic lesions of the liver are not specifically addressed, with the singular exception of focal nodular hyperplasia, because of its histological and imaging differential diagnostic relevance.
Many refinements to the fifth-edition WHO classification of liver tumours have been made in this sixth edition, in part supported by novel molecular findings. Hepatocellular adenoma represents a convincing example of consequent morphology-supervised molecular profiling, making it an almost completely morphomolecularly subtyped neoplasia, including the newly defined sonic hedgehog hepatocellular adenoma. Subtyping of hepatocellular adenoma has gained high clinical relevance and has fuelled implementation of refined morphological criteria and molecular testing in routine diagnostics.
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), which was one single entity in the fifth edition, has been divided into two entities, namely large duct iCCA, histomorphologically and biologically resembling extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and small duct iCCA, which shares etiological, pathogenetic, and imaging features with hepatocellular carcinoma. These two entities have very different etiologies, molecular alterations, growth patterns, and clinical characteristics, illustrating the necessity to balance anatomically and (histo)pathogenetically based classifications. It will also be necessary to implement the change in classification into clinical research and study protocols. The definition and distinct features of combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma supported by molecular findings have also become clearer.
Nevertheless, primary liver cancer shows a spectrum of differentiation, in some cases making its strict typing arbitrary; the authors have addressed this aspect by emphasizing the diagnostically relevant definitions, considering genomic data, and aiming to focus on distinct, primarily morphological classification criteria applicable in most institutions worldwide.
New findings have led to the definition and acceptance of novel distinct neoplastic precursor lesions of the biliary tract, such as intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasm and intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm of the bile ducts; these entities occur in the extrahepatic and large intrahepatic bile ducts. The mass-forming premalignant bile duct lesions are described in detail in the chapter on section tumours of the gallbladder and extrahepatic bile ducts and are only listed in this chapter, with the exception of mucinous cystic neoplasm, which almost exclusively occurs in the intrahepatic biliary tree.
Liver neoplasms exhibit several unique and specific aspects of clinical, diagnostic, and research impact. Hepatocellular carcinoma is not just one of the most frequent malignancies worldwide; it is the paradigm of cancer induced by infection and by metabolic and toxic agents linked to chronic necroinflammation, while genetic cancer syndromes do not play a recognizable role in hepatocarcinogenesis. Although these findings have been translated into primary and secondary preventive measures, as well as model systems of disease, the prognosis for clinically apparent hepatocellular carcinoma remains poor. On the other hand, iCCA stands out as a malignancy with a high percentage of cases showing genetic alterations and offering targets for systemic treatment approaches.
