2.1.2.3 Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Definition

Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm displaying squamous cell differentiation characterized by keratinocyte-type cells with intercellular bridges and/or keratinization.

ICD-O coding

80700/3 Squamous cell carcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B70.1 Squamous cell carcinoma of oesophagus

Related terminology

Sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma

Acceptable: carcinosarcoma; pseudosarcomatous squamous cell carcinoma; squamous cell carcinoma with a spindle cell component.

Subtype(s)

Verrucous squamous cell carcinoma (80510/3); sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma (80741/3); basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (80830/3)

Localization

Squamous cell carcinoma is located most commonly in the middle third of the oesophagus, followed by the lower third {28725168;17823842;10903602}. In a large Japanese series, slightly more than half (55%) of squamous cell carcinomas occurred in the middle oesophagus, 32% in the lower oesophagus, and 14% in the upper oesophagus {10903602}.

Clinical features

Early-stage disease is generally asymptomatic and discovered incidentally or during surveillance in patients with oesophageal squamous dysplasia/intraepithelial neoplasia. If present, its symptoms may include heartburn and oesophageal discomfort. The most common clinical symptom is dysphagia, which usually indicates advanced disease. Other symptoms associated with advanced tumours include chest pain, painful swallowing, and weight loss {39550174}. Upper oesophageal tumours may be associated with hoarseness due to recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement.

The gold standard for diagnosis is upper GI tract endoscopy and biopsy for histological analysis. Any persistent ulceration or refractory stricture in the oesophagus should raise concern for malignancy. Image-enhanced endoscopy including narrow-band imaging and chromoendoscopy may help identify early-stage lesions by highlighting an abnormal vascular pattern of intrapapillary capillary loop or lack of Lugol’s iodine epithelial uptake associated with neoplasia {26376349;27807370}. EUS is useful for evaluating the depth of invasion into the oesophageal wall and may reveal regional lymph node metastasis; however, it is unreliable in diffusely infiltrating disease. Endoluminal excision, either endoscopic mucosal resection or endoscopic submucosal dissection for lesions > 20 mm, is the best staging procedure for determining the depth of invasion in superficially invasive disease. CT imaging is the primary staging tool for advanced disease and for assessing treatment response following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, while MRI and 18F-FDG PET provide complementary information {39550174;26376349}.

Epidemiology

Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is the seventh leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide {38572751;35671803}. There is marked geographical variation in the incidence of disease among regions and even within individual countries. The highest incidence rates (> 50 cases per 100 000 person-years) are found in the Asian oesophageal cancer belt, which stretches from eastern to central Asia. Intermediate rates (10–50 cases per 100 000 person-years) are observed along the Indian coast of Africa, in southern Brazil and Uruguay, and in parts of the Caribbean {38572751;35671803;18679388}. The incidence of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma has been decreasing in North American and European countries as well as Japan over the past decades, whereas the incidence of oesophageal adenocarcinoma has been increasing. Overall, oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma has a male prevalence, with the highest M:F ratio in Japan and some North American and European countries, but not in high-incidence nations such as China and the Islamic Republic of Iran {18679388;38572751;35671803}. The median age at diagnosis is 67.5 years {29950901}. Multiple synchronous or metachronous squamous cell carcinomas can occur in the oesophagus and other parts of the aerodigestive tract (e.g. the oral cavity and oropharynx). This finding may be related to the frequent exposure in these regions to risk factors such as tobacco smoke and alcohol consumption, which drive widespread field effect {8655677;10896918;30337457}.

Etiology

The etiology of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is multifactorial, and the contribution of various factors varies between populations. The main risk factors are presented in #49884Box 2.02.

Pathogenesis

Oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma develops through stepwise accumulation of genetic abnormalities {30337457;28052061}. TP53 mutation is a key early driver mutation {28757263}. The key genetic abnormalities identified in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma {26376349;28823862;28757263;34663923} are summarized in #49882Box 2.03.

Macroscopic appearance

Early-stage lesions are often slightly elevated (Paris 0-IIa), flat (Paris 0-IIb), or slightly depressed (Paris 0-IIc), although many patients show a combination of the endoscopic patterns. Ulcerated (Paris 0-III) lesions are associated with a high risk of deep invasion. Advanced squamous cell carcinoma often presents as an ulcerated or stricturing mass lesion {38568243}.

Histopathology

Grading

Grading is based on the degree of cytological atypia and mitotic activity, and the presence of keratinization.

Grade 1 (well-differentiated) squamous cell carcinoma contains enlarged cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and keratin pearl production. Cytological atypia is minimal, and the mitotic count is low. The invasive margin is pushing, and the cells remain well ordered.

Grade 2 (moderately differentiated) squamous cell carcinoma has evident cytological atypia and the cells are less ordered. Mitotic figures are easily identified. There is usually surface parakeratosis, but keratin pearl formation is infrequent.

Grade 3 (poorly differentiated) squamous cell carcinoma consists predominantly of basal-like cells forming nests, which may show central necrosis. The tumour nests consist of sheets or pavement-like arrangements of tumour cells, with occasional parakeratotic or keratinizing cells.

A three-tiered system as per the above is commonly applied; however, a two-tiered system (grade 1–2 vs grade 3) may be clinically relevant, because the pathological distinction between grade 1 and grade 2 shows high interobserver variation.

Therapy effects

Systemic neoadjuvant management with radiotherapy can alter both the tumour cells and the peritumoural stroma with macroscopic tumour regression. Cellular changes include nuclear enlargement or shrinkage, nuclear vacuolation, apoptosis, and necrosis. Keratin released from the dying cells may accumulate, undergo dystrophic calcification, and elicit a giant cell reaction. A neutrophilic or chronic inflammatory cell response may be seen. There is fibrosis and sometimes stromal elastosis. Vascular alterations may include myxohyaline intimal proliferation, telangiectasia, organizing thrombi, and obliterating endarteritis, as well as reactive endothelial cells. Stromal cells with marked reactive atypia are frequently observed.

The extent of tumour regression is an important prognostic factor. It is graded on histological examination by comparing the amount of residual tumour with the amount of therapy-induced fibrosis or estimating the residual tumour. Widely used tumour regression grades are the Mandard system, the Becker system, the College of American Pathologists (CAP) system based on a modified Ryan system, and the Japan Esophageal Society grading system (see #50070Table 2.02 in section Adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction).

Subtypes

Verrucous squamous cell carcinoma {12757157;29748561;27310830} is a subtype often arising in the setting of chronic injury, oesophagitis, or previous oesophageal injury. Most cases are identified in the lower third of the oesophagus, as a protuberant mass. An association with HPV has been reported {28052061;38726899;37792307}. The tumour most commonly shows minimal cytological atypia, minimal mitotic activity, and surface papillary projections. Keratinization occurs abruptly, with no intervening granular cell layer, and cup-like collections of keratin are present at the base of the papillary structures. The invasive front is characterized by broad bulbous pushing projections. The tumour is slow-growing, and metastases are uncommon. Other very well differentiated squamous carcinomas are difficult to diagnose on superficial biopsies, which are often superinfected with Candida spp. Extrapolating from observations on carcinoma cuniculatum, which shows a burrowing pattern of invasion, biopsy criteria to diagnose squamous carcinoma in this setting include the detection of seven or more of the following: hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, dyskeratosis, deep keratinization, intraepithelial neutrophils, neutrophilic microabscess, focal cytological atypia, and koilocyte-like cells {23348898}.

Sarcomatoid squamous cell carcinoma typically has a polypoid growth pattern (type 0-I). Microscopically, there is a biphasic pattern of neoplastic squamous epithelium and spindle or pleomorphic sarcomatous cells harbouring mesenchymal differentiation. Squamous elements are typically well to moderately differentiated or may occasionally be carcinoma in situ alone. The sarcomatous component is a high-grade malignancy, which may show heterologous differentiation {21732788;38512393}.

Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma shows a marked male predominance and accounts for approximately 5% of all oesophageal cancers {15387711;19738459;9149011}. Unlike similar tumours in the oropharynx, oesophageal basaloid squamous cell carcinoma has no association with HPV infection {19738459}. The tumours show a solid or nested growth pattern of basaloid cells, sometimes with central comedonecrosis and occasionally with pseudoglandular or cribriform formations. Deposits of pale eosinophilic, basement membrane–like materials are frequently observed in the solid or nested areas. It may also include duct-like structures that are interpreted as ductal differentiation {18847449;37462266}. This tumour morphology must be distinguished from the rare primary adenoid cystic carcinoma {15387711} (see section Oesophageal mucoepidermoid, adenoid cystic, and adenosquamous carcinomas). Areas of squamous cell carcinoma in situ or conventional invasive squamous cell carcinoma are commonly found. Immunohistochemical exclusion of neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) or malignant melanoma may be opportune.

Differential diagnosis

In small biopsy specimens, it may be difficult to differentiate conventional squamous cell carcinoma from other subtypes of squamous cell carcinoma, as well as from undifferentiated carcinoma or adenocarcinoma (see section Oesophageal undifferentiated carcinoma, including those with SWI/SNF alterations, and section Adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction).

Cytology

Cytological sampling using directed mucosal brushings and blind sampling using techniques including sponge sampling may be efficacious for screening in high-risk populations and facilitate primary diagnosis of early squamous neoplasia, although the evidence is still developing {28778650;33128785}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: histological evidence of invasion of neoplastic squamous epithelium.

Staging

The staging of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for this site [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]] [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. Depth of invasion provides a clinically relevant distinction between early-stage disease, which can be curatively treated endoscopically, and advanced disease, which necessitates surgical management. Lesions limited to the lamina propria without lymphovascular invasion carry a negligible risk of regional lymph node metastasis and are curable by local endoluminal resection, whereas lesions invading the muscularis mucosae carry an increased risk of locoregional lymph node metastasis (see #49883Table 2.03). For these patients, complete staging and consideration of additional treatments (chemotherapy and/or surgery) or strict endoscopic follow-up should be undertaken on an individual basis in a multidisciplinary discussion. Risk of locoregional lymph node metastasis increases with submucosal vertical invasion depth measured from the deepest fibre of the muscularis mucosae. Deep submucosal invasion (> 200 μm) carries a high risk of locoregional metastasis and necessitates definitive surgical management (see #49883Table 2.03) {23399555;32072683;19903192}. Lymphovascular invasion is a risk factor for lymph node metastasis in early-stage carcinoma and is known to be associated with poorer outcomes {39589535;36152081}. High-grade squamous dysplasia frequently extends along submucosal gland ducts into submucosal gland complexes, which should be distinguished from bona fide vertical invasion {33974941;25434658}.

Prognosis and prediction

Despite advances in the screening and treatment of oesophageal cancer, a considerable proportion of patients remain ineligible for curative treatment, and more than half of patients with locally advanced cancer treated with curative intent will inevitably have uncontrolled tumour recurrence {39550174}. The most important prognostic factor is staging, which is used to determine the most appropriate treatment regimen for a given patient. For patients with locally advanced disease, the first line of treatment involves either neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and surgery or neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by immunotherapy (for patients without pathological complete response after neoadjuvant therapy), and postoperative radiation for patients without R0 resection. There is no global consensus on how to define an involved circumferential margin in surgical specimens, whether as a margin of < 1 mm or as the presence of tumour cells at the resection edge {22161989}. Overall survival increases with wider distance to the circumferential margin {28697493}.

Superficial disease, which accounts for as many as 40% of all cases in Japan, is associated with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 87%. The 5-year survival rate with clinical stage IV is < 25% {36152081}.

Patients with metastatic disease at presentation and low PDL1 expression have been treated with systemic chemoimmunotherapy; however, the survival benefit in this group is less pronounced than that in individuals with high PDL1 expression {36473145}. Given the high recurrence potential of oesophageal cancer, ongoing surveillance is essential for timely treatment of recurrence.

Genetic factors

·    TP53 mutations are found in 59–93% of all cases {24670651;24686850;25151357}; in most cases, this is a key driver mutation developing at the stage of intraepithelial neoplasia (preinvasive carcinoma)

·    Mutations in other cell-cycle regulatory genes, such as CDKN2A (P16), RB1, NFE2L2, CHEK1, and CHEK2, have also been identified {24670651;24686850;25151357}, as have amplifications of cell-cycle regulatory genes (CCND1, CDK4, CDK6, and MDM2)

·    Amplification or mutation of EGFR in 19% of tumours and alterations of PIK3CA, PTEN, or PIK3R1, all of which are believed to activate the PI3K pathway, in 24% of tumours {28052061}

·    Single-base substitutions 2 and 13, suggesting that APOBEC activation is a crucial step in tumour development {34663923}

·    Chromosome aneuploidy (in particular, gains in chromosomes 3, 10, 12, and 20) {28757263;28052061}

Epigenetic factors

·    DNA methylation, chromatin modification, RNA editing, and loss of genomic imprinting are common in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma {28052061;28757263}

Inherited factors

·    Tylosis, an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a missense mutation in RHBDF2, results in overactivation of the EGFR signalling pathway {22265016}

·    Fanconi anaemia, an autosomal recessive disorder, affects the Fanconi anaemia pathway, which is normally active in DNA repair {19686080}

Tobacco usea

·    Smoking or chewing is the most important risk factor in high-income countries {39550174;18679388;28823862}; much weaker factor in low-incidence areas

·    Risk is dependent on both intensity and duration of use, with cessation being associated with decreasing risk after 5 years and a return to the baseline risk level of a lifelong non-smoker after 10–20 years

·    Tobacco-specific nitrosamines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are believed to be the main carcinogenic substances {28823862}

Alcohol consumptiona

·    Consumption of > 3 drinks per day is associated with a risk almost 5 times that in individuals who consume ≤ 1 drink per day {18679388;28823862}

·    The mechanism of carcinogenesis remains uncertain, but acetaldehyde, a metabolite of ethanol, is a known carcinogen capable of causing DNA abnormalities and structural and functional alterations to protein {28823862}

·    ALDH2 and ALDH1A1 mutations, which are found almost exclusively in eastern Asian populations, lead to the accumulation of acetaldehyde following the consumption of alcohol. These mutations are associated with a dose-dependent increase in the risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma. For example, heterozygous and homozygous ALDH2 mutations, respectively, are associated with risks 4 times and 13 times that in individuals without ALDH2 mutation {17674367}

Drinking very hot beveragesb

·    Drinking hot beverages such as tea or maté appears to be an important risk factor in Uruguay, southern Brazil, northern Argentina, the United Republic of Tanzania, and the Islamic Republic of Iran {18679388;28823862}

Genetic factors

·    Several genetic diseases (e.g. tylosis, a keratosis disorder caused by an autosomal dominant mutation in RHBDF2 at 17q25.3 {18679388;22265016}, and Fanconi anaemia {28823862})
