15.0.2.1 Liver metastases

Definition

Metastases are tumours arising at any site via the local or distant spread of carcinomas, neuroectodermal tumours (including melanoma), germ cell tumours, or sarcomas, discontinuous from the primary tumour.

ICD-O coding

Malignant neoplasm, metastatic (Code according to histology of the tumour.)

ICD-11 coding

2D80.0 Malignant neoplasm metastasis in liver

2D80.1 Malignant neoplasm metastasis in intrahepatic bile duct

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The liver, in addition to being the most common site of metastases, is also the first and only area of spread in 30–40% of patients with gastrointestinal cancer {18517275}. Metastasis can occur as a single tumour nodule, oligometastatic lesions (e.g. liver metastasis of colorectal cancer, renal cell carcinoma metastasis), or a multinodular or diffuse multiorgan manifestation, depending on the tumour type and the organs involved {18517275}.

Haematolymphoid neoplasms may show liver involvement as part of primary systemic disease or discrete nodular involvement, but the term “metastasis” is not used for these neoplasms.

Clinical features

Cases of liver metastasis outnumber primary liver carcinomas by a factor of 2.5–40 {17293303}. Hepatic metastasis plays a specific role because of its anatomical, diagnostic, clinical, and therapeutic peculiarities. The liver is the primary and most common site of haematogenous metastasis from all organs drained by the portal venous system (most prominently the colon). The lesions display the full spectrum of single-nodule, oligonodular, and diffuse metastasis. Intraorgan metastasis is a clinically relevant primary dissemination step for primary liver carcinomas such as hepatocellular carcinoma and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (unlike for carcinomas of other organs). The clinical signs depend on the type and extent of the hepatic manifestations. The most common symptoms include various degrees of abdominal pain followed by weight loss, jaundice, bleeding, and loss of liver functions. Some metastatic malignancies are associated with defined paraneoplastic symptoms.

Clinical and diagnostic consequences largely depend on the therapeutic options. A single metastasis or single-organ involvement may be treated by resection or other locoregional therapies (e.g. endoradiotherapy for hepatic metastases of colorectal cancer) {17925551}. Otherwise, systemic therapies such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy, as well as molecularly targeted therapies, may be appropriate, depending on the tumour type. Specific molecular pathology diagnostics are often necessary to determine potential molecular targets {18097131} and may in some cases contribute to the definition of the respective tumour entity. Liver metastases are typically amenable to biopsy and further diagnostics for management purposes (including targeted therapies).

While the majority of liver metastases are from known primaries {27421135}, cancer of unknown primary site (CUP) accounts for < 5% of all cancer cases {11818207;21968687}. However, because of its high mortality rate, its relative contribution to cancer death is higher. There is no sex predilection. History of smoking is a risk factor. CUP typically presents with a short history and progressed metastatic disease, which may show an atypical pattern of distribution. Neuroendocrine and squamous cell carcinomas are managed differently from most cases of adenocarcinoma and undifferentiated CUP, which are usually managed with platinum-based combination chemotherapy {26314775} or molecularly targeted treatment. Efforts to determine the primary site depend on the clinical constellation and diagnostic options. In adenocarcinoma type of CUP, the possibility of an intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma should always be considered. In neuroendocrine CUP, the very rare entity of primary neuroendocrine tumour (NET) of the liver should be considered {34152286}.

Epidemiology

In a SEER database from 2.4 million cancer patients, 5.14% of cancer patients presented with synchronous liver metastases. The vast majority of liver metastases are adenocarcinomas (75%), most frequently originating in the colorectum (46%), followed by pancreas (10%) and breast cancer (8%) {32562887;27421135}. For colorectal cancer, the liver is the most frequently affected organ in metastatic disease, varying from 73% in adenocarcinomas to 32% in signet-ring cell carcinomas {24504447}.

Etiology

See the respective primary tumour section.

Pathogenesis

Metastases from the gastrointestinal system preferentially occur in the liver because of drainage by the portal venous system. Several tumour entities are associated with unusual and more frequent than expected hepatic metastasis; for example, uveal melanomas frequently and preferentially spread to the liver, most likely due to a favourable environment {30910033}.

Macroscopic appearance

The size, number, location, and macroscopic appearance of metastatic liver lesions depend mainly on the type of tumour, the stage of disease, the type and extent of preoperative treatment administered, and the patient’s condition (e.g. immune response).

Histopathology

Most metastatic lesions are histologically similar to the primary tumour, but loss and even changes of differentiation may occur in metastasis, especially under therapeutic pressure or hypoxic conditions. Metastases of tumours with mixed differentiation may show only part of differentiation spectrum of the primary tumour.

For resection of liver metastases (see also #50118Box 15.01), it is important to determine the number and size of lesions {28858385}, the growth pattern {35996090}, the extent of invasion, and involvement of resection margins {36684119;34132929;39183490}. Locoregional extension, by vascular, biliary tract, or perineural invasion, should also be included {36440692;34796521;33165892;29203074}. If systemic treatment has been delivered before resection of these metastases, scoring of the regression grade is possible {24045448}.

Cytology

Cytological evaluation follows the criteria for the respective primary tumours.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Depending on the primary tumour type, liver metastases may require molecular pathological analysis for various reasons. The typing of certain tumours, such as sarcomas and paediatric neoplasms, can critically depend on molecular analysis. If the link between a metastasis and one or more primary tumours is in question, molecular analysis may help demonstrate a clonal relationship. In some cases, typical molecular alterations may support the determination of the primary site and should be mentioned in the diagnostic report. In some cases of presumed adenocarcinoma, CUP-specific molecular alterations (FGFR2 rearrangements, IDH-family mutations) may permit reclassification as small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma.

Predictive molecular tumour analysis is a rapidly evolving field, with high relevance for liver metastases. The molecular markers to be analysed depend on the tumour type and the available therapeutic options. Entity-agnostic molecular testing (e.g. analysis of microsatellite instability, tumour mutation burden, and several translocations) is also evolving in the context of various clinical trials and immuno-oncological therapies. Molecular pathological analysis can facilitate the detection of hereditary tumour syndromes, in particular in younger patients and in the setting of co-occurrence of different, especially uncommon, tumour types.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: for metastasis: sufficient morphology, immunohistochemistry, and clinical correlation to be confident of the primary site of origin; for CUP: primary site of origin not identified after comprehensive clinical, radiological, and immunohistochemical assessment.

Desirable: advanced molecular testing may be beneficial in selected cases based on local resources.

Staging

The TNM staging of metastatic disease in the liver follows the criteria established for the respective tumour entities. Metastatic cancers are considered usually stage IV. There is no specific staging system for CUP.

Prognosis and prediction

Although in general metastatic disease is associated with a poor prognosis, recent developments show that in an increasing number of cases, treatment results in favourable outcomes. Oligometastatic disease, resectability (e.g. of liver metastases of colorectal cancer), and the presence of specific molecular therapeutic target structures (depending on the tumour type) are positive prognostic and predictive factors. Microsatellite instability generally indicates better prognosis (even in metastatic disease) and favourable response to immuno-oncological treatment. The presence of microsatellite instability in metastatic lesions varies widely among tumour entities, ranging from virtual absence (e.g. in hepatocellular carcinoma) to substantial proportions (e.g. in colorectal cancer).

Prognosis depends on the extent of metastatic involvement of the liver (which may determine operability) and other sites, the patient’s general health condition, the treatment options available, and the response to therapy. A lower frequency of hepatic metastasis has been reported in the setting of cirrhosis, but it is unclear whether this is attributable to a higher frequency of primary liver tumours, increased mortality due to cirrhosis, surveillance bias, a (so far not demonstrated) protective effect of cirrhosis itself, or altered intrahepatic and portal venous blood flow {25378650}. Experimental evidence of metastasis promotion by steatohepatitis has been reported.

CUP has a poor prognosis {39145069}, with reported median survival times of 6–9 months; mean survival times < 1–2 years, attributable to therapeutic progress, have been reported in selected cohorts. Prognostic factors include the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, extent of organ involvement, amenability to locoregional treatment, and type of CUP. Neuroendocrine and squamous CUPs have a better prognosis than other types.
