15.0.2.2.1 Peritoneal metastatic non-appendiceal tumours

Definition

Peritoneal metastases from non-appendiceal tumours are secondary malignant tumour localizations on the peritoneal lining of the abdominal cavity; the histopathological appearances and neoplastic growth properties vary depending on the primary tumour type and site.

ICD-O coding

Malignant neoplasm, metastatic (Code according to histology of the tumour.)

ICD-11 coding

2D91 & XH8D74 Peritoneal carcinomatosis

Related terminology

Acceptable: peritoneal metastases; peritoneal carcinomatosis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

All sites of the peritoneal cavity can be affected, including omentum and ovaries {21159590;31082158}. Metastatic tumour distribution is partly dependent on movement of the peritoneal fluid, which accumulates in dependent areas in the pelvic cavity due to gravity, or in sites at which ascitic fluid is resorbed, such as the greater and lesser omentum and the right hemidiaphragm, a pattern known as redistribution phenomenon {21159590}. Mobile parts of the intestine such as the small bowel may be spared early in the metastatic process.

Clinical features

The clinical features are partly dependent on the primary tumour type and site, which may influence how extensively the tumour metastasizes. The clinical features are often nonspecific, including abdominal discomfort, symptoms of intestinal obstruction, ascites, and weight loss {19325052}.

Epidemiology

In published case series, peritoneal metastases affect women more often than men, occurring usually in the fourth to seventh decades of life {28746986}, depending on the features of the primary tumour. Peritoneal metastases are described in 50–80% of patients with advanced ovarian, colorectal, pancreatic, and gastric cancers {16432354;24222942;25891650;28956065}. Appendiceal tumours most frequently present with synchronous peritoneal metastases (23%), followed by colonic cancer (3.1%) and rectal cancer (0.6%) {29576463}. For metachronous metastases, these percentages are 4.8%, 1.4%, and 0.7% respectively {29576463}. Peritoneal sarcomatosis is particularly related with recurrence of intra-abdominal sarcomas, which represent about one third of all soft tissue sarcomas {30603133}. This is more common in children than in adults {25564159}.

Etiology

Peritoneal metastases are most frequently derived from colorectal cancers and appendiceal tumours. In colorectal cancer, both mucinous carcinomas as well as signet-ring cell carcinomas are associated with an increased risk of peritoneal metastases, as is the poorly cohesive carcinoma NOS (diffuse) subtype of gastric cancer {24504447;34768402}. Additional risk factors for colorectal cancer peritoneal metastases include advanced tumour stage, right-sided location, poor differentiation, BRAF mutations, and female sex {35795042}. Molecular studies provide evidence of a direct link between presence of colorectal cancer tumour deposits and peritoneal metastases {28029327}.

Mucinous and some adenocarcinomas of other organs, such as the pancreas, stomach, biliary tract, and ovary can metastasize to the peritoneum, and rarely, the clinical presentation for metastatic mucinous carcinomas is indistinguishable from mucinous carcinoma peritonei of appendiceal origin {30531244;22406366;36700339}.

Pathogenesis

The association of peritoneal metastases with advanced tumour stage and non-radical surgery suggest that direct spill/spread or direct seeding is one of the more important pathways towards peritoneal metastases. The occurrence of peritoneal metastases from extraperitoneal organs (e.g. breast cancer, lung cancer, or melanoma) suggests that tumour cells also reach the peritoneum via the lymphatic and hematogenous systems {30139510;36700339}. In general, the patients with more aggressive tumours are at risk {35795042}, as described in Etiology. Transcriptomic studies have shown that the poor-prognosis mesenchymal subtype found in primary colorectal cancers, gastric cancers, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas is associated with peritoneal metastases {36700339}. A direct molecular link exists between peritoneal metastases and the presence of tumour deposits around colorectal cancers {39183541}, rather than with lymph node metastases.

Macroscopic appearance

Peritoneal metastases present in various sizes as either isolated larger deposits, confluent masses, or miliary lesions {25303976}, which can mimic benign inclusion cysts. Peritoneal sarcomatosis forms discrete masses of variable sizes or diffuse thickening of the peritoneal surface.

Histopathology

Histopathological appearances are according to the primary site of the malignant neoplasm. Immunohistochemical stains for organ- or tissue-specific biomarkers may help elucidate the suspected primary site when it is not clinically or radiologically known. Essential and desirable reporting elements are listed in #54210Box 15.02.

Cytology

Peritoneal metastasis material (such as ascitic fluid) that contains varying numbers of atypical epithelial cells, singly and in 3D clusters, with or without mucin, is diagnostic for peritoneal metastases.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Immunohistochemical analysis for biomarkers may elucidate the suspected primary site of origin, when it is not known.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraperitoneal deposits of cytologically malignant neoplastic epithelial cells, showing infiltrative growth, with or without extracellular mucin.

Desirable: immunohistochemical biomarker staining results consistent with suspected primary site of origin.

Staging

Staging is according to the primary site of malignancy. In general, peritoneal metastases are M1 (“PER” can be added as a specification). Intraperitoneal metastases from appendiceal tumours (all types, not restricted to pseudomyxoma peritonei) are staged as M1a. For colorectal cancer, peritoneal metastases are staged as M1b.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognostic factors depend on the primary tumour. The prognosis for cases with a colorectal origin is dependent on the extent of the metastases (as indicated by the peritoneal cancer index [PCI], since this determines the eligibility for cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy [HIPEC]) {31367152;35008221;36700339}. Both patients with a rectal primary and those with lymph node involvement have a worse outcome {32027455}. Cytoreductive surgeries with microsatellite instability have a better outcome {34887523;34783946}. Molecular analysis for treatment selection is based on the primary tumour origin.

Essential reporting elements:

·    Primary site

·    Tumour grade

·    Size and location (regional distribution to determine peritoneal cancer index)

Desirable reporting elements:

·    Immunohistochemical analysis for biomarkers consistent with suspected primary site of origin

·    Peritoneal regression grading score (PRGS) {30911613;34676284} in cases with prior therapy
