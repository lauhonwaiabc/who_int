11.0.1 Tumours of the pancreas: Introduction

The classification of epithelial neoplasms of the pancreas is primarily based on the direction of differentiation of the neoplastic cells (ductal, acinar, etc.) and on the gross configuration of the tumour (solid, cystic, or intraductal).

Most epithelial neoplasms of the pancreas have a definable direction of differentiation that recapitulates a normal cell type in the adult gland [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Ductal differentiation is reflected in the formation of lumina or true papillae by the neoplastic cells. Ductal differentiation is often, but not always, accompanied by the production of mucin, which can be visualized on routinely stained slides, with histochemical stains for mucin, or using immunohistochemical labelling for glycoproteins such as CA19-9, CEA, and mucins, in particular MUC1, MUC2, MUC5AC, and MUC6 {34615717}. Mucin production is absent in some ductal-type neoplasms, such as serous neoplasms, intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasms, and undifferentiated carcinomas. Acinar differentiation is defined by the production of pancreatic exocrine enzymes by the neoplastic cells, which can be demonstrated using immunohistochemical labelling for trypsin, chymotrypsin, lipase, and carboxyl ester hydrolase (as detected by immunolabelling using antibodies [clone 331.1] to BCL10) {23026929}. Neuroendocrine differentiation is demonstrated by immunolabelling for the general neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin, INSM1, and chromogranin A [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]] {34048981}. Primary pancreatic mesenchymal and haematolymphoid neoplasms are classified using the same criteria as in other anatomical sites. The only well-characterized neoplasm of the pancreas for which there does not appear to be a cell in the normal pancreas with an analogous direction of differentiation is the solid pseudopapillary neoplasm {26148796}.

The appearance of pancreatic neoplasms on imaging and macroscopically also contributes to their classification. The characterization of a lesion as solid is usually straightforward. Cystic appearance can occur through one of three mechanisms: (1) true cyst formation as seen in serous cystic neoplasms and mucinous cystic neoplasms; (2) ductal dilatation as occurs in intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs), intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasms, and intraductal oncocytic neoplasms; and (3) degenerative cystic change, which can occur in any typically solid neoplasm, such as invasive ductal adenocarcinoma, and which is particularly common in solid pseudopapillary neoplasms.

Nearly 90% of malignant pancreatic neoplasms in adults are invasive ductal adenocarcinomas; as such, the term “pancreatic cancer” is synonymous with ductal adenocarcinoma {34527218}. Cystic and intraductal neoplasms make up 4–5%, well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) 3–4%, and acinar cell carcinoma and other uncommon entities account for the remaining 2–3% of pancreatic neoplasms {34527218}. The increased use of cross-sectional imaging has resulted in greater detection of asymptomatic small PanNETs and of asymptomatic IPMNs {28448665;39243580;34554237;35348774;29333072}. IPMNs now constitute 60% of cyst-forming neoplasms of the pancreas, raising clinical challenges of when to observe and when to resect these lesions {29333072;18716113;33593706;28116593}.

The incidence of most pancreatic neoplasms increases with age, and most ductal adenocarcinomas occur in the elderly {33058868;34527218}. Pancreatic neoplasms are rare in childhood {12037756;29697193}. The most prevalent neoplasms in the first decade of life are pancreatoblastomas, acinar cell carcinomas, and PanNETs. In the second decade, solid pseudopapillary neoplasms, PanNETs, and acinar cell carcinomas are most prevalent {39579111;39473958;29697193}. Ductal adenocarcinomas and intraductal neoplasms are extremely rare in childhood, but make up the majority of neoplasms in adults.

Pancreatic neoplasms are driven by inherited deleterious germline variants and by acquired genetic alterations, and recent improvements in genomic sequencing have substantially increased our understanding of these genetic drivers and their clinical applications. A number of deleterious germline variants have been identified that increase the risk of pancreatic neoplasms, and the pancreatic neoplasms that arise in these patients may be uniquely targetable therapeutically {29506128;26658419;38662083;19264984;26180923;26028255}. The somatic genetic drivers of most types of pancreatic neoplasms have also been characterized, sometimes revealing patterns of genomic alterations highly characteristic of an entity (e.g. MEN1, DAXX, and ATRX mutations in well-differentiated PanNETs; PRKACA and PRKACB fusions in intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms; and GNAS and RNF43 mutations in IPMNs) {21252315;24604757;18772397;37029223;28199314;11943721;11891193;25266736;26909576;31678302}. Some somatic mutations are targetable therapeutically, highlighting the clinical value of sequencing all pancreatic malignancies {32135080}.

Transcriptomic analyses suggest new approaches to categorizing pancreatic neoplasms {39283131}. For example, basal and classic subtypes of ductal adenocarcinoma have been proposed with possible implications for responses to therapy and patient survival {39283131;35979597}. Phenotypic plasticity and heterogeneity within individual ductal adenocarcinomas are, however, common and extensive, and ductal adenocarcinomas often have a mix of basal and classic phenotypes {37311751;36459568;33051307}. The tumour microenvironment is also recognized to be important, but its characterization is not, as of yet, clinically important in day-to-day practice.

Precursors to invasive ductal adenocarcinoma include microscopic pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia, large numbers of which are present in most adults, as well as the increasingly detected macroscopic precursor neoplasms, IPMNs, intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms, intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasms, and mucinous cystic neoplasms {29333072;18716113;38693266}. All these precursors are now classified into two tiers of dysplasia, on the basis of the highest grade of dysplasia detected {26559377}. When invasive carcinoma is present in association with a macroscopic precursor lesion, each component should be diagnosed separately and the invasive component graded and staged using the parameters for invasive carcinomas (e.g. invasive well-differentiated colloid carcinoma arising in association with an IPMN).

As was true in the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours, neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the pancreas are separated into well-differentiated PanNETs and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.who.int/579. Bookref]] {30140036}. PanNETs are graded on the basis of the proliferation rate of the neoplastic cells into low (G1), intermediate (G2), and high (G3) grades. NENs with more than one direction of differentiation encompass a range of neoplasms with mixed differentiation, such as mixed ductal–neuroendocrine carcinoma and mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas. These tumours are now divided into two broad conceptual categories. Those in which the cells with differing directions of differentiation are geographically separated into discrete zones can be designated as mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs). Those in which the neoplastic cells with differing directions of differentiation are intimately admixed, or in which individual cells have more than one direction of differentiation, can be placed in the conceptual category of amphicrine-like carcinomas.

Other subtypes remain as classified in the fifth edition.
