11.1.2.2 Pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma

Definition

Acinar cell carcinoma is a malignant pancreatic neoplasm composed of epithelial cells that express exocrine enzymes. Squamoid nests are not present.

ICD-O coding

85500/3 Acinar cell carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C10.Y & XH3PG9 Acinar cell carcinoma of pancreas

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Acinar cell cystadenocarcinoma (85510/3); mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma (81542/3); mixed acinar–neuroendocrine–ductal carcinoma (81543/3); mixed acinar–ductal carcinoma (85520/3)

Localization

Acinar cell carcinomas may arise in any portion of the pancreas, though they most commonly occur in the head, followed by the tail and the body {23026929}.

Clinical features

Presenting symptoms are usually related to tumour growth and/or metastatic spread, including weight loss, abdominal pain, vomiting, and nausea. Jaundice is uncommon. Patients with extensive metastatic disease may show symptoms due to lipase hypersecretion, which include subcutaneous fat necrosis and polyarthralgia {23026929}. Rarely, particularly in young patients, increased blood levels of AFP may be observed, which can serve as a useful marker for monitoring response to therapy {10987254}.

Epidemiology

Acinar cell carcinoma is a rare neoplasm that represents only approximately 0.2–2% of pancreatic neoplasms in adults and 2.8% in children {18656619;36161533;39473958}. In adults, the average age at diagnosis is about 60 years (range: 20–88 years) {33939665;34885230;37029223;34680244}. There is a male predominance (M:F ratio: 2–2.5:1) {18656619;23026929;36161533;37029223;34680244}.

Etiology

Although most acinar cell carcinomas occur sporadically, rare cases have been reported in association with Lynch syndrome, Carney complex, familial adenomatous polyposis, and in patients with deleterious germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 variants {21900385;23026929;22516243;14586255;22042785;37607324}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of acinar cell carcinoma is complex, involving chromosomal losses and gains, alteration in APC/β-catenin pathway, oncogenic gene fusions, and mutations in genes coding for members of the homologous recombination and DNA damage response pathway. Common somatic molecular alterations found in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (KRAS), pancreatic cystic neoplasms (GNAS and RNF43), and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs) (MEN1, DAXX, and ATRX) are largely absent in acinar cell carcinoma {24293293;25266736;37266563}. Acinar cell carcinomas are characterized by significant chromosomal imbalances and frequent allelic losses and gains {24293293;25298229}. The APC/β-catenin pathway is frequently altered, primarily through somatic APC gene loss and/or promoter hypermethylation, observed in > 50% of cases {11891193;24590585}. In contrast, intragenic mutations in APC or CTNNB1, which are mutually exclusive, are observed less frequently {11891193;24590585}. The APC/β-catenin pathway is thought to play a role in early tumorigenesis contributing to increased cell proliferation {17596282}. Oncogenic gene fusions are important drivers in approximately 30% of acinar cell carcinomas. Recurrent BRAF or RAF1 rearrangements are the most prevalent, observed in up to 25% of these cancers {25266736;28884748;32358589;34614142}. Of these, SND1::BRAF fusion is the most common, while other BRAF fusion partners include HERPUD1, GATM, FARP1 (GLCC1), and AGAP3 {28884748;32358589;37266563;35949061}. RAF1 fusion partners include GATM, GOLGA4, PDZRN3, HERPUD1, and TRIM33 {32358589}. RET fusions are the third most common gene fusions, occurring in up to 7.5% of cases {31558784;32358589}. Novel gene fusions such as SEL1L::NTRK1 and ALK::KANK4 have been reported in acinar cell carcinomas in paediatric patients {33406492;35005993}. A significant proportion of acinar cell carcinomas harbour somatic and germline mutations in genes coding for members of the homologous recombination and DNA damage response pathways {25266736;22042785;31002019}. Recent studies have highlighted a strong germline component with deleterious germline pathogenic BRCA2 variants detected in 35% of patients with pure acinar cell carcinomas {27165126;25743105}. This indicates a strong familial predisposition and potential link to other BRCA-related malignancies {37607324}. Other germline pathogenic variants reported in patients with an acinar cell carcinoma include BRCA1, PALB2, ATM, and CHEK2 {37607324}. Somatic TP53 mutations and/or gene loss have been identified in 12–24% of acinar cell carcinomas {25266736;24293293;26586531;19720778;37266563;37029223}. The concomitant presence of both TP53 mutations and gene loss has been found to correlate with a worse prognosis {26586531}. Loss of CDKN2A/CDKN2B occurs in about 15% of acinar cell carcinomas {29109526;25266736;37029223}, and its expression is reduced in > 50% of cases {29721608}. SMAD4 mutations are found in about 15–20% of acinar cell carcinomas {29109526;25266736;37266563;37029223}. MYC alterations, including gene amplification and/or chromosome 8 polysomy, have been described in a subset of acinar cell carcinomas and in mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas {25298229;29721608}.

Genetic alterations characteristic of PanNETs are not observed in mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas, whereas somatic alterations of PTEN, TSC2, and NF1 have been found in mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas but not in pure acinar cell carcinomas {25266736}. PRKAR1A, a responsible gene for Carney complex {25266736}, and MYC are also mutated in mixed but not in pure acinar cell carcinomas {26586531}. BRAF rearrangements, especially SND1::BRAF fusions, occur more commonly in mixed acinar–ductal carcinomas (70–80%) than in other acinar cell carcinomas {24590585;28884748}.

Macroscopic appearance

Acinar cell carcinomas are typically well circumscribed, partially encapsulated, solid tumours with an average greatest dimension of 80–100 mm. They have a homogeneous pink to tan cut surface and a fleshy or friable consistency. Rarely, an intraductal growth simulating intraductal neoplasms has been described {17325477;23515859}. Haemorrhage and necrosis are frequent {23026929}. A rare subtype of acinar cell carcinomas characterized exclusively by variable-sized cysts is referred to as acinar cell cystadenocarcinoma {15619219}.

Histopathology

Acinar cell carcinomas are morphologically highly cellular, with scant fibrous stroma showing a lobular growth pattern, often accompanied by areas of necrosis. Tumour cells have moderate amounts of granular eosinophilic cytoplasm containing zymogen granules, which are PASD-positive. Nuclei are generally uniform and characteristically feature a single prominent nucleolus. The mitotic count is variable but generally high {23026929}.

Acinar cell carcinomas may have different architectural features. The acinar pattern is characterized by structures resembling normal acini, with basally located nuclei and sometimes with minute lumina; the glandular pattern by acinar structures with dilated lumina; the trabecular pattern by ribbons; and solid pattern by large sheets of cells without lumina. The latter two patterns closely resemble PanNETs. The most prevalent patterns are acinar and solid, although a mixture of patterns is commonly observed within a single tumour.

Rare patterns including oncocytic, spindle, clear, and pleomorphic cell types have been described {23026929}. Papillary features sometimes with intraductal extension and often associated with cyst formation have been reported {17325477;20534994}. The lack of squamoid nests is essential for differentiating acinar cell carcinoma from pancreatoblastoma (see section Pancreatoblastoma).

Immunohistochemistry plays a key diagnostic role in demonstrating the expression of exocrine enzymes. Highly specific and sensitive markers include trypsin, chymotrypsin, CPA1, CPA2, and carboxyl ester hydrolase (detected by anti-BCL10 antibody, clone 331.1) {19066953;23026929;34889867;36815573}. Acinar cell carcinomas may also express CK7, CK19, CK20, EMA, and PDX1. Nuclear expression of β-catenin is found in about 10% of cases {23026929}. Markers typically expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma, such as AFP, HepPar1, glypican-3 (GPC3), and albumin mRNA (by in situ hybridization), can be found in acinar cell carcinomas {27425386}. Scattered neuroendocrine cells positive for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and/or INSM1 can be observed but should make up < 30% of the neoplasm. A microsatellite-unstable phenotype, assessed using immunohistochemistry for MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6, has been identified in 8–14% of acinar cell carcinomas {25058881}.

Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas

Pancreatic carcinomas with mixed differentiation are rare, and in many, the primary component demonstrates acinar differentiation. Neoplastic cells with each line of differentiation must make up ≥ 30% of the tumour for it to be classified as a mixed carcinoma. The most common is mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinoma, which is defined on the basis of the immunohistochemical finding of expression of both acinar and neuroendocrine markers {8037290;32111396}. Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas can be conceptually divided into two groups. Cases with intimately admixed acinar and neuroendocrine components conceptually fit the definition of amphicrine-like carcinoma (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification). Cases in which the acinar and neuroendocrine components are distinct fit the definition of a mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN; see section Pancreatic mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms). Mixed acinar–neuroendocrine carcinomas are best regarded as a subtype of acinar cell carcinoma, because they share the latter’s clinical behaviour and genomic features {25266736;32111396}. Genome-wide epigenetic profile analysis shows that pure acinar cell carcinomas, mixed acinar cell carcinomas, and pancreatoblastomas are all similar at the epigenetic level and are distinct from neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) and ductal adenocarcinomas {34969956}.

Mixed acinar–ductal carcinomas

Mixed acinar–ductal carcinomas have mostly one of two different patterns {20182344}. Some have extensive intracellular or extracellular mucin accumulation in association with acinar elements. There may be nests of acinar cells floating in colloid-like pools of mucin, or the neoplastic elements may have a combination of typical acinar elements mixed with nests of columnar or signet-ring cells with cytoplasmic mucin. Mucin staining (with mucicarmine or Alcian blue) is positive, as is immunohistochemical labelling for trypsin, chymotrypsin, and BCL10. Other mixed acinar–ductal carcinomas have an individual gland pattern of infiltration with an associated desmoplastic stromal response (reminiscent of infiltrating ductal adenocarcinoma), but nonetheless show substantial acinar differentiation by immunohistochemistry.

Rare mixed acinar–neuroendocrine–ductal carcinomas show mixed morphology, with acinar patterns, cells with neuroendocrine differentiation, and areas simulating infiltrating ductal adenocarcinoma, with immunohistochemical staining supporting the three lines of differentiation {20182344;29085438}.

Cytology

Aspirate smears of acinar carcinomas are moderately cellular with a generally clean background, although cytoplasmic granules and naked nuclei can be observed. Neoplastic cells are arranged in irregularly shaped groups, small glandular structures, or large sheets, although isolated neoplastic cells can be seen {37469257;38973328} [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]]. Tumour cells are polygonal with abundant finely granular cytoplasm and uniform nuclei. The chromatin is coarsely clumped, with one or two evident nucleoli.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant epithelial neoplasm of the pancreas with evidence of acinar differentiation confirmed by specific markers, such as trypsin, BCL10, CPA1, or CPA2. Squamoid nests must not be present.

Staging

The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems for acinar cell carcinoma are the same as those used for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]} [[American Joint Committee on Cancer, American College of Surgeons. AJCC Cancer Staging System. Version 9. Chicago (IL): American College of Surgeons; 2020. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The survival of patients with acinar cell carcinoma is poor, with a median survival time of about 19–47 months and a 5-year survival rate of 25–43% {18656619;23026929;34023183;33939665;32541633}. Although the prognostic value of several morphological features, immunohistochemical markers, and molecular abnormalities has been investigated in recent years, only stage has proved to be a consistent and independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis {23026929}.

Patients with defects in DNA damage repair may respond to platinum-containing agents or poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors {31157963;28596308}, and a few such acinar cell carcinoma cases have been reported to respond to these agents {38733420;36523391;30407325;39772831}. In cases with BRAF alterations {25266736;28884748;34614142}, MEK inhibition may also be considered; BRAF/MEK-inhibitory therapy has been successful in controlling tumours in a few cases of acinar cell carcinoma with a BRAF rearrangement or BRAF p.Val600Glu (V600E) mutation {38922339;38340027;32843432;37266563;39772831}.
