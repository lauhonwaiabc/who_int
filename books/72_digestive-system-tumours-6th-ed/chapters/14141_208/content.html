14.1.4.1 Jejunal and ileal neuroendocrine neoplasms

Definition

Jejunoileal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are malignant epithelial neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation that arise in the jejunum and ileum.

Localization

The majority of unifocal jejunoileal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) (~77%) are located in the distal small intestine involving the terminal ileum, with the remaining found in the upper small intestine, including the jejunum and the proximal ileum (23%) {23579432;30054825;32931025}. Multifocality is a common feature, with most occurring in the distal small intestine {23579432}. Additionally, NETs can also arise in Meckel diverticula and are found in 2.2% of resected symptomatic cases {15729078}. In contrast, jejunoileal neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) and MiNENs are exceedingly rare, with limited data available in current literature {35178624}. Some population-based studies have reported on small intestinal NECs, without distinguishing between duodenal and jejunoileal cases {29211313}.

Clinical features

Most jejunoileal NETs are non-functioning and asymptomatic, with symptoms typically arising at advanced stages due to small bowel obstruction or carcinoid syndrome. Carcinoid syndrome, characterized by diarrhoea, bronchospasms, flushing, and tricuspid valve fibrosis, caused by serotonin production, is reported in 32% of patients, primarily those with metastatic disease {28238592}.

Epidemiology

The incidence of small intestinal NETs has risen steadily over the past 30 years, to 1.2 cases per 100 000 person-years clinically {28448665}. However, the incidence of jejunoileal NETs varies between ethnic groups, with a much lower incidence in Asians and pacific populations {18565894;35178624}. An autopsy study in Malmo, Sweden, reported a frequency of 1.12% digestive tract NETs in 16 294 autopsies performed in a 12-year period (1958–1969) {961424}. Jejunoileal NETs exhibit a slight male predominance. They are more commonly diagnosed in patients aged > 60 years but can occur across a wide age range (thirties to eighties) {39287239}.

Etiology

The etiology of sporadic jejunoileal NETs is largely unknown. Around 8% demonstrate mutations in CDKN1B, but whether it can be considered a driver gene mutation remains controversial, as it occurs rather late in tumour development {24185511;25586243;34737276;38123518}. Approximately 5% of patients with jejunoileal NET have an affected first-degree relative, but the genetic basis is unknown. IPMK mutations have been identified in one family, but the same gene has been found to be wildtype in 32 other families with ≥ 2 jejunoileal NETs {25865046}.

Pathogenesis

The molecular pathogenesis of sporadic jejunoileal NET is poorly understood, as driver mutations have not been clearly identified.

Jejunoileal NETs frequently show large-scale chromosomal abnormalities (i.e. whole-chromosome/whole-arm), a very low rate of somatic mutations {19458023}, and a high rate of epigenetic changes {22986706;26169971;27306880}. Typical is chromosome 18 deletion in 60–90% of jejunoileal NETs, already detectable in early lesions {11337378;11679943;15920555}. So far, there is no definitive evidence of driver/risk genes, even though candidates have been proposed {24351681;27222126;37453564;38123518}. Only one study reported driver mutations (including TP53 and RB1) in half of metastatic small intestinal NETs {32931025}. Chromosome 14 gains are typically found in advanced lesions and metastatic foci {19458023}. Mutations in CDKN1B (which encodes p27) are found in ≤ 10% of ileal NETs {24185511;25586243}. Numerous other mutations, mainly involving the mTOR, PDGFR, EGFR (HER1), and TGF-β pathways, have been described, but only in individual cases or at very low rates {23676460;26965582}.

In terms of morphology, the development of multiple minute tumours from deep crypt enterochromaffin cells (EC cells) has been described {36936198}.

Macroscopic appearance

The majority of jejunoileal tumours arise in the distal ileum and have a mean diameter of approximately 20 mm {30054825}. NETs arising in Meckel diverticula have a mean diameter of 7 mm {30706377}. Tumours usually present as mucosal/submucosal nodules with intact or slightly eroded mucosa. Deep infiltration of the muscular wall and subserosa is common. In the subserosa, a pronounced desmoplastic reaction is often observed, causing mesenteric retraction that can result in kinking of the intestine and luminal obstruction. Subserosal tissue and sclerotic mesentery frequently contain metastatic foci, representing either positive lymph nodes or mesenteric tumour deposits.

Jejunoileal NETs are multifocal in approximately 40% (range 26–56%) of cases, with the number of tumours per patient ranging from 2 to 100 {9070484;28902780;30054825;32291827}.

Histopathology

NET

Jejunoileal NETs display the classic well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]] and are composed of uniform tumour cells with round to oval nuclei with finely granular (salt-and-pepper) chromatin and plentiful amphophilic cytoplasm, typical of NETs in other sites. They usually show little pleomorphism, aside from endocrine atypia, and are composed of nests of tumour cells with peripheral palisading. In regions of sclerosis, the growth pattern may break down into single-file cells and small nests of cells. Lymphovascular and perineural invasion are common. Jejunoileal NETs often metastasize to regional lymph nodes or to mesenteric soft tissue. In the latter situation, mesenteric tumour deposits have an irregular contour and can show entrapped nerves and thick-walled blood vessels {37639446}.

NEC and MiNEN

Jejunoileal NECs and MiNENs are extremely rare, as no such tumours have been described in the jejunum or in ileum to date. If recognized, they should be characterized by the criteria provided in the introduction (section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification).

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Jejunoileal amphicrine-like carcinoma may occur as amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma also with goblet cell features {33500237;27124941}.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis reveals expression of cytokeratins (AE1/AE3 cocktail; CK8 and CK18; negativity for CK7), as well as the neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1. This includes tumours arising in Meckel diverticula. Typical for jejunoileal NETs is the diffuse expression of serotonin, CDX2, and PITX2 {36089904;38309431}. NETs of the proximal jejunum are usually serotonin negative {23579432}. Transcription factors such as TTF1, SATB2, and ISL1 are usually negative {31233624}. More than 90% of the tumours show membranous positivity for SSTR2 {12021920} with low to moderate intensity {36010956}. RB1 and p53 expression should be wildtype; aberrant staining may be useful to support a diagnosis of NEC, especially in the differential with G3 NETs {34499237}.

Grading

Jejunoileal NENs are graded using the same system used for other digestive NENs (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification). More than 95% of jejunoileal NETs fall into the G1/G2 category and have rare mitotic figures. Only 4% of NETs are in the G3 category {34499237}; they may show rare or abundant mitotic figures and minimal necrosis, but they otherwise have the same histology as low-grade jejunoileal NETs.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

NET

Essential: well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: serotonin and SSTR2 positive immunohistochemistry.

NEC

Essential: poorly differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage with either small cell or large cell carcinoma pattern; sheets or trabeculae of poorly differentiated cells; neuroendocrine marker expression; high Ki-67 proliferation index and or mitotic count.

Desirable: reduced/absent SSTR2 expression; loss of RB1 expression or abnormal p53 expression.

MiNEN

Essential: a mixed tumour composed of two morphologically recognizable components, either type ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine marker expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count to grade the NET component or to prognostically stratify the NEC component; grading of the non-neuroendocrine component.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Essential: a mixed tumour with no morphologically distinct zones of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation; ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine markers expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index.

Staging

The staging of jejunal/ileal NETs is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for this site. For NECs, MiNENs, and amphicrine-like carcinomas, the staging follows the criteria of jejunoileal carcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with jejunoileal NETs often present with metastatic disease {24384494}, which has been estimated to occur in 35% of cases in large population-based studies {18565894} and in > 60% of cases from larger referral centres {21641888;21984144;29491050}. The most common metastatic sites are locoregional lymph nodes and the liver {24384494}. However, despite this advanced presentation at the time of diagnosis, most patients have prolonged survival due to the usually low proliferation rate of the tumours. Patients with localized disease have a 5-year overall survival rate of 70–100%; in patients with distant metastases, the rate is 35–60% {20662794;21984144;29391119}. Results from outcome studies suggest that the long-term recurrence rate is approximately 50% {20662794;21641888;25196446}. Patients with nodal metastases, large mesenteric tumour deposits, extramural venous invasion, or perineural invasion have a higher risk of recurrence and/or poor outcome {29391119}.

Three molecular subgroups with potential prognostic impact have been described: jejunoileal NET with chromosome 18 loss (55%), which correlated with CpG island methylator phenotype negativity, CDKN1B mutation, and favourable prognosis; no whole-arm copy-number variation (19%), which correlated with CpG island methylator phenotype positivity and intermediate prognosis; and multiple whole-arm copy-number variations (26%), which correlated with poor prognosis {26169971}. Low levels of chromosomal instability in small intestinal NETs were found to be associated with longer patient survival {30667365}. Gain of chromosome 14 was found to be an unfavourable prognostic factor {19458023}.
