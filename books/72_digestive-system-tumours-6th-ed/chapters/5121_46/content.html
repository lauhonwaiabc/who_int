5.1.2.1 Jejunoileal adenocarcinoma

Definition

Jejunoileal adenocarcinoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm characterized by infiltrative invasion, originating in the jejunum or ileum, with glandular differentiation and/or mucin production.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B80.10 Adenocarcinoma of jejunum or ileum

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); medullary carcinoma (85100/3); signet-ring cell carcinoma (84900/3); adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); low-grade tubuloglandular carcinoma (82114/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3); carcinoma with mesenchymal differentiation (80330/3)

Localization

Jejunum and ileum

Clinical features

Adenocarcinomas of the jejunum and ileum typically present in the sixth decade of life with symptoms related to obstruction or bleeding {20609724}. Abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, and anaemia are the most common presenting symptoms {20609724;28982111}. Symptomatic presentation is more likely in jejunoileal tumours than in duodenal tumours {28982111}. Advanced stage (stage III or IV) at initial presentation occurs in about 65% of patients; the liver and peritoneum are the most common sites of distant metastasis {28982111}.

Epidemiology

The overall age-standardized incidence of small intestinal adenocarcinoma is estimated to be around 0.8 cases per 100 000 person-years in men and 0.51 cases per 100 000 person-years in women {31630251;22405637}. A median age at presentation of 50–60 years is reported, and the duodenum is the most frequently involved segment, while reported involvement of the jejunum (11–25%) and ileum (7–13%) is less common {15274064;28982111}.

Etiology

Genetic syndromes including familial adenomatous polyposis, Lynch syndrome, and Peutz–Jeghers syndrome increase the relative risk of jejunoileal adenocarcinoma, but the cumulative lifetime risk is low {35565398}. Crohn disease is an important predisposing risk factor, with a reported prevalence of 1.15/1000 patients, and the ileum is the most common (84%) site of involvement {34704127}. Patients with coeliac disease have a 14-fold higher risk of small intestinal adenocarcinoma compared with the general population {39694214}, and the jejunum is the most common site of involvement in these cases {12702783}. Environmental factors like tobacco use, alcohol, and animal fat consumption have also been reported in epidemiological studies as risk factors for small intestinal adenocarcinomas {8019367;9052748;11075867}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of adenocarcinoma of the jejunum and ileum is not well understood, because of the rarity of these tumours. Jejunal and ileal adenocarcinomas show adjacent residual adenoma in a quarter of cases, suggesting an adenoma–carcinoma pathway like in the colorectum {2380402;53095;7248908}. Dysplastic lesions resembling inflammatory bowel disease–associated colorectal dysplasia are frequently found in ileal adenocarcinomas associated with Crohn disease {10366146;25029614;28664941;33963925;37988281}. Likewise, most ileal pouch adenocarcinomas are associated with prior or synchronous pouch dysplasia, and progression of pouch dysplasia appears more likely in patients with a family history of colorectal cancer {25137416}. In contrast, precursors are rarely identified in adenocarcinomas associated with coeliac disease {9121177;28664941}.

The five most commonly altered genes in jejunoileal adenocarcinomas are TP53 (47–74%), KRAS (32–52%), APC (20–24%), PIK3CA (15–25%), and SMAD4 (10–20%), similar to alterations observed in duodenal and colorectal adenocarcinomas {28617917;29522538;35778698;38745088;38062562}. Mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency / microsatellite instability (MSI) has been reported in a variable but relatively high proportion (9–31%), with higher frequencies observed in stage II disease {28617917;32761330;35778698;37236851;38745088}. MMR deficiency can result from germline (constitutional) variants in Lynch syndrome {33046565} or from MLH1 promoter hypermethylation {16619216;27258561;28333239;30381262;33199489}.

The molecular features of Crohn disease–associated and coeliac disease–associated adenocarcinomas appear to be distinctive. Crohn disease–associated ileal adenocarcinomas harbour IDH1 mutations (almost always the Arg132Cys variant) in approximately 20% of cases and are often associated with MGMT methylation. These mutations are very rare in small bowel adenocarcinomas not associated with Crohn disease {25029120;31388669;38745088;37988281}. IDH1 alterations seem to occur early in carcinogenesis since the same mutation can be detected in the carcinoma and the adjacent dysplasia {37988281}. TP53 mutations occur in most Crohn disease–associated adenocarcinomas {31388669;38745088}. Very rare cases of EBV-positive Crohn disease–associated ileal adenocarcinomas have also been reported {28752215}.

Two main transcriptomic subtypes of coeliac disease–associated small bowel adenocarcinomas have been identified: the MSI-immune subtype and the mesenchymal subtype {31696360}. There is a high frequency of MMR deficiency / MSI (50–73%), typically due to MLH1 promoter hypermethylation {15466202;20179237;29522538;28333239}. The WNT pathway is frequently activated (> 90%) in coeliac disease–associated adenocarcinomas {28664941;31696360}, which often show APC promoter hypermethylation and LINE1 hypomethylation {20179237;28333239;39601242}.

Macroscopic appearance

Adenocarcinomas of the jejunum and ileum tend to be annular constricting, ulcerated, or occasionally polypoid lesions {53095;2380402}. They are typically solitary, large tumours (30–50 mm) {53095;2380402;38411920}. Crohn disease–associated ileal adenocarcinomas present as mass-forming lesions in only about one third of cases, despite their large size {31388669}. Poorly cohesive carcinomas often exhibit a diffuse-infiltrative appearance characterized by the thickening and rigidity of the intestinal wall {34628432}. This appearance may mimic fibroinflammatory stenosis in Crohn disease, increasing the risk of overlooking the lesion during gross examination {34628432}.

Histopathology

Histologically, most adenocarcinomas of the jejunum and ileum resemble conventional colorectal adenocarcinoma, consisting of glands lined by columnar cells with elongated nuclei, often accompanied by luminal necrosis. Rare neoplastic Paneth cells, neuroendocrine cells, squamous cells, and signet-ring cells may also be present, and the degree of mucin production is variable. A high density of intraepithelial and stromal tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes may be seen in a subset of tumours {31576398;32066859}. Intermediate to high (≥ 5) peritumoural tumour budding is present in most jejunoileal adenocarcinomas {31418007;32781596}. Lymphatic and venous invasion are detected in approximately 50% {38411920}, and subserosal and serosal involvement is very common.

Histological subtypes

Although most jejunal/ileal adenocarcinomas are classified as adenocarcinoma NOS {28664941}, several distinct histopathological subtypes are recognized.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma: In this subtype, > 50% of the tumour is composed of pools of extracellular mucin with malignant epithelium in clumps, strips, or single cells, some of which may show signet-ring cell morphology. It accounts for 5–10% of small intestinal adenocarcinomas {33849741;31388669;33443865;38411920}. MUC2 is typically expressed {38062562}. The prognosis for patients with mucinous adenocarcinoma is likely similar to that for patients with adenocarcinoma NOS {33849741}.

Medullary carcinoma: In this subtype, > 50% of the tumour is characterized by solid sheets of malignant cells in a syncytial growth pattern with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, variable cytoplasm, and prominent infiltration by lymphocytes and/or neutrophils. A glandular component may also be present {39192803}. It is a rare subtype that accounts for up to 7% of resected jejunoileal adenocarcinomas {39192803}. Medullary carcinoma is associated with coeliac disease, MMR deficiency due to MLH1 promoter hypermethylation, and a favourable prognosis {26599717;39192803}.

Low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma: Similar to its colorectal counterpart, this subtype is characterized by haphazard, very well differentiated glands without evidence of desmoplastic stromal response. The small glands are lined with cuboidal to low columnar cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, resembling tubular adenocarcinomas of the stomach {39164422}. These tumours have been reported in approximately 20% of Crohn disease–associated adenocarcinoma cases {16861975;31388669}.

Poorly cohesive carcinoma (including signet-ring cell carcinoma): This subtype shows single cells, small clusters, or cord-like structures, with or without signet-ring cell features, infiltrating the stroma and making up > 50% of the tumour {34628432}. This subtype is associated with Crohn disease and accounts for approximately 10% of jejunoileal adenocarcinomas {34628432}. The prognosis for patients with poorly cohesive carcinoma is worse than that for patients with adenocarcinoma NOS or mucinous adenocarcinoma {33849741;34628432}. Poorly cohesive carcinomas harbour TP53 mutations, KRAS amplifications, or RHOA mutations, while MSI is rare {36812376}.

Undifferentiated carcinoma: Morphologically, this subtype is similar to those in other parts of the digestive tract and lacks morphological and immunohistochemical evidence of glandular, squamous, or neuroendocrine differentiation. Undifferentiated carcinomas are typically composed of dyscohesive rhabdoid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and some may form nests embedded in a myxoid matrix. They are characterized by loss of SWI/SNF protein expression, including SMARCA2 (BRM), SMARCA4 (BRG1), SMARCB1 (INI1), and ARID1A {26551623;34812766}. Minor areas of glandular differentiation may be present. The prognosis is generally poor {26551623;34812766}.

Adenosquamous carcinomas: This subtype shows both adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma in varying proportions and is extremely rare in the jejunum and ileum {2409665;8266218;10420235;16143036;14505135}.

Other subtypes described in the colorectum, such as serrated adenocarcinoma, micropapillary adenocarcinoma, adenoma-like adenocarcinoma, and lymphoglandular complex–like adenocarcinoma, have not been described adequately in the jejunum and ileum.

Immunohistochemistry

CK7 is infrequently expressed (20–30%) in jejunal and ileal adenocarcinomas without a predisposing condition but is found in approximately half of Crohn disease–associated ileal adenocarcinomas and has been associated with a worse prognosis {33963925}. An intestinal immunophenotype (CDX2+/MUC1−) has shown better prognosis in multivariate analysis {33443865}. SATB2 is expressed in approximately 20% of jejunal and ileal adenocarcinomas, limiting its use in their distinction from colorectal carcinoma {27169755;33228145}.

Grading

The grading of jejunal and ileal adenocarcinomas is based on the extent of gland formation, similar to that of colorectal adenocarcinomas. A two-tiered system is applied: low-grade (also referred to as well to moderately differentiated) and high-grade (also referred to as poorly differentiated). Since adenocarcinomas often show morphological heterogeneity {3384983}, grading is based on the least differentiated tumour component. Like colorectal carcinomas, they should be reported as high-grade when containing at least one focus lacking gland formation that occupies a minimum area of 0.196 mm2 (generally corresponding to a 40× objective lens microscopic field) {32868525}. Any tumour without such a focus should be reported as low-grade. The invasive front, where tumour budding and poorly differentiated clusters may occur as a sign of epithelial–mesenchymal transition, should not be assessed when grading the tumour, although such findings should be reported separately. Cribriform architecture is considered a pattern of glandular differentiation and does not indicate high-grade morphology. Applying this grading system, poorly cohesive adenocarcinoma and the signet-ring cell and undifferentiated subtypes should be considered high-grade. Pure low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma should be considered low-grade. Medullary carcinoma demonstrates solid morphology but favourable clinical behaviour; given this unusual combination, classification as high-grade is considered inappropriate and grading should be reported as “not applicable”. Other subtypes should be graded using the scheme above {32868525}, and any given case may therefore be either low-grade or high-grade. High-grade adenocarcinomas account for 13–30% of cases {38411920;29123252}.

Differential diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses include metastases or direct extension to the jejunum or ileum from adenocarcinomas of other sites, particularly colorectal cancer. The latter can mimic an adenoma when extending into the small intestinal mucosa. Primary jejunal and ileal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) and the very rare primary squamous cell carcinoma are other considerations in the differential diagnosis. Undifferentiated carcinoma should be distinguished from dedifferentiated carcinoma, metastasis from non-small cell lung carcinoma, sarcomas {33506327}, and melanoma.

 

Cytology

Cytology is not clinically relevant for primary diagnosis, but it can be useful for staging peritoneal involvement.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

MMR protein immunohistochemistry and/or MSI assay are required to evaluate a possible hereditary cause of jejunal and ileal adenocarcinoma and are also useful for selection for immunotherapy {26028255}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant neoplasm with glandular differentiation and/or mucin production originating in the jejunum or ileum.

Staging

Jejunal and ileal adenocarcinomas are pathologically staged according to the latest edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

Overall survival in jejunal and ileal adenocarcinoma appears to be worse than that in colonic and duodenal adenocarcinoma {20334897;12432298;25310836;24139211}. The median survival time is approximately 24 months, compared with 74 months for those with duodenal adenocarcinoma {20334897}. Nodal metastasis is the strongest prognostic factor {20334897;15274064}. The number of lymph nodes retrieved can be indicative of outcome with a cut-off point of 8 lymph nodes predicting improved survival {20715162}.

PD1 inhibitor immunotherapy may improve overall survival in patients with MSI-high and/or MMR-deficient tumours {33883178}. In one multicentre study, 50% of patients with advanced MSI-high small bowel adenocarcinoma achieved partial response to PD1 inhibitor (pembrolizumab) immunotherapy and were alive without disease progression {33883178}.
