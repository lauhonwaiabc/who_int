6.0.1 Tumours of the appendix: Introduction

 

The most common tumours of the appendix are of epithelial origin, which are covered in this chapter. Benign neoplasms of the appendix are broadly classified into appendiceal serrated lesions, appendiceal mucinous adenomas (described as a separate entity from appendiceal mucinous neoplasms such as low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms and high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms), and conventional appendiceal adenomas. In terms of malignant tumours, the appendix is the site of appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, appendiceal adenocarcinomas, and appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinomas. Appendiceal mucinous neoplasms include both low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms and high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms {26492181}, which can generate abundant mucin that may accumulate in the peritoneal cavity, forming pseudomyxoma peritonei. These are described together with other peritoneal metastases in section Other tumours of the digestive system. Mucinous carcinoma peritonei is the histological counterpart of pseudomyxoma peritonei, a clinical syndrome characterized by deposits of mucinous tumour within the peritoneal cavity secondary to mucin-producing epithelial neoplasms, especially appendiceal mucinous neoplasms, among others.

Colorectal-type adenocarcinomas are relatively rare in the appendix, and when they occur, they resemble their colorectal counterparts morphologically and genetically. Goblet cell adenocarcinoma is an amphicrine-like carcinoma (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system) composed predominantly of goblet-like mucin-producing cells with a minor, but variable, component of neuroendocrine cells and Paneth-like cells, intimately admixed together. These tumours are included here and staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system for appendiceal adenocarcinomas rather than that for appendiceal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), because of their more aggressive course.

In this sixth-edition volume, appendiceal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are covered in a separate chapter, section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system. This classification reflects our current understanding of the histopathological features, behaviour, and genetic abnormalities associated with these lesions. Mesenchymal lesions of the appendix are covered in section Mesenchymal tumours of the digestive system. Haematolymphoid tumours are rare at this site and are covered in section Haematolymphoid tumours of the digestive system.
