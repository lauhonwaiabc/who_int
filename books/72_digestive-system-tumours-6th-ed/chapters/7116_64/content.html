7.1.1.6 Conventional colorectal adenoma

Definition

Conventional colorectal adenoma is a premalignant neoplasm with a tubular and/or villous architecture composed of crypts lined by dysplastic epithelium.

Localization

Adenomas occur throughout the colorectum, from the ileocaecal valve to the anorectal junction.

Clinical features

Most patients with an uncomplicated adenoma are asymptomatic. However, occult bleeding is common and serves as the basis for common screening tests such as faecal occult blood or faecal immunochemical tests {29580179}. Symptoms and signs can occur with larger lesions, including evident bleeding, abdominal pain, and change in bowel habits. Rarely, distal large villous adenomas can produce secretory diarrhoea with electrolyte imbalance (McKittrick–Wheelock syndrome) {30322287}.

The endoscopic appearance of the polyps influences the ease of detection by imaging and visualization and the ease of endoscopic removal. Magnification / high definition, dye sprays, modified light wavelengths, and colon dilation by insufflation or luminal fluid instillation are used to improve endoscopic detection rates. Removal of adenomas by snare polypectomy, endoscopic mucosal resection, endoscopic submucosal dissection, endoscopic full-thickness resection, or surgery reduces the risk of colorectal adenocarcinoma.

Epidemiology

The incidence of colorectal adenoma is age-dependent, with lower rates described in younger individuals and incidence peaking after 70 years of age; men are more often affected than women. Over 40% of patients in screening studies show more than one adenoma {26288577}. However, epidemiological characteristics of conventional adenomas overlap with those of colorectal adenocarcinoma. A systematic review detected a prevalence of young-onset adenomas of 9% {32428708} and confirmed an increase of risk with age {35801765}.

Etiology

The etiological characteristics of conventional adenomas overlap with those of colorectal carcinoma (see section Colorectal carcinoma).

Pathogenesis

Adenoma formation involves changes from normal colonic mucosal epithelium to the benign precursor (adenoma), which increases the risk of evolution to adenocarcinoma in a minority of adenomas. The transitions that confer a growth advantage are associated with alterations in a small number of driver genes (mainly APC, KRAS, SMAD family genes, and TP53) {22278361;25535351;17932254;28947883}. Some of these genetic changes occur before morphologically identifiable tumour formation, in stem or progenitor cells {23345422}.

The earliest changes often involve alterations in APC function, usually by mutations that truncate the APC protein that reduces degradation of β-catenin, allowing it to accumulate and dysregulate WNT signalling {9311916}. Increased numbers of stem cells and loss of their hierarchy are evident as compared with non-neoplastic mucosa. The resultant altered morphology becomes recognizable histopathologically as conventional adenomatous dysplasia. Single crypt dysplasia (monocryptal adenomas, similar to dysplastic aberrant crypt foci in experimental models) shows abnormal epithelial proliferation of cells with altered differentiation and increased rate of crypt fission leading to formation of microadenomas, characterized by a few crypts demonstrating dysplastic epithelium. Inherited germline (constitutional) APC pathogenic mutations cause familial adenomatous polyposis, in which large numbers of adenomas are seen (> 100 adenomatous polyps in classic familial adenomatous polyposis syndrome), including monocryptal adenomas and microadenomas (see section Familial adenomatous polyposis (APC)).

The enlargement of microadenomas to form small adenomas and eventually large adenomas (≥ 10 mm) occurs through accumulation of further molecular abnormalities affecting a small number of key signalling pathways. Activating mutations of the KRAS oncogene contributes to growth dysregulation through the MAPK signalling pathway {9020477}. Abnormalities that disrupt the TGF-β growth inhibitory pathway are most commonly due to deletion (and some mutations) of SMAD4. Abnormalities of the PI3K pathway, due to deletion or mutation of the tumour suppressor gene PTEN or activating mutations of the oncogenes such as PIK3CA, inhibit apoptosis and promote neoplastic cell survival {27321283;25247763}. TP53 alterations, most frequently caused by mutation at hotspot sites within the gene, deregulate p53 protein function. Even one mutant TP53 allele expressing mutated p53 polypeptide is enough to deregulate a p53 tetramer containing other non-mutated p53 polypeptides. This allows the cell to survive substantial DNA damage and other cellular stresses, by inactivating the p53-mediated response to DNA damage that either triggers apoptotic cell death (via BAX and other proteins) or induces cell-cycle arrest, primarily through p21, to allow an opportunity for repair of small amounts of DNA damage {7665248}.

As the adenoma expands in size, neoangiogenesis occurs to increase blood supply, and a tumour stroma develops, including inflammatory and immune cells, as well as stromal myofibroblasts within the surrounding lamina propria. Adenomas in patients with Lynch syndrome harbour a constitutive / germline variant in MLH1 or MSH2 (or rarely MSH6, PMS2, or EPCAM) and a subset evolve into mismatch repair–deficient adenocarcinoma after acquisition of a second hit to the normal MMR allele [[Dabir PD, Bruggeling CE, van der Post RS, et al. Microsatellite Instability Screening in Colorectal Adenomas to Detect Lynch Syndrome Patients? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Eur J Hum Genet. 2020 Mar;28(3):277–86. PMID:31695176.]] {32141610} (see section Lynch syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)).

Macroscopic appearance

Adenomas may have a pedunculated, sessile protuberant, slightly elevated, flat, or depressed appearance. The size of the adenoma (largest dimension in millimetres) is important for defining its level of risk in screening programmes and stratification of patients for colonoscopic surveillance {32572858;32044092;31776230;33713493}. Further research is required to determine whether assessment of polyp size for this purpose should be on the basis of endoscopic or pathological evaluation {27102832}.

Endoscopic classification schemes have been developed to describe polyp characteristics. The Paris classification scheme {14652541} is the most widely used and describes polyps as pedunculated (Ip), sessile (Is), slightly elevated (IIa), flat (IIb), or depressed (IIc). Other commonly used classification schemes include Kudo (evaluation of pit patterns) {11315901} and UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (evaluation of colour, vessels, and surface patterns) {23910062}.

Histopathology

Subtyping of conventional adenomas

Three subtypes of conventional adenomas can be distinguished on the basis of their villosity. In tubular adenomas, the normal crypt architecture is largely conserved, with variable elongation of the crypts and an increase in the number of glands. The crypt epithelium shows enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei, with varying degrees of nuclear spindling and stratification and with loss of polarity. There is pseudostratification and loss of differentiation with decreased numbers of goblet and absorptive cells. A small villous component (< 25%) is acceptable in tubular adenomas. Tubular adenomas are the most common group of conventional adenomas detected during colonoscopy, particularly in the population screening setting {29087393;29968244;25123305}. Villous structures resembling small intestinal villi in > 25% of the adenoma are required for the diagnosis of tubulovillous adenoma. If > 75% of the adenoma has a villous architecture, it is diagnosed as villous adenoma. Despite the poor intraobserver variability in subtyping conventional adenomas {23611360;25004943;23348206}, this approach is still used clinically. By tradition, this is the manner in which adenomas are histologically characterized despite the limited clinical evidence behind this approach.

Adenomas demonstrating features of both conventional adenoma and traditional serrated adenoma (TSA) are advanced TSAs and should be graded on the basis of the degree of nuclear atypia (see section Colorectal traditional serrated adenoma).

Advanced adenomas

The term “advanced adenoma” was coined for use in the international comparison of population screening programmes, defining individuals at increased risk of subsequent colorectal neoplasia. This term originally encompassed all adenomas with a size ≥ 10 mm, tubulovillous or villous architecture, and/or high-grade dysplasia (HGD). The removal of advanced adenomas has the largest effect on cancer prevention, and it forms the basis of population screening {16996358;19171141}. In some surveillance guidelines, villous component is no longer included as a defining feature of advanced adenoma {32572858;31776230}, on the basis of its poor reproducibility {23611360;25004943;23348206} and some evidence that villous histology does not independently confer a subsequent increased risk of CRC {31563625;28457708}. However, as most tubulovillous or villous adenomas are ≥ 10 mm in size, and therefore still meet the criteria for advanced adenoma, the impact of this definitional change is limited, and restricted to those < 10 mm in size.

Rare morphological subtypes have been described in conventional adenomas. The most common is the Paneth cell–rich subtype, which can be observed in as many as 20% of adenomas. This is more common in proximal adenomas and in male patients {19269007;27188450;23232853}. Other subtypes are extremely rare {32981102} and include squamous metaplasia (also described as squamoid morules) {6895511;16374071}, clear cell metaplasia {10217566;18611940}, osseous metaplasia {22108510}, and signet-ring cell–like lesion {14576475;30782486}. Collectively, these occur in < 1% of colorectal adenomas. The only clinical significance is that some of these, in particular squamous metaplasia {28855108} and signet-ring cell–like lesion {14576475}, may mimic HGD or carcinoma. Adenomas may also rarely demonstrate small foci of neuroendocrine differentiation {25149552}, less than the 30% required for classification as a mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN) (see section Colorectal neuroendocrine neoplasms). These tiny nests of neuroendocrine cells can mimic adenocarcinoma {29116005}.

Grading of adenomas

For dysplasia grading of conventional adenomas, a two-tiered stratification is used: low-grade dysplasia and HGD. HGD is assessed primarily on adenoma architecture, characterized by complex glandular crowding and irregularity, cribriform architecture, and intraluminal necrosis. These features can be observed at low magnification. The architectural features of HGD are typically accompanied by cytological features of loss of cell polarity, nuclear enlargement, prominent nucleoli with a dispersed chromatin pattern, and often atypical mitotic figures {11981343;25619115}. These cytological changes alone, particularly when confined to the superficial luminal aspects of the adenoma, unaccompanied by any architectural abnormalities, are not sufficient for a diagnosis of HGD. Intramucosal adenocarcinoma is now recognized as a rare but definite phenomenon in the large intestine and is distinguished from HGD (see section Colorectal carcinoma).

Epithelial misplacement

Epithelial misplacement, previously referred to as pseudoinvasion, represents prolapse of adenomatous epithelium into the polyp head, stalk, or deeper, often accompanied by extracellular mucin, haemorrhage, and/or haemosiderin, indicating a traumatic component to the pathogenesis. These appearances can sometimes mimic malignancy, specifically stage pT1 colorectal cancer, and in particularly difficult cases, adenocarcinoma can coexist with misplacement. This phenomenon is especially common within screening programmes based on faecal occult blood tests or faecal immunochemical tests, because such polyps are prone to luminal bleeding {4810096;27713422;34773294}.

Reporting criteria for conventional colorectal adenoma are given in #52704Box 7.02.

 

Cytology

Cytopathology is not commonly used in the evaluation of colorectal adenomas.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: non-invasive villous and/or tubular epithelial neoplasm.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Most adenomas do not progress, as evidenced by the high incidence of adenomas in populations relative to that of colorectal adenocarcinoma. The likelihood of carcinoma being present at the time of identification of an adenoma, or developing subsequently, is greater in individuals with an advanced adenoma profile at baseline colonoscopy (see Advanced adenomas, above).
