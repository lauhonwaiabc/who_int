16.1.1.8 Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4)

Definition

Juvenile polyposis syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in SMAD4 or BMPR1A, characterized by multiple juvenile polyps and predisposition to colorectal cancer.

MIM numbering

174900 Juvenile polyposis syndrome, infantile form

174900 Polyposis, juvenile intestinal

175050 Juvenile polyposis / hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia syndrome

ICD-11 coding

2B90.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of colon

Related terminology

Acceptable: generalized juvenile polyposis; juvenile polyposis coli; juvenile polyposis of infancy; juvenile polyposis of the stomach; familial juvenile polyposis; combined juvenile polyposis / hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu) syndrome.

Not recommended: chromosome 10q22.3-q23.2 deletion syndrome; hamartomatous gastrointestinal polyposis.

Subtype(s)

Colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis; juvenile polyposis of infancy

Localization

The polyps in JPS predominantly arise throughout the colon, ranging in number from 1 to > 100. Gastric and small intestinal polyps are found in as many as 85% and 14–33% of patients, respectively {25390638;26721304}.

Clinical features

The diagnostic criteria for JPS are summarized in #43507Box 16.03. Patients usually present with gastrointestinal bleeding, manifesting as haematochezia. Melaena, prolapsed rectal polyp, passage of tissue through the anus, intussusception, abdominal pain, and anaemia are also common. Juvenile polyposis can present as either colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis, or as juvenile polyposis of infancy, a subtype with severe symptoms (see #43506Table 16.14) {16685657;18510548;38801072}.

SMAD4 variant carriers often have more severe gastric polyposis, a higher risk of gastric cancer {28340255;12417513;12136244;16152648;17873119}, and hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia {15031030;38627541}, whereas carriers of BMPR1A variants are more likely to have cardiac defects. However, not all patients with a pathogenic variant fulfil the clinical criteria of JPS, and the clinical disease spectrum in unselected series can be broad {37354305}.

Epidemiology

The incidence has been estimated to be 1 case per 100 000–160 000 person-years in European populations. As many as half of all new cases arise in patients with no related family history {17311531;25645574}. JPS is associated with a 34-fold increased relative risk of colorectal cancer and a cumulative risk of colorectal cancer of 39–68% by the age of 60 years {17303595;25645574}. The mean age at colorectal cancer diagnosis is 44 years (range: 15–68 years). Gastric, duodenal, and pancreatic cancers are also reported in JPS, but no formal risk analysis for these malignancies has been reported {9869523}.

The lifetime risk of gastric cancer ranges from 10% to 30% {25645574;36265529}. The median patient age of upper gastrointestinal carcinoma is 58 years (range: 21–73 years) {25645574}. Gastric and small bowel carcinomas together are estimated to occur at about one fifth the frequency of colorectal cancer in patients with JPS {17303595}.

Etiology

In an unselected series, a germline pathogenic variant (GPV) in SMAD4 was identified in 60% of patients with a clinical phenotype of juvenile polyposis, while 30% carried a pathogenic variant in BMPR1A, and 10% had no known genotype {37659672;37354305}. Most variants are point mutations or small base-pair deletions, 5–15% of variants are larger deletions of one or more exons or the entire SMAD4 or BMPR1A gene, and about 10% of variants involve the gene promoter {18178612;17873119;18823382}. Juvenile polyposis of infancy is associated with contiguous deletion of BMPR1A and PTEN genes at chromosome 10q23 {16685657;18510548;21834858}. Germline pathogenic variants in ENG have been found in patients fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for JPS, but the role of ENG remains unclear {27146957;17204053}. Either a family history of polyps, cancer, and extraintestinal findings, or a minimum of 3–5 juvenile polyps is important to ascertain before embarking on genetic testing {27146957}.

Pathogenesis

The progression to cancer in JPS is believed to occur through a landscaper mechanism, where an abnormal stromal environment leads to the transformation of polyps into adenomas, which can then become cancerous {15017003}. This transformation occurs through altered cell communication and signalling pathways between the stromal and epithelial cells. Particularly relevant in this context are signalling molecules of the BMP pathway, which are exclusively expressed by stromal cells. Epithelial cells respond to BMP signals through BMPR1A receptor and SMAD4 signalling intermediate {30246251}. Loss or inhibition of this stromal-epithelial interaction recapitulates a juvenile polyposis phenotype {15017003}.

Macroscopic appearance

Juvenile polyps are typically spherical with a smooth surface due to erosion.

Histopathology

Smaller juvenile polyps are indistinguishable from sporadic juvenile polyps. They have an eroded surface, abundant oedematous stroma with inflammatory cells, and cystically dilated glands with reactive epithelium. In the multilobulated or atypical variety, the lobules may be either rounded or finger-like. There is a relative increase in the amount of epithelium over stroma. Glands show more budding and branching but less cystic change than in the classic solitary polyp. Foci of low-grade dysplasia are seen in approximately 10% of colorectal JPS polyps, while < 1% of colorectal JPS polyps show high-grade dysplasia {22965402}, particularly in atypical or multilobulated juvenile polyps {21412070}. Gastric juvenile polyps show irregular hyperplastic glands mostly lined by foveolar epithelium and abundant oedematous stroma. Dysplasia is found in approximately 15% of these polyps, although this number may be liable to referral bias {25390638}.

It can be difficult to confidently differentiate between syndromic juvenile polyps, Peutz–Jeghers polyps, and sporadic mimickers on the basis of histological features alone. This is particularly true for smaller lesions, both in the upper GI tract as well as in the large bowel, as these incipient lesions do not show full-blown features described above. Without genetic confirmation of juvenile polyposis, it is often preferable to use more inclusive terminology, such as “hamartomatous polyp NOS”, and to recommend additional endoscopic examination with sampling of intestinal polyps and genetic testing in equivocal cases {20924281;26721304}.

Loss of SMAD4 immunostaining can be found in about half of all colonic and gastric juvenile polyps from patients with a germline SMAD4 variant, and it is indicative of somatic inactivation of the SMAD4 wildtype allele. In contrast, loss of SMAD4 expression is never seen in sporadic juvenile polyps or juvenile polyps from BMPR1A variant carriers. Loss of epithelial SMAD4 expression in the polyps of individuals with JPS is therefore specific for an underlying GPV in SMAD4 and can serve as a first screening for an underlying genetic defect. Loss of SMAD4 expression is not required for polyp formation or obligatory for neoplastic progression of a juvenile polyp {20682711;28340255}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

GPVs in SMAD4 or BMPR1A can be demonstrated using next-generation sequencing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria for JPS are summarized in #43507Box 16.03. Phenotypic subtypes of JPS and associated genetic defects are presented in #43506Table 16.14. See also the summary in #59817Table 16.15.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with juvenile polyposis of infancy rarely survive past 2 years of age, because of severe diarrhoea, anaemia, and hypoalbuminaemia. Patients with colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis are mainly at risk of colorectal and upper gastrointestinal cancer. Treatment should focus on preventing malignancy by close surveillance. Prophylactic colorectal and/or gastric surgery should be considered for patients who have a polyp burden that cannot be managed endoscopically, polyps with high-grade dysplasia, or a strong family history of gastrointestinal cancer {22171123;25645574}.
