11.1.1.3 Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia

Definition

Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN) is a microscopic, non-invasive, flat or micropapillary, epithelial neoplasm with glandular differentiation confined to the pancreatic ducts.

ICD-O coding

81480/0 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade

81480/2 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.8 & XH6AF9 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, low-grade, of pancreas

2E61.Y & XA3QC5 & XH28N7 Glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, high-grade, of pancreas

Related terminology

High-grade pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia

Acceptable: carcinoma in situ.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Some studies show that PanIN is more common in the head of the pancreas {445339;28196020}, while others show no preferential distribution {38693266}.

Clinical features

Given their microscopic nature, PanIN lesions do not produce symptoms, generally cannot be detected on preoperative imaging studies, and are typically found incidentally in pancreatic specimens resected for other reasons. However, PanIN content has been correlated with intrapancreatic fat content, which can be measured on MRI {38194643}. In addition, in patients with a familial predisposition to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), PanIN lesions, especially high-grade lesions, may be associated with lobulocentric atrophy and tend to be multifocal {19996207;16931950}. It may be possible to detect the resultant multiple foci of lobulocentric atrophy on imaging studies such as EUS, suggesting a potential screening tool for identification of individuals at higher risk of invasive neoplasia {16931950;37491554;16682259}.

Epidemiology

Low-grade PanIN is a common incidental finding in the general population and can be found in more than half of all individuals aged > 50 years with thorough sampling of the pancreas {23748606}. The incidence is described to be associated with age, with its occurrence being rare in patients aged < 40 years. In contrast, high-grade PanIN lesions rarely occur in healthy pancreata, although they may be observed in the elderly {31339204}.

Etiology

Like PDAC, PanIN has been suggested to be associated with advanced age, obesity, pancreatic fatty infiltration, and diabetes mellitus {445339;17996793;17414117;25700304;28196020}. Also, PanIN lesions are more numerous and of a higher grade in the pancreata from patients with a familial predisposition to PDAC {25959245;19996207;16931950}.

Pathogenesis

Multiple studies suggest that high-grade PanIN is the primary precursor to PDAC {14559982;1277176;9500216}. Molecular studies have supported this hypothesis by showing that PanIN lesions share critical somatic genetic changes with adjacent PDAC, and that the histological progression of PanIN is associated with the accumulation of these molecular abnormalities {11431719;10766191;17520196;27926367;28188630}. The evolution from the first genetic change in normal cells to invasive carcinoma appears to take decades, and the relatively common occurrence of low-grade PanIN lesions, most of which never progress to PDAC, suggests that this phase of carcinogenesis lasts many years {20981102}.

The histological progression of PanIN to invasive carcinoma is mirrored by genetic progression, manifesting as either an increasing prevalence of molecular aberrations overall, or a trend towards a higher degree of clonality for any individual molecular aberration in high-grade PanIN than that in low-grade PanIN {18039136}. For example, > 90% of PanIN lesions of all grades harbour KRAS mutations; however, the mutant allele fraction (a measure of clonality) is substantially higher in high-grade PanIN lesions than it is in low-grade lesions {22226782}. Although the precise sequence of alterations is not well defined, certain genetic abnormalities, such as telomere shortening and activating mutations of the KRAS oncogene, are early changes, observed even in low-grade PanIN, and probably contribute to disease initiation {12414502}. In contrast, biallelic inactivation of CDKN2A and mutation of TP53 are observed in high-grade PanIN lesions, suggesting an association between these late changes and disease progression {29684357;28188630}. Mutations and homozygous deletions of SMAD4 are not observed in high-grade PanIN lesions, indicating that inactivation of this gene predominantly occurs in bona fide invasive carcinomas {28188630;29684357}. In contrast to invasive PDAC, widespread copy-number alterations are uncommon in PanIN {38693266;35944927}.

The molecular features of PanIN differ slightly from those of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs), the next most common precursor lesion to PDAC. Specifically, certain genetic alterations common in IPMN, such as activating mutations of GNAS and inactivating mutations of RNF43, are rare in PanIN lesions {22158988;21775669;28188630;25840541}. Recent genome-wide methylation and gene expression analyses have shown a great similarity between PanIN and gastric-type IPMN, which could point to a common origin of these two lesions {35944927}. On the other hand, studies based on high-resolution 3D reconstructions have shown that some lesions classified as PanIN according to conventional 2D criteria actually fulfilled the size criteria for an IPMN, suggesting that misclassification of PanIN using conventional 2D methods cannot be excluded {38764379}.

Macroscopic appearance

PanIN lesions are usually not grossly visible but may be associated with changes such as lobulocentric atrophy in the immediately adjacent pancreatic parenchyma {16021508;16931950;19208745;19260742;37491554}.

Histopathology

PanIN lesions are composed of cuboidal to columnar epithelial cells producing varying amounts of mucin {11342768;15252303}. PanIN lesions are graded on the basis of the highest degree of cytoarchitectural atypia as low-grade or high-grade. Low-grade PanIN lesions are flat or papillary, with basally located or pseudostratified nuclei and mild to moderate cytological atypia. Marked architectural alterations (cribriforming, micropapillae, and budding of epithelial cell clusters into the duct lumen) are absent. Mitoses are infrequent. High-grade PanIN lesions are typically micropapillary or papillary, with marked loss of cellular polarity, irregular nuclear stratification, budding of cell clusters into the lumen, severe cytological atypia, and mitoses. Uncommonly, there can be cribriform architecture. Occasionally, intraluminal necrosis may be observed {15252303;26559377}.

The immunohistochemical labelling pattern of PanIN lesions parallels that of PDAC. None of the lesions express MUC2, but most express MUC1, MUC4, and MUC5AC {12379756;13679454}. Alterations in other glycoproteins include cytoplasmic expression of CEA and aberrant expression of B72.3, both of which are more common in high-grade PanIN than in low-grade PanIN. An increasing Ki-67 labelling index has been shown with increasing grade of PanIN {11950919}. Recent studies have shown that aberrant expression of p53 is rare and that SMAD4 immunolabelling is retained in high-grade PanIN lesions {28188630}.

Differentiating PanIN and IPMN may be diagnostically challenging. Size is the main feature used to distinguish these lesions: PanIN lesions are usually < 5 mm, whereas IPMN lesions are usually > 10 mm in greatest dimension on a standard histological section {15252303;26559377;25775066}. As noted earlier, 2D analysis may not be suitable to correctly assess the size of 3D lesions {38764379}. The direction of differentiation of the neoplastic cells is also important. The epithelial cells in almost all PanIN lesions have gastric differentiation, whereas those in IPMN can have various differentiation {8712298;15223952;16088402;20139757}; lesions with intestinal or oncocytic differentiation most likely represent IPMN or intraductal oncocytic papillary neoplasms (IOPNs). The term “incipient IPMN” or “incipient IOPN” can be applied to lesions 5–10 mm in diameter with long finger-like papillae, intestinal or oncocytic differentiation, or with a somatic GNAS mutation {26559377;25775066;24525507}.

Intraductal spread of invasive carcinoma (ductal cancerization or colonization), which is seen in as many as 70% of surgically resected PDACs, can be impossible to distinguish from high-grade PanIN {16708301}. If present, continuity of the intraductal lesion with invasive carcinoma in the adjacent stroma and abrupt transition from morphologically normal epithelium to markedly atypical epithelium within the same duct are helpful in recognizing intraductal spread {26559377}. Loss of SMAD4 expression also favours intraductal spread {28188630;29660164}.

A pattern of vascular invasion by PDAC may also histologically mimic PanIN. The presence of a subtle circumferential layer of smooth muscle fibres indicates vascular invasion {19092633;22082604}.

Cytology

Because the lesions are microscopic and not detectable preoperatively, PanIN is almost never encountered in clinical cytology samples or in EUS-FNAB specimens.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: microscopic foci of non-invasive low-grade or high-grade epithelial dysplasia within ducts.

Staging

High-grade PanIN is categorized as Tis (carcinoma in situ).

Prognosis and prediction

Low-grade PanIN is extremely common and of no proven clinical significance. In contrast, high-grade PanIN, especially in isolation, may have clinical significance and may serve as a surrogate marker for invasive carcinoma elsewhere in the organ {14559982;9500216;25775066;26559377}. Therefore, in the absence of an established invasive carcinoma, if high-grade PanIN is present at a margin, additional surgery may be justifiable {11431719;25775066;26559377}. However, once invasive carcinoma is present, it is highly unlikely that the patient’s clinical course will be affected by the presence of any grade of PanIN, even at the margin {26559377;21537863}.
