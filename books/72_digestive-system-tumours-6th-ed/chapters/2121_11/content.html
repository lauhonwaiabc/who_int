2.1.2.1 Adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction

Definition

Adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction is an oesophageal malignant epithelial neoplasm with glandular or mucinous differentiation.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B70.0Z Adenocarcinoma of oesophagus, unspecified

2B71.0 Adenocarcinoma of oesophagogastric junction

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Nearly all adenocarcinomas of the oesophagus occur in the lower oesophagus or the oesophagogastric junction. The oesophagogastric junction is a site of frequent tumour development; distinguishing adenocarcinoma of the oesophagogastric junction from a proximal gastric adenocarcinoma is a key element of proper staging and clinical management. Ideally, the oesophagogastric junction should be defined endoscopically (by either the most proximal gastric fold or the distal end of the palisade vessels); however, this information is not always available and hiatal hernia, background Barrett oesophagus, or tumour involvement distorts these landmarks in many patients. In the current Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging system, cancers that involve the oesophagogastric junction and whose epicentre is within the proximal 20 mm of the cardia are staged as oesophageal cancers. Tumours whose epicentre lies further than 20 mm into the stomach are staged as gastric cancers, even if the oesophagogastric junction is involved [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. An adenocarcinoma in the distal oesophagus proximal to these landmarks is classified as an oesophageal adenocarcinoma (OAC). Rarely, adenocarcinomas can occur in the middle or upper third of the oesophagus. Such cases most likely develop from mucosal glands, submucosal glands, or ectopic columnar epithelium in the oesophagus (also known as inlet patches or ectopic gastric mucosa) {26610162;29249048}.

Clinical features

The most common symptoms at presentation are dysphagia, anaemia, reflux, weight loss, and pain {39550174}. The diagnosis of OAC is confirmed by endoscopic biopsy. EUS can be used to determine the extent of local involvement of the carcinoma, as well as peri-oesophageal lymph node metastasis. EUS staging is less accurate for pT1 adenocarcinomas {23205307}. Suspicious lymph nodes can be sampled with EUS-guided FNA. Radiological imaging (CT, PET/CT, or MRI) is essential for staging to identify any distant metastases and for assessment of response to neoadjuvant therapy {29666803}.

Epidemiology

The global incidence rate of OAC is 0.9 cases per 100 000 person-years (1.4 in men and 0.4 in women) {35671803}. The incidence of OAC is highest in North America, northern and western Europe, Australia, and New Zealand, which together account for 46% of all cases worldwide. The rate of increase in incidence over the past four decades is the highest of any cancer, especially in high-income countries {28780073}. Although the increase in the OAC incidence rate appears to have slowed in recent years, stage-specific trend analyses suggest that the change in overall incidence largely reflects a plateau in the incidence of early-stage disease, possibly due to greater awareness and better detection, whereas late-stage disease incidence has unfortunately continued to increase {20501776;39366329}. There has also been an increase in the incidence of OAC in high-income countries {37956470;25957861}. The mean patient age at presentation is in the seventh decade of life {39550174}, but incidence appears to have increased in patients aged < 50 years {33328255}. OAC predominantly occurs in men, with an M:F ratio of incidence of as high as 6:1 (see #50069Box 2.01) {32918910}. The time trend of increasing incidence is roughly the same in both sexes {39366329}.

Etiology

OAC is associated with advanced age. Risk factors for OAC include gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, obesity, and male sex; other modulating factors include kyphoscoliosis (due to its association with gastro-oesophageal reflux disease), smoking, Helicobacter pylori infection, antireflux therapies, and dietary factors (see #50069Box 2.01 for more details). This carcinoma is more often seen in White populations {35671803}. Approximately 7% of cases of Barrett oesophagus and OAC show familial aggregation {28780073}; genome-wide association studies have identified SNP loci with increased risk for either or both entities {35882562}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of OAC involves genomic instability, epigenetic modification, microRNAs, and environmental factors {39950103}. OACs demonstrate a highly heterogeneous genetic landscape. The most commonly mutated genes include TP53, CDKN2A, ARID1A, and SMAD4 {27595477;35301290}, and a high mutation load (averaging 7.33–9.99 mutations per Mb) is typical. There tumour mutation burden increases during progression from Barrett oesophagus to metastatic disease {37454136;26192915}. Frequent mutation signature changes characterize clonal evolution {37454136}. Single-base substitution signatures SBS17a/b (T>C/G mutations in trinucleotide context) are the most prevalent signatures {27595477;35301290;37454136}, likely triggered early in preneoplastic stages and linked to TP53 mutations, increased proliferation, genomic instability, and disease progression {37454136}.

Although point mutations are abundant, oesophageal carcinogenesis is also driven by structural alterations and copy-number variations, with increasing complexity of chromosomal alterations during tumour progression {27595477;37794034}. TP53 mutations enable multigenerational genome evolution through whole-genome duplication, chromothripsis, and dicentric chromosome formation, resulting in complex chromosomal rearrangements and increased copy-number variations (CNVs) with activation of oncogenes and inactivation of tumour suppressors, leading to cancer development {31152823;35301290;37794034;39950103}.

Epigenetic modification also plays an important role in oesophageal carcinogenesis. There is progressively increased CpG island methylation and decreased DNA methylation outside the CpG islands during the progression of Barrett oesophagus to OAC {35301290}, which may lead to silencing of tumour suppressors related to multiple signalling pathways {32032585;39950103;35301290}. Dysregulation of microRNAs may also contribute to oesophageal carcinogenesis {19074875;21407181;32093260}.

Macroscopic appearance

Despite the well-known association between OAC and its precursor, Barrett oesophagus {33234525}, most patients with OAC present at advanced disease stages {39366329}. At diagnosis, OAC can appear as strictures, polyps, or fungating or ulcerative lesions. Adjacent to the adenocarcinoma, there may be Barrett oesophagus. Early-stage carcinomas may present as small nodules or be detected on surveillance biopsy {27855466}. Endoscopically, the Paris classification is used to describe lesions {14652541}.

Histopathology

Adenocarcinomas can show tubular, papillary, mucinous, and poorly cohesive patterns, with limited evidence of the relevance of these patterns.

The tubular pattern is the most common. It is characterized by irregular, single, or anastomosing tubular glands lined by moderately atypical neoplastic epithelium with irregular, oval, or round anisochromatic nuclei that abruptly transition from non-neoplastic epithelium. Neoplastic glands often show variable amounts of intracellular mucin production and may also show cystic dilatation or intraglandular necrosis.

The papillary pattern is characterized by papillae, with rare cases showing micropapillary architecture {27553658}. The mucinous pattern generally shows tumour cells floating in abundant extracellular mucin.

Pure mucinous carcinomas are rare, with isolated reports of mixed mucinous and signet-ring cell patterns {25314219;15788671;23317610}. The prevalence of poorly cohesive patterns, including signet-ring cell patterns, is reported as about 8% {21123634;23719932}. The signet-ring cell pattern shows cells with large intracellular mucin vacuoles that displace the nucleus to the periphery. The poorly cohesive non–signet-ring cell pattern, more commonly seen in gastric adenocarcinoma, appears to be uncommon in the oesophagus and gastro-oesophageal junction.

Rare adenocarcinomas of the oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction may show enteroblastic differentiation and be composed of cuboidal or columnar cells with prominent clear cytoplasm growing in tubular, papillary, solid, and yolk sac–like patterns resembling primitive intestine-like structures {37027114}. Fetal gastrointestinal epithelial markers such as AFP, glypican-3, and SALL4 have been widely used to immunohistochemically confirm enteroblastic differentiation {25893262}. The prevalence of adenocarcinoma with enteroblastic differentiation in the distal oesophagus and oesophagogastric junction is currently unclear, although one large cohort study reported a prevalence of 0.7% (6 of 826) among patients with OAC {38866822}. A possible association with loss of SWI/SNF marker labelling has been reported {38866822} (see section Oesophageal undifferentiated carcinoma).

Therapy effect

Most locally advanced OACs amenable to surgical resection undergo neoadjuvant systemic treatment, which can cause morphological changes in the primary tumour bed and metastatic lymph nodes. In patients with complete or substantial partial response to neoadjuvant management, the lesion may regress and appear as an irregular white scar with fibrosis on cut section. Histological examination may reveal cytoplasmic changes (including vacuolation) and nuclear changes (including nuclear enlargement, anisochromasia, and hyperchromasia) in residual tumour cells, as well as reactive changes in stromal cell populations. Scattered neuroendocrine differentiation in postneoadjuvant surgical specimens reflects treatment effect and should not be regarded as evidence of mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN).

Invasion

Distinguishing early invasive adenocarcinoma from high-grade dysplasia (see section Barrett dysplasia) can on occasion be challenging, in particular on superficial biopsy material. The most reproducible features of invasion into the lamina propria include anastomosing tubules, fused glands, detached single neoplastic cells in the stroma, expansile glands with cribriform or complex architecture, and confluent sheets of tumour cells. Of note, desmoplastic stromal reaction is observed mostly in deeper invasive submucosal tumours and is not prominent in superficially invasive lesions.

The presence of duplicated muscularis mucosae is a well-known pitfall that may lead to overdiagnosis of submucosal invasion or erroneous interpretation of the deep second layer as the muscularis propria {31907377;18059229}. Tumour invasion into the space between the first and second muscularis mucosae carries the same low risk for nodal metastasis as intramucosal adenocarcinoma and is staged as pT1a {33753417}.

Grading

Grading applies to tubular and papillary adenocarcinomas and not to any of the other subtypes. Grading is preferably performed using a two-tiered system. Poorly differentiated or high-grade adenocarcinoma (previously grade 3) comprises poorly formed glands, may display solid areas, and by definition reveals < 50% glandular differentiation. Well-differentiated to moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (previously grade 1 and grade 2) reveals ≥ 50% glandular differentiation.

Immunohistochemistry and histochemistry

OAC has a consistent cytokeratin expression pattern of CK7+, CK19+, and CDX2+ and usually lacks CK20 immunolabelling, which is similar to gastric adenocarcinoma {12218578;31786484}. PAS after diastase, Alcian blue, and mucicarmine are histochemical stains that may identify cytoplasmic mucin droplets in a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma.

Differential diagnosis

It can be difficult to distinguish a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma from a poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma or a neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) in a biopsy of a lower oesophageal or oesophagogastric tumour. Squamous cell carcinomas are strongly positive for CK5/6, p63, and p40, whereas adenocarcinomas are strongly positive for CDX2 and CK7 and negative for squamous markers. The diagnosis of undifferentiated carcinoma should be considered for cases with an equivocal immunohistochemical pattern and no morphologically identifiable squamous or glandular differentiation features (see section Oesophageal undifferentiated carcinoma). Some poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas may show marked neuroendocrine differentiation {16955509;37071391}. If there are unequivocally separate morphological components, one of which shows neuroendocrine differentiation, then MiNEN should be considered.

Cytology

Cytology is most relevant in the context of EUS-FNA staging of clinically suspicious lymph nodes. Malignant glandular epithelial elements suggest the presence of an adenocarcinoma, whereas malignant squamous elements suggest squamous cell carcinoma. A mixture of cytologically malignant squamous and glandular cells should prompt suspicion for adenosquamous or mucoepidermoid carcinoma (see section Oesophageal mucoepidermoid, adenoid cystic, and adenosquamous carcinomas).

Staging

Staging of OACs follows the UICC system.

Prognosis and prediction

The most important parameter for predicting the prognosis of patients with OAC is pathological stage {39550174}. Other pathological parameters associated with survival include tumour size and histological features such as tumour type, grade, lymphovascular invasion, clearance of surgical margins, and response to chemo(radio)therapy in patients who have undergone neoadjuvant systemic management {39550174}. Prognostic data on recognized OAC morphological patterns are often derived from smaller studies.

An adenocarcinoma in the upper GI tract that infiltrates the lamina propria or muscularis mucosae without invading the submucosa is defined as intramucosal (pT1a) adenocarcinoma. Endoscopic resection with or without radiofrequency ablation is the standard of treatment for pT1a OAC without adverse pathological features (i.e. no lymphovascular invasion and no poor tumour differentiation) {37813356}. Submucosal (pT1b) OAC is defined as “low risk” if submucosal invasion depth is < 500 μm (measured from the deepest fibre of the native/deepest muscularis mucosae) and neither lymphovascular invasion nor poor tumour differentiation are found. These low-risk submucosal OACs can be safely treated by complete endoscopic resection. Submucosal OAC with submucosal invasion > 500 μm into the submucosa, and/or (lympho)vascular invasion, and/or a poor tumour differentiation should be considered pT1b (high risk) {37813356;26526079;31907377}.

Complete pathological response and downstaging to facilitate margin clearance on surgical resection are the primary goals of preoperative neoadjuvant management. There is no global consensus on what constitutes involvement of circumferential margin, with some labelling margins of < 1 mm as involved and others defining it as the presence of tumour cells at the resection margin {22161989}. At present, measuring and reporting of distance to the margin is recommended. Overall survival increases with wider distance to the circumferential margin {28697493}.

The extent of tumour regression following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy is an important prognostic factor associated with improved disease-free and overall survival {39550174}. Tumour regression is graded on histological examination by comparing the amount of residual tumour with the amount of therapy-induced fibrosis or the estimation of the residual tumour. Widely used tumour regression grades are the Mandard system, the Becker system, the College of American Pathologists (CAP) system based on a modified Ryan system, and the Japan Esophageal Society grading system (see #50070Table 2.02 for more details). Although regression of metastatic lesions can occur in lymph nodes, tumour regression scores are based on findings in the primary tumour bed only. Sampling the entire tumour bed before classifying a tumour as ypT0 prevents understaging and is of prognostic significance {26076061}.

Amplification of the gene ERBB2 (demonstrated by in situ hybridization) and the resulting overexpression of the protein ERBB2 (HER2) (demonstrated by immunohistochemistry) in OAC and oesophagogastric junctional adenocarcinoma are predictive of response to HER2-targeted therapy. The HER2 immunoscore is defined as 0 (negative), 1+ (low), 2+ (weak to moderate), or 3+ (strong) on the basis of membrane staining {25596252}. Adjunct in situ hybridization is recommended in the event of equivocal (2+) immunohistochemical findings. Minimum biopsy guidelines have been proposed {26135662}.

The use of immune checkpoint inhibitors is recommended for patients with high microsatellite instability / mismatch repair–deficient advanced unresectable adenocarcinoma of the oesophagogastric junction {33792646;34102137;38382001;37875143}. Immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins and PCR-based microsatellite instability testing have shown nearly identical results {28241187}. While scoring guidelines are under development, the combined positive score (CPS) for PDL1 is defined as the number of PDL1-expressing cells (tumour cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of tumour cells, multiplied by 100 {34102137}.

Claudin-18.2 expression on > 75% of tumour cells is a biomarker for selecting patients who may benefit from targeted monoclonal antibody or antibody–drug conjugate–tailored therapy {37524953;37068504}. Other targets of therapy under development include FGFR2b {38308771;39854659}.

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)

·    GORD is the single most important risk factor, although the relationship is complex as only a subset of patients with GORD develop Barrett oesophagus and are at risk of OAC

·    The risk of OAC in patients with Barret oesophagus is 10–55 times the risk in the general population {29671158}

·    Individuals with reflux disease without oesophageal erosion do not seem to be at increased risk for OAC {37704252}

Obesity

·    Abdominal obesity is strongly associated with an increased risk of GORD {29671158}

·    Obesity might also increase the risk of Barrett oesophagus and OAC independent of GORD; this may be related to proinflammatory cytokines (e.g. insulin-like growth factors) {29671158}

Male sex

·    OAC and Barrett oesophagus are characterized by a strong male predominance, with an M:F ratio in OAC incidence as high as 6:1 {32918910}

·    Estrogen might protect against (and androgen might promote) the development of OAC {29037468;29342161}

·    Sex-specific genetic variants associate with gastro-oesophageal reflux and OAC risk in male individuals {32918910}

Smoking

·    Smoking increases the risk of Barrett oesophagus and OAC, although the association is less strong compared with other risk factors {29671158;29037468}

Helicobacter pylori infection

·    H. pylori infection is inversely associated with the risk of OAC {29671158;29037468}; a possible mechanism is reduced volume and acidity of gastric juice in atrophic gastritis after longstanding infection

Antireflux therapies

·    GORD symptom control and reflux oesophagitis healing are clear indications for antireflux therapies; studies support a chemopreventive effect with regard to Barrett oesophagus progression to OAC {30057104}

·    Patients with Barrett oesophagus who undergo antireflux surgery do not have a lower risk of OAC than those using antireflux medication {37690771}

Medications

·    NSAIDs (including acetylsalicylic acid) and statins have shown inverse associations with the development of OAC {29671158;29037468}, but large controlled clinical trials are needed to confirm the protective effect of these drugs

Dietary factors

·    The consumption of dietary fibre, vegetables, vitamins, minerals, meat (in particular red meat), and fats has been associated with different risks of reflux oesophagitis, Barrett oesophagus, and OAC; however, these associations require confirmation in larger population studies {29037468}
