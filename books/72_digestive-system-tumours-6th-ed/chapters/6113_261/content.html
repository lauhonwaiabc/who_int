6.1.1.3 Appendiceal mucinous adenoma

Definition

Appendiceal mucinous adenoma is characterized by a circumferential proliferation of neoplastic mucinous epithelial cells confined to the mucosa with an intact muscularis mucosae, originating in the appendix.

ICD-O coding

84800/0 Mucinous adenoma

ICD-11 coding

2E92.4Y & XA8PW4 & XH2M29 Mucinous adenoma of appendix

Related terminology

Not recommended: mucinous tumour of uncertain malignant potential; mucinous cystadenoma of the appendix; borderline tumour of the appendix.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Mucinous adenomas can occur throughout the appendix, with localized or diffuse involvement.

Clinical features

Appendiceal mucinous adenomas are clinically asymptomatic. They may be detected during abdominal imaging studies performed for other reasons or when patients undergo appendectomy for acute appendicitis {35676331}. In this situation, patients usually have symptoms of right lower quadrant pain, fever, and leukocytosis {31857681}.

Epidemiology

Appendiceal mucinous adenomas are rare lesions that tend to occur in adults in the sixth decade of life (median: 55 years, range: 42–68 years) {35676331}. Men and women are similarly affected {32796173;35676331}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Information regarding the molecular features of appendiceal mucinous adenomas are relatively lacking, as they were grouped with low- and high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms in most studies {21443421;23403822;26160192;24821835;31111538;32821969;33712927;39400480;31491041}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most mucinous adenomas do not produce substantial appendiceal dilatation, although radiographically visible fluid-filled masses may be seen in a minority of cases {31857681;32796173;35472721;35676331}. By definition, a mucinous adenoma should not display mucin on the serosal surface.

Histopathology

Mucinous adenoma is composed of neoplastic columnar epithelium with an intact muscularis mucosae around the entire periphery of the neoplasm that is clearly distinguished from the underlying submucosa. Reduction or near absence of lamina propria may be observed. A circumferential proliferation of columnar cells with a filiform, villous, undulating, or flat architectural appearance is observed. Many lesions consist of tall columnar cells containing abundant mucus. Others, especially those with undulating or flat architecture, may have attenuated epithelium with little intracytoplasmic mucin. Most mucinous adenomas show low-grade cytological abnormalities characterized by a monolayer of cells with small or slightly enlarged, basally oriented nuclei that are usually hyperchromatic. Mitotic figures are infrequent. Occasionally there may be high-grade dysplasia, which can be focal and characterized by architecturally complex epithelium and high-grade cytological abnormalities. These areas contain cribriform glands, micropapillae, or neoplastic epithelium arranged in a single layer. Neoplastic cells show nuclear stratification and hyperchromasia with loss of nuclear polarity, cellular necrosis, and readily identifiable mitotic figures.

The entire appendix should be submitted and examined histologically to distinguish a mucinous adenoma from low- or high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms. The presence of incomplete muscularis mucosae, evidence of pushing or diverticulum-like growth of neoplastic epithelium, mucin pools in the appendiceal wall, extensive fibrosis of the wall, or extra-appendiceal mucin or neoplastic epithelium should prompt a diagnosis of low- or high-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms.

Appendiceal mucinous adenomas should also be distinguished from postinflammatory reactive changes and other mimics of neoplasia. Non-neoplastic mucosal hyperplasia develops following an episode of acute appendicitis {31857681}. It features goblet cell–rich epithelium as well as non-goblet epithelial cells and Paneth cells, well-formed crypts supported by lamina propria, and frequent inflammatory changes in the wall, possibly with mural mucin pools {31166613;19623035;31857681}. Serrated lesions generally have complex serration and lack the filiform, villous, and undulating patterns typical of a mucinous adenoma, and most do not show cytological dysplasia.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: proliferation of mucin-containing columnar cells supported by an intact muscularis mucosae.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

All available data suggest that mucinous adenomas of the appendix are benign and cured by appendectomy alone {12883241;19641451;28970138}, although the number of mucinous adenomas with high-grade dysplasia reported in the literature is limited {35676331}. Cases for which a diagnosis of mucinous adenoma is considered should be submitted entirely for histological evaluation to ensure that the lesion is truly confined to the mucosa. Involvement of the resection margin does not influence prognosis {35676331;24971927}.
