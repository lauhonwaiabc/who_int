5.1.1.1 Conventional jejunoileal adenoma

Definition

Jejunal and ileal adenoma is a small bowel glandular epithelial benign neoplasm, displaying conventional (adenomatous) intestinal dysplasia.

Localization

Adenomas occur throughout the small intestine, but jejunal and ileal locations are uncommon compared with the duodenum.

Clinical features

Much of the jejunum and ileum is endoscopically inaccessible. Adenomas at these sites are identified during investigation or surgical management of symptoms related to intussusception or small bowel obstruction or following progression to invasive carcinoma {23809242}. Consequently, the adenomas are mostly larger than those found in the duodenum or the large bowel. A minority of jejunal and ileal adenomas are identified as incidental lesions by conventional or video capsule endoscopy or during small bowel resection for another cause.

Epidemiology

The exact incidence of jejunal and ileal adenomas is unknown, but they are less common than their duodenal counterparts {31392054;2167140} and are rare compared with colorectal adenomas. The most extensive case series have come from the United Kingdom and the USA {2167140;7248908}; however, sporadic reports have come from Japan, China, and Europe, suggesting a worldwide distribution. A slight male predominance has been described, and most patients are in their fifth or sixth decade of life {7248908}.

Etiology

Adenomas in the jejunum and ileum are proportionally more likely to originate in a genetic syndrome than adenomas in the large intestine. Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is associated with jejunal and ileal adenomas on video capsule endoscopy in up to 87% of patients {22704577} and accounts for more than twice as many adenomas at these sites as sporadic lesions {2167140}. The presence of duodenal adenomas and high Spigelman stage predict an increased likelihood of jejunal and ileal adenomas {20027680}. FAP is also associated with the development of adenomas in the ileal pouch following proctocolectomy in up to three quarters of patients {24187452}. In a published series, 57% of small bowel cancers arising in patients with Lynch syndrome develop in the jejunum and ileum {22704577}. Small bowel adenomas were present in 6% of asymptomatic patients with Lynch syndrome undergoing video capsule endoscopy {20821360}. Elevated dysplasia morphologically similar to conventional adenoma can develop in areas of chronic inflammation related to Crohn disease {25029614;28040346}. There are good data for establishing a relationship between coeliac disease and the development of small intestinal adenomas {32679218}, including those arising in the jejunum and ileum {12865284}. Adenomas can develop in an ileal conduit formed after cystectomy {6726922}.

Pathogenesis

Little is directly known about the pathogenesis of jejunal and ileal adenomas. Between 12% and 24% of distal small bowel adenocarcinomas have residual adenoma at their edge, supporting an adenoma–carcinoma pathway similar to the colorectum and non-ampullary duodenum {53095;2380402}. In the latter sites, mutations in APC leading to WNT/β-catenin activation and, less commonly, mutations in KRAS are the primary pathways to tumorigenesis {33922305}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of jejunal and ileal adenomas is identical to their colorectal and non-ampullary duodenal counterparts. They are smooth, sometimes tan, sessile or pedunculated growths in the mucosa (for further characterization, see section Conventional colorectal adenoma). Adenomas in the setting of FAP are usually multiple, sessile, and predominantly located in the mucosal folds {14652558}.

Histopathology

All jejunal and ileal adenomas reported to date are generally indistinguishable from their colorectal counterparts. Three architectural subtypes of conventional adenomas can be distinguished on the basis of their villosity or villousness: tubular, tubulovillous, and villous. In tubular adenomas, the villous architecture is lost, with variable elongation of the crypts and increased number of glands. The epithelium shows enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei, with varying degrees of nuclear spindling and stratification and with loss of polarity. There is pseudostratification and loss of differentiation with decreased numbers of goblet and absorptive cells. A small villous component (< 25%) is acceptable in tubular adenomas, and a large villous component (> 75%) is found in villous adenomas; the remainder are tubulovillous adenomas.

For dysplasia grading of conventional adenomas, a two-tiered stratification is used: low-grade dysplasia (LGD) and high-grade dysplasia (HGD). HGD is assessed primarily on adenoma architecture, characterized by complex glandular crowding and irregularity, cribriform architecture, and intraluminal necrosis. These features can be observed at low magnification. The architectural features of HGD are typically accompanied by cytological features of loss of cell polarity, nuclear enlargement, prominent nucleoli with a dispersed chromatin pattern, and often atypical mitotic figures{11981343;25619115}. It is emphasized, however, that these cytological appearances alone are insufficient for HGD without the accompanying architectural abnormalities.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: presence of a villous, tubular, or tubulovillous glandular neoplasm with conventional (adenomatous) dysplasia, but absence of invasion.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of jejunal and ileal adenomas is likely similar to that of the large bowel adenomas when matched for size {20399906}. As in the colon, the risk of developing adenocarcinoma is related to the size of the adenoma, the villous component, and the presence of HGD {7248908}. In the duodenum, diameter ≥ 20 mm and HGD predict malignant progression {21139577}, and this is likely to apply to the jejunum and ileum.

Essential reporting criteria:

·    Size

·    Histological architectural subtype (tubular, tubulovillous, or villous)

·    Dysplasia (low-grade or high-grade)

·    Resection margin status
