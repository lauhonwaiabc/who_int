14.2.4.3.4 Glucagonoma

Definition

Glucagonoma is a functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (PanNET) composed of cells producing glucagon and preproglucagon-derived peptide, with uncontrolled glucagon secretion, causing glucagonoma syndrome.

ICD-O coding

81520/3 Glucagonoma

ICD-11 coding

2C10.1 & XH4NQ8 Glucagonoma of pancreas

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Glucagonoma predominantly involves the tail of the pancreas {29435000;38039101}. GCHN affects the pancreas diffusely.

Clinical features

Diagnosis of the tumour depends on elevated glucagon levels and the typical triad of glucagonoma syndrome: skin rash (necrolytic migratory erythema, occurring in ~85% of patients), diabetes mellitus, and weight loss {38039101}. The necrolytic migratory erythema is usually located in the groin and migrates to the limbs, buttocks, and peritoneum. Angular stomatitis, cheilitis, or atrophic glossitis is present in approximately 40% of patients. Other features are amino acid deficiency, normochromic normocytic anaemia, onychodystrophy (in female patients), and widespread venous thrombosis with pulmonary embolism in late-stage disease. In rare cases, glucagonomas are associated with cardiomyopathy. The average time from symptom onset to diagnosis is 31.4 months {29435000}. Metastatic disease at initial diagnosis is common, mostly affecting the liver, followed by lymph nodes, mesentery/peritoneum, bone, lung, and spleen {29435000}.

Elevated glucagon levels without glucagonoma syndrome may also be found in patients with GCHN (see section Glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia (GCGR)). Mild elevation of plasma glucagon levels (< 500 pg/ml) may be seen in other conditions such as pancreatitis, cirrhosis, and diabetes mellitus.

Epidemiology

Glucagonoma accounts for about 0.5% of all PanNETs and is the fourth common functioning PanNET, after insulinoma, gastrinoma, and VIPoma {17896148;22261919;39331358}. Its incidence is approximately 1 case per 20 million person-years. The average patient age at diagnosis is 52–59 years, and there is a slight female predominance {9880781;38039101}.

GCHN is an extremely rare condition, and some represent an autosomal recessive disease (see section Glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia (GCGR)).

Etiology

There are no known etiological factors specific to sporadic glucagonoma. Glucagonoma may rarely occur in patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) syndrome.

Pathogenesis

FOXA2, which plays a crucial role in islet development, is reported to be deregulated in MEN1-associated glucagonomas {28188614}. Like in non-functioning PanNETs, somatic mutations of MEN1, DAXX/ATRX, and alternative lengthening of telomeres are frequently found in sporadic glucagonoma {29688432;39331358}. A glucagonoma with MUTYH mutation and high tumour mutation burden has been reported {39331358}. Excessive glucagon produced by the tumour increases both amino acid oxidation and gluconeogenesis from amino acid substrates in the liver, consequently causing glucagonoma syndrome.

GCHN results from GCGR gene mutations that lead to deficient receptor expression predominantly in the liver. The impaired glucagon signalling in the liver causes elevated serum amino acid levels, which in turn stimulate glucagon cell secretion and hyperplasia via feedback mechanisms leading to hyperglucagonaemia (see section Glucagon cell hyperplasia and neoplasia (GCGR)).

Macroscopic appearance

Glucagonomas are usually solitary, well-demarcated tumours, varying in size from 30 to 70 mm {8606627;29435000}. Rarely, they are cystic {10457311;20922407}. They occasionally infiltrate the pancreatic tissue or the spleen.

In PanNETs developing in GCHN, the pancreas may be diffusely involved and grossly rigid, with multiple white-yellow fused nodules.

Histopathology

Glucagonomas display the classic well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. Most tumours are G1 and G2, but some may progress to G3 PanNETs. Lymphovascular invasion and perineural invasion are common {39331358}. The tumour cells express cytokeratin, neuroendocrine markers, glucagon, SSTR2, and often PP {8606627}. These tumours typically express ARX and frequently coexpress PDX1 {39331358}. DAXX or ATRX expression may be lost when the corresponding genes are mutated.

Glucagonomas should be distinguished from non-functioning glucagon-producing PanNETs, which are composed of glucagon-immunoreactive cells but lack symptoms of glucagonoma syndrome.

Cytology

The glucagonoma cytology is the same as that of other PanNETs and is diagnosed as category 7 (malignant) when using the WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology (WHO System) {38709670}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical evidence of hyperglucagonaemia and associated syndrome; well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count; germline testing for patients with hyperglucagonaemia without symptoms or with multiple neuroendocrine tumours (NETs).

Desirable: glucagon expression; SSTR2 positive immunohistochemistry; DAXX and ATRX for prognosis assessment (selected cases).

Staging

The staging of glucagonomas is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for NETs of the pancreas.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis depends on tumour stage and grade {17896148;25602797;38039101}. The overall 5-year survival rate has been estimated as close to 70%, with a mean survival time of > 6 years {21470282;8606627;38039101}. In patients with non-metastatic glucagonoma who had curative surgery, the 5-year overall survival has been reported to be 100% {38039101}. However, data on prognosis are limited; and in another retrospective study, the average survival time was only 32.1 months {29435000}. Death is usually related to tumour progression rather than tumour-related functional complications {8606627;17873310}.
