9.1.4.1 Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, small duct

Definition

Small duct intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (SD-iCCA) is a malignant epithelial neoplasm with biliary differentiation. It is typically composed of tubules with narrow lumina without significant mucin production.

ICD-O coding

81600/3 Cholangiocarcinoma, NOS

81602/3 Cholangiocarcinoma, small duct

ICD-11 coding

2C12.10 Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: intrahepatic bile duct carcinoma, small duct type.

Not recommended: peripheral cholangiocarcinoma; cholangiocellular carcinoma; intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, cholangiolar type; intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, bile ductular type; intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, peripheral type; cholangiolocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Cholangiolocellular SD-iCCA (81603/3); ductal plate malformation–like SD-iCCA (81603/3); lymphoepithelioma-like SD-iCCA (81605/3); solid-tubulocystic SD-iCCA (81604/3)

Localization

SD-iCCA develops in the liver, particularly in its peripheral parts {22271564}. Periductal or intraductal extension can occur.

Clinical features

SD-iCCA is often undiagnosed until it reaches a relatively large size. Approximately 50% of patients with SD-iCCA have underlying chronic liver diseases or cirrhosis of various etiologies {28338651;38924592;24406866}, and SD-iCCA is sometimes identified during surveillance or in the liver explant or resection for suspected hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Imaging shows a mass-forming tumour with variable degrees of hypervascularity and delayed contrast enhancement. Compared with LD-iCCA, upstream bile duct dilatation is less frequent {35949291}.

CA19-9 and CEA are well-known tumour markers for SD-iCCA, although CA19-9 levels in cases of SD-iCCA are generally lower than in LD-iCCA {38924592;27259014}. The clinicopathological features differentiating SD-iCCA and LD-iCCA are included in #49443Table 9.14.

Epidemiology

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), as a whole, is the second most common primary hepatic malignancy next to HCC, and rates are rising worldwide; it accounts for about 10–15% of primary liver cancers {21326720;25204668}. Incidence rates vary from 0.3 to 85.0 cases per 100 000 person-years {37709624}. There are global variations in the incidence of iCCA, with Asia having the highest global incidence, while the highest age-standardized incidence rates are seen in north-eastern Thailand (85 cases per 100 000 person-years). Incidence rates in the Western Hemisphere are lower (range: 0.5–3.4 cases per 100 000 person-years), with the highest rates seen in Italy (3.4 cases per 100 000 person-years) {37709624}. iCCA is more frequent in men, and the mean age of patients is 55–75 years {39813555}.

Epidemiology data specific for SD-iCCA are limited, so far. As SD-iCCA shares the same predisposing chronic liver diseases as HCC, its relative incidences follow the distribution of these chronic liver diseases.

The proportion of SD-iCCAs among iCCA cases varies by region, ranging from 41% to 84% in surgically treated cases. The proportion is higher in Europe (67–73%) and the USA (84%) than in Asia (41–55%) {36137398;38409827;30001234;31735647;28338651;27259014}.

Etiology

Approximately 50% of patients with SD-iCCA have an underlying chronic liver disease {28338651;38924592;24406866}. Like HCC, chronic parenchymal liver diseases (e.g. chronic viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver injury, metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease) are common, while large duct cholangiopathies (e.g. sclerosing cholangitis, hepatolithiasis, choledochal cysts, fluke infection) are uncommon (see #52262Box 9.05) {31536748;38924592}. SD-iCCA is rare in patients with genetic cancer syndromes.

Pathogenesis

Based on morphological features and experimental models, the origin of SD-iCCA from terminal bile ducts, bipotent progenitor cells, and even transdifferentiated mature hepatocytes has been suggested {18393293;28043904;23023701;35124821}. Key molecular abnormalities for classic SD-iCCA are described in #50144Table 9.15.

Precursor lesions of SD-iCCA are undefined. Bile duct adenoma and von Meyenburg complex have been reported as possible precursor lesions, but definite evidence is lacking. Premalignant precursors of LD-iCCA (biliary intraepithelial neoplasia, intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct [IPNB], intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm [ITPN], mucinous cystic neoplasm) are not linked to SD-iCCA {28823571}.

Macroscopic appearance

SD-iCCA is typically a mass-forming tumour with a white, relatively homogeneous, fibrotic cut surface {31735647;28338651}. The tumour is well circumscribed but lacks a fibrous capsule. Periductal extension into adjacent large ducts can be seen in 12% of cases (mixed mass-forming and periductal-infiltrating growth patterns) {31735647}. Rarely, SD-iCCA shows secondary intraductal polypoid proliferation; the intraductal elements can be mistaken for IPNB or ITPN. Unlike LD-iCCA, capsular retraction is absent, and the background liver often exhibits features of chronic liver disease.

Histopathology

Although the prototypic appearance of SD-iCCA is an anastomosing tubular adenocarcinoma, diverse growth patterns (small glandular, trabecular, cord-like, solid) can be observed in the same tumour. The lumen of the cancerous tubules is typically small or slit-like and devoid of significant mucin. The background stroma is fibrotic {24406866;25966428;28338651}. SD-iCCA may exhibit replacement growth into the adjacent liver parenchyma typically with a thick trabecular arrangement mimicking HCC. In early small tumours, pre-existing portal tracts can be preserved inside the mass lesions. In large tumours, the stroma in the central parts may become extensively sclerotic.

The tumour cells are relatively small, and cuboidal or low columnar with variable degrees of nuclear atypia. The cytoplasm is usually eosinophilic, but clear cell change or vacuolization can occur. Lymphovascular and perineural invasion is infrequent particularly in small lesions {28338651}. SD-iCCA is histologically classified into well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, and poorly differentiated cases. However, grading is sometimes challenging, because tubule formation is ambiguous even in well-differentiated cases and histological intratumoural heterogeneity is relatively high.

Rarely, SD-iCCA shows polypoid growth into large bile ducts, and the intraductal elements potentially mimic IPNB or ITPN {39757374}. Invasive adenocarcinomas derived from IPNB or ITPN typically show large duct–type morphology; therefore, the intraductal elements associated with small duct–type iCCA should be interpreted as secondary intraductal extension.

Histological features that aid in distinguishing SD-iCCA from LD-iCCA include (1) the absence of severe cellular atypia, (2) small to medium-sized cuboidal or columnar cells exhibiting eosinophilic cytoplasm, (3) absent or only focal mucin production, (4) irregular glandular structures with narrow lumina, and (5) apical EMA expression patterns. However, histological categorization of iCCA can be challenging or inconclusive in some cases of iCCA, because of hybrid morphology or poor differentiation {38409827;28608943;22271564}. This issue is more common in needle biopsy specimens. In those cases, the following five aspects need to be considered: (1) a small duct–type appearance can be observed at the invasive front of LD-iCCA, therefore, central parts need to be assessed {28338651}; (2) the presence of biliary intraepithelial neoplasia suggests that LD-iCCA is more likely, although SD-iCCA can show intraepithelial cancerization, which should be distinguished from biliary intraepithelial neoplasia; (3) clinicopathological correlation is important, and consideration should be given to the presence or absence and the type of underlying hepatobiliary disease, as well as the gross/imaging features; (4) immunostaining is useful for subtyping (see below); and (5) molecular tests may identify genetic alterations that are fairly specific for a subtype.

Immunohistochemistry

Biliary markers such as CK7, CK19, and CA19-9 are commonly expressed in both SD-iCCA and LD-iCCA. However, the expression pattern of EMA helps to distinguish between them, with apical staining in SD-iCCA and cytoplasmic expression in LD-iCCA {22271564;35032970}. C-reactive protein, albumin in situ hybridization, and osteopontin are also useful for iCCA subtyping, as they are commonly positive in SD-iCCA (> 90%) and typically negative in LD-iCCA (positive in < 10%) {33321161;38584480;38749660}. N-cadherin, CD56 (NCAM), and BAP1 (loss of expression) can also be used as markers, but the sensitivity for the diagnosis of SD-iCCA is lower {33321161;28338651}. C-reactive protein immunostaining is also helpful for differentiating SD-iCCA from metastatic cancers, as it is only rarely expressed in adenocarcinoma of extrahepatic origin (< 10%) {28945626}.

Histological subtypes

See #50144Table 9.15.

Cholangiolocellular subtype: This subtype is characterized by anastomosing or branching cords and/or elongated ductular configurations occupying > 80% of the tumour {18393293;22271564}. The proportion criterion is important, as the focal presence of ductular elements is relatively common in SD-iCCA. Small cuboidal cells with round to oval nuclei, granular chromatin, and scant cytoplasm are arranged in partly anastomosing, small ductules with narrow lumina, resembling reactive ductules. A hyalinized fibrotic stroma is commonly observed, particularly in central areas. On immunostaining, the cytoplasmic expression of CD56 (NCAM) and the luminal expression of EMA are sometimes observed. Molecular abnormalities in this subtype are similar to those in conventional SD-iCCA {22271564;31186529}.

Ductal plate malformation–like subtype: This shows varying sized branching and curvilinear tumour structures resembling ductal plate malformation, frequently with inspissated bile and fibrotic stroma {23073321}. The tumour cells are low columnar to cuboidal with minimal or no mucin production. Similar to the cholangiolocellular pattern, this tumour is often immunoreactive for CD56 (NCAM) and EMA (on apical membrane). This subtype shares molecular abnormalities with conventional SD-iCCA, particularly FGFR2 and ARID1A alterations, but lacks IDH1 mutation {34810298}.

Cholangiolocellular and ductal plate malformation–like subtypes are associated with a better prognosis {28608943;34810298}.

Lymphoepithelioma-like subtype: This subtype is rare and more frequent in eastern Asia. Large polygonal cancer cells with vesicular nuclei and distinct nucleoli are arranged in nests against a background of prominent tumour-associated lymphocytic infiltration. Well-differentiated adenocarcinoma elements may coexist. Alterations in IDH1/2, BAP1, and FGFR2 are usually absent. Favourable overall survival is reported {24804938}. It comprises two distinct phenotypes: the EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER)-positive group, which is strongly associated with female sex, younger age, and glandular differentiation; and the EBER-negative group, which is frequently linked to viral hepatitis, cirrhosis, and mutations in TERT promoter or TP53 {33859071}.

Solid-tubulocystic subtype: This typically occurs in women (median age: 42 years) without underlying liver disease {38047392;34298064}. Small to medium-sized neoplastic cells with limited to moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm are arranged in a solid, tubular, or cystic/follicular pattern in varying proportions some with sharp transition between patterns {28232156;34298064}. The solid component may show sheet-like, trabecular, or nested growth. The tubular component can show eosinophilic luminal secretions resembling thyroid follicles. Both microscopic and macroscopic cystic areas can be present. The tumour cells express CK7, CK19, and albumin on in situ hybridization, while hepatocellular markers are negative {34298064}. Positive staining with inhibin is a consistent immunohistochemical feature of the tumour but is not specific, as inhibin can occasionally be positive in iCCA {28232156;34298064}. Patchy chromogranin and synaptophysin expression makes neuroendocrine tumour (NET) a possible differential. However, INSM1 and SSTR2A are negative {34298064}. All reported cases harboured an NIPBL::NACC1 fusion gene {33999553}. It has been proposed that the tumour has a better prognosis than conventional cholangiocarcinoma {38047392}; however, local recurrence and metastasis have been reported in several cases {28232156;34298064}. This uncommon subtype has also been referred to as cholangioblastic cholangiocarcinoma or NIPBL::NACC1 fusion hepatic carcinoma {38047392;33999553}.

For more detailed information about the differential diagnosis of glandular lesions of the liver, see #48807Table 9.16.

 

Cytology

SD-iCCA samples are obtained by FNA from a hepatic mass. Cytological examination is not suitable for subtyping of iCCA.

Specimens may be hypocellular because of the desmoplastic stroma, and contain small, irregular clusters of dysplastic cells. The cells are relatively small and cuboidal with round nuclei and increased N:C ratio. Although cellular atypia may be relatively mild, irregular nuclear membranes, hyperchromasia, and loss of cellular polarity are at least focally observed. Peripherally located nuclei and a vague tubular arrangement indicate glandular differentiation. A necrotic background is uncommon {15952192;35565430}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular pathology is usually not used for the diagnosis of SD-iCCA with the exception of controversial cases.

Unlike LD-iCCA, extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and gallbladder cancers, which share many molecular abnormalities, SD-iCCA has distinct genetic alterations that can be used for differential diagnosis. These include potentially actionable alterations, such as IDH1/2 mutations (20%) and FGFR2 fusions (5–30%) {38924592;28338651;27259014}.

Mismatch repair deficiency / microsatellite instability is observed in 6% of iCCAs and is purportedly more common in SD-iCCA than in LD-iCCA {33998775;38362598}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a biliary-type adenocarcinoma with small duct–type histological features; lack of large duct–type characteristics; exclusion of metastatic carcinoma.

Desirable: immunohistochemical and molecular characteristics supportive for small duct subtype in selected cases.

Staging

Staging of SD-iCCA follows the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]]. The same staging system is currently used for both SD-iCCA and LD-iCCA.

Prognosis and prediction

SD-iCCA has a postoperative 5-year survival rate of approximately 60%, which is better than that of LD-iCCA (20%), worse than that of HCC (80%), and similar to that of CK19-positive HCC (60%) {38924592;31017316}. In unresectable cases, drugs targeting FGFR2 fusions or IDH1/2 mutations improve survival. Mismatch repair deficiency / microsatellite instability is observed in 6% of iCCA and is purportedly more common in SD-iCCA than in LD-iCCA {33998775;38362598}.
