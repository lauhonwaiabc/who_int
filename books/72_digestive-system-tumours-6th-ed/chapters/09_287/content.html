0.9 Foreword

Each edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours (also known as the WHO Blue Books) has incorporated sequential advances in knowledge, transitioning from little more than a simple list of diagnostic terms to a comprehensive, evidence-based, hierarchical tumour classification, incorporating multiple tumour characteristics and providing International Classification of Diseases codes (ICD-O, ICD-11). This sixth edition builds upon the strong foundation established in previous editions, especially the fifth. The content from the fifth edition has been reviewed and updated or rewritten as necessary, on the basis of new research evidence and feedback from end-user surveys. We have maintained the classification structure (site, category, family, type, and subtype) and the process (led by the WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board) introduced in the fifth edition, while embarking on several important new initiatives.

With the aim of enhancing the quality and worldwide applicability of the classification, seven subcommittees have been formed to guide the editorial board in specific areas identified as requiring refinement. The role of each subcommittee is outlined in #57741Fig. A. We consider this input especially relevant in the current era of rapid advancement in molecular genetics and computational pathology, which has raised justifiable concerns about how such developments may impact regions or settings lacking these facilities. In addition, the editorial board now includes a special representative from a resource-challenged setting, as well as representatives from the fields of molecular genetics, radiology, and oncology, each contributing their perspective from their setting or specialty. Coauthors from high-incidence countries for important cancer types have also been included wherever possible, bringing their practical experience at the bench to the teams of authors.

Thanks to their input, we have adopted a more layered approach to defining the essential and desirable diagnostic criteria for many tumours in this edition, balancing scientific advances with global relevance. The threshold for defining a new tumour on the basis of published evidence has now been accepted as a minimum of two case series with at least 15 cases each, from two independent research groups. The definitions of a tumour subtype and a pattern have also been refined, with a subtype defined as “a tumour that is recognized as being different from the main tumour type in at least one dimension (clinical, histological, or genetic), preferably with a different treatment or outcome” and a pattern as “a tumour that differs from the main tumour type in morphological aspects only, but not in its underlying biological or molecular features, treatment, or outcome”.

The research evidence referenced in the fifth edition has been summarized through the WHO Classification of Tumours Evidence Gap Map project (WCT EVI MAP; https://wct-evi-map.iarc.who.int/project-overview/), initiated in 2022 by the WHO Classification of Tumours programme at the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) and collaborating partners. This evidence has been presented as citation maps, which enable expert editors and authors to identify areas where the previously cited evidence was absent and/or of low level, prompting them to modify the content and bridge these evidence gaps in the sixth edition.

New images have been provided wherever possible, with at least one gold-standard image for each tumour type, while figures from the fifth edition remain available on the WHO Blue Books website (https://tumourclassification.iarc.who.int/). The website also now hosts whole slide images for almost all tumours, with deep zoom functionality enabling users to explore these images as if viewing slides under a microscope.

ICD-O-4 codes have been developed for this edition (an important update from the ICD-O-3.2 codes used in the fifth edition), in response to the need for a more granular coding system capable of accommodating all separate tumour types and subtypes. The update uses five-digit morphology codes (versus four-digit codes in ICD-O-3.2), with alphanumeric values that provide more leverage to accommodate all tumour types and subtypes. Topography codes have also been updated for this volume, with new codes introduced for the gastro-oesophageal junction, previously coded with the gastric cardia. A site-level change has been made for anal skin cancer, the topography of which has now changed from skin to anus.

Continuing our efforts to improve consistency and standards within the WHO Blue Books, cytogenetic descriptions follow the guidelines in ISCN 2024 [[Hastings RJ, Moore S, Chia N, editors. ISCN 2024: An International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (2024). Basel (Switzerland): Karger; 2024.]], nucleic acid and protein sequence variants are annotated using the MANE Select v1.4 transcript set {35388217}, and chromosome bands and copy-number coordinates are based on GRCh38/hg38 in the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu) {39460617}, unless stated otherwise. Like in the previous edition, where possible, mitotic counts have been converted from the traditional denominator of 10 HPF to a defined area expressed in mm2 {34079071} (see #60386Table A).

Another improvement in this edition is the updated approach to referencing. The substantial reference lists for these books are now published online only, allowing our readers to retrieve published literature more efficiently, in alignment with recent ICMJE recommendations  [[International Committee of Medical Journal Editors [Internet]. International Committee of Medical Journal Editors; 2026. Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals. Updated 2026 Jan [cited 2026 Jun 1]. Available from: http://www.ICMJE.org.]]. The references for a given section in the volume, with direct links to PubMed abstracts, are accessible via a QR code in each section. A full reference list for the volume is also available to download from https://publications.iarc.who.int/662.

A point of discussion for this edition has been whether to use the term “germline” or “constitutional” to describe pathogenic sequence variants that occur in every cell of the body (i.e. not somatic). A constitutional pathogenic variant is defined as genetic variant present in all cells of the body (including germ cells), whereas a germline pathogenic variant technically is a genetic variant occurring only in germ cells (i.e. known familial transmission). However, as these terms are still often used interchangeably, we have chosen to include the clarification “germline (constitutional) pathogenic variant” at the beginning of each applicable section and use “germline pathogenic variant” thereafter.

Like in all IARC publications and other WHO information products, English usage in the WHO Blue Books follows WHO house style. This means that spellings typically follow UK conventions (e.g. ageing, anaemia, artefact, centre, gynaecology, haematology, oedema, oesophagus, programme but computer program, tumour), with some exceptions (e.g. estrogen, etiology, fetus, leukocyte). The general rule is to follow the first spelling listed in the latest edition of the Oxford English Dictionary (e.g. organization and categorize rather than organisation and categorise); however, in some cases, a different spelling has become established usage within IARC/WHO, and the recommendations of international nomenclature-setting bodies take precedence where applicable.

This sixth-edition volume, compiled by a panel of international experts from across the globe, provides a tumour classification based on the best evidence currently available. This volume is intended to support pathologists and non-pathologists alike, not only in routine clinical work but also in conducting research and collecting cancer registry data impacting epidemiological information for the prevention and early detection of cancer.

We have continued to prioritize the needs of WHO Blue Books users worldwide while incorporating new knowledge. In this context, tests using immunohistochemical and molecular markers are listed within desirable diagnostic criteria for most tumours; however, in settings with facilities for these tests, it is prudent to perform the tests to establish or confirm the diagnosis. We also recommend that, to the extent possible, pathologists in all settings embark on acquiring these facilities to empower themselves, as keeping abreast of the latest scientific advances is imperative for the benefit of our patients.

 

Dr Dilani Lokuhetty

Head, WHO Classification of Tumours Programme

12.1.1: Haematolymphoid tumours of the digestive system: Introduction

  

During our review of the beta version, we noted two clinically and pathologically significant entities that appear to be absent from the current chapter: extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTCL) and intestinal chronic active Epstein-Barr virus disease (iCAEBD). We respectfully propose their inclusion, supported by the following considerations: Regarding extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma: While ENKTCL is classically associated with the upper aerodigestive tract, accumulating evidence confirms that the gastrointestinal tract represents an important extranodal site for this entity . In Asian and South American populations, primary intestinal ENKTCL (PI-ENKTCL) accounts for a notable proportion of gastrointestinal T/NK-cell lymphomas, with an incidence of approximately 3.1% among non-Hodgkin lymphomas . Clinically, PI-ENKTCL exhibits aggressive behavior, with a 5-year overall survival rate of 40-50% and a high risk of complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation . Pathologically, it is characterized by angioinvasion, significant necrosis, and a cytotoxic immunophenotype, with consistent association with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection . Given its distinct clinicopathological features, prognostic implications, and relative frequency in specific geographic regions, the inclusion of ENKTCL in the digestive system lymphohematopoietic tumour chapter would ensure comprehensive coverage of common T/NK-cell lymphomas affecting the gastrointestinal tract and facilitate accurate diagnosis. Regarding intestinal chronic active Epstein-Barr virus disease: iCAEBD is a rare but clinically challenging entity defined by persistent EBV infection of intestinal lymphocytes, leading to chronic gastrointestinal symptoms and distinctive pathological changes . Its histopathological features—including lymphoid infiltration, epithelial damage, and EBV positivity in tissue lymphocytes—often overlap with inflammatory bowel diseases (such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis) and other intestinal inflammatory conditions, resulting in frequent misdiagnosis . Unlike systemic chronic active EBV disease, iCAEBD has a unique predilection for the intestinal tract, with clinical manifestations predominantly localized to the gastrointestinal system . The ability to distinguish iCAEBD from inflammatory bowel diseases is critical, as misclassification can lead to inappropriate treatment and adverse outcomes. We believe that listing iCAEBD as a separate entity would draw attention to its unique clinicopathological profile, provide clear diagnostic criteria, and assist pathologists in avoiding diagnostic pitfalls. We recognize that the WHO classification process requires rigorous evaluation of clinical relevance, reproducibility, and evidence-based data—principles that align with our suggestions . Both ENKTCL and iCAEBD meet these criteria: they have well-defined clinicopathological features, are clinically relevant to digestive system pathology, and their inclusion would enhance the classification’s completeness and practical utility.

2025-12-30 02:08:01

Thank you for your correspondence regarding the haematolymphoid chapter in the beta version of 6th edition of the WHO classification of digestive system tumours (DIG6). We appreciate the time and expertise you put into reviewing the draft and providing the thoughtful suggestions.

With regard to extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL), we fully agree that the gastrointestinal tract represents an important site of involvement, particularly in certain geographic regions. As you rightly note, primary intestinal presentations can be clinically aggressive and pose significant diagnostic challenges. That said, based on current considerations for DIG6, ENKTL does not appear to demonstrate clinicopathological features sufficiently distinct from its more typical upper aerodigestive tract counterpart, apart from differences related to anatomical site and associated clinical manifestations. In addition, at this stage of the editorial process, it is not feasible to introduce a new dedicated section for ENKTL within the digestive system chapter. However, in view of the importance of this point, we will add a note in the EATL section noting that ENKTL should be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Regarding intestinal chronic active Epstein–Barr virus disease (iCAEBD), we agree that EBV-driven lymphoproliferative disorders involving the gastrointestinal tract are diagnostically challenging and clinically significant. However, it remains uncertain whether cases described as intestinal-limited CAEBV are sufficiently distinct from systemic chronic active EBV disease to warrant separate classification at this time. Further, systemic CAEBV may involve the gastrointestinal tract, and disease progression to ENKTL or aggressive NK-cell leukaemia further complicates clear nosological boundaries. In some reported series, limited clinical and molecular data make it difficult to determine whether certain cases represent true localized disease or part of a broader systemic process. We believe this may be an appropriate topic for discussion in the next edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours.

14.2.5.3: Pancreatic mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms

Histopathology

In this edition, important diagnostic considerations have been explicitly pointed out in the paragraph regarding pancreatoblastoma. Given that neuroendocrine differentiation in pancreatoblastomas is generally detected with the help of immunohistochemical markers and cannot be recognized in routinely stained sections, should they be considered more akin to amphicrine-like neoplasms or bona fide MiNEN?

2026-02-07 01:46:54

Thank you for your comment. We have reviewed this suggestion and do not believe that a change is required.

The definition of pancreatic MiNENs is “epithelial neoplasms composed of morphologically and immunohistochemically recognizable malignant neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components.”

For amphicrine-like carcinoma, three criteria need to be satisfied: (1) there should be morphological evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation in ≥ 30% of neoplastic cells, (2) the morphological evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation should be confirmed by immunohistochemistry (again requiring the expression of at least two neuroendocrine markers), (3) the tumour should not fulfil the criteria for MiNEN.

For pancreatoblastoma, both the definition and the essential diagnostic criteria are morphology-based. The Histology subsection mentions that a neuroendocrine component is typically detected on immunohistochemistry.
