9.1.1.2 Hepatocellular adenoma

Definition

Hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) is a benign liver neoplasm composed of cells with hepatocytic differentiation developing in a non-cirrhotic liver.

ICD-O coding

81700/0 Hepatocellular adenoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E92.7 & XH68V1 Hepatocellular adenoma of liver

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

HNF1A-inactivated hepatocellular adenoma (81702/0); inflammatory hepatocellular adenoma (81703/0); β-catenin–activated hepatocellular adenoma (81704/0); β-catenin–activated inflammatory hepatocellular adenoma (81705/0); sonic hedgehog–activated hepatocellular adenoma (81706/0)

Localization

HCA shows no lobar or segmental preference.

Clinical features

HCAs manifest with abdominal pain, a palpable mass, or haemorrhage; however, most are incidentally discovered on imaging. HCAs can be single or multiple. When the number of HCAs exceeds 10, the condition is known as adenomatosis {10636105;18339077}. Haemorrhage occurs in 20–25% of all HCAs. Risk factors for clinically significant haemorrhage are size > 50 mm; specific subtypes (see #49384Table 9.02); or specific conditions, such as pregnancy {27939373;34334644;34687281}. Transformation into hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is uncommon (4–8%) and occurs mainly in men {18246041;19664629;30190977;34368664}; the risk varies between HCA subtypes and is higher in HCAs ≥ 50 mm as well as in some clinical settings (glycogenosis, anabolic steroid use, vascular liver diseases) {26119687;34856012}. Although malignant transformation is more common in men than in women, most cases of typical HCAs still have a benign outcome {37245629}. Characteristic features have been described on imaging for each subtype with some overlap (see #49384Table 9.02) {16954922;16966481;18688875;30218633;33146794;38012454}, but histological evaluation represents the cornerstone for HCA diagnosis.

Epidemiology

HCA is rare, with an incidence of 3–4 cases per 100 000 person-years among women who have used oral contraceptives over a sustained period {221698;33005655}. This is at least the observed incidence in Europe and North America; the rates may be lower in Asia {21175790;33005655}. About 85% of HCAs occur in women of childbearing age {221698;27939373}; HCAs are rare in children {32476794;38699071}, men {34155783;37245629;38735503;38782104}, and people aged > 65 years {35383587}. The incidence in males seems higher in prepubescent children and elderly patients aged > 60 years {34148984}.

Etiology

Oral contraceptive use is a major risk factor, present in 80% of young women with HCA in North America and Europe. The risk increases with the duration of oral contraceptive usage. HCA can decrease in size after oral contraceptive cessation, treatment or suppression of risk factors, or after menopause. Androgenic and anabolic steroid uptake (e.g. for body building or aplastic anaemia) are also risk factors {15495253}. Other risk factors include hereditary diseases, such as glycogenosis types 1 and 3, galactosaemia, hereditary tyrosinaemia type 1, familial polyposis coli {37872280}, polycystic ovary syndrome, and β-thalassaemia. The incidence of associated syndromes or underlying conditions seems to be higher in children, particularly prepubescent children {34148984}. The recent increase in HCA has been attributed to obesity and metabolic syndrome {17596890;22429502;23509632}, which favour the development of some subtypes like inflammatory HCA and sonic hedgehog HCA (shHCA; see #49384Table 9.02).

Pathogenesis

Most HCA subtypes (inflammatory HCA [IHCA], β-catenin-activated HCA [b-HCA], β-catenin-activated IHCA [b-IHCA], and shHCA) require somatic driver alterations in one allele to activate the respective tumorigenic pathway {19585623;23485860;16496320;19020503;27939373}. HNF1A-inactivated HCA (H-HCA) and HCA in familial adenomatous polyposis require mutational inactivation of both alleles of the respective genes for tumorigenesis {12355088;14598263;37872280}. HCA develops out of microadenomas, which are also found in the surrounding parenchyma of adenomatosis cases. With growth of HCA, regressive changes and haemorrhage (especially in IHCA and shHCA), and consequently the risk of rupture, increase {27939373}. Termination of oral contraceptive or anabolic steroid uptake may stop HCA growth and may even lead to regression.

Macroscopic appearance

HCAs are typically soft, poorly defined tumours that lack a fibrous capsule {25232451}. They can rarely protrude from the liver. Intratumoural changes such as necrosis, haemorrhage, and fibrosis can be present. The lesions range in size from microscopic to > 200 mm. Hundreds of minute lesions may be visible in hepatocellular adenomatosis. The background liver is typically non-cirrhotic and usually devoid of chronic liver disease. However, nodular lesions with features similar to those of IHCA have been rarely reported in cirrhosis due to chronic alcohol abuse and/or metabolic syndrome {25318388;26516697}. In shHCA, the background liver usually shows significant steatosis or steatohepatitis.

Histopathology

HCAs, supplied by unpaired arteries, are composed of benign cells with hepatocellular differentiation arranged in plates 1–2 cells thick and occasional pseudoglands. The cytoplasm can be normal, clear, or steatotic, or it can contain pigment such as bile or lipofuscin {26205181}. Nuclear atypia is mild and may be related to ischaemic changes, but mitoses are absent. Each HCA subtype has distinct clinicopathological features (see #49384Table 9.02).

H-HCAs often have lobulated contours and typically exhibit diffuse macrosteatosis/microsteatosis; ballooned, swollen, and/or clear cells; and occasional pseudoglands. Microadenomas may be present in the background liver and are the hallmark of adenomatosis. Some atypical cases have no or only minor steatosis, myxoid stroma, areas with prominent pseudoacini, and marked pigment deposition {26205181;34232602;35104461;35383587}.

IHCAs are typically characterized by sinusoidal dilatation, congestion, inflammation, thick arteries, and variable ductular reaction leading to an appearance of pseudoportal tracts. Steatosis can be present.

b-HCAs are divided into subgroups on the basis of immunophenotypic features and molecular characteristics (see below and #49384Table 9.02). They do not show any specific feature on H&E sections but, depending on the mutation, cytoarchitectural atypia such as occasional pseudoglands, irregular nuclei, bile or lipofuscin pigment deposition, or numerous abnormal vessels can be present.

b-IHCAs exhibit associated features of IHCA and b-HCA but mostly resemble conventional IHCA, and the WNT signalling activation can only be recognized by immunohistochemistry.

shHCAs are composed of bland and mostly small hepatocytes exhibiting either clear cytoplasm or retracted eosinophilic cytoplasm with dark nuclei, probably ischaemia related. Numerous foci of haemorrhage and/or cavities either empty or filled with blood, fibrin, or serous fluid are frequently present.

In children, particularly prepubescent children with associated syndromes or underlying disorders, the proportion of b-HCA is higher, while the relative proportion of HCA in children without associated syndromes or disorders is similar to that in adults {34148984}. Similarly, in patients aged > 60 years, H-HCAs are overrepresented, accounting for just over two thirds of all HCAs, and show a higher rate of malignant transformation {35383587}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of HCA can be difficult, particularly on biopsy. Nodularity, fibrous septa, and ductular reaction can be seen in IHCA and can mimic focal nodular hyperplasia. Map-like glutamine synthetase (GS) staining in focal nodular hyperplasia and positive staining with inflammatory markers (serum amyloid A [SAA] and C-reactive protein [CRP]) in IHCA help in the differential diagnosis.

Mild cytological atypia can occur in HCA, and foci of reticulin loss can be seen adjacent to haemorrhage or in areas with steatosis {22498821}. Capillarized sinusoids (CD34+), a frequent characteristic of HCCs, can be seen in some HCAs (see #49384Table 9.02) {33560657}. The presence of thick cell plates, frequent pseudoglands, small-cell change, mitoses, and loss or fragmentation of the reticulin network are typical of HCC. Positivity for glypican-3 favours HCC but is of limited sensitivity {26769138}. Immunohistological stains used for HCA subtyping (such as liver fatty acid–binding protein [L-FABP], CRP, SAA) are not helpful in the distinction from HCC {26927891}. When morphological findings, GS pattern, and/or other immunostaining results are equivocal, molecular/cytogenetic testing for CTNNB1 mutation, TERT promoter mutations, and copy-number alterations can be helpful {23084586;27177928;31189995}.

Non-epithelial tumours such as epithelioid angiomyolipoma {29512829} and hepatic small vessel neoplasm {27090685} can be mistaken for HCA.

Cytology

Cytology is not suitable for HCA diagnosis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The molecular pathology (genotype/phenotype) of HCA is summarized in #49384Table 9.02.

H-HCA accounts for 30–35% of all HCAs {12355088;14598263;17363580}. FABP1, positively regulated by HNF1A, is a gene coding for L-FABP, which is typically expressed in the cytoplasm and (sometimes) nuclei of normal hepatocytes and is downregulated in H-HCA. A complete lack of L-FABP expression by immunohistochemistry is a useful diagnostic marker for H-HCA.

IHCA, which accounts for 35–40% of all HCAs, is characterized by activating somatic mutations in genes involved in the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway {19020503;24735922}. IHCAs show increased and diffuse expression of inflammation-associated proteins, such as SAA and CRP, at both the mRNA and protein levels. Serum CRP can be elevated, sometimes in association with fever and anaemia, which can regress after resection.

b-HCA accounts for 10% of all HCAs, and b-IHCA for 10–15%. These subtypes show activation of the WNT signalling pathway, which results from mutations or deletions involving the CTNNB1 gene (encoding β-catenin). Large deletions and most hotspot mutations in exon 3 lead to high levels of β-catenin activation, whereas exon 3 Ser45 and exon 7/8 mutations lead to moderate and weak activation of the pathway, resulting in different immunohistochemical patterns of GS expression {17663417;27177928;27469330;33560657}. Diffuse homogeneous GS staining is observed in cases with mutations that lead to strong β-catenin activation, whereas more modest activation (such as Ser45 mutation) leads to a diffuse heterogeneous staining (starry-sky pattern) {27177928}. Most HCAs with mutations in exon 7/8 show weak patchy GS staining. In addition, b-HCA with exon 3 Ser45 and exon 7/8 mutations can show a rim of strong peripheral GS staining; in these cases, CD34 is usually diffusely expressed in the tumour, except for this rim {33560657;38304236}. Therefore, GS immunohistology is a good surrogate marker to identify different types of CTNNB1 mutations, whereas nuclear β-catenin, even in the case of strong activating mutations, can be focal or even absent. However, molecular analysis (on frozen or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded material) should be performed in cases with inconclusive GS pattern, especially in biopsies, if risk assessment will influence the choice of treatment. The risk of malignant transformation is high in cases with non-Ser45 exon 3 mutations and low/absent in cases with exon 7/8 mutations.

The term “borderline lesion” has been used for b-HCA with focal cytoarchitectural atypia and/or reticulin abnormalities (but insufficient for definite diagnosis of HCC) {24735922;35954333}. Other groups have proposed the term “atypical hepatocellular neoplasm” or “hepatocellular neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential” for all β-catenin-activated tumours (except those with exon 7/8 mutation), irrespective of cytoarchitectural atypia, in view of the associated high risk of malignant transformation {23084586;27469330;31189995}. Molecular and cytogenetic changes typical of HCC, such as TERTpromoter mutations, are more common in b-HCA/b-IHCA with malignant transformation {23084586;24735922;36583256;37169258}.

b-IHCAs have immunohistochemical and molecular features of both IHCA and b-HCA, and they carry the same risk of malignant transformation as the respective b-HCAs.

shHCA accounts for 5–10% of HCAs and is characterized by activation of the sonic hedgehog pathway due to small somatic deletions of INHBE leading to INHBE::GLI1 fusions {27939373}. These cases may be identified by immunostaining for PTGDS {27939373}, which is a specific but not sensitive marker. Overexpression of the enzyme ASS1 is more sensitive but not entirely specific, as it can also be positive in HCA presenting with haemorrhage {28646562;32490318;33754354}. The expression of L-FABP is often decreased. shHCAs are reportedly associated with a high risk of haemorrhage, even for small nodules, and have been found until now only in women, mostly those older than 45 years.

Unclassified HCAs, which now account for < 5% of all HCAs, lack well-defined pathological or genetic findings. Making this diagnosis implies that all other subtypes and possible differential diagnoses have been ruled out by histology, immunohistochemistry, or molecular analysis.

Different HCA subtypes (and also focal nodular hyperplasia) may coexist in the same liver {27939373}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a benign hepatocellular tumour; specific histological criteria according to subtype, with corresponding immunohistochemical defining markers.

Desirable: GS pattern identifies b-HCA and b-IHCA with exon 3 (non-Ser45) mutations that have an increased risk of malignant transformation.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

The management guidelines for HCA {25135008;27085809} recommend biopsy for the diagnosis of HCA, and surgical resection for tumours that are > 50 mm or irrespective of size if other high-risk features for malignant transformation are present, such as male sex, androgen-associated HCA, β-catenin activation (exon 3), and/or borderline features for HCC {29751879}. Ablative therapy can be used as an alternative to surgery. The treatment is similar for multiple tumours and is targeted towards nodule(s) with high-risk features. Because β-catenin activation can direct management, especially in small and multiple tumours, accurate assessment of GS staining is crucial for diagnosis and should be confirmed by molecular analysis if needed. When tumours are multiple, they can be of different subtypes, and the management is guided by those having high-risk features; in highly selected patients, liver transplantation might be an option {35018302}.
