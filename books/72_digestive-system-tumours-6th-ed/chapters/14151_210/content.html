14.1.5.1 Appendiceal neuroendocrine neoplasms

Definition

Appendiceal neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are malignant epithelial neoplasms with neuroendocrine differentiation that arise in the appendix.

Related terminology

Acceptable: well-differentiated endocrine tumour/neoplasm; poorly differentiated endocrine carcinoma; mixed exocrine–endocrine carcinoma.

Not recommended: carcinoid; composite carcinoid-adenocarcinoma.

Subtype(s)

L-cell tumour (81521/3); glucagon-like peptide–producing tumour (81522/3); PP/PYY-producing tumour (81523/3); serotonin-producing neuroendocrine tumour (82410/3); enterochromaffin-cell neuroendocrine tumour (82410/3); large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80130/3); small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (80410/3); mixed large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80132/3); mixed small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma–adenocarcinoma (80451/3); amphicrine-like carcinoma (85743/3); amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma (85744/3); amphicrine-like squamous cell carcinoma (85745/3)

Localization

Appendiceal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) mainly occur at the tip of the appendix (in 67% of adult patients and 73% of paediatric patients) {29557879;25280658}. Appendiceal neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) have no specific preferential localization.

Clinical features

There are no specific symptoms attributed to appendiceal NETs, which in 80% of cases are found incidentally after surgery for acute appendicitis {29557879}. NECs often present with advanced disease, similar to other appendiceal carcinomas.

Epidemiology

A SEER Program database analysis classified appendiceal NET as the fifth most frequent gastrointestinal NET, after those of the small intestine, rectum, pancreas, and stomach {28448665}. Appendiceal NETs present with an incidence of 0.15–0.6 cases per 100 000 person-years, with a slight female predominance and the highest incidence before the age of 40 years {28448665;22262080;29374682;29245922}. However, epidemiological data have some limitations, because some registries and databases have regarded these neoplasms to be benign and therefore excluded them, whereas others have included goblet cell adenocarcinoma with the NETs {27379210}. Appendiceal NETs frequently occur in children, and they also have an excellent long-term outcome in this age group {25280658;26663900}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of appendiceal NETs is largely unknown. They may arise from neuroendocrine cells within the mucosal crypts, but some evidence also suggest an origin from subepithelial neuroendocrine cells {1577412}. Although molecular data are still limited, appendiceal NETs seem to lack mutational changes in common cancer-associated genes {29634977}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopically, appendiceal NETs appear as well-demarcated yellowish (after formalin fixation) nodules. Because many small incidental NETs are not visible macroscopically and therefore not recognized by the surgeon at resection {35072428}, embedding of the entire tip of the appendix in two longitudinal pieces is recommended. Most appendiceal NETs are < 20 mm in diameter (52–62% are < 10 mm, 28–30% are 10–20 mm, and 8–19% are > 20 mm) {29557879;29245922}.

Histopathology

NET

Appendiceal NETs display the classic well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. Enterochromaffin cell (EC-cell) NETs are the most common and consist of uniform polygonal tumour cells frequently arranged in large nests, often with peripheral palisading and glandular formations, similar to ileal EC-cell NETs. The bulk of the tumour (even in small tumours) often lies in the deep muscular wall and subserosa {16253897}. In the muscular wall, the nests can be replaced by small tumour cell ribbons. About one third of appendiceal NETs infiltrate the mesoappendix, including 50–82% that infiltrate the mesoappendix with a thickness of < 3 mm {29557879;23426123}. L-cell NET is a subtype of appendiceal NET with distinct trabecular or glandular/tubular growth pattern. Similar to rectal L-cell NETs, they are formed by cells producing GLP-1 and other proglucagon-derived peptides. Lymph node metastasis seems to be less frequent with these tumours {33589240}. If a tubular morphology is prominent, they must be distinguished from adenocarcinoma and goblet cell adenocarcinoma. Care must be taken not to misinterpret stromal retraction artefact as vascular invasion. The vast majority of both EC-cell and L-cell appendiceal NETs are G1 (86–91%) or G2 (9–14%) {29557879;23426123}. It is not mandatory to specify L-cell NET or EC-cell NET in pathological reports, because this has no established prognostic value and no treatment consequences.

NEC

Appendiceal NECs display the classic poorly differentiated, neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage, either small or large cell type [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. Exceedingly rare, they are morphologically identical to colonic NECs of either small cell or large cell type {23749920;29557879;23426123}. They may arise in association with a glandular mucosal precursor lesion.

MiNEN

Appendiceal mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNENs) display the classic morphological features of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine neoplasia [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2025. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 10). https://publications.iarc.who.int/645. Bookref]]. True MiNENs of the appendix are rare and comparable to colonic MiNENs, showing a combination of NEC and adenocarcinoma.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Appendiceal amphicrine-like carcinoma may occur as amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma with goblet cell features (see section Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma).

Immunohistochemistry

Appendiceal NETs, like other NETs, are positive for keratin, usually only keratin 8 and 18, chromogranin A, and synaptophysin. There are often S100-positive spindle cells surrounding the nests. Production of serotonin can be demonstrated by immunohistochemistry in EC-cell NETs. L-cell NETs express chromogranin B rather than chromogranin A {7599788}. As most laboratories use chromogranin A routinely, the tumour may show negative staining. Also, L-cell NETs are often positive for CK20 and SATB2, making the differential diagnosis with goblet cell adenocarcinoma challenging.

Grading

Appendiceal NENs are graded using the same system used for other gastroenteropancreatic NENs (see section Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification).

Cytology

Not clinically relevant as most are incidental findings

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

NET

Essential: well-differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage; neuroendocrine marker expression; Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count.

Desirable: SSTR2 expression; positive immunohistochemistry for hormones (e.g. serotonin, GLP-1).

NEC

Essential: poorly differentiated neuroendocrine morphology of epithelial lineage with either small cell or large cell carcinoma pattern; sheets or trabeculae of poorly differentiated cells; neuroendocrine marker expression; high Ki-67 proliferation index and or mitotic count.

Desirable: reduced/absent SSTR2 expression; loss of RB1 expression or abnormal p53 expression.

MiNEN

Essential: a mixed tumour composed of two morphologically recognizable components; type ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine marker expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index and/or mitotic count to grade the NET component or to prognostically stratify the NEC component; grading of the non-neuroendocrine component.

Amphicrine-like carcinoma

Essential: a mixed tumour with no morphologically distinct zones of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation; ≥ 30% of the neoplasm; neuroendocrine marker expression in the neuroendocrine component.

Desirable: assessment of Ki-67 proliferation index.

Staging

The staging of appendiceal NETs is in line with the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) systems for this site. For NECs, MiNENs, and amphicrine-like carcinomas, the staging follows the criteria of appendiceal adenocarcinoma.

Prognosis and prediction

The value of follow-up data for appendiceal NETs is still problematic because most registry-based studies dated before 2010 are compromised by inconsistent coding of the neoplasms; also, institutional retrospective studies were small and provided limited long-term survival data. However, most patients with appendiceal NET have an excellent outcome, the majority being cured by appendectomy alone {28641291;23426123}. About 8% of appendiceal NETs measuring < 10 mm {6650751;38430702} and 20% of those measuring 10–20 mm {36640790;29557879} are associated with microscopic lymph node metastases, but there is no clear evidence that the microscopic lymph node metastases lead to decreased survival {26663900;25039014;21294132;29245922}. Data regarding the relation of grade and mesoappendiceal fat invasion as a predictor of adverse outcome or lymph node metastasis are contradictory {36640790;29995678;23426123;29557879}. Therefore, only for the rare appendiceal NET > 20 mm is hemicolectomy still considered, because of the elevated risk of lymph node metastases (30–86%) {34599571;23051855;21294132}. Liver and other distant metastases are exceedingly rare.

Appendiceal NECs and MiNENs with a carcinoma component are high-grade malignant neoplasms, with a poor survival similar to that of their counterparts elsewhere in the GI tract.
