11.1.2.3 Pancreatoblastoma

Definition

Pancreatoblastoma is a malignant epithelial neoplasm of the pancreas with predominantly acinar differentiation and squamoid nests.

ICD-O coding

89710/3 Pancreatoblastoma

ICD-11 coding

2C10.Y & XH27L5 Pancreatoblastoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

No preferential location within the pancreas

Clinical features

The presenting features of pancreatoblastoma are nonspecific, and many cases are discovered incidentally. Common symptoms include abdominal pain, weight loss, nausea, and diarrhoea. Jaundice is uncommon {7503360}. Radiologically, pancreatoblastomas present as ill-defined large solid and occasionally multilocular cystic masses with frequent calcifications {39299885}. An abdominal mass is often palpable, especially in children. Isolated case reports have described patients with Cushing syndrome as a result of the inappropriate secretion of ACTH by the tumour {26881602;25353285}, and symptomatic hypercalcaemia due to a PTH secretion {39611185}. Serum AFP is elevated in two thirds of children, with levels often exceeding 1000 µg/L {11466723}, but AFP is not consistently elevated in adults {16769539}. When elevated, AFP levels can be used to track response to therapy. Serum CEA levels may also be elevated in children.

Epidemiology

Pancreatoblastoma is a rare neoplasm, with an incidence of about 0.04 cases per 1 million person-years {29285688;22012608}. In childhood, it is one of the most frequent pancreatic neoplasms, accounting for approximately 25% of pancreatic neoplasms occurring in the first decade of life. This neoplasm occurs equally in both sexes, and the median age at diagnosis in children is 5 years {12037756;28693851;39473958}.

Pancreatoblastoma is an exceptionally rare diagnosis in adults, with only 74 cases reported in the medical literature {29285688;27761191;23991735}. Hence, little is known about the epidemiology in adults. In the case reports and small series developed so far, a slight male predominance (M:F ratio: 1.2:1) has been described and a mean age at diagnosis of 41 years (range: 18–78 years) {19077457;34326617}.

Etiology

The precise etiology is unknown. Although most cases are sporadic, associations with genetic syndromes (Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis) and corresponding deleterious germline variants are described {11696422;2845376}.

Pathogenesis

Loss of heterozygosity of the short arm of chromosome 11p is commonly identified {11696422;11943738}. The locus is also affected in children with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, where the maternal allele is typically lost. The loss of 11p also occurs in > 80% of sporadic pancreatoblastomas. Alterations in genes coding for members of the WNT/β-catenin signalling pathway have been reported in 50–80% of pancreatoblastomas {11696422;12533831;24293293}. These alterations most often involve mutation of CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin), which results in the abnormal nuclear accumulation of the β-catenin protein. Proteomic analysis has shown upregulation of members of the WNT/β-catenin signalling and IGF2 pathways {39363045}. APC gene abnormalities are reported in pancreatoblastomas arising in patients with and without familial adenomatous polyposis {11696422;29535845;24293293}. KRAS and TP53 mutations, typically found in ductal adenocarcinoma of the pancreas, are not identified. Loss of SMAD4 expression is infrequent. Two recent studies found MCL1 amplification and FGFR pathway alterations in a subset of pancreatoblastomas that may provide therapeutic targets {35668118;32156527}.

Macroscopic appearance

Pancreatoblastomas are usually large at presentation, ranging from 15 to 200 mm (mean: 100 mm) {7503360}. Most are solitary, at least partially well-circumscribed or encapsulated, solid masses. Sectioning reveals tan to whitish-yellow soft lobules separated by fibrous stromal bands. Some may contain cavities due to haemorrhagic necrosis and cystic degeneration, which appear as heterogeneous or multiloculated masses on radiological imaging. Congenital cases in patients with the Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome may be predominantly cystic {2845376}. Biliary and pancreatic ductal dilatation may be present.

Histopathology

The tumours are composed of highly cellular lobules separated by fibrous bands, producing a geographical or lymphoid follicle-like, multiphasic low-power appearance. The neoplastic cells within the lobules usually have an organoid arrangement of acinar, solid, or trabecular formations akin to acinar cell carcinomas.

The neoplastic cells may have a number of directions of differentiation, but, by definition, neoplastic cells with acinar differentiation must be present. The neoplastic cells with acinar differentiation are polarized around small lumina and have nuclei with a single prominent nucleolus. Nuclear atypia is generally modest {7503360}. Acinar differentiation by the neoplastic cells is supported by the presence of PASD-positive cytoplasmic granules, as well as by immunohistochemical labelling for pancreatic enzymes (trypsin, chymotrypsin, and lipase) and with antibodies to BCL10 (clone 331.1 labels carboxyl ester hydrolase).

Squamoid nests are a defining component of pancreatoblastoma and are needed to establish the diagnosis {188539}. They are composed of distinctive cells with eosinophilic to clear cytoplasm in formations varying from islands of epithelioid cells to whorled nests of spindle cells with a squamous appearance. The cells may even demonstrate overt keratinization. Their nuclei are larger and more oval-shaped than those of the surrounding cells. They lack cellular atypia, prominent nucleoli, and mitoses. Occasionally, nuclear clearing due to the intranuclear accumulation of biotin may be seen {9796720}. Squamoid nests are positive for EMA. It is important to keep in mind that the density and distribution of these squamoid nests vary by region as well as by neoplasm, and they may be difficult to detect in small samples.

In addition to the acinar component and squamous nests, a well-differentiated neuroendocrine component is typically detected with the help of specific immunohistochemical markers (chromogranin A, synaptophysin). Glandular spaces lined by mucin-containing neoplastic cells and a more primitive round cell component can also be focally present.

The stroma of pancreatoblastomas is often hypercellular, in some instances achieving a neoplastic appearance. Rarely, heterologous stromal elements, including neoplastic bone and cartilage, have been reported. Pancreatoblastomas in adults have histological features similar to those in children; however, the stromal bands may be less abundant and less cellular {7503360}. An abnormal (nuclear and cytoplasmic) immunolabelling for β-catenin and the overexpression of its target gene CCND1 are seen in most cases. In some cases, immunolabelling for β-catenin produces a patchy or mosaic pattern of nuclear labelling, and in others, it is limited to the squamoid nests. Immunohistochemical positivity for AFP is detectable, especially in cases with elevated serum AFP.

Cytology

FNA specimens are composed of neoplastic cells in a loosely cohesive and/or clustered cellular pattern. Most of the neoplastic cells are polygonal with round to oval nuclei; one or more small nucleoli; and a granular, amphophilic, or eosinophilic cytoplasm. These nuclear features are similar to those of acinar cell carcinoma. The squamoid nests are usually more difficult to appreciate and may be detected in cell block preparations. Optically clear nuclei may be recognized in some of the neoplastic cells forming the squamoid nests {31581358;38709670}. Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: neoplastic cells with acinar differentiation and cells forming squamoid nests.

Desirable: nuclear β-catenin labelling especially in squamoid nests.

Staging

The TNM classification of pancreatoblastomas follows the criteria for staging ductal adenocarcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

All pancreatoblastomas are malignant; and local invasion, recurrence, and distant metastasis are common. They can invade adjacent structures such as the spleen, colon, duodenum, portal vein, and common bile duct. Metastases are present in 17–35% of patients at the time of the diagnosis, and some patients develop metastases later in the course of their disease {15256806}. The liver is the most common site of distant metastasis, followed by the lung. Surgical resection of the tumour is the mainstay of therapy, with or without radiation and combination chemotherapy.

Some tumours are curable, and prognosis depends on stage. The overall 5-year survival rate of patients with pancreatoblastoma is approximately 50%. The postoperative prognosis in patients with localized surgically resectable disease is favourable, with a 5-year survival rate of 65%, whereas patients with non-resectable disease do not usually survive beyond 5 years {15256806}. After complete resection, 20% of patients with pancreatoblastoma develop local recurrence after a median of 20 months, and 25% develop metachronous metastases {15256806}. The outcome in children may be more favourable than that in adults. One of the reasons appears to be that children often present with non-metastatic and well-encapsulated neoplasms {24081396;21696948}.
