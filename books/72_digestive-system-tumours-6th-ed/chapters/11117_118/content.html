11.1.1.7 Mucinous cystic neoplasm of the pancreas

Definition

Mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) of the pancreas is a non-invasive locule-forming and typically mucin-producing epithelial neoplasm associated with distinctive ovarian-type subepithelial stroma.

ICD-O coding

84701/0 Mucinous cystic neoplasm with low-grade dysplasia

84700/2 Mucinous cystic neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia

ICD-11 coding

2E92.8 & XH6NK7 Mucinous cystic neoplasm with low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of the pancreas

2E61.Y & XA3QC5 & XH81P3 Mucinous cystic neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia of the pancreas

Related terminology

Mucinous cystic neoplasm

Acceptable: mucinous cystadenoma.

High-grade mucinous cystic neoplasm

Not recommended: carcinoma in situ.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The majority (> 95%) of MCNs occur in the body or tail of the pancreas {25517958;37922344;39395314}.

Clinical features

Small tumours (< 30 mm) are usually found incidentally. Larger tumours may produce symptoms due to compression of adjacent structures, often accompanied by a palpable abdominal mass. Imaging studies reveal a large, well-defined cystic lesion with thick-walled locules that do not connect to the pancreatic ducts {19260748;15185076;20924309}. A fistula may develop between one of the cysts and adjacent structures such as the stomach or bowel. Features suggestive of an associated invasive carcinoma include large tumour size (> 50 mm), irregular thickening of the cyst wall, intracystic mural nodules, and elevated serum CA19-9 levels (> 37 kU/L) {25517958;27760255;36822873}. Preoperative cyst fluid CEA levels {22301608;20797749;20658204} and molecular analysis {23743931;20043327;22376266;36209796} may also supplement other clinical findings in assessing the risk of carcinoma in MCNs.

Epidemiology

MCN is a relatively uncommon tumour that accounts for approximately 8–10% of pancreatic cystic lesions and was detected in 25% of all resected cystic neoplasms of the pancreas during studies of large surgical series {15185076;28002329}. This tumour has a female predominance (> 95%). It is usually seen at an age of 40–60 years, with a median age at diagnosis of 45 years (range: 14–95 years) {36114217;32670685;25517958;37922344;39395314}. Patients having an MCN with associated invasive carcinoma are 5–10 years older than patients with a non-invasive MCN, suggesting that progression occurs over a period of years {15185076;37922344}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

Pancreatic MCNs share many clinicopathological features with their counterparts in the hepatobiliary tree, ovary, and other organs {16445818;29016404;30229909}. It has been hypothesized that ectopic ovarian stroma incorporated in the pancreas during embryogenesis may become activated in the setting of a hormonal imbalance, releasing growth factors and causing nearby pancreatic ductal epithelium to proliferate and form a cystic neoplasm {12684617;30229909}. This hypothesis does not account for MCNs in male patients. Another possibility is that the ovarian-type stroma represents persistent fetal periductal mesenchyme, which proliferates in response to hormonal stimulation [[Hruban RH, Pitman MB, Klimstra DS. Tumors of the pancreas. Washington, DC: American Registry of Pathology; 2007. (AFIP atlas of tumorpathology, series 4; fascicle 6). Bookref]] {29693047;24067880;35228647}.

The epithelial component of MCNs often harbours somatic activating mutations in codon 12 of KRAS (50–66% of cases), as well as loss-of-function alterations in RNF43 {21775669;26253305;35066614;28570009;40122523}. Mutations of TP53 are rare and usually occur in the setting of high-grade dysplasia or an associated invasive carcinoma {23200980;26253305;28570009}.

Macroscopic appearance

MCNs range from 20 to 350 mm in greatest dimension (mean: 60 mm), but MCNs with associated invasive carcinoma are larger (mean: 90 mm) {25517958}. MCNs are typically unifocal and composed of multiple cysts, each with a fibrous wall of variable thickness, occasionally containing calcifications. The tumours often have a cyst-within-a-cyst appearance. The cysts contain variably thick mucin and/or haemorrhagic, necrotic material, and they do not communicate with the pancreatic ducts. The internal surfaces of the cysts may be smooth; however, high-grade MCNs often have nodules or papillary projections {25517958;27760255;36822873}. An associated invasive carcinoma may not be identifiable grossly when it is small; larger carcinomas appear as solid, sclerotic masses within or adjacent to the cystic neoplasm.

Histopathology

The cysts of MCNs are lined by neoplastic epithelium and have underlying ovarian-type stroma. The epithelium is predominantly columnar, with mucin-producing cells; however, foci composed of cuboidal cells lacking mucin, similar to non-neoplastic ductal cells, can occur as well, and (rarely) the entire neoplasm lacks mucinous epithelium {27673548;35192699;31077597}. The epithelium can be focally or extensively denuded, producing cysts lined by granulation tissue and sclerotic stroma resembling a pseudocyst. On the basis of the highest degree of cytoarchitectural atypia in the epithelium, MCNs are categorized as low-grade or high-grade (two-tiered grading system) {11342768;15252303;26559377}. Low-grade MCNs are characterized by mild to moderate atypia and may or may not have papillary projections and mitoses. High-grade MCNs have severe atypia, with the formation of papillae with irregular branching and budding, nuclear stratification with loss of polarity, nuclear pleomorphism, and prominent nucleoli; mitoses are numerous.

The distinctive ovarian-type stroma consists of densely packed spindle-shaped cells with round or elongated nuclei and sparse cytoplasm. Ovarian stroma is required for the diagnosis {16327281} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. The presence of ovarian stroma is a useful diagnostic clue when the epithelial lining is extensively denuded. The stroma frequently contains clusters of epithelioid cells with round to oval nuclei and abundant clear or eosinophilic cytoplasm, resembling luteinized cells {10199470;35192699}. In large MCNs or in those occurring in postmenopausal patients, the stroma may be hypocellular and hyalinized. Similarly, the amount of ovarian stroma may be decreased around areas of high-grade dysplasia and invasive carcinoma {25517958}.

About 15% of MCNs have an associated invasive carcinoma, which typically occurs in large (> 50 mm) tumours with gross papillary nodules {21128317;39395314;27760255;36822873;29434957;28416122;25517958;39395214}. It is important to distinguish high-grade MCNs (high-grade dysplasia) from MCNs with associated invasive carcinoma, since the prognosis for the two groups after surgical resection is vastly different. The invasive component is usually tubular-type adenocarcinoma, but other subtypes have been described, including adenosquamous carcinoma and undifferentiated carcinoma with osteoclast-like giant cells; colloid carcinoma does not occur in MCNs {10658914;11145249;27028547;25517958}. The invasive component can be focal and limited to the septa of the cysts. Therefore, thorough (if not complete) sampling is needed to rule out invasion {25517958}.

The neoplastic epithelial cells are immunoreactive for CK7, CK8, CK18, CK19, EMA, CEA, and MUC5AC {17520198} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Non-invasive MCNs express SMAD4 protein but lack MUC1 expression, whereas an invasive carcinoma component may lose expression of SMAD4 but express MUC1 {11914624;11075857}. The ovarian-type stroma expresses PR (60–90%), ER (30%), SMA, and desmin {25441310;35192699}. Luteinized cells stain for tyrosine hydroxylase, calretinin, and α-inhibin {27060902;35192699}.

Distinguishing between branch duct–type intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms and MCNs can be difficult. Two features distinguish these entities: MCNs do not grow within the pancreatic duct system, and they contain (by definition) ovarian-type stroma. Differentiating a pseudocyst from an MCN may be a problem, as MCNs can have substantial degenerative changes and denuded epithelium. In these cases, extensive sampling may be needed to identify the mucin-producing epithelial lining or the ovarian-type stroma of MCN [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]].

Cytology

Aspirates contain varying amounts of thick mucin and often only few epithelial cells [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]]. The ovarian-type stroma is often not present in aspirates. The cytology for MCN is therefore not specific for an MCN, and only the nonspecific diagnosis of mucin-producing neoplastic cyst can be made in most cases {25775066;27926784;30257067}. The cysts of MCNs typically do not communicate with each other, and dysplasia can be focal. Hence, the cytology for MCN is not sensitive for detection of high-risk/high-grade cyst (50%).

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in postoperative specimens. In the preoperative setting, molecular testing of pancreatic cyst fluid may be useful {36209796;26253305}. KRAS and GNAS mutations show high sensitivity (90%) / specificity (100%) for a mucin-producing cyst and, when combined with TP53/SMAD4/CTNNB1/MTOR alterations, molecular testing has high sensitivity (88%) / specificity (98%) for high neoplastic grade.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a non-invasive, grossly visible, multilocular cystic lesion, occurring almost exclusively in female patients, with no communication to the ductal system, lined by cuboidal or columnar neoplastic epithelium, and containing ovarian-like stroma.

Desirable: immunolabelling of the stroma for PR or ER.

Staging

High-grade MCNs are categorized as Tis (carcinoma in situ). Staging of an invasive carcinoma arising in an MCN is based on the invasive component and follows the protocol for pancreatic carcinomas [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

If invasive carcinoma is ruled out by thorough sampling of the neoplasm, the prognosis for surgically resected non-invasive MCNs is excellent, with a 5-year survival rate of 100% {15551256;18362619;20924309;22902940;27760255}. However, invasive carcinomas arising in MCNs have an aggressive clinical course, with 3-year and 5-year survival rates of 44% and 26%, respectively. The prognosis for these patients depends on the size (T stage) of the invasive component, nodal and distant metastasis, and resectability {25517958;39068306}. Earlier data suggesting that invasive carcinomas limited to the septa of the cysts are unlikely to metastasize {34062050} conflict with a recent report of metastases from microscopic, cyst-limited (pT1a) carcinomas {25517958}.
