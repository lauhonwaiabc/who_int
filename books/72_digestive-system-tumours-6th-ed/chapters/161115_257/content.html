16.1.1.15 Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1)

Definition

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is an autosomal dominant multicentric tumour syndrome caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic sequence variants in MEN1.

MIM numbering

131100 Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type 1; MEN1

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.0 Multiple polyglandular tumours

Related terminology

Acceptable: MEN1 syndrome.

Not recommended: multiple endocrine adenomatosis type 1; familial Zollinger–Ellison syndrome; Wermer syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Patients with MEN1 present with multifocal endocrine neoplasms in the pancreas, duodenum, and less commonly in the stomach.

Clinical features

In the GI tract, the most common manifestations of MEN1 syndrome are pancreatic and duodenal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), followed by gastric NETs. These NETs may present with mass lesions or symptoms associated with hormone excess, similar to sporadic tumours (e.g. gastrinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, VIPoma) or be identified by screening programmes in asymptomatic carriers {30202829}. Outside the GI tract, there maybe be multifocal endocrine or neuroendocrine neoplasms in the parathyroids (including ectopic or supernumerary parathyroid glands in the mediastinum), anterior pituitary, adrenal, thymus, and lungs. Various non-endocrine lesions may occur in the skin, central nervous system, and soft tissues including adrenocortical tumours, angiofibromas, collagenomas, lipomas, leiomyomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas {22723327;25099597;33564173;11739416;32130200}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is estimated at 3–20 per 100 000 population {33249439}. The mean age at diagnosis of symptomatic disease is the fourth to fifth decades {28132167;21950691;11836268;26126205;30637623;23933118;33249439}. Most endocrine manifestations show an equal sex distribution or a slight female predominance, but in some series pancreatic NETs (PanNETs) show a male predilection {21551167}. The prevalence of gastroenteropancreatic NETs is 53–59% in MEN1 registries {28132167;21950691;11836268;26126205}. Gastric ECL-cell NETs occur in 23–29% of patients {11344233;18270260}.

Etiology

Germline pathogenic variants (GPVs) in MEN1 are responsible for the development of MEN1 syndrome and are passed on as an autosomal dominant trait {23933118;33249439}. About 10% of patients have de novo GPVs in MEN1 with no family history {9463336;23933118}.

Pathogenesis

GPVs in MEN1 occur in the majority of patients with MEN1 and include a large variety of genetic abnormalities {17879353;26767918;33249439}. These are accompanied by loss of the wildtype allele in > 90% of MEN1-driven tumours. The 610 amino acid protein product called menin is involved in multiple complex signalling mechanisms, which play a role in tumour suppression {9103196;9215690;17879353;22829075;23652667;23850066;26560942;28899949;33249439}.

Macroscopic appearance

The MEN1 pancreas undergoing surgical resection often contains only a few grossly recognizable tumours. They are usually between 10 and 30 mm in size and occasionally cystic {20922407}. Typically, they are accompanied by multiple small (5–10 mm), barely recognizable tan to yellow tumours. In the duodenum, the NETs may present as multiple barely visible small nodules detectable only on careful grossing, or as large obvious tumours indistinguishable from sporadic NETs. In the stomach, MEN1 tumours correspond to type 2 gastric ECL-cell tumours that form multiple mucosal nodules in a hypertrophic corpus mucosa {33506527}.

Sometimes functional neoplasms are so small that careful correlation with advanced imaging techniques (e.g. CT, MRI, EUS, 68Ga-DOTATATE/DOTATOC PET/CT, 18F-FDG PET) are required for identification {30202829;26242621;28263205;28790162;32971521}. Extensive sampling of pancreas, stomach, and duodenum, even when they are grossly normal, is recommended as there are often subscopic precursor lesions or microtumours {23478233;26113081}.

Histopathology

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1–related pancreatic disease

The hallmark of MEN1-related pancreatic disease is the presence of multifocal PanNETs {16699310;24718881}. Many of the tumours may be small and morphologically indistinguishable from large islets of Langerhans but still show evidence of clonality such as loss of the wildtype allele of MEN1 by molecular studies or hormone restriction by immunohistochemistry {17179192}. Histologically, the tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. Glucagon is the most frequently identified hormone, followed by pancreatic polypeptide, insulin, and somatostatin {24718881}. Multihormonality is common {8898842;16699310}. Ectopic hormone production including gastrin, calcitonin, ACTH, VIP, GHRH, and PTHrP may occur in PanNETs but is rare {9920064;21483254;16699310}. Because MEN1 inactivation can occur in about 40% of sporadic PanNETs {33459926}, loss of menin expression by immunohistochemistry cannot be used to distinguish MEN1 syndrome in the absence of multicentric microtumours.

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1–related duodenal disease

In almost all patients with MEN1 along with Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, gastrin excess is due to multicentric duodenal NETs {15887103;17312377;17985091;1968616;1678681;23478233;24718881}. They arise in a background of neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia. Focal aggregates of hyperplastic gastrin-cells, but also somatostatin cells, can be identified in crypts and between Brunner glands. The tumours are often small and may be difficult to detect {18520436}. They are typically centred in the mucosa and submucosa of the upper duodenum and show a trabecular to pseudoglandular pattern. As many as 80% of tumours give rise to periduodenal and/or peripancreatic lymph node metastases, which are often much larger than the corresponding primaries. Peripancreatic lymph nodes completely replaced by metastatic tumour and near the pancreas can easily be misinterpreted as pancreatic primary tumours. The individual MEN1-related duodenal NETs resemble sporadic tumours; however, their multicentric nature and association with gastrin cell hyperplasia are clues to the diagnosis of MEN1. Rare examples of MEN1-related duodenal NETs can be somatostatin-positive {15887103;24718881;23478233}.

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1–related gastric disease

An ECL-cell hyperplasia to neoplasia progression sequence due to gastrin hypersecretion by an associated duodenal gastrinoma is the hallmark of MEN1-related gastric disease and defines the tumours as gastric type 2 NETs {23478233;25053544;25740317}. The patients who are affected show multiple small low-grade EC-cell tumours of variable sizes (often 5 to 10 mm) that have developed in a hypertrophic corpus mucosa together with ECL-cell hyperplasia. After long-term use of acid blockers, hypertrophy of the corpus mucosa disappears {23478233;25053544;25740317}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual endocrine tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline MEN1 testing is usually offered to (1) an index patient with clinical diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome (based on 2 or more MEN1 syndrome–related tumours), (2) an asymptomatic first-degree relative of a patient with MEN1 pathogenic variant (typically as early as < 5 years), (3) symptomatic first-degree relatives (with at least one MEN1-related tumour) of a proband with MEN1 pathogenic variant, (4) patients with primary hyperparathyroidism-related multiglandular parathyroid disease or parathyroid adenomas in young individuals (< 30 years), and (5) gastrinoma or multifocal PanNETs at any age {33249439}.

In general, DNA sequence analysis (exons and splice junction variants) and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) for duplication/deletion analysis (screening for larger alterations) are used in most clinical laboratories {33249439}. Around 10% of patients with clinically diagnosed MEN1 may be negative for MEN1 pathogenic variants on testing {23933118}. Some have multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 syndrome (due to GPVs in CDKN1B) {23933118;30990521;35323929}, or, more rarely, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 syndrome (MAX variants) {33367756}. However, the commonly used screening tools may fail to identify somatic mosaicism or all GPVs in MEN1 {33249439}; and whole-exome or whole genome deep sequencing may be indicated in specific circumstances {33249439}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • Clinical diagnostic criteria: at least two MEN1 syndrome–associated tumours OR
  • Familial diagnostic criteria: at least one MEN1 syndrome–associated tumour and a first-degree relative with a confirmed diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome OR
  • Genetic diagnostic criteria: GPV in MEN1

Staging

Malignant tumours are staged using the same American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) / Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM systems applied to sporadic equivalents.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with MEN1 are at increased risk of death due to the disease and its complications {11038219;19949948;23645327;33249439}. Variant-negative patients with 2 out of 3 main MEN1 manifestations generally have a different phenotype and clinical course with an older age at presentation and a normal life expectancy {27842554;32703679}. Some studies suggest the existence of high-risk MEN1 variants affecting the JUND {23376981} or the FOXN3 interacting domains {25210877}. Well-established causes of high morbidity are thymic NETs, duodenal NETs, and PanNETs, along with rare cases of aggressive adrenal tumours {11038219;19949948;23645327;24915123;24389729}. Female sex, family history of MEN1, and recent diagnosis are associated with a lower risk of death {19949948}. At the tumour level, the prognostic factors are the same as those applied to sporadic tumours, with tumour stage, size, and grade being the most important parameters {23645327}.
