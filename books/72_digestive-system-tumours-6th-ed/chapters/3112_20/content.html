3.1.1.2 Gastric hyperplastic polyps

Definition

Hyperplastic polyps of the stomach are benign, non-neoplastic epithelial lesions characterized by elongated or tortuous gastric foveolae set in an expanded oedematous stroma and accompanied by varying degrees of inflammation.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

DA44.0 Hyperplastic polyp of stomach

Related terminology

Acceptable: foveolar hyperplastic polyp; inflammatory-type polyp; regenerative polyp.

Subtype(s)

Polypoid foveolar hyperplasia; mucosal prolapse polyp

Localization

The location of gastric hyperplastic polyps is related to the nature of gastric mucosal injury that predisposes to polyp development. Many hyperplastic polyps are located in the antrum, reflecting the tendency of Helicobacter pylori–related chronic gastritis and chemical gastropathy to be related to this lesion. However, those associated with autoimmune gastritis are detected in the body or fundus {30353753}.

Clinical features

Hyperplastic polyps may be multiple and are typically asymptomatic. They frequently show superficial erosions, and, in this situation, bleeding from the polyp may result in iron-deficiency anaemia. This is particularly true in polyps that occur in patients with portal hypertensive gastropathy {34432677;30155451}. Large polyps located in the pyloric channel may rarely cause gastric outlet obstruction {12143253;16496067}. Polypoid foveolar hyperplasia and white elongated lesions are asymptomatic and do not usually pose any clinical problems {29870991}. Polypoid nodular scars should not be misinterpreted as a sign of cancer recurrence following endoscopic submucosal dissection {30302377}.

Epidemiology

There is no evidence of a sex-specific distribution in the occurrence of hyperplastic polyps. They typically occur in the sixth or seventh decade of life (median age: 66 years), and most arise in the setting of chronic mucosal injury. Thus, their epidemiology mirrors predominantly that of H. pylori–related gastritis, chemical gastropathy, and pernicious anaemia–type autoimmune gastritis. The overall prevalence of hyperplastic polyps is approximately 2% among patients undergoing upper endoscopic examination and constitute an important proportion of all gastric polyps in this population (up to 50% in certain studies) {19491866;35183117}.

Etiology

Hyperplastic polyps reflect an exuberant epithelial proliferation in response to mucosal injury due to a variety of conditions.

Pathogenesis

Chronic mucosal injury resulting from inflammation (i.e. chronic gastritis), chemical irritation by bile or acid, or intermittent ischaemia in the context of altered blood flow leads to epithelial proliferation with disproportionate expansion of the foveolar compartment {3722405;12021855}. Because they frequently arise in a background of chronic gastritis or gastropathy, hyperplastic polyps can develop foci of dysplasia or even adenocarcinoma {7903667;12395375}. Malignant transformation is associated with mutations of TP53 and overexpression of p53 {12395375;26325218}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most gastric hyperplastic polyps appear as small (< 10 mm) dome-shaped polyps, with surface erythema. Larger polyps are lobulated or pedunculated and frequently show surface erosions.

Histopathology

Hyperplastic polyps of the stomach are characterized by the expansion of the foveolar component relative to other mucosal elements. Therefore, they contain hyperplastic foveolae with an elongated, distorted, branching, or dilated appearance. The lamina propria is frequently oedematous, inflamed, and rich in vasculature. The hyperplastic foveolae can contain globoid cells, which are not dysplastic and represent distended goblet cell–like mucinous cells resembling signet-ring cell carcinoma in situ. Small, haphazardly distributed bundles of smooth muscle cells may be present. Between 1% and 20% of hyperplastic polyps harbour dysplasia, and approximately 2% contain foci of carcinoma {8601386}, especially when the polyps are > 10 mm in size {8601386;12395375}. Larger polyps, particularly those of the distal antrum, may contain thick-walled vessels and bundles of smooth muscle cells emanating from the muscularis mucosae {21451363}. Some authors have suggested that such polyps result from mucosal prolapse.

Several other types of gastric polyps have overlapping clinical and pathological features with gastric hyperplastic polyps.

“Polypoid foveolar hyperplasia” is a term that may be applied to small (< 10 mm) similar appearing polyps consisting of elongated pits embedded in minimally oedematous lamina propria {21451363}. Similar proliferative foci described as white elevated lesions show features akin to those of polypoid foveolar hyperplasia and have been associated with the use of proton-pump inhibitors {37157963}.

Gastritis cystica polyposa/profunda reflects polypoid mucosal hyperplasia in the context of a Billroth II procedure {5007382;3341916}. In this situation, the removal of the pyloric sphincter results in the free reflux of alkaline enteric contents into the gastric remnant, resulting in severe chemical gastropathy and antralization of the oxyntic mucosa. Biopsies demonstrate the replacement of oxyntic glands by simple pyloric-type glands. Some cases containing cystically dilated glands in the submucosa are classified as gastritis cystica profunda.

Portal hypertensive polyps result from mucosal hyperplasia in the setting of portal hypertension {22002002;32184817;34432677}. These lesions may be multiple and tend to occur in the body/fundus. They contain variably regenerative foveolar epithelium with dilated pits supported by lamina propria with increased vascularity. Dilated mucosal capillaries may contain early organizing thrombi and be associated with extravasated red blood cells. Polypoid nodular scars are protruded regenerative changes occurring in the antrum after endoscopic submucosal dissection and exhibit histological features consistent with hyperplastic polyps {30302377}.

Gastric hyperplastic polyps should also be differentiated from several types of polyps, including fundic gland polyps, foveolar- and intestinal-type adenomas, pyloric gland adenomas, and hamartomatous polyps associated with juvenile polyposis and Cowden syndrome. Gastric inverted polyp (hamartomatous inverted polyp) is a solitary polypoid lesion characterized by the downgrowth of hyperplastic and cystic glandular components into the submucosa. The muscularis mucosae, continuous from the normal layer, demarcates the lesion {33399336}. Although some authors consider this type of lesion to represent a hamartomatous inverted polyp, the presence of abundant smooth muscle cell bundles and cystically dilated submucosal glands raises the possibility that they represent a pattern of mucosal prolapse.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a polyp with a predominant component of elongated and tortuous glands lined by foveolar epithelium; oedematous and inflamed lamina propria.

Desirable: cystically dilated glands; erosions, ulcers.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

Foci of dysplasia and adenocarcinoma have been reported in hyperplastic polyps > 10 mm in size {8601386;12395375}. When the foci are present, the biological risk of these changes is similar to that of neoplasia developing in flat mucosa.
