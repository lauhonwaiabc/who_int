13.1.6.5 Malignant gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumour

Definition

Malignant gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumour (GNET) is an aggressive malignant neoplasm showing neuroectodermal differentiation with characteristic EWSR1 fusions.

ICD-O coding

90442/3 Malignant gastrointestinal neuroectodermal tumour

ICD-11 coding

2B5Y & XA6452 Other specified malignant mesenchymal neoplasms of small intestine

Related terminology

Not recommended: clear cell sarcoma–like tumour of the gastrointestinal tract; osteoclast-rich tumour of the gastrointestinal tract with features resembling clear cell sarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

GNETs most commonly arise in the small bowel, particularly the ileum. Gastric, colonic, oesophageal, and anal examples have also been reported {29970252;31651526}. Despite its name, GNET can rarely arise outside the GI tract {36934861}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with local symptoms and signs related to bowel obstruction and intussusception, particularly abdominal pain. Systemic symptoms such as fever, anorexia, and weight loss are reported less commonly.

Epidemiology

GNET is rare, with about 100 cases reported in the literature, typically affecting young to middle-aged adults (median age: 33 years, range: 10–81 years), with sex parity {29970252}.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

GNET is postulated to arise from a gastrointestinal neuroectodermal precursor cell, possibly autonomic (neural crest), which has lost the ability to differentiate along a melanocytic lineage {17000668;22592145}. Most cases demonstrate EWSR1 gene fusions, with ATF1 or CREB1 as the partner.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours are usually centred on the muscularis propria of the GI tract, forming an often ulcerated endophytic, polypoid, or transmural mass, resembling carcinoma. Median tumour size is 45 mm (range 15–135 mm) {29970252}. The cut surface is solid, lobulated, and grey to white, with haemorrhage {31651526}.

Histopathology

GNET is characterized by epithelioid and/or spindle cells occurring in various growth patterns: solid sheets, nests, pseudopapillary, pseudoalveolar, trabecular, fascicular, microcystic, and rosette-like arrangements. Tumour cells demonstrate relatively monotonous rounded nuclei with vesicular chromatin, small to prominent nucleoli, and moderate amounts of eosinophilic (and sometimes clear) cytoplasm. Up to 50% of cases show focal multinucleated giant cells of osteoclast-type {12754623;22592145} (distinct from the wreath-like giant cells seen in clear cell sarcoma). Mitoses are readily identified, and necrosis can be focally present.

GNET typically expresses S100 protein and SOX10, whereas melanocytic markers (HMB45, melan-A, and MITF) are negative. CD56 and synaptophysin are also variably positive. Although DOG1 can be expressed, KIT (CD117), as well as keratins, chromogranin, CD34, and desmin are negative.

Despite sharing some morphological, immunophenotypic, and molecular genetic features, GNET warrants separate classification from gastrointestinal clear cell sarcoma {29970252;29491075}. The latter shows melanocytic differentiation, expressing HMB45, melan-A, and MITF. The differential diagnosis also includes malignant melanoma and perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa), both of which can be distinguished by staining for melanocytic markers and the absence of an EWSR1 fusion.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The molecular genetic hallmark of GNET is an EWSR1 fusion, with ATF1 being the most common partner, followed by CREB1.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: compatible histological features; positivity for S100 protein and SOX10; EWSR1 gene rearrangement.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

GNET is an aggressive malignant neoplasm, with poor prognosis. Metastases can be seen in up to 50% of patients, typically to liver, lymph nodes, and peritoneum {22592145;28887884}. Median survival is reported to be 9.5 months {29970252}.
