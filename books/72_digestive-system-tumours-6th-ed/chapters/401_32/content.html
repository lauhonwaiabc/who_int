4.0.1 Tumours of the duodenum and ampulla: Introduction

 

In this sixth edition, tumours of the duodenum and ampulla are grouped in a new chapter because of their overlapping epidemiological and pathogenetic features. Indeed, concerning neoplastic diseases, the duodenum is the most frequently involved segment of the small intestine [[Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program (Internet). Bethesda (MD): National Cancer Institute; 2020. Cancer Stat Facts: anal cancer. Available from: https://seer.cancer.gov/statfacts/html/anus.html. Bookref]] {38230766;35001295}, with the ampulla being the most common localization, followed by the bulb and the descending duodenum {31487680;25310836}. In addition, neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are now covered in a separate chapter, as was already the case for haematolymphoid and mesenchymal tumours and for genetic tumour syndromes in the previous edition.

The slight increase in incidence of small bowel cancer over the last few decades is mainly because of an increase in duodenal tumours {27546842;27170100;33712346}, which could be related to mucosal irritating factors, such as gastric acids, bile and pancreatic enzymes, or Helicobacter pylori infection, resulting from lifestyle habits {30884915;34000722}. Chronic inflammatory conditions, such as coeliac disease and Crohn disease, are also associated with an increased risk of duodenal cancer, but the association is less strong than it is for jejunal adenocarcinoma {35001295}. Genetic syndromes, such as Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, and Peutz–Jeghers syndrome, are all associated with an increased risk of developing ampullary and extra-ampullary duodenal cancer {34000722}. In particular, the ampulla and the periampullary duodenum represent the most common localization for familial adenomatous polyposis–associated adenomas and adenocarcinomas in the small intestine {34624304;37985239;38142452}.

As explained in detail in the following sections, there are four main types of cancer occurring in the duodenum/ampulla: non-ampullary adenocarcinoma (associated with an adenomatous component in about one third of the cases, described in section Non-ampullary adenoma) {27739441}; ampullary duodenal adenocarcinoma (most commonly presenting as an exophytic mass growing on the duodenal surface of the ampulla, obstructing the orifice of the papilla and usually associated with an adenomatous component); intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm–associated adenocarcinoma (characterized by intra-ampullary growth leading to a symmetrical, raised papilla); and ampullary ductal adenocarcinoma (growing in the ampulla and originating from non-adenomatous high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of the ampullary ducts) {38938087}. Adenomas associated with both ampullary duodenal cancers and intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasms are described in detail in section Ampullary adenomatous neoplasm.

The anatomical complexity of the ampulla drives this subdivision and makes tumour staging difficult {23062420}. The four main types of duodenal cancers may have different directions of differentiation, and they have different prognoses. The prognosis of non-ampullary duodenal cancer is similar to that of ampullary duodenal adenocarcinoma {27739441} and of cancers originating from intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm, which is in turn significantly better than that of cancers arising deep within the ampulla (ampullary ductal carcinomas) {22350721;23026934;38888860}, with one multicentre retrospective study reporting a 5-year survival rate of 54% for all ampullary adenocarcinomas {37149485}.

New information at the molecular level is incorporated throughout the chapter (when relevant). Mismatch repair deficiency / microsatellite instability and ERBB2 (HER2) overexpression/amplification represent the most promising predictive markers in both extra-ampullary duodenal cancer and ampullary cancer.
