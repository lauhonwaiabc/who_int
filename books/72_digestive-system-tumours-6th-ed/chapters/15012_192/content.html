15.0.1.2 Mucosal melanoma of the digestive system

Definition

Mucosal melanoma of the digestive system is a primary malignant melanocytic neoplasm arising in the digestive tract.

ICD-O coding

87200/3 Melanoma, NOS

87211/3 Nodular mucosal melanoma

87460/3 Mucosal lentiginous melanoma

ICD-11 coding

2C00.1 & XH4846 Melanoma, NOS, of anus or anal canal

2C00.1 & XH4QG5 Nodular melanoma of anus or anal canal

2C00.1 & XH5QP3 Mucosal lentiginous melanoma of anus or anal canal

Related terminology

Acceptable: mucosal lentiginous melanoma; anal melanoma; anorectal melanoma; oesophageal melanoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

In the digestive tract, these rare tumours typically arise in the anorectal region {24272143;9781962}. Less common locations include the distal oesophagus and very rarely other sites {9369967;9921971}. Melanoma involving the small intestine or gallbladder is usually metastatic in nature.

Clinical features

The clinical signs and symptoms depend on the anatomical location affected. Diagnosis is often delayed. Anorectal melanomas often present with rectal bleeding, a mass, pain, or a change in bowel habits, and they are often misdiagnosed as haemorrhoids because of their dark colour {19774417;29492238}. Patients with oesophageal melanoma may present with nausea, dysphagia, pain, or symptoms of reflux {14646623}.

Epidemiology

Primary melanoma involving the digestive tract accounts for < 1% of gastrointestinal malignancies and about 1% of anorectal malignancies overall. Mucosal melanomas account for approximately 1% of all melanomas in most European populations and for 25–50% of melanomas in dark-skinned populations {24272143}.

Etiology

The etiology of mucosal melanomas is unknown. Unlike cutaneous melanomas, they show no association with ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure {28467829}.

Pathogenesis

Unlike cutaneous melanomas, mucosal melanomas show a low mutation burden, with no UV radiation signature. Instead, they are characterized by numerous chromosomal structural variants and abundant copy-number changes, including multiple high-level amplifications {28467829;23620124}. Somatic mutations include KIT, NF1, SF3B1, NRAS, BRAF, and other genes {27739435;31383963}. Compared with cutaneous melanomas, one may find relatively more non–Val600 (V600) BRAF mutations, but the frequency of any BRAF mutation in mucosal melanoma is significantly less than that in cutaneous melanomas {15578519;28467829;28720667;28228113;31398831}.

Macroscopic appearance

Primary mucosal melanoma usually presents as a large, often ulcerated mass {11891956}. It may involve the surrounding surface mucosa and display dark pigmentation. Amelanotic tumours, however, may be grossly indistinguishable from adenocarcinoma.

Histopathology

Melanoma in situ of the associated squamous epithelium typically displays a predominantly lentiginous growth pattern, but there may also be pagetoid upward migration. Some tumours, especially large ulcerated ones, may lack a histopathologically detectable adjacent melanoma in situ and may therefore be classified as nodular melanoma. The invasive tumour is usually composed of sheets or expansive nodules of large pleomorphic epithelioid or (less commonly) malignant melanocytic spindle cells {29763716;12873174}. Pigmentation is variable and may be absent. Necrosis is rare. The nuclei often have vesicular chromatin and prominent nucleoli. Occasionally, small or naevoid cells may predominate. Less frequently, a lentiginous growth of individual atypical melanocytes in the basal layer may occur, sometimes with nests or confluent growth. A subepithelial lymphocytic infiltrate is common. Anogenital melanomas may occasionally be associated with a conventional melanocytic naevus. A rare melanoma of the digestive tract may be derived from a blue naevus.

Cytology

FNA specimens are usually obtained from metastases. The typical cytological appearances include a dissociated population of large epithelioid (or sometimes spindle) cells {17276948}. Isolated giant cells and intranuclear pseudoinclusions are often present. Cytoplasmic pigment is usually present in a minority of cells. Pigmented macrophages may be seen in the background.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Mutation analysis of mucosal melanomas is clinically indicated when systemic treatment with targeted therapy is being considered {15578519;28467829;28720667;28228113}. The most common somatic mutations of anorectal melanomas involve KIT (24%), followed by NRAS (18%) {31383963}. Molecular testing may also be of clinical value for the distinction from metastatic melanoma or sarcoma.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstrated malignancy and melanocytic differentiation; relation to the mucosa of the digestive tract; no evidence of previous or synchronous melanoma outside the digestive tract.

Staging

There are no Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging criteria for gastrointestinal melanomas (including anorectal melanomas). One study has suggested staging analogous to version 9 of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) system {31383963}.

Prognosis and prediction

Unlike cutaneous melanoma, primary melanoma of the GI tract remains associated with relatively poor overall survival despite the use of multiple treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapies {29748886;28600969;12595048;19774417}. This is partly attributable to delayed detection. The reported 5-year survival rates for patients with anorectal melanoma are approximately 20% {8409692;19774417}. Patients presenting with regional or distant metastases have significantly shorter disease-specific survival compared with those with disease localized to the primary site. Increasing tumour thickness and lymphatic invasion have also been correlated with shorter survival {27533633}. Outcome after systemic immunotherapy for metastatic mucosal melanoma appears to be poorer than that for cutaneous melanoma, but the data are limited {27533633}.
