11.1.1.2 Serous neoplasms of the pancreas

Definition

Serous neoplasms of the pancreas are epithelial tumours composed of uniform cuboidal, glycogen-rich cells that often form cysts containing straw-coloured fluid. The diagnosis of malignancy is restricted to cases with unequivocal distant metastasis beyond the pancreatic/peripancreatic bed.

ICD-O coding

84410/0 Serous cystadenoma, NOS

84410/3 Serous cystadenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2E92.8 & XH8TJ0 Serous cystadenoma, NOS, of pancreas

2C10.Y & XH7A08 Serous cystadenocarcinoma, NOS, of pancreas

Related terminology

Serous cystadenoma

Not recommended: microcystic adenoma; glycogen-rich adenoma; oligocystic ill-demarcated adenoma.

Subtype(s)

Microcystic serous adenoma (84414/0); macrocystic (oligocystic) serous cystadenoma (84412/0); solid serous adenoma (84411/0); von Hippel–Lindau syndrome–associated serous cystic neoplasm (84413/0); mixed serous–neuroendocrine neoplasm (84411/3)

Localization

Serous cystadenomas can occur anywhere in the pancreas, but they arise most frequently (50–75%) in the pancreatic body or tail {21468008;22415666;26045140;26559376}. Most are unifocal; however, patients with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome have multifocal disease that can involve the full length of the gland {22370733;28697137;23543325}. Serous cystadenocarcinomas often arise in the body and/or tail of the pancreas.

Clinical features

Serous cystadenoma

Patients may have symptoms related to local mass effect, such as nonspecific abdominal and back pain, a palpable mass, nausea and vomiting, diabetes mellitus, and weight loss {18382099;17440789;18815545;26045140}. Jaundice caused by obstruction of the distal common bile duct is unusual, even in neoplasms within the pancreatic head. However, 60% of patients are asymptomatic at clinical presentation {21242709;22415666;26045140}.

A classic CT finding is a well-circumscribed and multilocular cystic/microcystic mass {19542405;27614585}. Approximately 30% of serous cystadenomas have a central scar with a sunburst calcification pattern. On MRI, serous cystadenomas are usually hyperintense on T2-weighted images and hypointense on T1-weighted images {17650223;19542405;19959519}. Occasionally, intracystic debris (especially haemorrhage) alters this signal intensity pattern. The thin septa of the neoplasm are well depicted on T2-weighted images, but the central scar is not. EUS reveals an echogenic mass with numerous cysts, which produce a characteristic honeycomb pattern {22415666}. The sensitivity of EUS can be increased by using it in conjunction with needle-based confocal laser endomicroscopy, but this technique can only be used on larger cysts {25325684;28286093}. The cysts do not communicate with the pancreatic ductal system, but upstream ductal dilatation has been documented in 10% of cases {26045140}. Despite the typical radiographical appearance of most cases, the probability of uncertain diagnosis or even misdiagnosis based on preoperative imaging is high {21468008;22415666}. Serum tumour markers are generally within normal limits.

Serous cystadenocarcinoma

Serous cystadenocarcinomas have been reported to account for 1–3% of serous neoplasms. However, when applying strict criteria requiring the presence of true distant metastases, < 20 cases have been reported {9933679;9436861;9931400;15104296;1568167;17440789;18305183;12949436;19060596;22009426;22189969;22369840;22400805;22415666;23370947;2909198;27549181;26045140;26998825}, and true malignancy may occur in as few as 0.2% of serous neoplasms {26045140}. These patients are aged 52–86 years, and two thirds are women. The signs and symptoms associated with serous cystadenocarcinomas include abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, jaundice, weight loss, diarrhoea, and a palpable mass {9933679;9436861;9931400;15104296;1568167;17440789;18305183;12949436;19060596;22009426;22189969;22369840;22400805;22415666;23370947;2909198;27549181;26045140;26998825}. Radiological imaging typically reveals a large cystic mass in the pancreas with associated liver lesions. Serum CEA and CA19-9 levels are usually normal or slightly elevated.

Epidemiology

Serous cystadenomas are uncommon and account for 1–2% of all primary pancreatic neoplasms and 6–16% of surgically resected cystic lesions {15185076;22415666;22770958;28970292}. The mean age at presentation is 58 years (range: 18–91 years), with a female predominance (M:F ratio: 1:3) {18815545;26045140;22415666;26559376}.

Etiology

There are no known etiological factors, although some cases are associated with deleterious germline variants in VHL. As many as 90% of patients with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome develop serous cystadenomas {11040195;11073821;9327728;22370733}.

Pathogenesis

Alterations in the regulation of the VHL/HIF pathway, similar to other glycogen-rich neoplasms, appear to be the major driver of this tumour {3354751;16517478;19077470}. VHL is considered the main tumour suppressor gene responsible for the formation of both familial and sporadic serous cystadenomas. Somatic mutations in VHL (located on chromosome 3p25.3) and loss of heterozygosity of 3p occur in 50% and 90% of cases, respectively {9327728;22158988;26253305}. Allelic loss of chromosome 10q has also been reported in 50% of serous cystadenomas {11141506}.

Macroscopic appearance

Microcystic serous cystadenomas account for 45% of serous neoplasms. They are usually well-circumscribed, slightly bosselated, rounded lesions, 10–250 mm in diameter {637043;17440789;15185076;26559376}. On cross-section, they are sponge-like and composed of numerous thin-walled tiny cysts (> 1–10 mm in diameter). The cysts at the periphery are often larger than those near the centre of the tumour. A central scar is often present and may be calcified. Macrocystic (oligocystic) subtypes tend to be poorly demarcated, and a central scar is usually absent {15185076;15104296;1644432;26559376}. Macrocystic serous cystadenomas are composed of 1 (unilocular) to < 10 (multilocular) large cysts 10–30 mm in diameter and can grossly mimic mucinous cystic neoplasms and intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms. Solid serous adenomas are well-circumscribed neoplasms with a solid gross appearance, like that of well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs), and are usually 20–40 mm in diameter {17393319;18440191;18648150}. Gross cyst formation is not seen. Diffuse serous cystadenomas replace the entire pancreas with innumerable cysts and are closely associated with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {19129617}.

Histopathology

Serous cystadenoma

Microcystic serous cystadenoma: The cysts are lined by a single layer of cuboidal to flat epithelial cells with clear cytoplasm, and well-defined cytoplasmic borders. The cells have a small round nucleus with dense homogeneous chromatin and an inconspicuous nucleolus. Owing to the presence of abundant intracytoplasmic glycogen, PAS staining is positive, but PASD staining is negative. Nuclear atypia and mitoses are almost always absent, but symplastic changes may occur {26559376}. Blunt papillary projections, tufting, and micropapillae may be found focally, but well-formed or complex papillae are unusual {26559376}. Microscopic intraparenchymal satellite nodules can be seen {26559376}. The epithelium is immunoreactive for inhibin, GLUT1, and MUC6. Underlying the epithelium is an interweaving network of capillaries. The central scar consists of hyalinized stroma with few clusters of tiny cysts.

Macrocystic (oligocystic) serous cystadenoma: Although the cysts themselves are larger, the cyst lining is microscopically identical to that of the microcystic subtype.

Solid serous adenoma: This tumour subtype is composed of small back-to-back acini with no or minute central lumina and cytological features that are otherwise typical of a serous cystadenoma.

VHL-associated serous cystic neoplasm: As many as 90% of patients with von Hippel–Lindau syndrome develop multiple microcystic and macrocystic serous cystadenomas {11040195;28697137;22370733;23543325}. Microscopically, these cysts are virtually indistinguishable from those occurring sporadically. The lesions vary from single, minute dilatations of the centroacinar lumen to involvement of the entire gland – known as diffuse serous cystadenoma {19129617}.

Mixed serous–neuroendocrine neoplasm: In rare cases, serous cystadenomas are associated with well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PanNETs). The neuroendocrine proliferation can be independent or intermingled with the cysts {16644876;14521452}. Mixed serous–neuroendocrine neoplasms are highly suggestive of von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {11040195;19238077;22370733;23543325}. However, in a few cases, no genetic syndrome is identified. It is unclear whether these rare cases represent true mixed neoplasms with both exocrine and endocrine differentiation, or whether they are coincidental collision tumours.

Serous cystadenocarcinoma

Malignant behaviour of serous neoplasms has been reported (serous cystadenocarcinoma), but it is extraordinarily rare, and the diagnosis of malignancy in pancreatic serous neoplasms is restricted to cases of unequivocal distant metastasis (almost always to the liver) beyond the pancreatic/peripancreatic bed.

Although atypical and potentially a sign of aggressive behaviour, vascular, perineural, and direct extension into adjacent organs and lymph nodes is not sufficient for the diagnosis of serous cystadenocarcinoma {26045140}. These neoplasms are grossly and microscopically similar to microcystic serous cystadenomas, but they tend to be larger, with a mean diameter of 100 mm. A subset of benign serous neoplasms shows cytological atypia or are locally aggressive, with lymphovascular and perineural invasion, as well as direct invasion into the spleen, stomach, and lymph nodes; these tumours may be at higher risk of metastasis and subsequently classified as serous cystadenocarcinoma {17440789}. Some serous neoplasms reported as being cytologically malignant have not behaved aggressively, and overt cytological features of malignancy have not been reported in truly malignant (metastatic) cases {18305183;22189969}.

Cytology

FNA specimens are usually paucicellular {18260088;27889754}. Smears predominantly contain proteinaceous debris and blood with few (if any) epithelial cells. When seen, the neoplastic epithelium forms small monolayered fragments of cuboidal cells with round, uniform nuclei and scant to moderate, often clear cytoplasm. If sufficient material is present, ancillary studies for glycogen, inhibin, and MUC6 are useful. Preoperative biochemical and molecular cyst fluid analyses are also helpful in establishing a diagnosis {26253016;26253305;28970292}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in postoperative specimens. In the preoperative setting, molecular testing of pancreatic cyst fluid may reveal VHL alterations and/or loss of chromosome 3p in the absence of other somatic mutations {26253016;28970292;22158988}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a neoplasm, often cystic, composed of cuboidal, bland glycogenated epithelium.

Staging

Serous cystadenocarcinomas are staged as carcinomas of the exocrine pancreas [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis for patients with a serous cystadenoma is excellent {22415666;26045140;26559376}. These tumours grow at a reported annual median growth rate of 4–6 mm, with larger tumours growing faster {16135927;26045140}. Considering the indolent behaviour and the potentially substantial postoperative morbidity and mortality, conservative management has been proposed when serous cystadenomas are small and asymptomatic and show typical radiological and biochemical and molecular cyst fluid characteristics {26231607;29574408}. Surgical resection is almost always curative. Postoperative follow-up is warranted in serous cystadenomas with locally aggressive features such as direct extension into adjacent structures. These can rarely recur or even metastasize (reclassified as serous cystadenocarcinoma) {17440789}.

For serous cystadenocarcinoma, the most common site of metastasis is the liver {26559376;9933679}. However, in these patients, a definite clonal relationship between the serous neoplasms in the pancreas and the liver has yet to be established {26559376}. Regardless, these neoplasms are slow-growing and characterized by an indolent behaviour. In fact, only one death has been attributed to extrapancreatic dissemination {19060596}, and long-term survival in the presence of liver lesions interpreted as metastasis is usual {26045140;26559376}. Surgical resection may be helpful for symptomatic disease.
