2.1.1.3 Oesophageal squamous dysplasia

Definition

Squamous dysplasia of the oesophagus is an unequivocal neoplastic alteration of the squamous epithelium without invasion.

ICD-O coding

80770/0 Oesophageal squamous dysplasia, low-grade

80770/2 Oesophageal squamous dysplasia, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.0 & XH3Y37 Oesophageal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (dysplasia), low-grade

2E60.1 & XH9ND8 Oesophageal squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (dysplasia), high-grade

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Squamous dysplasia can occur anywhere in the oesophagus, and it is likely to follow the distribution of squamous cell carcinoma.

Clinical features

Most patients are asymptomatic, so dysplasia may be detected incidentally or during surveillance of patients who have had squamous cell carcinoma or are at high risk. However, larger areas of dysplasia or erosion, as well as coexisting squamous cell carcinoma, can cause symptoms such as dysphagia and haemorrhage. Patients at high risk for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) are usually surveyed using a combination of Lugol’s chromoendoscopy and narrow-band imaging {25608626}. With Lugol’s iodine, low-grade dysplasia appears as an unstained or weakly stained area; high-grade dysplasia is consistently unstained {26170975}. On narrow-band imaging, dysplastic lesions appear as areas of brownish discolouration {24751580;28086818}. Abnormalities on narrow-band imaging reflect the changes of intrapapillary capillary loops {28386209}.

Epidemiology

In case series, the reported prevalence of oesophageal squamous dysplasia varies from 3% in some parts of the world to as high as 38% in countries with the highest prevalence of OSCC such as China and the Islamic Republic of Iran {23549398}. In series from China, most examples are high-grade {23549398;32618654}. The prevalence and sex distribution of squamous dysplasia mirror those of OSCC. Dysplasia is most common among male patients in the fifth and sixth decades of life {16118042;32302087;37480179}.

Etiology

The risk factors for oesophageal squamous dysplasia are similar to those for OSCC.

Pathogenesis

Mutations in TP53 (and p53 overexpression) and CDKN2A are the key drivers {23549398;32759723;39723635}.

Macroscopic appearance

Squamous dysplasia can appear as a small flat lesion but is often occult.

Histopathology

The diagnosis of dysplasia requires the presence of both cytological and architectural atypia. Cytological atypia is characterized by nuclear atypia, including enlargement, pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, loss of polarity, and overlapping. Architectural atypia is characterized by abnormal epithelial maturation. Sharp demarcation from normal to abnormal may be present. Aberrant p53 expression by immunohistochemistry can be demonstrated in dysplastic mucosa {10993651}. Grading of squamous dysplasia is controversial; there are two main systems in use. Pathologists in some countries use a system with three tiers (mild, moderate, and severe). In other countries, a two-tiered (low-grade vs high-grade) system is used, which is currently recommended by WHO. Low-grade dysplasia is defined as dysplasia predominantly involving the lower half of the epithelium, with mild cytological atypia. Nuclear polarity is maintained, with no atypical mitoses. High-grade dysplasia is diagnosed when more than half of the epithelium is involved or when severe cytological atypia is present (regardless of the extent of epithelial involvement). High-grade dysplasia includes the group of lesions also termed “carcinoma in situ” in Japan and other parts of Asia {17617215}.

Cytology

Currently, there is no established practice of using cytology alone for the diagnosis of squamous dysplasia.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Squamous dysplasia

Essential: cytological atypia (nuclear atypia: enlargement, pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, loss of nuclear polarity, and overlapping); architectural atypia (abnormal epithelial maturation).

Low-grade dysplasia

Essential: involvement of the lower half of the epithelium, with mild cytological atypia.

High-grade dysplasia

Essential: involvement of more than half of the epithelium, or severe cytological atypia (regardless of the thickness of epithelial involvement).

Staging

High-grade dysplasia is Tis.

Prognosis and prediction

According to a Dutch study, the annual risk of OSCC was 4.0% in patients with mild to moderate dysplasia and 8.5% in patients with higher-grade dysplasia {37480179}. In parts of the world with a high incidence of squamous cell carcinoma, such as China, detection of squamous dysplasia by screening and early treatment of the disease holds promise for the prevention of progression to OSCC {23549398}. Endoscopic resection for high-grade dysplasia may prevent progression to OSCC over time and detect occult OSCC {10699887}.
