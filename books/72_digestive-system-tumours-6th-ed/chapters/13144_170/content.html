13.1.4.4 Angiosarcoma

Definition

Angiosarcoma is a malignant neoplasm showing endothelial differentiation, frequently with high-grade nuclear atypia, and variable degrees of blood vessel formation.

ICD-O coding

91200/3 Angiosarcoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B56.3 Angiosarcoma of liver

2B56.Y Angiosarcoma, other specified primary site

Related terminology

Acceptable: haemangiosarcoma.

Not acceptable: malignant angioendothelioma; malignant haemangioendothelioma; lymphangiosarcoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Primary angiosarcomas can arise at any site within the GI tract, predominantly in the liver. Angiosarcoma can also be metastatic (e.g. from skin, breast, spleen) or multifocal {15104292}.

Clinical features

The presenting symptoms of gastrointestinal or hepatic angiosarcomas are nonspecific and may include abdominal pain, mass, or ascites {36688020;30456677}. Acute abdomen can rarely occur in hepatic angiosarcomas due to rupture, and in gastrointestinal tumours due to bleeding, bowel obstruction or perforation, intra-abdominal haemorrhage, and intussusception. Metastases can be multifocal within the bowel wall (diffuse angiosarcomatosis). Hepatic angiosarcomas may rarely cause fulminant hepatic failure.

On imaging, angiosarcomas can form mass lesions with various enhancement patterns or ill-defined nodules. They typically lack characteristic imaging features; biopsies are used to make the diagnosis.

Epidemiology

Angiosarcoma has a peak incidence in the seventh decade of life but can rarely affect children {18987547}. There is a male predominance. In the liver, angiosarcoma is the most common primary malignant mesenchymal neoplasm in adults {26008857}.

Overall, primary angiosarcomas are four times more common in the liver than in the intestinal tract {35234280}. In the GI tract, over half (~55%) occur in the small bowel, about 35% in the colon, with the remainder arising in the stomach, pancreas, and gallbladder {15104292;35234280}.

Etiology

Primary GI tract angiosarcomas arise de novo or in the field of prior therapeutic abdominopelvic irradiation {36688020}, typically after long latency (usually > 10 years). Patients with hepatic angiosarcomas may have had exposure to carcinogens such as vinyl chloride monomers, arsenic, aristolochic acid, and anabolic-androgenic steroids {91848;7586214;7199426}. End-stage renal disease has been reported as a risk factor for hepatic angiosarcomas {36815532}. Historically, iatrogenic thorium oxide (Thorotrast) was an etiological factor {574080}. Most angiosarcomas have no identifiable risk factors.

Pathogenesis

The genetic changes in angiosarcomas vary by site of origin and by risk factors, in particular whether they are primary or secondary malignancies {34638300;36497420}. Most primary angiosarcomas (~90%) have low tumour mutation burdens {34638300}. Data are limited by the small numbers of cases, especially for intestinal tract angiosarcomas, but visceral angiosarcomas in general have frequent mutations in TP53, ATRX, ARID1A, and MED12. Hepatic angiosarcomas have frequent mutations in TP53 and ATRX, along with KDR amplifications/gains and deletions in CDKN2A/B {36815532}. A ROS1::GOPC/FIG fusion has been reported {30719217}. ATRX mutations can be associated with the alternative-lengthening-of-telomeres phenotype {26190196}. Data are very limited, but MYC amplifications appear uncommon in conventional hepatic or intestinal tract primary angiosarcomas {37357916;34638300}.

Macroscopic appearance

Angiosarcomas may be haemorrhagic, are often ulcerating, and can have a diffusely infiltrating pattern or form single or multinodular masses. In the GI tract, they can extend transmurally and include the mesentery. Hepatic angiosarcomas are most often mass forming, but the sinusoidal subtype shows sinusoidal spread without forming well-defined masses. Most angiosarcomas are not resected, but for those that are, the macroscopic appearances are variable, ranging from firm greyish-white tissue to sponge-like tumours, and to haemorrhagic tumours with cystic spaces.

Histopathology

Most angiosarcomas are high-grade neoplasms with nuclear atypia, prominent mitotic activity, and coagulative necrosis. The tumours can be epithelioid, spindled, or vasoformative, often with multiple patterns in the same tumour. Vascular channels are often poorly formed, with a complex dissecting pattern into fibrous tissue, or they may be compressed, with predominantly solid morphology and only subtle cleft-like spaces to suggest vascular differentiation. The endothelial cells lining the vascular structures may form buds, hobnails, or papillary-like projections. Less frequently, angiosarcomas appear to be low-grade with well-formed vascular channels lined by minimally atypical, spindled cells.

Hepatic angiosarcomas often show spread of tumour cells away from the main tumour mass along the hepatic sinusoids. In addition, primary hepatic angiosarcomas can have a peliotic growth pattern {30986799} or show extensive spread along the sinusoids without forming a distinct mass lesion {37357916}. The sinusoidal growth pattern can be very easily mistaken for an unusual hepatitis or vascular outflow disease, and several biopsies are often obtained before the correct diagnosis is made. There are no immunohistochemical stains that can distinguish hepatic angiosarcomas from angiosarcomas metastatic to the liver, so the distinction relies on imaging and clinical findings.

Epithelioid morphology is common in GI tract and hepatic angiosarcomas {15104292;30986799}. Epithelioid angiosarcomas show solid architecture and large atypical epithelioid or polygonal cells with ovoid vesicular nuclei, prominent large central nucleoli, and abundant cytoplasm, resembling carcinoma, melanoma, or lymphoma; vasoformative areas may be absent or subtle. Epithelioid angiosarcomas are easily misdiagnosed and have a poor prognosis {37054784}.

Angiosarcomas typically show nuclear ERG positivity and variable expression of CD31, CD34, as well as von Willebrand factor (factor VIII–related antigen) {7512718;21317715;11474291}. FLI1 is also typically positive but lacks specificity, limiting its use. In low-grade lesions, increased Ki-67 staining, p53 overexpression, or MYC nuclear staining can support a diagnosis of malignancy {37357916}. Distinction from epithelioid haemangioendothelioma is important and is based on morphology and immunohistochemistry for CAMTA1 and/or molecular studies. Immunohistochemistry should be interpreted in the context of morphology and an immunohistological panel, particularly for tumours with an epithelioid pattern, as there can be aberrant expression of other markers, including CD117 {10824925}, EMA, and keratins {17284115;32947230}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not required, but assessment for WWTR1::CAMTA1 may be useful in rare cases if an epithelioid haemangioendothelioma cannot be excluded on the basis of morphology and immunostains {40040722}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: malignant endothelial histology; nuclear pleomorphism.

Desirable: positive vascular markers by immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Staging of angiosarcoma is currently not performed, because of its generally aggressive natural history. Grading systems that predict clinical outcomes have been proposed for angiosarcomas in other organ systems {36376999}, but they have not yet been adapted for GI tract and liver angiosarcomas.

Prognosis and prediction

Most angiosarcomas of the GI tract or liver are highly aggressive, and overall survival time is typically < 6 months; treatment with radiation, chemotherapy, or resection, however, may extend survival {35234280;36688020}. Best survival outcomes are for R0 resections; some patients survive several years {30456677}. Additional poor prognostic indicators include older age, male sex, large tumour size, and epithelioid tumour morphology {30456677;36688020;37054784;35234280}.
