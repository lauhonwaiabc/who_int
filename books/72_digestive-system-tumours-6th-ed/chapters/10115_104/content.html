10.1.1.5 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm of the bile ducts

Definition

Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm (ITPN) of the bile duct is a non-invasive, intraluminally growing epithelial neoplasm without significant mucin production and primarily composed of high-grade dysplastic epithelial cells that form tubules and, to a lesser extent, papillary structures.

ICD-O coding

85037/2 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm

ICD-11 coding

2E61.3 & XA6R80 & XH3PE9 Intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm of bile duct, NOS

Related terminology

Not recommended: intraductal tubular carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Approximately 70% of all ITPNs are intrahepatic, followed by perihilar (20%) and extrahepatic (10%) locations {26111977}.

Clinical features

ITPN may be detected incidentally, while other patients present with nonspecific symptoms including loss of appetite or abdominal pain. Weight loss and jaundice may occur {26111977}. Serum markers such as CA19-9 and CEA may be elevated. Imaging may reveal a filling defect in the affected bile duct segment, bile duct dilatation, a solid mass, and, in the intrahepatic location, a cyst {23073323;21813159}.

Epidemiology

Biliary ITPNs are rare tumours of unknown incidence that account for approximately 15% of intraductal neoplasms of the bile duct {36821179}. They occur more frequently in women aged around 60 years (M:F ratio: ~1:2), with patients’ age ranging from 38 to 78 years (median: 63 years) {23073323;26111977}.

Etiology

No specific etiology has been definitively linked to ITPN, because of its rarity. In a few cases, underlying liver/biliary diseases, such as calculi, liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and chronic hepatitis B have been identified {20712650;33468537}. However, the limited number of cases prevents any conclusive statement regarding a specific etiology of biliary ITPN.

Pathogenesis

Although data on pathogenesis are limited, biliary ITPNs appear to be characterized by distinct genetic and epigenetic features. Notably, with the exception of CDKN2A (P16), biliary ITPN lacks hotspot mutations in common driver genes of pancreatobiliary carcinogenesis, such as KRAS, GNAS, and IDH1/IDH2 mutations {26111977;32181510;33468537;34205964;36821179}. Instead, a number of low-prevalence genetic alterations have been described, including alterations in genes coding for members of pathways related to the cell cycle, chromatin remodelling (the SWI/SNF complex, BAP1, KMT2C, etc.), and receptor tyrosine kinases (e.g. FGFR) {26111977;33468537;34205964;36821179}. These findings highlight a KRAS-independent genetic profile. However, the common mutations in chromatin-remodelling genes found in both biliary ITPN and cholangiocarcinoma suggest a close genetic relationship between these two tumour types.

Furthermore, epigenetic studies of DNA methylation patterns reveal subtle differences between intraductal papillary neoplasms of the bile duct and biliary ITPN, indicating that these two neoplasms may have distinct cellular origins {33468537}. Indeed, an origin of ITPN from peribiliary glands/cysts (MUC6-positive) and of intraductal papillary neoplasms of the bile duct from the lining epithelium of the bile ducts (MUC5AC-positive) has been suggested {21813159}.

Macroscopic appearance

ITPNs present as solid, whitish to tan nodular masses that partially or completely fill dilated bile ducts. Punctate or geographical necrosis may be observed {23073323;21813159}. Cyst formation is rare, and overt mucin secretion is absent. The average diameter of biliary ITPN is 50 mm (range: 6–150 mm). Invasive carcinoma derived from ITPN appears as a whitish, solid nodule with an irregular contour.

Histopathology

Microscopically, biliary ITPNs resemble their pancreatic counterparts. They typically have a homogeneous, multinodular appearance and consist of back-to-back tubular glands that fuse to form cribriform structures {23073323;21813159;36821179}. The nodules have a smooth contour and generally contain minimal stroma. Papillary and micropapillary areas may be present, but they are usually not prominent and are poorly formed. Some cases demonstrate a more solid architecture with spindle-shaped nuclei. The intraductal location is sometimes evidenced by the presence of the original bile duct epithelium at the periphery of the nodules. The tumour cells are cuboidal to columnar with an amphiphilic cytoplasm and enlarged nuclei, sometimes with prominent nucleoli, fulfilling the criteria for high-grade dysplasia. Nuclear grooves may be present. Mitotic figures are often readily identifiable. Focal clear and oncocytic cell changes may also be observed. In some cases, intraluminal eosinophilic secretions resembling colloid are present. Intracellular mucin is typically not detectable. Necrosis is common (present in 85% of cases), usually as spotty or comedo-like necrosis; extensive necrosis is rare (< 5%) {23073323;21813159;35934108;36821179}.

The majority of cases (80%) show progression into invasive carcinoma {26111977}. In most cases (~60%), the invasive carcinoma is a conventional tubular carcinoma of large duct type {33468537}. In 25% of the invasive carcinomas, an intraductal-like pattern with extensive comedonecrosis is observed. Additionally, a tubulocystic pattern, characterized by dilated tubular structures with bland cytology, has been described {26111977}.

The neoplastic cells consistently immunolabel for pancytokeratins and commonly express CK7 and CK19. MUC1 is expressed in 80% of the cases, while MUC6 is expressed in 30–100% {23073323;26111977;36821179}; MUC2 and MUC5AC are consistently negative in biliary ITPN {23073323;26111977;36821179}.

Gastric- and pancreatobiliary-type IPNB can be challenging differential diagnoses. The presence of mucinous features, along with MUC5AC expression and a spectrum of dysplasia, favours a diagnosis of IPNB {35934108}.

Cytology

Although no systematic analyses have been conducted, case reports describe highly cellular, relatively cohesive clusters with cribriform and tubular patterns and tumour cells without intracytoplasmic mucin in cytological smears of ITPN {18831024;26390029;27459312} [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: intraductal nodules composed of non-invasive back-to-back tubular glands, forming large cribriform structures; absence of detectable intracytoplasmic mucin.

Desirable: lack of MUC5AC expression.

Staging

ITPNs are staged as pTis. Staging of invasive carcinoma developed from ITPNs is based on the size of the invasive component and follows the staging protocol for bile duct cancer of the respective localization [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with an ITPN have excellent survival rates (100% at 1 year, 90% at 3 years, and 90% at 5 years) after resection {26111977}. Patients with an invasive carcinoma that developed from an ITPN show a 5-year survival rate of 75%, which is significantly better than the survival rate of patients with a cholangiocarcinoma arising in the absence of an ITPN {33468537}.
