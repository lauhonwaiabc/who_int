9.1.1.1 Focal nodular hyperplasia of the liver

Definition

Focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH) of the liver is a mass-forming, non-neoplastic hepatocellular lesion caused by localized vascular abnormalities.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

DB99.Y & XH0M86 Focal nodular hyperplasia of the liver

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

FNH can occur anywhere in the liver.

Clinical features

FNH is solitary in two thirds of cases {2813344}, but multiple FNH occurs in the setting of vascular disease and chemotherapy {2813344;37806391;39186145}. Most cases are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. Large lesions can rarely present with abdominal pain or compression of adjacent organs, but there is no established risk for haemorrhage or malignant transformation. FNH regression has been shown on serial imaging studies {28058027;18855931}.

The background liver is usually normal, but FNH can occur in association with vascular diseases (e.g. portal venopathy, Budd–Chiari syndrome, hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia), vascular neoplasms (haemangioma), bordering mass lesions, and in cirrhosis {9462648;12122868;12493020;29804132}. It can also occasionally coexist with hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) {12812524;26274031}. FNH may occur several years after chemotherapy or stem cell transplantation in both children and adults {29323545;39186145}. These are observed most after treatment of breast and colorectal cancers, and after use of chemotherapeutic agents such as cyclophosphamide and oxaliplatin.

In 90% of cases, an accurate diagnosis is possible by imaging, using contrast-enhanced ultrasonography or contrast-enhanced MRI {17090710;18094294;22282184}. The typical imaging findings are described in #43571Table 9.01. Biopsy is indicated in cases with an atypical course or unusual imaging features.

Epidemiology

The incidence of benign liver tumours has increased because of frequent use of medical imaging, and they are more commonly diagnosed in high-income countries due to widespread use of these techniques. FNH is the second most frequent benign liver nodule (after haemangioma) {2813344;33005655}, found in 0.8% of adult autopsies {2813344}. FNH has an incidence of 0.3–3% {33005655} and occurs predominantly in middle-aged women (M:F ratio: 1:9) {33005655}.

Etiology

An association of estrogens with growth has been suggested given the female preponderance and reports of growth in size during pregnancy {11854634;17261770}. However, these relationships have not been confirmed in other studies, and there is no conclusive evidence of a relation with oral contraceptive use or pregnancy {10702207;17261770}.

Pathogenesis

An association with local or systemic vascular anomalies and the presence of unusually large vessels suggest that FNH is a focal, hyperplastic response to increased blood flow {4065824}. A current hypothesis is that the primary insult is outflow obstruction and the resulting congestive injury leads to parenchymal collapse and fibrosis, arteriovenous shunting, and loss of portal veins and ducts. Sinusoidal injury and glutathione depletion by alkylating agents has been postulated in chemotherapy-related cases {39186145}.

Clonal analysis using the HUMARA test has demonstrated the polyclonal nature of FNH {9328310;15887105}. Studies have shown that the mRNA expression of angiopoietin genes (ANGPT1 and ANGPT2) involved in vessel maturation is altered, with an increased ANGPT1:ANGPT2 ratio compared with normal liver, cirrhosis, and other liver tumours {12612904;17997499}. The WNT signalling pathway is focally activated without mutations in CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin) or AXIN1 {18466996}. This activation explains the patchy increase in glutamine synthetase (GS) staining in hepatocytes.

Macroscopic appearance

Classic FNH is an ochre, firm, well-delineated, lobulated, and unencapsulated mass, with a size varying from a few millimetres to > 100 mm. The lesion is composed of 2–3 mm nodules, giving the lesion a multinodular appearance {25232451}, with a central stellate fibrous scar and radiating extensions that partially surround the nodules. A central scar is absent in 20–30% of cases, most often in small lesions, but fibrous septa and aberrant arterioles are present.

Histopathology

The nodules are composed of benign hepatocytes typically arranged in plates 1–2 cells thick. Wider plates can be focally present at the periphery, especially in larger lesions. The central scar contains one or more large dystrophic vessels, accompanied by numerous small arterioles. The central fibrous region has radiating branches containing arteries unaccompanied by portal veins or bile ducts, often with a lymphocytic or mixed inflammatory infiltrate. At the interface of fibrous regions and nodules, a ductular reaction is often present and highlighted by CK7 and CK19 immunostaining. Features of cholestasis, such as feathery degeneration of hepatocytes, copper deposition, and (less commonly) Mallory–Denk bodies can be present. The periseptal hepatocytes can be smaller in size and have a basophilic cytoplasm (intermediate hepatocytes). In some cases, one or more of the typical features (e.g. nodularity, ductular reaction) may not be present {10584697}. Steatosis, steatohepatitis-like features, and large-cell change can occur {10584697;23807780;28079599}. Larger size is more common, while pseudoacinar architecture, steatosis, and dystrophic vessels have been observed less often in the paediatric setting {37082924}. Patchy to diffusely increased sinusoidal staining is seen with CD34.

Immunohistochemistry for GS is useful for diagnosis, even on biopsies, showing broad, anastomosing areas of expression in hepatocytes adjacent to hepatic veins, leading to a characteristic map-like (geographical) pattern {18803590;23060349}. The anastomosing map-like pattern is not always discernible in needle biopsies, but instead, strong staining of broad groups of hepatocytes can be observed. In these cases, the overall morphology and lack of diffuse staining with other markers like serum amyloid A protein and C-reactive protein usually allow a definite diagnosis of FNH. The map-like pattern may not be present around the central scar and in areas with lesional fat.

Histological features such as fat, isolated arteries, and sinusoidal dilatation are more common in inflammatory hepatocellular adenoma, whereas nodularity, fibrous septa, and ductular reaction are more typical of FNH. However, there can be overlap of histological features between these entities, especially on needle biopsy. GS staining plays a key role in this distinction. An absence of map-like GS staining and positivity for serum amyloid A and/or C-reactive protein are typical of inflammatory hepatocellular adenoma. However, patchy serum amyloid A or periseptal C-reactive protein staining can be seen in FNH {23807780;37698958}. The map-like GS pattern can mimic the peripheral accentuation observed in HCA with certain CTNNB1 mutations (exon 3 Ser45, exon 7/8), especially in limited biopsies {33560657}.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is distinguished from FNH by cytoarchitectural atypia and disruption of the reticulin framework. The periphery of FNH may show wider cell plates and focal loss of reticulin, which can mimic well-differentiated HCC, especially on needle biopsies. Diffuse GS staining and positive glypican-3 (GPC3) favour HCC. Map-like GS staining can be erroneously interpreted as diffuse staining seen in β-catenin–mutated adenoma or HCC, especially in small biopsies. The patchy absence of GS staining in periseptal hepatocytes is typical of the map-like pattern and helps distinguish it from the diffuse GS staining pattern. It may not be possible to distinguish diffuse and map-like GS staining on small, limited biopsies.

In addition to classic FNH, some nodules that occur in vascular diseases have morphological features resembling FNH but show β-catenin activation with diffuse GS staining with or without nuclear β-catenin {29804132}. These nodules demonstrate CTNNB1 mutation on sequencing {37806391} and should be labelled as “β-catenin-activated hepatocellular adenoma” or “atypical hepatocellular neoplasm”, but not “FNH-like” as this designation can lead to an erroneous interpretation as a non-neoplastic nodule. The term “FNH-like” has been used in the literature to describe nodules that show classic features of FNH but occur in the setting of liver disease such as cirrhosis or vascular abnormalities (e.g. Budd–Chiari syndrome, Abernethy malformation) {12493020;17032200}. The term “FNH” is now recommended for nodules with morphological features typical or suggestive of FNH and map-like GS pattern irrespective of whether they occur in normal or diseased liver. The term “FNH-like” should be reserved for nodules that are morphologically suggestive of FNH but lack the typical map-like GS pattern.

On biopsy, cirrhosis can be a differential diagnosis of FNH when the presence of a mass-forming lesion is unknown.

Cytology

Cytology is not suitable for the diagnosis of FNH.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In challenging cases, sequencing analysis can help distinguish FNH from both hepatocellular adenoma and HCC. FNH is a benign non-neoplastic process, while hepatocellular adenomas are often characterized by pathogenic mutations (depending on subtype), and HCC may show mutations (such as TERT promoter, CTNNB1) and/or cytogenetic changes (gain of 1q, 7q, 8q; loss of 6p, 17q) {37536890}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: an unencapsulated nodule; nodular architecture; a central fibrous scar; dystrophic vessels; no features of malignancy.

Desirable: a map-like (geographical) pattern of staining with GS.

Staging

Not applicable

Prognosis and prediction

There is no risk of malignant transformation. Surgical resection is not indicated unless the lesion is large and symptomatic, or in rare instances when the diagnosis is not definitive by imaging or biopsy.
