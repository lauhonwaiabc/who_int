7.1.2.1 Colorectal carcinoma

Definition

Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) is a malignant epithelial tumour originating in the large bowel.

ICD-O coding

81400/3 Adenocarcinoma, NOS

ICD-11 coding

2B93.0 & XA4ZR7 Colorectal adenocarcinoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mucinous adenocarcinoma (84800/3); serrated adenocarcinoma (82130/3); micropapillary adenocarcinoma (82651/3); medullary carcinoma (85100/3); adenoma-like adenocarcinoma (82620/3); signet-ring cell carcinoma (84900/3); adenosquamous carcinoma (85600/3); squamous cell carcinoma (80700/3); low-grade tubuloglandular carcinoma (82114/3); lymphoglandular complex–like adenocarcinoma (80823/3); undifferentiated carcinoma (80200/3); carcinoma with mesenchymal differentiation (80330/3)

Localization

For practical purposes, CRCs are divided into three groups by location: right-sided or proximal colon carcinomas (arising in the caecum, ascending colon, or transverse colon), left-sided colon carcinomas (arising from the splenic flexure to the sigmoid colon), and rectal carcinomas.

Proximal colonic cancers are relatively more common in female patients, patients with black skin, and patients aged > 60 years. Most CRCs are left-sided or rectal, although this is evolving, as the decline in incidence of CRC over recent decades has predominantly been in left-sided colonic or rectal cancers {34276196}. This is probably due to increased application of endoscopy and greater effectiveness of screening, by faecal occult blood testing, faecal immunochemical testing, or flexible sigmoidoscopy, for the detection of distal cancers {21745014;33495268}. The increasing incidence of CRC among younger individuals (mainly younger than 50 years) is predominantly in distal cancers {36856579}.

The molecular background of CRC also shows location-specific features (see Pathogenesis, below). Treatment differs depending on the location, with a prominent role for neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy for rectal carcinomas. Surgical complications are more often observed in the treatment of proximal colonic cancers {28204867;37310528}. Tumour location is also related to survival, with worse outcomes associated with proximal colonic cancers {27787550;38651190}, and it also has an association with the success of systemic treatment {27907852;34061178;36648535}.

Clinical features

Common symptoms include persistent changes in bowel habits, abdominal pain, haematochezia, and those related to anaemia. Abdominal pain and haematochezia are relatively more common in younger patients {34405229}. With the widespread introduction of population screening {29580179}, the clinical spectrum of CRC is changing, with the diagnosis of increasing numbers of individuals without symptoms. Depending on the accepted screening strategy, these individuals’ tumours are diagnosed after detection by faecal occult blood test or faecal immunochemical test or incidentally during screening endoscopy {29580179}.

Endoscopic evaluation allows visualization of CRC and precursor lesions on the mucosal surface of the large bowel. Therapeutic removal of early lesions can also be performed, by snare polypectomy, endoscopic mucosal resection, endoscopic submucosal dissection, intermuscular dissection, or full-thickness resection {38670139}. Advanced endoscopy techniques, such as narrow-band imaging and magnifying chromoendoscopy {27644737}, supplemented by novel artificial intelligence developments {39398448}, improve endoscopic detection and evaluation of early CRC.

Imaging can be applied for non-invasive detection of CRC with the use of CT colonography, but definite diagnosis requires biopsy or resection. The main use of clinical imaging is for staging of CRC. Various techniques are applied. To determine invasion depth in early rectal neoplasia, endorectal ultrasonography can be utilized {31004599}. For more locally advanced lesions, assessment by MRI is more important, in particular to determine the relationship of the tumour with the mesorectal fascia {27384098}. The imaging evaluation of nodal status remains difficult {35566723}. To identify distant metastatic disease before extensive surgery is performed, CT or PET/CT can be used. The imaging evaluation of tumour response (restaging) after neoadjuvant treatment is important when considering next steps in management, especially in those patients following a watch-and-wait protocol rather than proceeding to resection {34109555;38185947}.

Epidemiology

CRC, with an estimated 1 926 425 new cases worldwide in 2022, is the third most common cancer in men and women, and the second highest in mortality with almost 0.9 million deaths {38572751}. The incidence varies widely globally, with the highest rates seen in the highest-income countries. In many low- and middle-income countries, the incidence of CRC is rapidly increasing, whereas stabilizing or decreasing rates have been observed in high-income, high-incidence countries {38572751}. This diversity is mainly due to differences in risk factors among populations, including lifestyle and environmental factors. Trends of increasing incidence in young adults have been reported in many countries in the past few decades {36856579;31097539;25251195}, with an associated increase in mortality in individuals of < 50 years {39576674}, impacting optimal approach to screening strategies. Although the lifetime risk of CRC described is similar in men (4.4%) and women (4.1%), because women have a longer life expectancy, the incidence rate is 31% higher in men. Men in their fifties and sixties are more frequently diagnosed with CRC {36856579}.

Etiology

A high incidence of CRC is consistently observed in populations with diets typical of high-income countries combined with a sedentary lifestyle. There are many proven or probable modifiable risk and risk-reducing factors. Established risk factors include consumption of processed and red meat {22864938;26514947}, consumption of alcohol {28407090;34543445}, smoking {19245853}, and excess body fat {27557308}. X- and γ-radiation also have sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity {23189752}. Consumption of dietary fibre and dairy products {28407090} and increased levels of physical activity {27510511} decrease the risk, as does prolonged use of NSAIDs and, in women, hormone replacement therapy {23563998;21671473}. The molecular pathways underlying these epidemiological associations are poorly understood, probably because of complex interactions. The effects of dietary interventions in CRC prevention remain unproven, and the only effective form of chemoprevention identified is regular use of NSAIDs, with patients with Lynch syndrome recommended to take daily aspirin {27919915;32534647}.

Genetic predisposition is an important risk factor for CRC, depending on the type of mutation. The lifetime risk can increase to 100% in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis. #44141Table 7.03 provides an overview of hereditary cancer syndromes that involve CRC. Tumour genetic syndromes can interact with environmental/lifestyle risk factors {37395714}. Genetic tumour syndromes are discussed in more detail in section Genetic tumour syndromes of the digestive system. Notably, most cases of serrated polyposis syndrome are sporadic, and this is no longer considered a hereditary syndrome given the lack of an established causative gene and increasing evidence of an environmental etiology (see section Serrated polyposis). Risk of CRC increases in the setting of a family history of CRC also, even with no identifiable hereditary cancer syndrome {22634755;27258162}.

Chronic bowel inflammation is an established risk factor for CRC. Inflammatory bowel diseases (Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis) are discussed in section Inflammatory bowel disease–associated dysplasia of the colorectum. Other rare but well-established risk factors include irradiation {29957373}, cystic fibrosis {29289528}, ureterosigmoidostomy {10613486}, and acromegaly {18567075}.

Pathogenesis

Most CRCs develop via the conventional pathway through the classic adenoma–carcinoma sequence of pathogenesis, and the remainder evolve via either the hypermutant pathway or the ultramutant pathway. This involves transition from normal mucosal epithelial stem cells through adenomas or serrated polyps to carcinomas, with stepwise accumulation of genetic and epigenetic abnormalities in highly variable patterns in the evolving tumour cells. Cancers show underlying molecular changes associated with three main mechanisms of genetic instability: (1) chromosomal instability resulting in high levels of DNA somatic copy-number alterations (SCNAs), with DNA gains/amplifications and losses/deletions affecting a relatively small group of genes {19911421;23417071}; (2) microsatellite instability (MSI) with a high mutation rate (hypermutant) due to defective DNA mismatch repair (MMR) affecting a large number of genes; or (3) defective proofreading function of polymerases with a very high mutation rate (ultramutant) affecting a very large number of genes (mostly passenger mutations with few driver mutations) {22810696;27325016}.

The most frequent and characteristic genetic changes include alterations to APC, KRAS, TP53, SMAD4, PIK3CA, and many other genes (84%, chromosomal instability pathway); the MMR genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 (13%, MSI hypermutant pathway); and the proofreading exonuclease domain of POLE or rarely POLD1 (3%, ultramutant pathway) {27325016}. These alterations are described in detail in the sections on section benign epithelial tumours and precursors.

Serrated pathway adenocarcinomas, some but not all of which are associated with defective MMR, account for about 10% of all CRCs and mostly arise from serrated polyp precursors, rather than conventional adenomas. Serrated polyps often have BRAF and (less often) KRAS activating mutations, and the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP), in which a range of susceptible genes develop epigenetic promoter hypermethylation that silences their expression. If MLH1promoter is methylated at both alleles, it leads to defective MMR and MSI {32340435;37880007}.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of CRC can be exophytic or endophytic. Most commonly, CRC forms an annular stricture, a polypoid mass, or a plaque-like ulcer with a circumferential rolled edge.

Histopathology

Most (> 95%) CRCs are adenocarcinomas. They present with unequivocal infiltrative growth, the vast majority through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa and deeper layers. For the rare diagnosis of intramucosal adenocarcinoma, unequivocal infiltration is restricted to the lamina propria. This might be most easily recognized in cases with overtly poor differentiation, signet-ring cells, or tumour budding {39648952;28711649}. Such intramucosal carcinomas have a very low incidence (< 0.1% of all CRCs), but there is a strong correlation with hereditary cancer syndromes {39648952}.

Most cases of CRC are diagnosed as adenocarcinoma NOS and often show features such as cribriform architecture, luminal dirty necrosis, and stromal desmoplasia. In addition, several histopathological subtypes can be distinguished, with specific clinicopathological and molecular characteristics (see #48835Table 7.04). The prognostic significance of histological subtypes of CRC and of predictive histological parameters commonly used in current practice are summarized in #44269Table 7.05.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma

Mucinous adenocarcinoma is traditionally defined as a tumour with > 50% of the lesions composed of pools of extracellular mucin that contain malignant epithelium forming clumps, layers, or individual tumour cells, potentially including some signet-ring cells. This is the most common CRC subtype, with a prevalence ranging from 5% to 20% {24728741}. There is no definitive prognostic difference compared with adenocarcinoma NOS {22259177}, although there is a relatively poor response to systemic treatment in the metastatic setting {26323388}. The proportion of cases with MSI is increased compared with adenocarcinoma NOS. The presence of MSI does not have independent prognostic value in mucinous adenocarcinoma {15017435;26769140}. Carcinomas with < 50% mucinous areas should be reported as having a mucinous component. Cases demonstrating abundant acellular mucin following neoadjuvant therapy should not be deemed mucinous adenocarcinoma.

Serrated adenocarcinoma

Studies have indicated that approximately 10% of all CRC can be classified as serrated adenocarcinoma {20594582;34105518;11241406}, although no minimum percentage cut-off point for diagnosis has been validated and interobserver variability likely exists. Accordingly, this subtype is defined by morphological similarities with serrated polyps, with glandular serration that can be accompanied by mucinous areas (often containing cell balls or papillary rods) and minimal necrosis {17204027;20594582}. The tumour cells often have abundant eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm with a low N:C ratio and vesicular nuclei. The prognostic relevance of this subtype is unclear {20594582;35789111;34105518}. Not all adenocarcinomas arising from serrated polyps show serrated morphology. Several studies have suggested an association with mutated BRAF and MSI/CIMP {32340435;37880007}, but other studies have found the prevalence of MSI and BRAF mutation appears similar to that in conventional CRC {35789111}.

Micropapillary adenocarcinoma

Micropapillary adenocarcinoma is characterized by small clusters of inverted eosinophilic tumour cells within retracted stromal spaces mimicking vascular channels; studies have indicated that ≥ 5% of the tumour should demonstrate this appearance for the diagnosis {21336262;23060121}. Its incidence ranges from 4% to 20% in single-centre series {17464318;21336262;23060121;27560620}. Tumour cells express stem cell markers and evidence of epithelial–mesenchymal transition. This subtype has a high risk of lymph node metastasis, even for cases not invasive beyond muscularis propria. Poor prognostic factors such as lymphatic invasion, extramural vascular invasion (EMVI), and perineural invasion are frequently present {21336262}. Despite this, many studies have not reported a worse stage-matched prognosis for micropapillary adenocarcinoma {22390187;27560620;37258254}, though some have {23060121;32481300;34105518}.

Medullary carcinoma

In medullary carcinoma, > 50% of the tumour is characterized by solid sheets of malignant cells with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, and a high mitotic rate, exhibiting regions of necrosis and prominent infiltration by lymphocytes and neutrophils. A glandular component may also be present. Single-centre studies estimate its prevalence at 4% {25572685;10561306}. In cancer registries, this subtype is under reported {20811712}, and diagnosis may be hampered by interobserver variability {28034727;33247957}. Medullary carcinoma is a morphological diagnosis; it very frequently demonstrates MSI, often in combination with BRAF mutations {25572685}, although this is not an absolute diagnostic criterion and therefore ancillary studies are not needed to suggest or confirm the diagnosis. Still, a diagnosis of medullary carcinoma should be issued with caution in a known microsatellite-stable CRC. Despite its solid growth pattern, medullary carcinoma is generally associated with a good prognosis {10561306}, likely due to its association with MSI. Of note, this type of CRC shows an aberrant immunohistochemical pattern, usually with loss of CDX2 and CK20 {11733373} and expression of inhibin. Neuroendocrine markers are negative.

Adenoma-like adenocarcinoma

Adenoma-like adenocarcinoma is defined as an invasive adenocarcinoma in which > 50% of the invasive areas demonstrate pushing invasion with villous adenoma-like architecture and low-grade cytological features {25913616}. Minimal desmoplastic reaction is observed, although a mucinous component may underlie the adenoma-like growth. Its incidence ranges from 3% to 9% {25913616;15489649}. This tumour is characterized by difficulties in establishing a diagnosis of invasion on biopsies, a high KRAS mutation rate, and a favourable prognosis {25913616}, particularly for purely adenoma-like examples {32991929}.

Signet-ring cell carcinoma

In signet-ring cell carcinomas, > 50% of the tumour cells have prominent intracytoplasmic mucin, causing displacement and moulding of the nucleus. These cells have minimal cytoplasm and form sheets of singly infiltrating or loosely adherent cells, with little to no formation of architectural configurations. They may float within mucin pools {38142679} or extensively infiltrate tissue; those with extensive diffuse infiltration are usually mucin-poor and may indicate a worse prognosis {23681075}. This subtype has a low prevalence of approximately 1% {22476818;34105518} and a predilection for the right colon. Tumours often present at advanced stages, and stage-corrected outcome is worse compared with that of adenocarcinoma NOS and mucinous adenocarcinoma {24841868}. Metastases develop rapidly and in multiple locations that are not typical for CRC {24504447}.

Carcinomas with both > 50% extracellular mucin and > 50% signet-ring cytology should be termed “mucin-rich signet-ring cell carcinomas”. These have a high incidence of MSI and a strong association with Lynch syndrome {22522845;23681075}. There is possibly also an enrichment of the MSI/PDL1 phenotype amenable to targeted therapy {28595259}. Carcinomas with signet-ring cells in < 50% of the tumour are categorized as having a signet-ring cell component. Poorly differentiated adenocarcinomas with similar architecture but without signet-ring cell features may be termed “poorly cohesive carcinoma”, although this tumour morphology appears rare in the colorectum.

Adenosquamous carcinoma

This rare subtype has an incidence of < 0.1% {22513428;10211505;39887413}, with histological features of both adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma in varying proportions. Rare cases have been linked to hypercalcaemia or inflammatory bowel disease, but not to HPV infection. Molecular findings include TP53 and APC mutations and MSI rate of up to 33% {36790480;38229035;39887413}. Adenosquamous CRC often presents at advanced stage and has a very poor prognosis {39887413}. Adenocarcinomas containing benign squamoid morules should not be interpreted as adenosquamous CRC.

Squamous cell carcinoma

Squamous cell carcinoma can very rarely occur in the colon or rectum, with or without anal involvement {25392258}. The histological features are the same as for squamous cell carcinoma elsewhere, and overlying mucosa may show squamous dysplasia or metaplasia. HPV infection is present in some cases {17527081}, and ulcerative colitis may also be a risk factor {28091842}. Molecular features include PTEN and PIK3CA mutations, similar to those in anal squamous cell carcinoma {36357817}. The prognosis appears poor {5125659;25392258}.

Low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma

Low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma is rare and almost exclusively arises in patients with inflammatory bowel diseases (both ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease). It is sometimes encountered incidentally. Rather than forming a discrete mass grossly, the tumour infiltrates as haphazard, well-differentiated glands without evidence of desmoplasia {16861975}. It may arise from low-grade conventional dysplasia or from non-conventional colitis-associated dysplasia {37055929}, and CK7 expression is sometimes present. IDH1 mutations are common {25029120}. Low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma carries a good prognosis, although rare examples develop high-grade morphology.

Lymphoglandular complex–like adenocarcinoma

Also known as dome carcinoma or gut-associated lymphoid tissue carcinoma, this rare form of CRC is characterized by prominent associated lymphoid tissue {29738362}. Its histology is reminiscent of lymphoglandular complexes, although these tumours are clearly malignant given the presence of haphazard glands, desmoplasia, necrosis, and/or single cells {38054635}. Most cases are confined to submucosa, but more deeply invasive examples have been reported. Few molecular data are available because of the rarity of this tumour, though some cases demonstrate MSI {38054635}. Although lymph node metastasis can occur, overall prognosis is good.

Undifferentiated carcinoma

As for undifferentiated carcinomas elsewhere in the digestive tract, these tumours lack morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular evidence of glandular, squamous, or neuroendocrine differentiation. They are typically large tumours that comprise dyscohesive rhabdoid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Some cases may form tumour nests embedded in a myxoid matrix {24503755;26551623}. Minor discrete areas of glandular differentiation may be seen elsewhere in the lesion. These tumours often, but not always, appear to have loss/coinactivation of multiple members of the SWI/SNF family of proteins, including SMARCA2 (BRM), SMARCA4 (BRG1), SMARCB1 (INI1), and ARID1A. Loss of nuclear immunostaining for the proteins relating to the affected genes can be seen {24503755;26551623;34951482}, affirming the diagnosis. MSI has been reported in some cases {32413172;34951482}. The prognosis is poor {34105518;34951482}.

Carcinoma with mesenchymal differentiation

This term describes a rare subgroup of CRC characterized by evidence of > 30% epithelial differentiation (gland formation, keratin expression) as well as > 30% heterologous or homologous mesenchymal differentiation (marked nuclear atypia, spindled histology). The histological findings can vary widely, including pure spindled morphology combined with keratin expression, admixed glandular and mesenchymal histology, or clear-cut sarcomatous features (e.g. osteosarcomatous or rhabdomyosarcomatous histology) {38155543}. Nearly all examples in the literature are single case reports. Molecular features can vary among cases, with a few demonstrating MSI or SWI/SNF complex abnormalities. These tumours often present at advanced stage {32632729}, and accordingly, prognosis appears poor {38767188}.

Grading

Grading of colorectal adenocarcinomas is based on assessment of gland formation, rather than cytological features. A two-tiered system is applied: low-grade (also referred to as well-differentiated to moderately differentiated) and high-grade (also referred to as poorly differentiated). Since these tumours often show morphological heterogeneity {3384983}, grading is based on the least differentiated tumour component. It should be reported as high-grade when containing at least one focus lacking gland formation that occupies a minimum area of 0.196 mm2 (generally corresponding to a 40× objective lens microscopic field) {32868525}. Any tumour without such a focus should be reported as low-grade. The invasive front, where tumour budding and poorly differentiated clusters may occur as a sign of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (see Important histological features below), should not be assessed when grading the tumour, though such findings should be reported separately. Cribriform architecture is considered a pattern of glandular differentiation and does not indicate high-grade morphology. This grading system offers the most prognostic value, especially for microsatellite-stable tumours {32868525}. Tumour grade should be reported as not applicable for pretreated tumours with neoadjuvant therapy.

Applying this grading system, signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma, micropapillary adenocarcinoma, and adenosquamous carcinoma subtypes should be considered high-grade. Pure adenoma-like adenocarcinoma and low-grade tubuloglandular adenocarcinoma should be considered low-grade. Medullary carcinoma demonstrates solid morphology but favourable clinical behaviour; because of this unusual combination, classification as high-grade is considered inappropriate and grading should be reported as not applicable. Other colorectal adenocarcinoma subtypes, namely mucinous, serrated, and lymphoglandular complex–like, should be graded using the scheme above {32868525}, and any given case may therefore be either low-grade or high-grade. Grading is not applicable to squamous cell carcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, and carcinoma with mesenchymal differentiation.

Important histological features
Lymphatic invasion

Lymphatic invasion (i.e. the presence of single cells or groups of tumour cells in lymphatic spaces, often defined as channels devoid of erythrocytes and lined by endothelial cells) is a morphological risk factor for the presence of lymph node metastases in pT1 and pT2 CRC {38709424}. Additional immunohistochemistry (e.g. D2-40) can be helpful with respect to both predicting lymph node metastatic disease and increasing interobserver reproducibility {37746764}.

Intramural and extramural vascular invasion

Vascular invasion can be subclassified according to localization: within the bowel wall (intramural vascular invasion [IMVI]) versus outside the bowel wall (EMVI). Both are associated with increased rates of metastatic disease and shorter survival. IMVI is reported to have an incidence of 4–40% (12.5% overall reported incidence) {28960400}. The incidence of EMVI is higher than that of IMVI, but this feature is still considered to be underreported. The negative prognostic impact of EMVI is higher than that of IMVI {28960400;26819536}. Features that are helpful in the recognition of EMVI are the orphan artery sign (a tumour nodule identified adjacent to an artery in a presumed vein) and the protruding tongue sign (a tongue of tumour protruding beyond the tumour border into a vein in the surrounding fat). Elastin staining or immunohistochemistry is useful to improve detection {23108018}.

Perineural invasion

Perineural invasion can be present in any of the three layers of the nerves (epineurium, perineurium, endoneurium). The incidence is reported to be about 20% in CRCs, with increased incidence associated with rectal cancers, higher stage, and the presence of other risk factors (e.g. vascular or lymphatic invasion) {26426380}. Perineural invasion is associated with local recurrence, distant recurrence, and decreased survival {26426380}.

Tumour budding, poorly differentiated clusters, and growth patterns

Tumour budding is defined as single cells or clusters of up to four tumour cells at the invasive margin of the tumour {28548122}. Budding grade is assessed on H&E staining in a hotspot at the invasive front using a three-tiered scoring system according to international consensus {28548122}. The presence of high-grade budding is associated with poor outcome in various subgroups of CRC, including pT1 cancers {38193866;37366086}. Its presence is associated with a poor response to neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer {39522647;38629323}. Budding is considered the morphological manifestation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition {21317460}.

Poorly differentiated clusters are groups of ≥ 5 tumour cells lacking gland formation {18438118} and are an adverse prognostic feature in CRC {27444718}. They are considered a biological continuum with tumour budding {35613045}. Both features are commonly analysed at the invasive front of the tumour. An alternative approach is the determination of growth pattern, where infiltrative and pushing growth patterns can be distinguished {3721406}. Infiltrative growth usually reflects the presence of tumour budding and/or poorly differentiated clusters. Hence, a pushing border is associated with improved outcome and lower stage.

Immune response

The relation of an immune response to CRC outcome has been proven. Intratumoural lymphocytes and a Crohn-like reaction are associated with better outcome {27172903}. Both features are associated with MSI {11413533;17631130}; however, the relation to outcome seems independent of MSI status {29382774;31743129}. A standardized examination of the presence of lymphocytes at the invasive front of the tumour, using immunohistochemistry for CD3 and CD8, showed that this feature has significant prognostic power {29754777}.

Resection margins and completeness of resection

The term “margin” refers to the areas of the resection or excision specimen that have been cut by the surgeon or endoscopist. Margins are not natural structures such as the serosal surface. Various margins can be defined: in resection specimens, the proximal and distal margins are the easiest to distinguish. Those margins are rarely positive, but when the tumour is located close to these longitudinal margins, the risk of local recurrence is increased, particularly in rectal cancer when no radiotherapy is administered before surgery {23331717}. The circumferential resection margin (considered positive if the distance between tumour and edge of resection is ≤ 1 mm) is more important, with a strong impact on local recurrence and overall survival in rectal cancer {18182672}. For caecal carcinomas, the retrocaecal non-peritonealized margin is also important, but less evidence is available {17764533}. The strongest association with outcome is present when the margin is involved by the primary tumour, tumour deposits, or multiple methods of spread {39154428}. Involvement due to a positive lymph node is not associated with poor outcome. For a local excision specimen, the most important margin is the deep margin; positivity suggests need for further treatment. In contrast, lateral/mucosal resection margin positivity does not influence local recurrence rates {36228648}.

In close correlation with the assessment of resection margins, the macroscopic evaluation of the completeness of the resection specimen and the achieved surgical plane of resection has been established as an important prognostic factor and as a quality indicator, particularly for rectal cancers. The optimal planes of surgery are the mesorectal fascia plane {11919228;16361623} and the mesocolic plane {18667357} for rectal and colonic tumours, respectively. Achievement of suboptimal planes of resection is associated with poor outcome.

Response to therapy

The application of neoadjuvant therapy, i.e. radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy for rectal carcinoma or systemic treatment for CRC, alters the histopathological tumour appearances. A spectrum of tumour response can be observed, varying from complete pathological response to no detectable effects, in both primary tumour as well as in lymph nodes {38192084}. Classification can be performed by assessment of downstaging (dependent on reliable pretreatment imaging) or evaluation of tumour regression grade (TRG). Various classification systems have been proposed, but all suffer from lack of reproducibility, although they have some features in common (see #49347Table 7.06) {25249540;31673084}. Grading of CRC after neoadjuvant therapy is not applicable. The presence of acellular mucin pools after neoadjuvant therapy does not classify a CRC as mucinous adenocarcinoma.

Criteria for reporting of CRC specimens are summarized in #44979Box 7.03.

 

Cytology

Cytology is not clinically relevant, except in the metastatic setting, where FNAC may be helpful.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a malignant epithelial tumour of large bowel with unequivocal evidence of invasion.

Staging

In staging, subdivision of pT1 tumours has been advocated for improved prognostication. Subdivision of pT1 CRC has gained increased attention with the higher caseload observed after the introduction of population screening. After local resection of these tumours, the risk of lymph node metastasis can be estimated on the basis of various factors, including invasion depth (for sessile polyps, the submucosal subdivision sm1–3 {8261988}; for stalked polyps, the Haggitt classification {4007423}), in combination with differentiation grade, presence of lymphatic invasion, and tumour budding {23884793}. Alternatively, the submucosal invasion depth can be reported in millimetres {23884793}.

Nodal status depends on the number of positive lymph nodes in the region of the primary tumour: no positive lymph nodes (pN0), 1–3 nodes (pN1), or ≥ 4 nodes (pN2). There has been a lot of discussion about the classification of discontinuous tumour deposits not associated with lymph nodes. In the previous (eighth) edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017. Bookref]], such tumour deposits were only considered in the absence of nodal metastases and categorized as pN1c, despite evidence that their prognostic value is independent of nodal status {28029327;36241558;36998291}. In the current edition [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]], tumour deposits may contain foci of extramural vascular invasion and/or perineural invasion, but demonstrate unequivocal tumour extension into surrounding soft tissue. When only micrometastases are present (no metastasis > 2 mm), this is indicated by the addition of “(mi)”. Isolated tumour cells are single cells or small clusters of cells ≤ 0.2 mm in greatest extent; these are not considered lymph node metastases [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017. Bookref]].

The presence of distant metastases can be classified as pM1a (metastases confined to one organ, without peritoneal metastases), pM1b (metastases in more than one organ), and pM1c (metastases to the peritoneum, with or without other organ involvement).

Prognosis and prediction

Two different approaches to the molecular pathological classification of CRC for prognosis and prediction have been used: genomic (DNA-based) classification, supported by an integrated molecular analysis performed by the Cancer Genome Atlas network in 2012 {22810696}; and transcriptomic (RNA-based, bulk, or single-cell) profiling that characterizes CRC using RNA sequencing or array-based technologies {27325016;38319359;33519906}.

Genomic classification

Genomic analysis classifies CRC into two main groups according to mutation rate: hypermutated cancers and non-hypermutated cancers. This corresponds well with the MSI and chromosomal instability pathways and is consistent with previous DNA-based classification systems.

Hypermutated cases (~13% of CRCs) have a high frequency of mutations, and most show MSI due to defective DNA MMR {32141610}. This includes both sporadic hypermutated MSI cancers with MLH1 promoter hypermethylation causing loss of MLH1 expression and inactivation of DNA MMR. Almost all of these tumours have the CIMP, with many other genes silenced by promoter hypermethylation. A small percentage (3–4%) of CRCs harbour either constitutive (Lynch syndrome) or somatic MMR gene mutations. A further approximately 3% of CRCs are ultramutated cancers, with extremely high mutation rates and a characteristic nucleotide base change spectrum (increased C>A transversions), resulting from the presence of a mutation that inactivates the proofreading function within the exonuclease domain of the DNA-replicating enzyme POLE, or rarely of POLD1 {23447401}. This results in failure to correct the misincorporation of nucleotides during DNA replication or repair by mutant POLE (or POLD1). Ultramutated and hypermutated cancers are usually combined into a single group with many more significantly recurrently mutated genes than the non-hypermutated CRCs, as shown in #44139Table 7.07. Hypermutated MSI cancers have far fewer DNA SCNAs than do non-hypermutated CRCs, but they show a very similar pattern of affected chromosome arms and subarms {22810696}.

Non-hypermutated cases (~84% of CRCs) have a low frequency of mutations (low tumour mutation burden) and are microsatellite-stable (MSS), but they have a high frequency of DNA SCNAs, often as chromosomal segment (arm and subarm) gains and losses, as shown in #44139Table 7.07. The genes that are significantly recurrently mutated in non-hypermutated MSS CRCs include APC (80%), TP53 (60%), and KRAS (45%), among many others at lower frequencies, as shown in #44139Table 7.07 {22810696}.

The most frequent alterations of signalling pathways by gene mutations, deletions, amplifications, and translocations are activation of the WNT, MAPK, and PI3K growth signalling pathways and inactivation of the TGF-β and p53 inhibitory pathways, which are relevant for targeted therapies {27325016}. The WNT signalling pathway is activated in 93% of non-hypermutated and 97% of hypermutated cancers, involving biallelic inactivation of APC or activation of CTNNB1 in > 80% of CRCs, together with changes to many other genes involved in regulation of the WNT pathway {22810696}.

Transcriptomic profiling

The Colorectal Cancer Subtyping Consortium (CRCSC) compiled bulk RNA expression profiling data from multiple studies and produced a consensus identifying four main consensus molecular subtype (CMS) groups {26457759}. Almost all hypermutated MSI cancers were classified as CMS1 (MSI-immune, 14%). The MSS cancers were subcategorized into three main groups: CMS2 (canonical, 37%), CMS3 (metabolic, 13%), and CMS4 (mesenchymal, 23%), with a residual unclassified group (mixed features, 13%). CMS1 CRCs were MSI/hypermutated because of MLH1 silencing via CIMP with frequent BRAF mutations, with a low number of SCNAs. This equates with the previously well-characterized sporadic MSI CRC subgroup. Gene expression profiling furthermore revealed evidence of strong immune activation in CMS1, consistent with pathological descriptions of prominent tumour-infiltrating CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and this indicates potential responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors {27325016}.

Several alternative transcriptomic classification systems have been proposed: the CRC intrinsic signature (CRIS) system focused on cancer cell intrinsic expression signatures in order to avoid stromal-derived features (five subtypes, CRIS-A to CRIS-E, and a non-classified group) {28561046}, although the importance of the tumour microenvironment stromal and immune components has emerged over the past decade; the single-cell intrinsic CMS (iCMS) system {35773407} used single-cell RNA-sequence data to derive two major subgroups (iCMS2 and iCMS3); and the pathway-derived subtypes system described three subtypes (PDS1, PDS2, and PDS3) showing a continuum of intrinsic biology associated with stem cell, signalling, and histopathological and clinical CRC attributes (see Fig. #58058) {38351382}. Transcriptomic analyses have shed new light on the classification of conventional adenomas versus serrated polyps and on the role and plasticity of cancer stem cells {38319359}. Use of immunohistochemistry, digital/computational pathology, and spatial transcriptomics to implement classification systems are developing fields. Transcriptomic classification systems based on biological processes indicated by gene expression patterns have enabled more biologically meaningful clustering of tumours, and are suggested as a basis for future clinical stratification in trials and other studies with potential for subtype-based targeted interventions, although further studies are required to validate their use {29242316;38319359;38351382}.

CRC is arguably one of the most studied cancers historically, resulting in very large numbers of potential prognostic and predictive biomarkers. #44373Table 7.08 presents the biomarkers routinely used as standard of care with clear-cut predictive value (e.g. RAS, BRAF, and MSI) and those in which prognosis and prediction are related but only partly established or still under investigation or development.

Established predictive biomarkers
RAS, RAF, and related genes

Mutations in RAS oncogenes are among the most common mutations in human cancers, and they are of biological significance in CRC. Two of the three RAS genes (KRAS and NRAS) have therapeutic significance in CRC. International guidelines {28350513;24996433} recommend the analysis of codons 12, 13, 59, 61, 117, and 146 in the KRAS and NRAS genes, because these codons generally correlate with resistance to monoclonal antibodies directed to the extracellular domain of EGFR (HER1) {16618717;25605843}. These monoclonal antibodies (of which cetuximab and panitumumab are leading examples) prevent EGFR dimerization and subsequent downstream oncogenic signalling pathways. Almost 50% of CRCs harbour a clinically relevant RAS mutation and should not be treated with anti-EGFR antibody therapy. Only 40–60% of RAS-wildtype cases also show response to this therapy {20526105}, suggesting a more complex biology.

In CRC, most BRAF mutations involve amino acid 600 (most frequently NM_004333.6:c.1799T>A p.Val600Glu [also known as BRAF V600E]), and these carry an adverse prognosis. Identification of somatic BRAF mutation is of use for the exclusion of underlying Lynch syndrome. In addition, BRAF may also have a predictive/therapeutic value: RAS and BRAF mutations are generally mutually exclusive, and although the evidence is less conclusive (owing in part to the lower prevalence of BRAF mutations in CRC compared with RAS-family mutations), several studies have reported that patients with BRAF p.Val600Glu (V600E) mutations do not benefit from anti-EGFR therapy {26989027}. BRAF and RAS mutations are overrepresented in some of the morphological subtypes of CRC (see #44373Table 7.08).

ERBB2 (HER2) overexpression or amplification may be evaluated in CRC tumour tissue (by immunohistochemistry or in situ hybridization) or sometimes in cell-free DNA, usually in stage IV cases, to predict response to anti-HER2 therapies {39587050}.

Rarely, CRCs harbour an NTRK fusion gene abnormality resulting in the formation of an oncogenic TRK fusion protein, driving constitutive kinase activity. These occur in 0.15–0.3% of all CRCs but are greatly enriched in MSI, BRAF-wildtype CRCs, occurring in 5% of this subset {31792356}. The TRK inhibitors (larotrectinib and entrectinib) represent potential therapeutic options {35701319}.

Microsatellite instability

MSI drives one of the key mechanisms of oncogenesis in CRC, and it is one of the tests of reference in the diagnosis of Lynch syndrome. In some countries with sufficient resources, reflex testing for MSI/MMR status is performed on all new cases of CRC. The presence of MSI is important in two clinical situations. First, MSI confers a good prognosis and is associated with probable lack, or reduction, of response to fluorouracil-based chemotherapy {20498393;24019539;21998335}. CRCs that are MSS, in the context of BRAF mutation, generally carry a poor prognosis. Second, the presence of MSI is important in the context of cancer immunotherapy. Studies have reported significant responses of MSI cancers (CRCs and others) to immune checkpoint (e.g. PD1/PDL1) inhibitors in patients who failed conventional therapy {26028255;28596308}.

Biomarkers partly established and/or under investigation and development
Cancer immunotherapy

The success of PD1/PDL1 inhibition in MSI CRC has been very encouraging, but it is limited by biology and disease stage, with this subset representing only 3–4% of stage IV CRC cases {21383284;18509181}. Some have suggested including PD1/PDL1 expression, tumour mutation burden, and/or assessment of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes / immunoscore as part of the analysis of eligibility for immunotherapies {38614266;38920285;37831146}. Further research is ongoing regarding the potential application of immunotherapies to earlier stage MSS CRCs {28492495}, which makes this a potentially interesting area of development in the future for biomarker analysis in CRC. The reproducibility of scoring systems has been validated in multicentre studies {29754777}, but currently this represents more of a disease classification and prognostic tool rather than a predictive tool.

Other predictive biomarkers

CRC transcriptome analysis is discussed above. In addition, there are specific gene expression signatures that have claimed to predict recurrence after surgery and may be used in patient stratification {27325854;22067406}.

PIK3CA, encoding the catalytic subunit of PI3K, is mutated in 10–20% of CRCs, mainly in exons 9 and 20. In RAS-wildtype CRC, PIK3CA mutations may be associated with a worse clinical outcome and with a negative prediction of response to targeted therapy by anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies {19223544;19366826}. In addition, mutations in PIK3CA may predict successful adjuvant therapy with acetylsalicylic acid in patients with CRC {23094721}.

Liquid biopsy, involving analysis of circulating tumour cells, cell-free nucleic acids (DNA, methylated DNA, mRNA, microRNA, long ncRNA), or proteins (such as CEA, CA19-9, IGFBP2, PKM2, DKK3, and many others) in the patient’s peripheral blood, has been investigated for potential application to the diagnosis of metastatic CRC, minimal residual disease, and to detection of predictive markers of response {31828035;32726923;37119736;38194840;33846436}. Among all the potential tests performed in blood, the detection of RAS and BRAF mutations in cell-free DNA {24658074;28885658} has found application in some centres, and cell-free DNA biomarker work is gaining prominence {31046124;36937316}. Stool-based biomarker and microbiome research is also expanding {39558327;38920285}.
