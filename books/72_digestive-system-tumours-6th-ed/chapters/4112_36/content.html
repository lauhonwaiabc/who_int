4.1.1.2 Ampullary adenomatous neoplasm

Definition

Ampullary adenomatous neoplasms are grossly visible intraepithelial neoplasms composed of dysplastic glandular epithelium occurring either on the duodenal surface of the papilla of Vater (i.e. ampullary duodenal adenoma) or within the ampullary channel (arising in the intra-ampullary tips of the common bile duct and/or intra-ampullary pancreatic duct, i.e. intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm [IAPN]).

ICD-O coding

82101/0 Ampullary adenoma, low-grade

82101/2 Ampullary adenoma, high-grade

ICD-11 coding

2E92.2 & XH3DV3 Adenoma, NOS, of duodenum

Related terminology

Not recommended: non-invasive pancreatobiliary papillary neoplasm with low-grade dysplasia; non-invasive pancreatobiliary papillary neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia.

Subtype(s)

Ampullary duodenal adenoma, low-grade (81441/0); ampullary duodenal adenoma, high-grade (81441/2); intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm with low-grade dysplasia (81631/0); intra-ampullary papillary-tubular neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia (81631/2)

Localization

Ampullary duodenal adenomas occur in the duodenal lumen-facing epithelium of the ampulla of Vater (also referred to as the papilla of Vater or major duodenal papilla). IAPNs are mass-forming intraepithelial neoplasms that occur within the intra-ampullary (intra-Oddi) epithelium of the common bile duct and/or pancreatic duct.

Clinical features

These neoplasms, especially the ampullary duodenal adenomas, are often asymptomatic and incidentally discovered on endoscopy. IAPNs may present with symptoms related to obstruction of the bile duct (jaundice, cholangitis) or, in rare instances, with pancreatitis due to obstruction of the pancreatic duct {21084962;38938087;39642968}. Other symptoms include nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, and anaemia. No specific symptom or constellation of symptoms is diagnostic {31463668;35286472}. Substantial weight loss in a patient with an ampullary lesion should raise the suspicion of malignant transformation.

Endoscopy is recommended for clinical diagnosis, supplemented with EUS, for evaluation of possible involvement of the pancreatic and bile ducts {38845233;33728632;32169282}. Ampullary duodenal adenomas appear as polypoid vegetative lesions readily identifiable endoscopically on the duodenal surface of the ampulla. IAPNs fill the ampullary cavity and typically form an intact mucosa-covered elevation of the ampulla with a patulous orifice from which granular material may protrude {38938087}.

Epidemiology

These neoplasms are uncommon in the general population. Although the exact incidence is unknown, there has been a perceived increase in incidence in recent years, attributed to increased detection during endoscopy for unrelated indications {35894762;33712346}. They occur in patients in their sixties (mean age: 62 years), with no evidence of a sex-specific distribution, although the perceived increase in incidence seems smaller in men than in women {33712346}. Patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) are typically younger (mean age: 39 years) {34624304;37985239;29122597;39642968}. In patients with FAP, the duodenum is the second most common site of polyp formation, and approximately 1 in 5 patients with FAP develop adenomas in the duodenal ampulla {24814771;38142452}. Data are scarce regarding the incidence in patients with other genetic syndromes, such as Lynch syndrome or MUTYH-associated polyposis {30448460;33130102}.

Etiology

As discussed in section Non-ampullary adenoma, more than half of small bowel neoplasms occur in the duodenum, mostly around the ampulla, suggesting that bile and/or pancreatic affluents may be carcinogenic {15652226}. This topographical pattern is also observed in patients with FAP, who are genetically predisposed to developing small bowel adenomas (see also section Genetic tumour syndromes of the digestive system). Risk factors for the development of sporadic cases are previous cholecystectomy and use of proton-pump inhibitors, while aspirin and light alcohol intake are associated with a reduced risk {35894762}.

Pathogenesis

Sporadic and FAP-related ampullary adenomas have similar molecular features affecting the WNT signalling pathway, including APC mutations {18670349}. KRAS mutations are detected in 30–40% of these tumours, whereas BRAF mutations are rare {19440799;18670349}. More than 80% of ampullary adenomas are microsatellite-stable, whereas 10% have microsatellite instability {19252434}. This molecular profile is consistent with the molecular changes identified in a large percentage of ampullary adenocarcinomas of intestinal type (see section Ampullary adenocarcinoma). There are currently no data specific for IAPN.

Macroscopic appearance

The appearance of ampullary duodenal adenomas is similar to that of their colorectal counterparts. Large size, erosion, fold convergence with the adjacent duodenal mucosa, and duct dilatation (sometimes observed endoscopically) raise the suspicion of malignancy.

IAPN typically presents as an ampulla that bulges onto the duodenal surface. The ampulla is covered with intact but stretched duodenal mucosa, and the orifice of the ampulla is dilated. On cut section, papillary/polypoid tumour fills and expands the ampullary channel. The epicentre of the lesion is in the ampullary channel. As a result, the bulk of the tumour (> 75%) is confined to the ampullary channel, and there is limited (< 25% of the lesion), if any, extension onto the duodenal surface or into the extra-Oddi ends of the common bile duct and/or main pancreatic duct {21084962;38938087}.

Histopathology

Most of the adenomas arising from the duodenal surface of the ampulla have intestinal differentiation, show a tubular or tubulovillous growth pattern, and resemble, in many respects, colorectal adenomas. Most have low-grade dysplasia (LGD) and consist of tall columnar cells with elongated nuclei and inconspicuous nucleoli. Paneth, goblet, or endocrine cells may be prominent {35286472}. Adenomas with high-grade dysplasia (HGD), which are also referred to as carcinoma in situ in some geographical regions, are characterized by an increased degree of architectural complexity, nuclear stratification, and nuclear atypia. Ampullary adenomas arising on the duodenal surface may extend along the surface epithelium of the papilla to involve the epithelium of the lumen of the ampulla. At the periphery of the polypoid lesion, flat adenomatous changes may also extend into the common bile duct or periampullary ductules within the sphincter of Oddi. FAP-associated adenomas are often multifocal but otherwise have the same histological features as sporadic lesions. Immunohistochemically, intestinal-type adenomas express markers of intestinal differentiation, including CDX2 and MUC2, and usually lack expression of MUC1. Coexpression of CK7 and CK20 is common {18670349}.

Occasionally, pyloric gland adenomas also arise in the duodenal surface of the ampulla, and the characteristics of these rare cases seem to be very similar to those of the cases described in the stomach and non-ampullary duodenum (see section Non-ampullary adenoma).

IAPN is in essence the intra-Oddi counterpart of pancreatic and biliary mass-forming intraductal neoplasms (intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm, intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct) defined as a non-invasive dysplastic, exophytic lesion growing predominantly within the ampullary channel, with minimal or no involvement of the extra-Oddi bile duct, pancreatic duct, or duodenal papilla {21084962;38938087}. IAPNs can harbour a spectrum of dysplastic grades, with > 80% of cases showing a mixture of LGD and HGD within the same lesion. There is often coexistence of intestinal and pancreatobiliary differentiation, with a predominantly non-intestinal phenotype in 45% {38938087}. The immunohistochemistry profile of these lesions often follows their direction of differentiation, with predominant MUC2 and CDX2 expression in the intestinal patterns, and predominant expression of MUC1, MUC5AC, and MUC6 in the pancreatobiliary phenotype. However, overlap is very common. Over 50% of cases coexpress CK7 and CK20.

Cytology

Most ampullary duodenal adenomas and IAPNs are accessible to conventional endoscopic biopsy, but brush cytology or FNAB is occasionally obtained for deeper-seated intra-ampullary neoplasms. Cytological features of lesions with LGD include clusters of cohesive, polarized cells with elongated stratified nuclei, mild nuclear pleomorphism, and a clean background. High-grade examples have more pleomorphic cells with a high N:C ratio, dissociated cells, and possibly necrosis {28900465}. Distinction between LGD and reactive atypia may be challenging. HGD is cytologically indistinguishable from invasive carcinoma. Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a grossly visible sessile or pedunculated intraepithelial neoplasm on the duodenal surface of the ampulla or within the ampulla.

Desirable: a tubular, tubulovillous, villous, or papillary growth pattern with dysplastic epithelial lining; intestinal and/or pancreatobiliary/gastric differentiation (for subtyping).

Staging

Neoplasms with HGD are regarded as pTis. When accompanied by an invasive carcinoma, these neoplasms should be staged based on the invasive component.

Prognosis and prediction

The risk of malignant transformation is lower in FAP-associated ampullary duodenal adenomas than in sporadic lesions {32145286;34624304}. Hence, sporadic adenomas are removed endoscopically or surgically following detection, whereas in patients with FAP, ampullary adenomas < 10 mm may be surveilled and monitored for progression in size and grade of dysplasia {38845233}. There is a high false negative rate on endoscopic biopsies {37595391}, because invasive carcinomas are often hidden in the deeper aspects of the lesions. The risk of progression appears to be lower in ampullary duodenal adenomas than in IAPNs, as the majority of the latter have HGD and/or an associated invasive carcinoma {38938087}. Invasive carcinoma is found in about 75% of resected IAPNs but can be subtle and easily missed, especially in biopsies or poorly grossed resection specimens. IAPNs without associated invasive adenocarcinoma have 3-year and 5-year survival rates of 100% after successful removal {21084962;38938087}, but recurrence may occur {35286472}.
