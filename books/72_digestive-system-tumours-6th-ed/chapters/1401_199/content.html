14.0.1 Neuroendocrine neoplasms of the digestive system: Introduction and classification

As neuroendocrine cells are present in almost every organ, neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) arise in all organs of the digestive system. NENs can be divided into epithelial NENs, which show cytokeratin expression, and the less common non-epithelial (or neuronal) NENs, which lack cytokeratin expression and are exemplified by paraganglioma (see section Paragangliomas) {35294740}.

Like in the fifth edition, epithelial NENs in this edition include well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), and mixed neuroendocrine–non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) {30140036}. NET and NEC are pure NENs, which are composed exclusively (defined as ≥ 70%) of cells with neuroendocrine differentiation.

Conceptually, the MiNEN category of neoplasia was built on the previously established category mixed adenocarcinoma–neuroendocrine carcinoma (MANEC) from the fourth edition, but it was expanded in the fifth edition to make it clear that some mixed tumours were low-grade and therefore not all were NECs {11914632;27169712;24213223}. MiNEN is defined as a category of neoplasia (rather than a specific diagnosis) that is composed of two morphologically distinct regions of different differentiation: one that demonstrates neuroendocrine differentiation, and the other showing a different (non-neuroendocrine) line of differentiation.

However, this approach still resulted in uncertainties on how to classify carcinomas where neuroendocrine differentiation is intermixed with non-neuroendocrine differentiation in a more subtle way rather than presenting as morphologically obvious areas of distinct differentiation {35317101;36646615;39436458}. The term “amphicrine” has been applied to these tumours, which are invariably carcinomas, and have been reported at almost every site of the digestive system {35317101;38178706;37581694;28053814;31832022;22918208;27684986;32534704;37249796;36646615;36654693;32982118;33269082;39234057;39421232;39436458}.

Therefore, in the present classification, the concept of amphicrine neoplasia is revisited and broadened using the term “amphicrine-like carcinoma” (ALC). “Amphicrine” literally means dual exocrine and neuroendocrine differentiation; however in this context, the term “amphicrine-like” is used as it refers to two similar, but slightly different, scenarios illustrated in Fig. #50148. There may be morphological and immunohistochemical features of neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation in the same tumour cells throughout a neoplasm. Alternatively, and more commonly, there may be an intimate admixture of morphologically similar cells, some of which express neuroendocrine markers and others that express non-neuroendocrine markers (such as mucin), with or without a third population expressing both neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine markers. In either scenario, the key distinction from MiNEN is that in ALC there are no morphologically distinct zones of different differentiation, rather this mixed neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation is spread evenly throughout the neoplasm. In ALC there is always a dominant non-neuroendocrine carcinoma component (more frequently an adenocarcinoma or rarely a squamous cell carcinoma). It is accepted that the diagnosis of an ALC, like a MiNEN, may be difficult to achieve in small biopsies.

Like MiNEN, ALC is not a specific diagnosis; rather it is a category of neoplasia. After a tumour is determined to be part of the ALC group, it should still receive a specific diagnosis based on the exocrine component. For example, a tumour should not be classified solely as ALC, but rather as amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma or amphicrine-like squamous cell carcinoma. The term “goblet cell adenocarcinoma” is the specific diagnosis given to the most common ALCs of the vermiform appendix. As this entity is now so well recognized and clinically distinct, it is acceptable to classify a tumour solely as a goblet cell adenocarcinoma without stating that it is part of the ALC family.

It is emphasized that the diagnosis of ALC requires both morphological and immunohistochemical evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation. Poorly differentiated carcinomas of many sites commonly include a few cells that express neuroendocrine markers, and this finding alone should not warrant reclassification as ALC. Similarly, it is common for pancreatic acinar cell carcinomas to include scattered cells that show weak expression of neuroendocrine markers, and their presence should not alone warrant classification as ALC.

Although this chapter predominantly discusses epithelial NENs, it also includes a section on the non-epithelial NEN paraganglioma (section Paragangliomas). Although paragangliomas do not arise from the luminal GI tract, they are presented there for convenience despite being in the differential diagnosis of NETs of both the luminal gut and the pancreas. The description of composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour (CoGNET) is also included (section Composite gangliocytoma/neuroma and neuroendocrine tumour) as it predominantly arises in the periampullary duodenum. Goblet cell adenocarcinoma, an example of ALC, is discussed with appendiceal neoplasms (section Appendiceal goblet cell adenocarcinoma).

NEN diagnosis

The definitive diagnosis of NEN requires multistep assessment. The first prerequisite is the presence of morphological features of neuroendocrine differentiation. The second prerequisite is that the morphological impression of neuroendocrine differentiation be proven by ancillary testing. In modern practice this almost always involves immunohistochemistry. Three immunohistochemical markers of neuroendocrine differentiation are in widespread use: the secretory proteins chromogranin A and synaptophysin, and the transcription factor INSM1. Although very specific, chromogranin A is relatively insensitive and is commonly lost in high-grade NENs such as NECs and in certain NETs (e.g. glicentin/PYY-producing L cell and somatostatin-producing D cell NETs) {35294740}. In contrast, synaptophysin is very sensitive but lacks specificity, as it may be expressed by other non-neuroendocrine neoplasms {35294740}. Although not completely sensitive or specific, INSM1 generally combines the better sensitivity of synaptophysin with the better specificity of chromogranin A {30154579}. A common scenario is that synaptophysin is diffusely positive with uniform intensity, but not necessarily strong (3+); while chromogranin A and INSM1 are equally diffuse but with an intensity that varies from weak to strong (1+ to 3+) within the tumour. Because no single marker is completely sensitive or specific, at least two of these three markers should be positive before neuroendocrine differentiation is considered definitively proven. A specific exception to this is the neuroendocrine marker low or absent small cell carcinoma (tuft cell–like), which, despite showing classic morphological features of a small cell NEC, commonly lacks expression of neuroendocrine markers but is usually positive for the transcription factor POU2F3 {35760287} and lacks expression of markers that suggest an alternative diagnosis, such as p40 (basaloid squamous cell carcinoma {35760287}) or CD99 (Ewing sarcoma or another EWSR1 rearranged neoplasm). It is accepted that in resource-poor settings, a presumptive diagnosis of NEN may have to be made on morphology with minimal immunohistochemical support.

NET and NEC

NET and NEC are the two classes of pure NEN. NET is a pure NEN with well-differentiated morphology. NEC is a pure NEN with poorly differentiated morphology and may be of small or large cell types. Besides neuroendocrine markers and Ki-67 proliferation index, which is mandatory to facilitate grading of NETs, other markers may be useful in NENs in specific circumstances. These include SSTR2, which may be helpful for theragnostic purposes in certain tumours and clinical circumstances, and hormone markers, which may be helpful to assess if a tumour is the cause of a specific clinical syndrome.

The distinction between NET and NEC is entirely morphological. It is not based on any single feature, but rather on a constellation of findings {33382490}. A well-developed organoid architecture with nests and trabecula readily appreciated at lower power, a relative lack of cytological atypia, rounded nuclei with easily appreciated neuroendocrine-type stippled chromatin, and relatively prominent granular cytoplasm – all favour NET. A sheet-like architecture or the presence of single infiltrating malignant cells, profound cytological atypia, nuclear moulding, necrosis, and apoptosis – all favour NEC. The Ki-67 proliferative index and mitotic count cannot be used to separate grade 3 (G3) NETs from NECs. However, many G3 NETs show only intermediate proliferative activity (for example a Ki-67 index between 20% and 55%), whereas many NECs have very high proliferative activity (commonly Ki-67 index > 55%, often significantly higher) {25723112;22967994}. The distinction between NET and NEC may be aided by ancillary immunohistochemistry including a mutant-type pattern of P53 expression, abnormal loss of RB1 expression, and, specifically in the pancreas, loss of expression of DAXX and ATRX.

NET and NEC evolution

NET and NEC are distinct tumour types with different morphologies, molecular signatures, natural histories, and responses to therapy. NETs may show grade progression from lower (grade 1 [G1] or grade 2 [G2]) to higher (G2 or G3) grade over time, with the higher-grade component often being found only focally within the primary neoplasm or upon disease progression at a metastatic site {25723112;26482044;27259015;31658459}. In most cases the higher-grade component retains a well-differentiated appearance along with the molecular characteristics of well-differentiated NETs but accompanied by a higher mitotic rate and Ki-67 index {27259015;36788102;34499237}. In contrast, NECs are de novo high-grade malignancies, although some may present in association with, or evolve from, other high-grade non-neuroendocrine carcinomas or their precursor lesions, sometimes after treatment.

In rare instances, NEC-like histological features (diffuse growth, high-grade cytological atypia, necrosis) have been reported to evolve from NETs over time, particularly after treatment {39382626;39556303}. Even more rarely, this is accompanied by the acquisition of certain additional molecular events more typical of NECs such as TP53 and, very rarely, RB1 mutations {39382626;39556303;39671088}. The development of NEC-like morphology during progression of lower-grade NETs is an adverse prognostic feature associated with rapid growth and metastasis, and resistance to NET therapies. However, it is still a matter of debate whether such tumours should be classified as G3 NET with NEC-like morphology or NEC arising from NET. In any case, it is emphasized that these events are very rare.

MiNEN

See #49826Box 14.01.

MiNEN is the class of non-pure NEN composed of two morphologically distinct neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine components. The categorization of MiNEN requires a four-step process. First, separate components, one of which shows classic neuroendocrine morphology, must be present and be recognizable by morphology. Second, both neoplastic components should account for ≥ 30% of the neoplastic cell population. A specific exception to the 30% rule is that when even a very minor neuroendocrine component of a tumour is an NEC of small cell carcinoma subtype, the small cell component should always be reported, giving its relative proportion, as it may dominate prognosis. Third, the NEN component should be unequivocally proven by the expression of at least two neuroendocrine markers. Fourth, the two components should be graded according to the current criteria for each tumour type with Ki-67 proliferation index clearly reported for the NEN component. Neoplasms in the MiNEN category should also receive a specific diagnosis on the basis of the components present, as MiNEN (neuroendocrine component – non-neuroendocrine component).

ALC

In the digestive system, carcinomas may show morphological features of neuroendocrine differentiation and express neuroendocrine markers but do not meet the diagnostic criteria for the MiNEN category requiring the presence of two morphologically distinct and separate components. This poses a diagnostic problem, especially when neuroendocrine differentiation is proven to be extensive, for example at or above the 30% threshold, by immunohistochemistry. Historically this has sometimes resulted in the peculiar diagnosis of “carcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation”, a term that may endanger the appropriate oncological management of patients. These tumours should now be considered as part of the broad group of ALCs. There are three criteria for the categorization as ALC. First, there should be morphological evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation in ≥ 30% of neoplastic cells. Second, the morphological evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation should be confirmed by immunohistochemistry (again requiring the expression of at least two neuroendocrine markers). Third, the tumour should not fulfil the criteria for MiNEN, i.e. there should not be different regions or nodules showing different differentiations, rather the mixed neuroendocrine and non-neuroendocrine differentiation should be evenly distributed throughout the tumour cell population either in the same cells or in scattered cells. ALC should be staged according to the relevant TNM systems applicable to their non-neuroendocrine carcinoma counterpart. At this stage, since little is known about the behaviour and prognostic stratification of ALC compared with other carcinomas, it is recommended that the Ki-67 proliferative index be assessed when possible. As the non-neuroendocrine and neuroendocrine components are intimately intermixed, if the Ki-67 proliferative index is assessed, this should be provided for the entire neoplasm rather than attempting to separately define it in different components. Like MiNEN, neoplasms in the ALC category should also receive a specific diagnosis on the basis of the components present, for example, amphicrine-like adenocarcinoma or amphicrine-like squamous cell carcinoma.

Grading NETs and tumour necrosis

See #50122Table 14.01.

NECs are high-grade by definition and are therefore not graded further. The approach to grading NETs on the basis of the Ki-67 index and mitotic count is unchanged from the fifth edition. Mitotic counts should be expressed per 2 mm2 in contiguous fields (if available) in the areas of highest mitotic activity, with the cell count beginning on a field containing a mitosis (if present). Assessment of the Ki-67 proliferative index should be undertaken in the areas of highest nuclear labelling (hot spots) in contiguous fields. The presence of any nuclear labelling with Ki-67 in a neoplastic cell that is above background is considered positive, and non-neoplastic cells (such as tumour-infiltrating inflammatory cells or stromal cells) should be excluded from both the numerator and denominator of the cell count. It is recommended to report the number of cells counted for the Ki-67 index determination. Manual counting of ≥ 500 neoplastic cells remains the gold standard to which all other methods of Ki-67 assessment should be compared. The application of digital image analysis technologies is acceptable, provided that individual platforms and laboratories have validated these image analysis approaches against the gold standard. In small biopsies it may be difficult to accurately grade a NET given the limited tissue available. In such cases it is recommended to report the area assessed (in mm2) for mitotic count (while still expressing the average mitotic count as mitoses/2 mm2) as well as the actual number of cells counted for the Ki-67 index. If the mitotic count and the Ki-67 proliferation index are discordant, the higher grade is assigned. However, usually the Ki-67 proliferation index is higher and determines the grade {24121170}.

It is emphasized that Ki-67 is a continuous variable. For example, there is no meaningful difference in outcome between a G1 NET with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 2.9% and a G2 NET with a Ki-67 index of 3%; nor between a G2 NET with a Ki-67 index of 20% and a G3 NET with a Ki-67 index of 20.1%. Rather, the prognosis progressively and gradually deteriorates as the Ki-67 increases. The Ki-67 index (together with the mitotic count) is useful to separate NETs into the broad grade groups of G1, G2, and G3 (see #50122Table 14.01) with the maximal benefit being to compare treatments and outcomes at a population level. Other cut-off points (e.g. 10%) may help tailor treatment at the individual level {25014687;33301235;38610840} or define entry criteria for clinical trials {33052555;37752423}. Along this line there have been recent proposals that G2 pancreatic NETs (PanNETs) may be subdivided into two grades (G2a for a Ki-67 index from 3% to < 10%, and G2b for a Ki-67 index from 10% to ≤ 20%) {38955993;39969798;32360001;34283399;31149288;40236435}. For these reasons, every effort should be made to provide an accurate Ki-67 proliferative index independent of its use to stratify patients into the currently defined grade groups. In the resource-poor setting, if Ki-67 is not available, it may be necessary to assign a presumptive grade based solely on the mitotic count. In this situation it should be clearly stated that this is an estimated rather than a definitive grade.

Although tumour necrosis is one of the features that favour the diagnosis of NEC over NET, the presence of necrosis alone is not sufficient to warrant classification as NEC, as tumour necrosis can also occur in otherwise classic NETs. The presence of tumour necrosis is a validated component of the grading systems of many NETs outside the digestive system, including the thyroid medullary carcinomas (the NETs of the thyroid) and the pulmonary NETs. There would be clear advantages in unifying the grading of NETs of all primary sites, and there was active consideration of whether the presence of tumour necrosis should be integrated into the grading of NETs of the digestive system. Despite some emerging evidence {35934531}, there are currently insufficient data to include tumour necrosis in the grading of digestive system NETs. To address this evidence gap, and to gather data that may support a unified grading system, it is recommended that the presence or absence of tumour necrosis should be assessed in all NETs. However, rather than being a component of the grade, the presence or absence of tumour necrosis should be reported alongside the grade (e.g. “G2 NET positive for tumour necrosis” or “G3 NET negative for tumour necrosis”).

For this assessment, only true tumour necrosis (and not infarct-like necrosis) should be reported. True tumour necrosis is often associated with degenerating cytoplasm, karyorrhectic nuclear debris, and an abrupt transition to viable cells. It often presents as small foci of comedonecrosis centrally within nests of tumour cells, or as necrosis of single cells adjacent to viable cells. In contrast, infarct-like necrosis may be associated with a fibroblastic reaction, granulation tissue, extravasated blood, haemosiderin deposition, a gradual transition to more viable areas, and an identifiable FNA tract. As all NECs are high-grade, separate reporting of tumour necrosis in NECs is not mandatory, but the presence of necrosis, especially if extensive, may support a diagnosis of NEC over G3 NET.
