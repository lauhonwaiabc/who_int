11.1.2.4 Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas

Definition

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) of the pancreas is a low-grade malignant tumour composed of poorly cohesive neoplastic cells delicately lining small branching vessels accompanied by degenerative changes producing finger-like structures (pseudopapillae) and lacking a specific line of differentiation that parallels cells seen in the normal pancreas.

ICD-O coding

84520/3 Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of pancreas

ICD-11 coding

2C10.Y & XH3AE9 Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of pancreas

Related terminology

Acceptable: solid pseudopapillary tumour.

Subtype(s)

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm with high-grade carcinoma (84521/3)

Localization

SPNs have a slight predilection for the tail of the gland {37236826;24622060;36813115;37523603}.

Clinical features

SPNs are often incidentally found by imaging or present with nonspecific symptoms such as abdominal discomfort and pain {37236826;24622060;37523603}. Patients with large neoplasms can develop an acute abdomen secondary to tumour rupture and intraperitoneal haemorrhage {40106120} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Tumour serum markers are normal, and therefore not useful diagnostically. Functional endocrine syndromes have not been described in association with SPN. Ultrasonography, CT, and MRI reveal a well-demarcated, variably solid and partially cystic mass, occasionally with calcifications.

Epidemiology

SPNs are rare, accounting for 0.9–2.7% of all exocrine pancreatic neoplasms and only 5% of cystic neoplasms {34353996}. The number of cases reported in English literature has increased 7-fold since 2000, but this probably reflects a better awareness of this entity rather than a true increase in incidence {24622060}. Young women (mean age: 28 years, range: 7–79 years) are more frequently affected, and SPNs account for about 30% of pancreatic neoplasms in women younger than 40 years. SPN can also be observed in men, although more rarely, and in general, these patients are 5–10 years older than women with an SPN {36813115;37523603} [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. SPNs are one of the most common pancreatic tumours in children {39473958}.

Etiology

Although rare, SPNs have been reported in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis {12399055;26027985;38728213}.

Pathogenesis

The striking female association, occurrence of SPNs more frequently in premenopausal women, and in vitro analysis of primary cell cultures of SPN revealing influence of estrogen on proliferation of the neoplastic cells suggest that female hormones may be involved in the development and growth of SPNs {26166473;20364070}.

The lack of a definitive direction of differentiation that parallels a normal cell in the pancreas (i.e. expression of PDX1, SOX9, PTF1A, and NKX2-2) and the potential role of hormones in the development and growth of SPNs support the theory that SPNs may be derived from pluripotent stem cells of the genital ridges possibly deposited into pancreatic parenchyma during embryogenesis {10881741;30130629}. This hypothesis is further supported by the reports of neoplasms morphologically identical to pancreatic SPNs involving the retroperitoneum, ovary, and testis {1705067;25186079;20871224;29705715}.

Virtually all (> 95%) SPNs harbour somatic point mutations in exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene, a gene that encodes for a protein that functions in the WNT/β-catenin signalling pathway {30972907;28054945}. CTNNB1 mutations lead to a β-catenin protein that escapes intracytoplasmic phosphorylation and that can therefore translocate into the nucleus where it forms complexes with the T-cell transcription factor / lymphoid enhancer-binding factor, thus activating the transcription of oncogenes such as MYC and CCND1. However, in about 10% of SPNs with cytoplasmic / nuclear β-catenin labelling, CTNNB1 mutations are lacking {18192886}; the cause remains unclear, and it may be due to somatic mutations in the APC gene itself {38728213}. Mutations in the CTNNB1 gene also explain the immunohistochemical overexpression of glutamine synthetase (GS), AMACR, AR, TFE3, LEF1, and CD138, which are downstream targets of WNT/β-catenin signalling {26578390;24072181;18192886;34048981}. SPNs do not harbour somatic mutations in genes commonly mutated in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma such as KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A (P16), and SMAD4.

The characteristic poorly cohesive appearance of SPNs may be caused by CTNNB1 mutation. Indeed, the loss of β-catenin at the cell membrane, which also alters the function of E-cadherin that normally interacts with β/γ-catenin, may result in the loss of cell-to-cell anchorage. In addition, several proteins involved in cell adhesion have been found to be altered in SPNs, when compared with normal pancreatic tissue, supporting their role in the poor cohesion of cells {18550473;18269583}.

Macroscopic appearance

SPNs are solitary, well-demarcated, and often large tumours (average size: 50 mm; range: 5–250 mm), with degenerative changes and intratumoural haemorrhage resulting in focal solid areas admixed with large areas of haemorrhage and cyst formation. Most SPNs are solitary, but multifocality has been also reported [[Klimstra DS, Hruban RH, Siegel CS, et al. Tumors of the pancreas. Arlington (VA): American Registry of Pathology; 2023 (AFIP atlases of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 15). Bookref]]. Small tumours tend to be more solid. The wall of the neoplasm may contain calcifications. Rarely, the tumour locally extends into the duodenal wall or other adjacent structures {37236826;24622060}.

Histopathology

At low magnification, these neoplasms have solid areas admixed with cystic and haemorrhagic areas containing pseudopapillary structures. At intermediate magnification, monomorphic neoplastic cells loosely cling to hyalinized or myxoid branching fibrovascular cords, forming pseudopapillae when the tumour cells separate due to poor cohesion, haemorrhage, and degenerative changes. Branching delicate vessels and poorly cohesive cells are easily appreciated on touch preparations made from the cut surface of surgically resected tumours. At high magnification, the cytoplasm of the neoplastic cells is eosinophilic or vacuolated, often containing small PASD-positive hyaline globules, which, on ultrastructural examination, correspond to zymogen-like α1-antitrypsin granules {21677537}. The nuclei of the neoplastic cells are round to oval, often grooved or indented and have finely dispersed chromatin without a prominent nucleolus. Bizarre degenerative-type nuclei may occasionally occur, but they do not have any prognostic significance. Glycogen is not prominent, and mucin is absent. Mitoses are uncommon. Cholesterol crystals surrounded by foreign body giant cells are often present, as are foamy histiocytes. Calcification and even ossification may occur. The tumour is generally well demarcated, but the neoplastic cells characteristically non-destructively infiltrate into the surrounding non-neoplastic pancreatic tissue, entrapping non-neoplastic acinar cells and islets of Langerhans {21677537}. Vascular and perineural invasion are rare.

In addition to these typical features, some patterns have been described including oncocytic, pigmented, hepatoid, and clear cells. Oncocytic SPNs show neoplastic cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, filled with mitochondria and may simulate oncocytoma or chromophobe renal cell carcinoma. Pigmented SPNs contain brown pigments, either lipofuscin or melanin {15871726}. In clear cell SPNs, neoplastic cells have clear cytoplasm resulting from the accumulation of multiple cytoplasmic vacuoles, which seem to be the result of distended mitochondria or endoplasmic reticulum {17001153;31958381}. In hepatoid SPNs, the morphology is very similar to hepatocellular carcinoma, and these tumours are also enriched in PAS-positive hyaline globules {35359182}.

Metastases generally have the same morphological features as the primary tumour {39099687}.

Extremely rare SPNs with foci of high-grade malignant transformation have been reported {15767807;32158585}. These SPNs, which are clinically extremely aggressive, are characterized by diffuse sheets of neoplastic cells with significant nuclear atypia, and abundant mitoses. Two cases have been reported to contain a focus of sarcomatoid (spindle cell) carcinoma {15767807}.

Immunolabelling of SPNs reveals nuclear/cytoplasmic expression of β-catenin {11943721}, LEF1 expression {39427587}, and a very characteristic dot-like paranuclear expression of CD99 {21566515}. Vimentin is consistently positive, and cytokeratins are detected in 30–70% of cases, with the percentage of cells labelling depending on the method of antigen retrieval used. As many as 50% of SPNs express KIT (CD117), but these neoplasms do not harbour KIT mutations {16778826}. The neoplastic cells can also label with antibodies to synaptophysin, PR, cyclin D1, CD10, CD56, SOX11, claudin-5, claudin-7, and α1-antitrypsin, but these markers are not specific for SPNs since they can be observed in other pancreatic neoplasms {10881741;21261715;32816310}. The neoplastic cells are usually negative for chromogranin A, trypsin, BCL10, and CEA {21261715;31958381}. Recently, ABCD1 expression has been demonstrated to be a new sensitive and specific marker of SPN, potentially useful in the differential diagnosis with other pancreatic neoplasms {38567813}. Aberrant expression of E-cadherin is a typical feature of SPN, and two distinct patterns of immunoreactivity can be observed depending on the antibody used: with the antibody directed against the cytoplasmic domain, the tumour cells have nuclear E-cadherin positivity, and with the antibody directed against extracellular fragments, they are negative for E-cadherin {18269583}.

Rare cases of SPN associated with a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (PanNET) have also been reported {28327291;25596255}, and some PanNETs can also have SPN-like morphology, resulting in a challenging differential diagnosis, for which chromogranin A (expressed in PanNETs but not in SPNs) is of primary importance {32488621;21677537}.

Cytology

FNA and FNAB specimens are generally richly cellular and characterized by delicate branching capillaries surrounded by poorly cohesive small and monomorphic neoplastic cells in a clean or, more frequently, haemorrhagic background. The cytoplasm of the cells is eosinophilic or foamy and may contain hyaline globules. The nuclei have indented or grooved membranes and may be stripped of cytoplasm (naked nuclei) {36165589;35204541}. SPNs are diagnosed as category 7 (malignant) using the WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology (WHO System) {38709670} [[International Academy of Cytology – International Agency for Research on Cancer – World Health Organization Joint Editorial Board. WHO Reporting System for Pancreaticobiliary Cytopathology. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (IAC-IARC-WHO cytopathology reporting systems series, 1st ed.; vol. 2). https://publications.iarc.who.int/621. Bookref]].

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is not clinically relevant in postoperative specimens. In the preoperative setting, molecular testing of pancreatic cyst fluid may reveal somatic activating mutation in exon 3 of CTNNB1 in the absence of other genetic changes {11731417;11943721;22158988}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a combination of solid and pseudopapillary structures composed of poorly cohesive neoplastic epithelial cells delicately lining small branching vessels; positive nuclear and cytoplasmic labelling for β-catenin.

Desirable: a neoplasm occurring in a patient of the characteristic age group and sex; cholesterol crystals with granulomatous reaction; PAS-positive hyaline globules; LEF1 expression.

Staging

The staging of SPN follows the staging of pancreatic exocrine carcinoma [[Brierley JD, Giuliani M, O’Sullivan B, et al., editors. TNM Classification of Malignant Tumours. 9th ed. Oxford (UK): John Wiley & Sons; 2025. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The long-term prognosis is generally excellent for localized, recurrent, and even metastatic disease, with long disease-free periods after complete surgical resection {37236826;36813115}. The reported overall and disease-specific survival rates are 99.8% and 100%, respectively. The 5- and 10-year relapse-free survival rates are 97.4% and 90.2%, respectively {37236826;36813115}. Only a few patients have died of a metastatic SPN, and some of these patients had SPNs with an undifferentiated component {19476869;15767807}.
