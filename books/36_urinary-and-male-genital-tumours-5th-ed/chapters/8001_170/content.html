8.0.0.1 Tumours of the testicular adnexa: Introduction

The testicular adnexa and anatomically associated parts include the embryologically diverse rete testis, epididymis, spermatic cord, testicular appendages, surrounding soft tissue, and mesothelium.

Primary paratesticular soft tissue tumours are rare entities. However, for genitourinary sarcomas, the paratesticular region is the site most commonly involved, and spermatic cord adipocytic tumours are the most frequent tumours at this anatomical site. They usually occur in the adult population, with the exception of lipoblastoma, which occurs in children. An important change in the fifth edition of the WHO classification of urinary and male genital tumours is that the soft tissue tumours of the paratesticular region and spermatic cord are now handled in section Chapter 10: Mesenchymal tumours. #31922Table 7.01 summarizes the soft tissue tumour types that can uncommonly be identified in this region.

For the fifth edition, this chapter on testicular adnexa tumours is organized systematically and describes rare and different entities whose place of origin is the common denominator. #22615Table 7.02 and #31978Fig. 7.01 summarize the tumour entities described in this chapter.

Other than soft tissue tumours, the majority of the tumours in the testicular adnexa are derived from mesothelium (tunica vaginalis) {12410753; 32619438}, with adenomatoid tumour being by far the commonest among these {32619438; 15502808}. Some others are believed to originate from multiple appendages/inclusions of Müllerian or Wolffian duct origin or to arise from Müllerian-type metaplastic changes in the tunica vaginalis mesothelium {32619438}.

Overall, the majority of adnexal tumours have indolent clinical behaviour, but a large proportion (23%) of others, including mesothelioma and different types of carcinoma, may be aggressive and have a poor outcome {30676333; 15502808; 32619438}. One of the major concerns with respect to carcinomas in the rete testis or epididymis is the distinction between a primary tumour and metastasis to these structures. Most metastatic tumours are encountered in patients with advanced cancer, but occasionally an adnexal tumour may be the presenting feature of the original neoplasm {12410753}. The commonest sites of origin of primary tumours metastasizing to this region are prostate, lung, skin (melanoma), large intestine, and kidney. Certain features may point towards the possibility of a metastasis, and these include bilaterality, lymphovascular invasion, and frequently an interstitial pattern of growth (between rather than from the rete epithelium) {15502808}.

Another change for this fifth edition is that sertoliform cystadenoma, which was traditionally included under testicular adnexal tumours, is now primarily considered a form of sex cord stromal tumour and as such is included in section Sertoli cell tumour . Although it has been postulated that cells at the junction between the seminiferous tubules and rete retain the ability to differentiate in the direction of Sertoli cells and may give rise to this neoplasm {11202545}, immunohistochemically and morphologically there are no certain grounds for this distinction {29240582}.
