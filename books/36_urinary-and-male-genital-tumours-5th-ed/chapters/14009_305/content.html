14.0.0.9 Tuberous sclerosis

Definition

Tuberous sclerosis (TS) is an autosomal dominant inherited disorder caused by pathogenic variants of TSC1 on 9q or TSC2 on 16p, which is multisystemic with variable clinical manifestations.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

191100 Tuberous sclerosis 1; TSC1

613254 Tuberous sclerosis 2; TSC2

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.2 Tuberous sclerosis

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Tuberous sclerosis 1; tuberous sclerosis 2

Localization

TS can cause lesions in any organ. In the majority of cases the disease involves the CNS (cortical dysplasias, subependymal nodules, and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas), the skin (angiofibroma), the kidney, the eye (retinal hamartomas), the heart (cardiac rhabdomyomas) and the lung (pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis). Angiomyolipoma is the most frequent lesion in the kidney, but renal cysts, polycystic renal disease, and even renal malignancies are also known.

Clinical features

TS is a multisystem disorder that can affect virtually any organ. There is considerable clinical variability due to variable penetrance. The clinical features of TSC1-related and TSC2-related diseases are very similar, but patients with TSC2-related disease show more clinical manifestations {11112665}. TS causes neurological disorders including developmental delay and epilepsy, and additional major features including dermatological, ophthalmological, renal, pulmonary, and cardiac lesions. Disease manifestations develop over the patient’s lifetime, and different features arise at different ages. Neurological disorders and heart lesions, which develop during embryogenesis, are detected in early childhood; skin lesions are present at all ages; pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis (a progressive cystic disease of the lungs) is identified in young female patients. TSC also affects the kidneys differently at different ages: cystic diseases are detected in childhood and infancy, and angiomyolipomas affect patients of all ages, from children to adults. Angiomyolipomas may cause the most severe symptoms because of spontaneous bleeding and aneurysms. In addition, renal malignancies may occur in patients with TS.

Epidemiology

Recent studies estimate an incidence of 10.0–16.7 cases per 100 000 births and a population prevalence of about 1 in 20 000 {2918523; 9605811; 10905251}. TS does not show a significant sex imbalance. The kidneys are affected in 60–80% of patients with TS {9628635; 17003820}. A study of 10-year-old patients with TS showed that 80% had a renal lesion; the majority of these were angiomyolipomas, and fewer were renal cysts {9628635}. Renal angiomyolipomas are more prevalent in female patients {28057044}. Polycystic renal disease has been identified in patients with TSC2 mutations; this is related to the contiguous deletions of the TSC2 and PKD1 genes on chromosome 16, which can cause both diseases {27226234}. In addition to angiomyolipomas, patients can also develop other malignancies {9500218} and renal cell carcinoma (RCC). A higher incidence of RCC and a lower average age of onset has been reported in patients with TS than in the general population {2014599; 8863669; 17003820}.

Etiology

TS was recognized as a genetic disease > 100 years ago; however, the underlying molecular etiology was not known until the identification of TSC1 and TSC2 {8269512; 9242607}. A large fraction (10–25%) of patients with TS have no mutation identifiable by conventional genetic testing {24053982}; therefore, the absence of a defined mutation does not exclude TS but suggests that patients have TS because of mutations in an as-yet-unidentified locus {11112665}.

Pathogenesis

TS is an autosomal dominant disorder; however, two thirds of cases are sporadic, without antecedent family history, reflecting a high spontaneous mutation rate. The genes responsible for the anomalies are TSC1 (located at position 9q34), encoding hamartin, and TSC2 (located at position 16p13.3), encoding tuberin. Different downstream protein cascades related to the pathogenesis of the disease have been identified; among these is the mTOR pathway. Tuberin and hamartin form an intracellular complex that activates GTPase, reducing mTOR stimulation. The study of mTOR pathway upregulation in patients with TS suggests therapeutic strategies {31018109}.

Macroscopic appearance

The majority of patients with TS show renal abnormalities. Angiomyolipomas (part of the PEComa family of tumours) are solid, unencapsulated, fat-containing lesions that may be large, bilateral, and multiple, and which may increase in size and number. The rates of angiomyolipomas that are > 30 mm and growing are higher in female patients than in male patients {28057044}. Renal angiomyolipomas in individuals with TSC1 mutations develop on average at a later age, are smaller in size, and are less likely to be growing than those that occur in the general population {28057044}. The other renal features are multiple renal cysts (22.8%), polycystic renal disease (proven TSC2 or PKD1 mutation; 3.5%), renal malignancy (1.4%), and impaired renal function (non–angiomyolipoma-related; 1.9%). Compared with patients with TSC1 mutations, those with TSC2 mutations had a higher occurrence of multiple renal cysts (13.3% vs 33.6%, respectively) and polycystic renal disease (0% vs 4.7%) {28057044}.

Histopathology

Alongside angiomyolipoma (and other PEComa-family tumours) and renal cysts, a broad spectrum of benign, non-aggressive/indolent, or low-grade tumours have been rarely reported {33526281}. Angiomyolipoma is a mesenchymal tumour characterized by abnormal vessels, smooth muscle cells, and adipose tissue. An uncommon monotypic, epithelioid pattern of angiomyolipoma has also been reported in TS. Epithelioid angiomyolipoma, which morphologically simulates RCC, shows HMB45-positive staining and cytokeratin-negative staining {9500218}. A range of renal carcinomas with different morphologies have been reported. Papillary RCCs with granular and eosinophilic cytoplasm, with a peculiar phenotype characterized by CK7 and CAIX expression, weak/focal CD10 expression, and negative staining for AMACR, have been described. Other tumours with overlapping morphology between renal oncocytoma and chromophobe RCC and RCC-NOS have also been described {24832166}. More recently, eosinophilic and cystic RCCs, RCCs with features similar to those of tumours previously described as renal angiomyoadenomatous tumour, and RCCs with smooth muscle stroma have been shown to be associated with TS {25093518; 29180251; 29941307; 29668487}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Morphologically similar tumours can arise sporadically. Therefore, the diagnosis of TS should be based on complex clinicopathological correlation. Most (but not all) patients with TS will have demonstrable germline mutations in either TSC1 or TSC2 {33041968}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: the 2012 International Tuberous Sclerosis Complex Consensus Group recommended that TS should be diagnosed by identifying a pathogenic mutation in TSC1 or TSC2, or two major clinical features, or one major feature with at least two minor features {28053551; 33041968; 24053982}; the major clinical features are hypomelanotic macules, angiofibromas or fibrous cephalic plaque, ungual fibromas, shagreen patch, multiple retinal hamartomas, cortical dysplasias, subependymal nodules or giant cell astrocytoma, cardiac rhabdomyoma, lymphangioleiomyomatosis, and (epithelioid) angiomyolipomas; the minor clinical features are confetti skin lesions, dental enamel pits, intraoral fibromas, retinal achromic patch, multiple renal cysts, and non-renal hamartomas {33041968; 30232410; 24053982}.

Desirable: one major feature or at least two minor features suggest possible TS {24053982}.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The main causes of death in TS in adults are renal disease (especially from large angiomyolipomas for spontaneous life-threatening bleeding), brain tumours, epilepsy, and pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis. With advances in medical care, death from renal disease in TS is much less likely than it was previously; however, it still represents an important cause of death for patients with TS. TS-associated renal neoplasia has been highlighted as having an indolent nature {33526281}. The rates of angiomyolipoma-related complications were higher in female patients than in male patients. In female patients, angiomyolipomas are also associated with the development of pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis. These associations may be related to ER and PR in the tumours {9688188}, although the modality of hormonal modulation of angiomyolipoma growth and pulmonary disease is unknown. The median age of death is 33 years, and death in childhood is extremely rare {27935023; 24053982}. The conventional therapy in patients with angiomyolipomas is surgical treatment. mTOR inhibitors have cytostatic effects and require long-term treatment. Preventive transarterial embolization may reduce the life-threatening bleeding.
