3.1.4.1 Oncocytoma of the kidney

Definition

Oncocytoma is a benign neoplasm with a solid, nested, or cystic architecture composed of eosinophilic cells, reflecting the presence of numerous mitochondria.

ICD-O coding

8290/0 Oncocytoma

ICD-11 coding

2F35 & XH9Z86 Benign neoplasm of urinary organs & Oxyphilic adenoma

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Oncocytomas are localized in the renal cortex. Large tumours may be seen in contact with or protruding into the paranephric fat or expanding into the medulla, renal sinus, or large renal veins.

Clinical features

The majority of oncocytomas are asymptomatic and detected on radiological investigations for unrelated symptoms. Rare patients present with haematuria, flank pain, dysuria, weight loss, and a palpable abdominal mass. No radiological feature is specific for oncocytoma. The diagnosis may be suggested by the presence of a stellate scar visualized on CT, ultrasound, or MRI {19348848}.

Epidemiology

Oncocytoma accounts for approximately 6–9% of renal tumours. The patient age range is broad (32–89 years), with a mean of 62 years. The M:F ratio is 2:1 {8990136; 16287444; 9255250; 21166703}.

Etiology

The etiology of oncocytoma is unknown. Rare hereditary cases have been described, and it can occur in Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome. Rarely, multiple oncocytomas can occur (renal oncocytosis) {10478670; 20305535}.

Pathogenesis

Oncocytomas show recurrent chromosomal losses (1, 14, 21, X, Y). A subgroup shows CCND1 rearrangements at the 11q13 locus {26655904; 31112346; 19386349}. Mutations of mitochondrial genes have also been found in oncocytoma {26655904}. Many cases have a normal karyotype {15993267}. Renal oncocytoma is believed to arise from the intercalated cells of renal distal tubules.

Macroscopic appearance

Oncocytomas are usually well-circumscribed unencapsulated tumours with a mahogany-brown to yellow-tan colour. A central stellate or peripheral scar is commonly present. Grossly visible haemorrhages may be present. Necrosis is extremely rare. Uncommon features are a cystic cut surface and growth extension into the paranephric fat or the large veins of the renal sinus. The average size is 44 mm {8990136; 1373578}.

Histopathology

Oncocytomas are composed predominantly of round to polygonal cells that have densely granular eosinophilic cytoplasm and round uniform nuclei with smoothly dispersed chromatin and a central small nucleolus, embedded in a hypocellular hyalinized or myxoid stroma {8990136; 9255250; 21166703}. Cytoplasmic clearing is rare. Occasionally there are isolated cells or groups of cells with degenerative-appearing hyperchromasia and pleomorphism. The tumours grow in solid nests, but microcysts and macrocysts or tubules are common. Although papillary architecture is absent, small focal papillary protrusions can be seen. Rarely, foci of dystrophic calcification or even ossification may be present. Some tumours are composed exclusively of haemorrhage-filled cysts and tubules. For tumours with this growth pattern, the term “telangiectatic oncocytoma” was proposed {23765540}. Other tumours show small cells with scarce cytoplasm (oncoblasts), and rare cases composed almost exclusively of oncoblasts have been described; in such cases, pseudorosette formation of small cells packed around hyaline material may be present {11584318; 21652056}. Mitosis is exceptionally rare and atypical mitoses are absent. Foci of coagulative necrosis are also absent {8990136; 9255250}. Fat or vascular invasion has no influence on the benign nature of oncocytomas.

Rarely, the kidney can be affected by numerous oncocytomas or oncocytic lesions. This is often associated with clusters of oncocytic cells within the interstitium between normal nephrons, small cysts lined by oncocytic cells, and oncocytic changes in non-neoplastic tubules (renal oncocytosis). In renal oncocytosis, tumours with hybrid oncocytic morphology and chromophobe renal cell carcinomas (ChRCCs) can also be found. Most of these cases are now believed to be manifestations of Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome.

The immunohistochemical profile of oncocytoma is variable, typically with positivity for KIT (CD117), S100A, E-cadherin, Ksp-cadherin, and pancytokeratin. CK7 labelling is usually negative, although single-cell positivity may be present {28315424}. Vimentin is generally negative, but focal strong positivity in oncocytes and positivity around the central scar can be observed {18081436; 29090117; 33183792}. Hale colloidal iron staining is negative, or it is only positive in the luminal aspects of oncocytic cells.

Differential diagnosis includes eosinophilic ChRCC and SDHB-deficient renal cell carcinoma. A definite diagnosis of oncocytoma cannot be made on a core biopsy because ChRCC can show intratumoural heterogeneity, with areas very similar to oncocytoma {28315424; 33183792}.

Cytology

The cytological appearance may raise the suspicion of an oncocytic lesion, but it is not diagnostic, and ChRCC cannot be excluded.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome, germline mutations in FLCN occur.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: sharply demarcated tumour with oncocytic cells forming solid nests, tubules, or microcysts; absence of raisinoid nuclei and perinuclear haloes.

Desirable: negativity for CK7, or positivity for CK7 only in single cells.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Oncocytomas are benign tumours.
