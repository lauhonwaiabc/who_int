3.1.2.2 Papillary renal cell carcinoma

Definition

Papillary renal cell carcinoma (PRCC) is a malignant neoplasm, usually circumscribed and characterized by papillary or tubulopapillary architecture, without specific features of other renal cell carcinomas (RCCs) with papillary architecture.

ICD-O coding

8260/3 Papillary renal cell carcinoma

ICD-11 coding

2C90.0 & XH1D07 Renal cell carcinoma of kidney, except renal pelvis & Papillary renal cell carcinoma

Related terminology

Not recommended: tubulopapillary renal cell carcinoma; renal papillary adenocarcinoma; chromophil renal cell carcinoma.

Subtype(s)

Subclassification into type 1 and type 2 is no longer recommended.

Localization

PRCC develops in the renal cortex of either kidney and can be multifocal or bilateral.

Clinical features

Many tumours are asymptomatic and identified incidentally on imaging studies {15964127; 11859199} PRCCs may be multifocal or bilateral, especially in the setting of chronic renal disease. The presence of innumerable papillary tumours with usually low-grade, basophilic morphology raises consideration of hereditary papillary renal carcinoma; however, multiple tumours occur in the absence of this syndrome.

Epidemiology

PRCC is the second most common type of renal cell carcinoma, accounting for 13–20% of renal epithelial tumours {11859199; 30507616; 24025519; 26052049}. Most PRCCs occur in adults, but occurrence in childhood is possible.

Etiology

PRCC does not have definite specific etiological factors that differ from those of other subtypes of kidney cancer; however, this tumour type is more common in chronic renal disease and acquired cystic disease {9503505; 16434887}. A subset of tumours occur in the setting of hereditary papillary renal carcinoma with a germline mutation of MET {7853572; 8308957; 16434318; 15371818; 26559379}.

Pathogenesis

PRCCs are molecularly heterogeneous, ranging from low-grade to high-grade tumours {25790038}. PRCCs commonly have gains of chromosomes 7 and 17, and loss of the Y chromosome {29454759; 26536169}. MET alterations are more common in the low-grade tumours {26536169; 10433944} and appear to be enriched in tumours with the recently described biphasic pattern {32770124}. High-grade PRCCs can have aberrations in CDKN2A and in MYC pathway and NRF2/ARE pathway genes {26536169; 17409424; 23365135}. Chromatin modifier pathways are commonly implicated in PRCCs {26536169}.

Macroscopic appearance

Usually, PRCCs are well circumscribed, with or without a fibrous capsule, and they may be bilateral and/or multifocal. The gross cut surface may be yellow, red-brown, or variegated, depending on the various components, such as haemorrhage, necrosis, foamy macrophages, cholesterol, or haemosiderin. The consistency may be granular, alluding to the papillary structures, or friable, with variable central necrosis or cystic degeneration. High-grade PRCCs are more often solitary and may have a more white and fibrous appearance {30507616; 31765248; 15994935}.

Histopathology

Overall, the classic pattern of PRCC (historically in the type 1 category) has papillary and tubular structures lined by cuboidal cells with scant or lightly basophilic cytoplasm, imparting an overall basophilic appearance at low magnification. Ancillary features that are often present but not required for diagnosis include foamy histiocytes and psammoma bodies {9199639}. The cytoplasm can vary in colour, having a clear or vacuolated appearance {18779729; 21074210} or being voluminous and eosinophilic, or it may have pigmentation due to haemosiderin {11169884; 2065568}.

Other morphological patterns that may be deceptive include predominant solid growth obscuring the papillary architecture {27209513}, predominant vacuolated cells mimicking clear cell RCC (CCRCC) {18779729; 21074210}, a biphasic pattern with squamoid cells in the centre of glandular lumina {29119638; 26999503}, or brisk inflammation mimicking Warthin tumour of the salivary gland {28325361}. Another pattern of PRCC is characterized by thinly branching papillae and low-grade oncocytic tumour cells with nuclei aligned towards the apex of the cell. Tumours with this distinctive pattern have been recently recognized as papillary neoplasms with reverse polarity. They appear to be consistently positive for GATA3 and negative for vimentin, with labelling for AMACR being less extensive {17949780; 31534204; 31135486; 32403965; 31997427; 31953522; 28984673}. CAIX (CA9) labelling is typically negative or focal, unlike in CCRCC. These tumours have recurrent mutations of KRAS, differing from typical PRCCs {28984673; 31534204; 31135486; 32403965; 31997427; 31953522}.

Immunohistochemistry typically shows diffuse strong labelling for AMACR, regardless of pattern. CK7 immunohistochemistry typically shows substantial positivity, but it is decreased or absent in eosinophilic tumours {9195569}. Other markers that are often positive include vimentin and CD10. In high-grade tumours, the differential diagnosis includes fumarate hydratase–deficient RCC, eosinophilic solid and cystic RCC, tubulocystic RCC, collecting duct RCC, or MiT family gene–rearranged RCCs. In these tumours, immunohistochemical surrogates or molecular techniques should be applied {26536169}.

The WHO Classification of Tumours / International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/ISUP) histological grading of renal cell tumour types {30565310; 24025520; 28718911} has been validated for PRCC {28718911}. It is based on nucleolar prominence/eosinophilia for grades 1–3 and prominent nuclear pleomorphism with tumour giant cells and sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation for grade 4 {24025520; 21716085}.

Cytology

Cytopathological features of PRCC include the presence of papillary structures with vascular cores, small cells with uniform nuclei, intracellular haemosiderin (both in tumour cells and in histiocytes), psammoma bodies, and abundant foamy macrophages. There may be a smaller number of single cells than in CCRCC {2065577; 3473868; 11169884; 2065568}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Currently there are limited molecular markers that can be used to confirm the diagnosis of PRCC. These include trisomy and tetrasomy of chromosomes 7 and 17 and loss of the Y chromosome {25790038; 29454759}. MET gene mutations are more frequent in low-grade PRCC tumours, if present {26536169; 10433944}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: variable proportions of papillary and tubular architecture lined by cuboidal to columnar cells, lacking extreme morphological pattern heterogeneity (which would suggest fumarate hydratase–deficient RCC) or the classic morphology of CCRCC.

Desirable: foamy histiocytes; psammoma bodies; diffuse strong immunohistochemical labelling for AMACR; normal positive (intact/retained) fumarate hydratase labelling.

Staging

Staging follows the same Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging systems as used for other subtypes of RCC. Although PRCC may grow into blood vessels or the renal sinus, this proclivity is less dramatic than in cases of CCRCC and tumours may reach a large size without involving these structures {29897139}.

Prognosis and prediction

PRCC is generally considered to have more favourable behaviour than other subtypes such as CCRCC or unclassified RCC {11859199}. Grading can be performed using the system of nucleolar prominence as for CCRCC {24025520; 30565310}.
