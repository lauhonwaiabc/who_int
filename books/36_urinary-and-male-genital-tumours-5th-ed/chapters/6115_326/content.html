6.1.1.5 Treatment-related neuroendocrine prostatic carcinoma

Definition

Treatment-related neuroendocrine prostatic carcinomas (t-NEPCs) are tumours demonstrating complete neuroendocrine differentiation or partial neuroendocrine differentiation with adenocarcinoma after androgen-deprivation therapy.

ICD-O coding

8574/3 Adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation

ICD-11 coding

2C82.Y & XH0U20 Other specified malignant neoplasms of prostate & Neuroendocrine carcinoma, NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Prostate or metastatic site

Clinical features

These tumours have clinical features similar or identical to those described for small cell neuroendocrine carcinomas (SCNECs) and large cell neuroendocrine carcinomas (LCNECs) – see section Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and section Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma .

Epidemiology

Primary SCNECs account for < 1% of prostate cancers, but most prostatic adenocarcinomas with a high Gleason grade show at least focal neuroendocrine differentiation. t-NEPCs represent 10–15% of castration-resistant prostate adenocarcinomas {26124369; 22586648}.

Etiology

t-NEPC arises by the transdifferentiation of a castration-resistant prostate cancer after potent androgen-deprivation therapy {29396873; 24705311}. Emerging evidence suggests that lineage plasticity plays an important role in the progression of advanced prostate cancer during a course of treatment with AR signalling inhibitors like enzalutamide or abiraterone {31061129; 26855148}. One form of lineage plasticity that has been observed is characterized by AR indifference and progression to SCNEC, which shows a distinct histomorphology and expresses neural-like markers {28586335; 33217316}. Unlike the more commonly recognized mechanisms of AR signalling inhibitor resistance due to AR mutations or amplification, Prostatic SCNEC no longer responds to AR-targeting therapy and the mean survival time is 12 months {31294484}.

Pathogenesis

There is mounting evidence supporting the notion that epigenetic events play a role in the mechanism of the transdifferentiation of prostate cancer to an AR-indifferent state under specific genomic conditions, including (but not limited to) losses of TP53, RB1, and PTEN {30287662}. Emerging evidence from genetically engineered mouse models of prostate cancer with combined Trp53 and Pten loss demonstrates that neuroendocrine tumour (NET) cells can directly arise from pre-existing luminal adenocarcinoma cells and not from a second, independent population of neuroendocrine cells {28411207}). These and other data suggest that lineage plasticity is driven by epigenetic changes that arise in a specific genomic context {28059768; 28481359}. In patient cohorts, SCNECs and t-NEPCs are characterized by an overexpression of several epigenetic regulators (e.g. EZH2) and a specific DNA methylation profile {26855148; 29460922; 29921838}. Insights into what additional epigenetic factors could provoke multipotent prostate cancer cells to transdifferentiate into t-NEPC are still emerging. MYCN and AURKA overexpression are observed in t-NEPC and may represent an opportunity for therapy {22389870}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of mixed neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) or those with treatment-related changes is not specific, and it is not different from that of other high-grade NECs, including SCNEC and LCNEC (see section Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, and section Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma).

Histopathology

The great majority of prostatic adenocarcinomas show some degree of neuroendocrine differentiation if tested for by neuroendocrine markers {31306859}. There is insufficient evidence for a prognostic or therapeutic role, and therefore routine immunohistochemistry of chromogranin A or synaptophysin in all prostate biopsies is not necessary {24724054; 26885643; 25450825; 24812138}. A subgroup of prostatic adenocarcinomas with Paneth cell–like neuroendocrine differentiation has also been described. Paneth-like cells have relatively small nuclei that lack prominent nucleoli, but they have typical eosinophilic cytoplasmic granularity. They show neuroendocrine differentiation by immunohistochemistry. The single-cell infiltrative pattern of this Paneth cell–like component should not upgrade the Gleason score {25277321; 26885643}.

Primary and posttreatment SCNEC and LCNEC are frequently admixed with areas of typical prostatic adenocarcinoma. About 50% of tumours with an SCNEC component represent mixed NECs with an adenocarcinoma component. The transition between both components is usually abrupt. The SCNEC component is not taken into account for Gleason grading, but overall such tumours have a Gleason score of ≥ 8 {18162772}. The SCNEC component shows nuclear p53 staining in most cases, thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1) positivity in about half of cases, and AR positivity in a subset; PSA and PAP are typically negative in the SCNEC component {24812138}.

t-NEPC is probably a separate clinical entity that has a spectrum of histological features including pure neuroendocrine morphology (most commonly small cell carcinoma) and mixed tumours with a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma component {23358695}. The histology and the immunohistochemical staining pattern of t-NEPC are similar to those of primary prostatic SCNEC. t-NEPC can show pronounced heterogeneity, ranging from adenocarcinoma to small-cell areas within the same metastatic deposit.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: prostatic carcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation, pure or admixed with generally high-grade adenocarcinoma; confirmatory immunohistochemical stains (synaptophysin, chromogranin); history of antiandrogen therapy.

Staging

The eighth-edition Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging system is used.

Prognosis and prediction

In more than half of the patients in whom it develops, t-NEPC develops within 24 months of androgen-deprivation therapy and the median survival time after transdifferentiation to t-NEPC is only 7 months {25225419}. Pure small cell carcinoma is associated with a worse overall survival than tumours with a mixed histology (8.9 months from t-NEPC diagnosis vs 26.1 months, respectively; P < 0.001) {31525487}.
