14.0.0.6 Succinate dehydrogenase-deficient tumour syndromes

Definition

Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-deficient tumour syndromes are a group of syndromes characterized by SDH-deficient neoplasia, usually associated with germline mutations in the SDH genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SDHAF2) or with SDHC epimutation.

Subtype(s)

See #21038Table 14.02 .

Localization

SDH mutations are more commonly associated with paragangliomas than with phaeochromocytomas. Therefore, in the genitourinary tract, paragangliomas of the urinary bladder or those arising in the periaortic sympathetic chain are more commonly SDH-deficient than are adrenal phaeochromocytomas. There is an inconsistent phenotype–genotype correlation between the individual SDH subunit that is mutated/epimutated and the most common location of the syndromic paragangliomas (see #21038Table 14.02, and #21039Table 14.03). For example, SDHB mutations are more common in intra-abdominal extra-adrenal paragangliomas, and SDHD mutations are more common in head and neck paragangliomas {29239034}. With one exception, all SDH-deficient gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GISTs) reported to date have arisen in the stomach {20305538; 21997692; 28324028}.

Clinical features

The SDH-deficient tumour syndromes are characterized by SDH-deficient phaeochromocytoma–paraganglioma, GIST, renal carcinoma, pituitary adenoma / pituitary neuroendocrine tumour (PitNET), and pulmonary chondroma {22544211; 29239034; 24625421; 20305538; 31789631; 32251007}. There are strong but inconsistent genotype–phenotype correlations so that different genes are more commonly associated with certain tumour combinations (see #21038Table 14.02, p. XXX, and #21039Table 14.03). For example, pulmonary chondromas are common in Carney triad but very rare in the other SDH-deficient syndromes, renal carcinomas are very rare in cases of SDHD mutation, and SDHA mutations are most strongly associated with SDH-deficient GIST.

Epidemiology

As many as 40% of paragangliomas and phaeochromocytomas in adults are hereditary {29239044} and close to half of all syndromic cases (more in childhood) will be associated with one of the SDH deficiency syndromes {22544211; 29239034; 21969497}.

SDH-deficient GISTs account for 3% of all GISTs in an unselected adult population, but they account for the great majority of GISTs in children {20305538; 21997692}. SDH-deficient pituitary adenomas / PitNETs are rare (< 0.3% of unselected tumours) {24625421}. Pulmonary chondromas are typically found in young women and are very rare outside the setting of Carney triad {18043038; 31236950; 19723753}.

Etiology

In contrast with many other hereditary syndromes, biallelic inactivation/mutation of the SDH genes in neoplasia, although described, is very unusual in the absence of germline mutation (or more rarely postzygotic epimutation or mosaicism) of one of the SDH genes {29239034}.

Pathogenesis

Biallelic inactivation of any one of the SDH genes leads to dysfunction of the SDH complex, a key respiratory enzyme on the inner mitochondrial membrane that links the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain {29239034; 22544211}. The subsequent accumulation of oncometabolites causes tumorigenesis by activating the pseudohypoxic pathway {32234487}.

Macroscopic appearance

Although multifocality is an important clue, the phaeochromocytomas, paragangliomas, pituitary adenomas / PitNETs, and renal cell carcinomas associated with SDH mutation grossly resemble their sporadic counterparts. SDH-deficient GISTs are commonly multilobulated or plexiform and more frequently show lymph node metastases {20305538; 21997692; 21753704}.

Histopathology

Loss of expression of SDHB (termed SDH deficiency) occurs whenever there is biallelic inactivation of any gene encoding a component of the SDH complex (i.e. SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, or SDHAF2) {22544211; 29239034; 24625421; 20305538; 31789631; 32251007}. Because somatic-only mutation of both alleles of an SDH gene is extremely rare {17634472}, SDH deficiency is usually associated with one of the SDH-deficient tumour syndromes. Tumours with biallelic inactivation of SDHA show SDHA loss in addition to SDHB loss {21752896; 23060355}. Normal SDHA and SDHB immunostaining is granular and cytoplasmic (mitochondrial). In SDH-deficient tumours, non-neoplastic cells such as fibroblasts and endothelial cells retain granular expression and act as internal positive controls. In some SDHD-related tumours, a cytoplasmic hue may be retained. When weak diffuse expression contrasts with strong granularity in internal positive controls the tumour should be considered SDH-deficient {19576851; 29239034}.

SDH-deficient renal cell carcinomas are now classified as a distinct entity (see section Succinate dehydrogenase–deficient renal cell carcinoma). Variant morphologies appear to be more frequent in renal carcinomas associated with SDHA mutation than those associated with SDHB or SDHC mutation {33664427}. Many SDH-deficient paragangliomas are characterized by well-formed, almost circular, nests of epithelioid cells, with clear or vacuolated cytoplasm {31789631}. However, morphology is inconsistent and does not replace SDHB immunohistochemistry, which is recommended in all phaeochromocytomas and paragangliomas. There are several morphological, clinical, and molecular clues to the identification of SDH-deficient GISTs (see #21040Table 14.04), but these can be subtle, and a low threshold for SDHB immunohistochemistry is recommended in any gastric GIST {20305538; 21997692; 21753704}. Experience with SDH-deficient pituitary adenomas / PitNETs is limited. According to some reports, they may be larger and more frequently prolactin-producing {25695889; 24625421; 24096523; 23633203; 29239034}, but suggestions of morphological clues have not been confirmed {25695889; 24625421; 23633203; 29239034}. SDH-deficient pulmonary chondromas lack atypia and frequently show calcification. In contrast to hamartomas, they do not show entrapped respiratory epithelium between the lobules of cartilage, or other mesenchymal elements (fat, smooth muscle, myxoid spindle cells). Although SDHB immunohistochemistry may assist in the diagnosis of syndromic chondromas, it is less reliable in this setting {31236950; 29239034}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of any SDH-deficient neoplasm is an indication for genetic counselling/testing. Immunohistochemistry for SDHA and SDHB can be useful to assess the pathogenicity of genetic variants of uncertain significance. Germline pathogenic variants in SDHA are both relatively common (estimated to occur in up to 0.3% of the population) {21752896} and demonstrate an extremely low lifetime penetrance (recently estimated as 1.7%) {30201732}. Therefore, the clinical significance of SDHA mutation, particularly if identified by large panel sequencing in the absence of SDH-deficient neoplasia, should be carefully considered and interpreted in the clinical context {21752896; 23060355}.

The term “Carney triad” is appropriate when no germline SDH mutation is identified in an individual with syndromic, but not hereditary, disease. Given that somatic biallelic mutation in the SDH genes is rare, it is reasonable to assign a presumptive diagnosis of Carney triad to any patient with an SDH-deficient neoplasm and no proven germline SDH mutation, unless biallelic somatic-only mutation has been demonstrated. Current evidence suggests that Carney triad is usually associated with somatic mosaic epimutation (promoter hypermethylation) of SDHC {24859990; 25540324; 33624983}, which can only be identified by specific DNA methylation assays that are not widely available. However, it should be noted that mosaicism for constitutional SDH mutations also occurs and might not be detected by sequencing when the variant allelic ratio is low {31046099}. Some patients previously given the clinical diagnosis of Carney triad on the basis of syndromic SDH-deficient neoplasia in the absence of identifiable mutation may carry deep intronic pathogenic variants that were previously unrecognized {33195952}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a confirmed pathogenic variant in one of the SDH genes, or the presence of more than one SDH-deficient neoplasm in an individual or family.

Note: Although somatic-only mutations do occur, they are extremely rare and becoming more uncommon with improvements in molecular techniques and increased awareness of epimutation of SDHC and somatic mosaicism {31046099; 33195952}; therefore, the identification of even a single SDH-deficient neoplasm is very suggestive of syndromic disease.

Staging

The individual tumours are staged according to the relevant Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging schemes.

Prognosis and prediction

In view of the strongly syndromic nature of SDH-deficient neoplasia, surveillance and follow-up for other SDH-deficient neoplasms is indicated even in cases where no germline mutation has been identified {22544211; 29239034}.
