14.0.0.12 Carney complex

Definition

Carney complex (CNC) is a rare autosomal dominant syndrome characterized by multifocal benign neoplasms mainly affecting the endocrine system, gonads, heart, and skin.

ICD-O coding

MIM numbering

160980 Carney complex, type 1; CNC1

ICD-11 coding

5A70.Y Carney complex

Related terminology

Acceptable: LAMB syndrome (lentigines, atrial myxoma, mucocutaneous myoma, and blue naevus); NAME syndrome (naevi, atrial myxoma, myxoid neurofibromas, and ephelides); Carney syndrome; myxoma syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The syndrome affects multiple organ systems (including the skin, heart, gonads, and endocrine system) with multiple, usually benign, neoplasms {4010501}. Overactivity of endocrine organs, especially Cushing syndrome, may lead to other widespread systemic changes {11549623}.

Clinical features

Although CNC may be diagnosed in infancy, it most commonly manifests at puberty with increasing variably pigmented lentigines, most commonly at the vermilion border of the lips, conjunctiva, and inner and outer canthi. Genital epithelia may also be affected. The constellation of benign neoplasms and hyperplasias includes cardiac tumours, large cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumours (LCCSCTs), follicular adenomas of the thyroid, ductal adenomas of the breast, blue naevi, pituitary adenomas (which may be functional), and osteochondromyxomas. Endocrinopathy may result from the pituitary adenomas, which may cause acromegaly, or primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease {27875378}, which may result in Cushing syndrome. Reported malignant tumours include follicular carcinomas of the thyroid and malignant melanotic nerve sheath tumours. LCCSCTs, which are seen only very rarely outside the context of CNC, occur in approximately one third of boys with CNC (arising in the first decade of life) and are seen virtually universally in men with CNC.

Epidemiology

CNC is a relatively rare syndrome, with < 1000 affected individuals having been identified. No ethnic or geographical predisposition has been described.

Etiology

One third of patients appear to acquire a new mutation during development and the remainder have an affected parent. CNC has an autosomal dominant mode of transmission {11549623}.

Pathogenesis

Most cases of CNC are caused by mutations in PRKAR1A, a gene encoding a regulatory subunit of PKA (cAMP-dependent protein kinase) involved in cAMP signalling {10973256}. Mutation causes constitutive activation and increased cell proliferation, leading to a constellation of hyperplasias and neoplasias.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

LCCSCT is described elsewhere in this volume (see section Large cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumour), and other tumours are delineated within the relevant volumes of the WHO Classification of Tumours series.

Cytology

Cytology is not relevant for most of the neoplasms, but it may be used in the diagnosis of thyroid nodules.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The majority of patients possess inactivating mutations in the PRKAR1A gene, located at 17q24.2, which encodes protein kinase cAMP-dependent type I regulatory subunit alpha (PRKAR1A) {15371594}. A subset of patients show mutations in a gene located at 2p16, the function of which is unknown {12676898}. Mutation in PRKAR1A can be detected by DNA sequencing or by targeted deletion/duplication analysis {24170103}. Immunochemistry for PRKAR1A in appropriate neoplasms is possible, and demonstrated loss may be followed up with mutation testing {24618615}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: two major criteria or a combination of one major criterion and one supplemental criterion (see #32024Box 14.01); some other conditions may raise suspicions for CNC.

Staging

LCCSCTs may be malignant and spread locally into the testicular appendages and distantly via the retroperitoneal lymph nodes {9351565}.

Prognosis and prediction

The penetrance of a pathogenic variant in PRKAR1A is nearly 100% in patients aged ≥ 50 years, so all patients need surveillance. Morbidity and mortality are mostly due to the non-gonadal manifestations, particularly cardiac myxomas and functional endocrinopathies. Echocardiographic surveillance for cardiac myxomas, annual testicular examinations, and thyroid examinations should be performed after puberty. Assessments of urinary free cortisol and serum IGF1 levels (to assess acromegaly) can also be undertaken {22219403}.
