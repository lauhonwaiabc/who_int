3.1.1.1 Clear cell renal cell carcinoma

Definition

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (CCRCC) is a morphologically heterogeneous malignant tumour derived from renal tubular epithelial cells. The neoplastic cells have predominantly clear and occasionally eosinophilic cytoplasm accompanied by an abundant network of blood vessels and associated with biallelic VHL inactivation.

Localization

CCRCCs are usually solitary within the renal cortex, occurring equally in either kidney. Less than 5% of CCRCCs are multifocal and/or bilateral, and together with an early age of onset this suggests a hereditary cancer syndrome such as von Hippel–Lindau syndrome {9751329}.

Clinical features

Clinically symptomatic renal carcinomas can cause haematuria and flank pain, with weight loss and fever in the late stages. Asymptomatic tumours are detected radiologically with ultrasound, CT, and MRI.

Because CCRCCs have a propensity to metastasize haematogenously, they may manifest at metastatic sites even before the primary renal tumour is discovered. Haematogenous spread through renal sinus veins, renal veins, and venae cavae leads to pulmonary metastases {15577678; 17170742; 18076920}. Tumour extension into lumbar veins allows spread to the low-pressure paravertebral venous plexus that connects to dural venous sinuses superiorly and pelvic veins inferiorly, thus resulting in metastases to the CNS, head and neck, and bone. These diverse metastatic pathways enable CCRCC to metastasize to unusual sites {3204107; 24327013}, such as the pancreas and gall bladder.

Epidemiology

CCRCC is the commonest type of sporadic RCC in adults, accounting for 60–75% of all RCCs {31374051}. Most CCRCCs develop in patients aged ≥ 60 years (median: 62 years). The overall incidence rate is estimated to be 3.59 cases per 100 000 person-years, and incidence is significantly higher in men than in women (1.94:1), and higher in White people than in Black people {30243799; 21741761; 30620402}.

Etiology

Most CCRCCs are sporadic. Rarely, they may be observed in various familial settings. Tumours associated with germline mutations of VHL (in von Hippel–Lindau syndrome) manifest as multiple CCRCCs and renal cysts, and at a younger age than sporadic CCRCCs {8493574; 19470766}. CCRCCs have been reported in various other familial cancer syndromes, such as constitutive chromosome 3 translocations, BAP1 tumour predisposition syndrome, Cowden syndrome, Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis {26559379; 31326218; 23764071; 23752087}, although the tumours in some of these syndromes may now be reclassified as other emerging entities rather than CCRCC.

A number of risk factors have been reported, including smoking, obesity, long-term dialysis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and exposure to aristolochic acid, asbestos, and trichloroethylene {15523697; 26149669; 30243799; 30605491; 25662835; 20448658; 25351205}.

Pathogenesis

Loss of the short arm of chromosome 3 is a ubiquitous somatic event, accompanied by the inactivation of the remaining VHL gene by mutation or methylation (in > 90% of cases) {18676741; 2885753; 23792563; 29617669; 29656891}. The VHL gene product (VHL protein) is a component of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, a key regulator of the cellular response to hypoxia. The E3 ubiquitin ligase complex promotes the degradation of its substrates including HIF1α. Loss of VHL results in the accumulation of HIF1α, leading to the constitutive expression of HIF target genes {33020645; 24821879}. Mutations in genes encoding other members of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex – such as ELOC (TCEB1) and CUL2 – occur rarely, and they are mutually exclusive with VHL mutations. Although there are differences between tumours with mutations in ELOC (TCEB1) and those with mutations in VHL, dysregulation of the VHL pathway may explain their overlapping morphology and immunohistochemical profile {25676555}.

Loss of chromosome arm 3p results in the simultaneous loss of three other tumour suppressor genes that are frequently mutated in CCRCC: PBRM1 (in ~50% of cases), SETD2 (in ~20%), and BAP1 (in ~15%) {23792563; 23620406; 23832661}. PBRM1 and SETD2 mutations frequently coexist {23832661; 26516698}. In contrast, PBRM1 and BAP1 mutations tend to be mutually exclusive and the tumours are phenotypically different {22683710; 23333114; 28473526}. Additional driver mutations and somatic copy-number alterations include (1) inactivating mutations in histone-modifying genes (KDM5C and KDM6A), (2) activating mutations in the mTOR pathway genes (TSC1, TSC2, MTOR, PIK3CA, and PTEN), (3) loss of TP53, and (4) losses of chromosomes 14 and 9 {23792563; 29656894; 29656895; 29617669}.

Varying driver gene alterations underpin CCRCC evolution and biology {31859241; 29656894}. CCRCCs with VHL loss as the only driver event are indolent, and they rarely metastasize. Like VHL, PBRM1 is often mutated early during tumour development {29656894}. PBRM1-mutated tumours with subsequent SETD2 mutations, driver somatic copy-number alterations, or PI3K pathway alterations have a more attenuated disease course {29656895; 23832661; 26516698}. In contrast, CCRCCs with BAP1 mutations or multiple driver mutations have aggressive phenotypes with early dissemination of disease. In addition, these molecular changes may influence metastatic organ tropism {29656895; 34178206}.

Recent large-scale gene expression analyses of metastatic CCRCCs identified unique molecular subsets with distinct drug-response characteristics {33157048; 33157049}. CCRCCs with a strong angiogenic gene signature had a favourable response to antiangiogenic therapies and were enriched for PBRM1 loss {33157048; 31859241}. In contrast, CCRCCs with an inflamed microenvironment were associated with the highest expression of PDL1, preferential responsiveness to regimes containing immune checkpoint inhibitors, and the highest rates of sarcomatoid change and BAP1 mutations {33157048; 29884728; 33157049}.

Macroscopic appearance

The size of CCRCCs is variable, ranging from 12 to 140 mm (mean: 57 mm) {11857297}, with a more recent study reporting a range of 23–170 mm (mean: 63 mm) {25550584}. An increasing use of imaging has increased the frequency of detection of small tumours. The tumour usually has a round or oval, pushing, expansile appearance, protruding from the renal cortex and sometimes displaying a pseudocapsule. The cut surface is usually golden-yellow with frequent haemorrhage giving a variegated reddish-yellow colour. Fibrosis and calcifications may be present. In low-grade tumours, cystic change is frequent. High-grade CCRCCs may have whitish, firm areas with frequent necrosis, often accompanied by extension into the renal vein and the perirenal and/or renal sinus fat.

Histopathology

The classic histological appearance of CCRCC is a nested, tubular, or alveolar growth pattern, composed of cells with optically clear cytoplasm; however, morphological variations are common, including cysts, haemorrhage, or a degenerative, scar-like component. There is a complex vascular network with capillaries surrounding essentially every nest of tumour cells, which can be a diagnostic clue to discriminating it from other neoplasms like chromophobe RCC. Rarely, this may lead to mimicry of a vascular neoplasm if the epithelial component is less conspicuous {23368867}. With increasing grade, it is not unusual for tumours to contain eosinophilic cells or hyaline globules, which may be misleading if cells with clear cytoplasm are not predominant, especially in a biopsy sample. Other morphological variations that may be deceptive include branched glandular structures or nuclear alignment, resembling clear cell papillary RCC {26457355; 26752401}. High-grade tumours sometimes contain bizarre, giant multinucleated tumour cells {24499686}. Sarcomatoid or rhabdoid features may be found in high-grade tumours, although these are not unique to this tumour type {11224597}.

Histological grading according to the WHO Classification of Tumours / International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/ISUP) histological grading of renal cell tumour types {30565310; 24025520; 28718911} (#21185Table 2.04) has been shown to significantly predict cancer-specific survival {28718911}. Grading is based on nucleolar prominence/eosinophilia for grades 1–3, and on prominent nuclear pleomorphism with tumour giant cells or sarcomatoid or rhabdoid differentiation for grade 4 {24025520; 21716085}. As CCRCC commonly displays grade heterogeneity, the current recommendation is that grading should be based on the highest grade represented by at least one high-power field {24025520; 30987774}, the area of which has not been specifically defined, although it is approximately 0.23 mm2 in most modern microscopes {34079071}. A recent study has shown that although cancers with overall grade 4 morphology had a significantly worse outcome than grade 3 cancers, those with < 10% grade 4 component were not associated with a significant survival difference from grade 3 cancers. The study also demonstrated a significant difference in survival between tumours with < 10% versus > 50% grade 4 areas {30987774}.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of CCRCC includes clear cell papillary renal cell tumour, MiT family translocation RCC, chromophobe RCC, and multilocular cystic renal neoplasm of low malignant potential (see #21188Table 2.05). Using immunohistochemistry, positivity for PAX8 can be used to support renal tubular origin {21552115; 21285870}; however, this overlaps with other neoplasms, including those of the thyroid and gynaecological tract, which are also commonly positive for PAX8. Some upper tract urothelial carcinomas are also positive for this marker {23453625}. CCRCC often has dual expression of cytokeratin markers (such as CAM5.2 or AE1/AE3) and vimentin. However, in a given tumour the labelling for a single cytokeratin marker may be limited. Vimentin labelling is more common with higher grade {9439398}, and it contrasts with the typically absent labelling in chromophobe tumours. KIT (CD117) is typically negative in CCRCC, unlike in oncocytoma and chromophobe RCC {17683191}. CAIX (CA9) is part of the hypoxia pathway related to VHL and shows extensive membranous immunohistochemical labelling diffusely throughout the tumour, contrasting with the usually focal labelling associated with necrosis or ischaemia in other tumour types {18300814; 32639054; 21088149; 18941400; 20359812}. Labelling for CAIX may be decreased with high-grade or poorly differentiated tumours. CK7 usually shows negative or only focal labelling in CCRCC, but it often marks cystic areas {22982885}, and diffuse labelling in rare tumours does not necessarily preclude the diagnosis {26457355; 26752401}. In contrast to clear cell papillary RCC, CCRCC usually shows limited CK7 labelling, positive CD10 labelling, and variable AMACR labelling, and it is usually negative for high-molecular-weight cytokeratin and GATA3 {27923499; 28705707; 23238627; 26457355}. Clear cell papillary renal cell tumour also discloses a cup-like pattern of CAIX reactivity, which differs from the circumferential reactivity observed in CCRCC {25550767}. RCC antigen is also commonly positive in CCRCC. Ultrastructural examination demonstrates that the clear cytoplasm results from abundant lipid and glycogen.

Cytology

Cytological diagnosis of RCC is useful in determining whether a metastatic lesion is of primary renal origin {27770513}, with cytological features dependent on histological tumour type. FNA of CCRCC yields cohesive clusters of clear cells with variable nuclear and nucleolar appearances, usually accompanied by fine vascularity. Differential diagnostic workup of a metastatic clear cell malignancy can be assisted by immunohistochemistry on a cell block preparation.

Cytology is also useful in the primary diagnosis of a radiologically indeterminate renal mass, or of an advanced renal cancer where surgery is contraindicated. An FNA is often obtained in conjunction with a core biopsy. Imprint smears of the core biopsy may also be analysed cytologically. Whereas CCRCC may be readily recognized in cytology specimens, mimics such as benign renal tubular epithelial cells harbouring clear cytoplasm, oncocytoma, angiomyolipoma, and other histological types of renal carcinoma should be carefully considered.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Inactivation of the VHL complex is the defining signature event in CCRCC {18676741; 29656894; 29617669}. Loss of chromosome arm 3p and inactivating intragenic mutations or promoter region methylation of the second VHL allele occurs in most (> 90%) sporadic CCRCCs. Loss of 3p may be identified through molecular cytogenetic or chromosomal microarray studies. Absence of these alterations is rare and may cause such RCCs to be misclassified {28731045}, although it is uncommon for all of these aspects (mutation, promoter hypermethylation, and copy-number analysis) to be evaluated in diagnostic practice. VHL inactivation results in overexpression of HIF1A and HIF target genes such as those involved in angiogenesis (e.g. VEGFA), glycolysis and glucose transport (e.g. SLC2A1 [GLUT1]), and erythropoiesis (e.g. EPO) that molecularly characterize CCRCC.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: renal mass showing solid, nested, tubular, haemorrhagic, or alveolar architecture composed of clear cells with fine arborizing vascularity.

Desirable: immunohistochemical positivity for CAIX (CA9), or molecular identification of VHL mutation or chromosome 3p loss if morphology is atypical.

Staging

Kidney-confined tumours are staged according to primary tumour size, with cut-off points at ≤ 40 mm (pT1a), ≤ 70 mm (pT1b), ≤ 100 mm (pT2a), and > 100 mm (pT2b). Regional extrarenal spread (pT3a) is defined as infiltration of the renal sinus fat or perinephric fat, involvement of the renal vein and/or segmental veins, or invasion of the pelvicalyceal system. Tumour extension into the inferior vena cava below the diaphragm is considered pT3b, while pT3c is defined as extension into the inferior vena cava above the diaphragm or invasion of the vena cava wall. Direct spread into the adrenal gland or extension beyond the Gerota fascia is considered pT4 [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]].

It is now recognized that the renal sinus is the principal pathway of extrarenal spread by RCC {15577678; 10716160} For CCRCC, infiltration of the renal sinus is correlated with tumour size {10716160; 33121821; 31219816}, meaning that incomplete sampling of the renal sinus may result in understaging {31219816}. In particular, it has been demonstrated that tumours > 70 mm in maximum extent almost always display renal sinus invasion on detailed sampling {33121821; 31219816}.

Prognosis and prediction

Stage at presentation, nuclear grade, sarcomatoid and rhabdoid differentiation, and tumour-type necrosis are significant predictors of outcome for CCRCC {24890805}.

Different forms of necrosis may be seen in CCRCC. Thromboembolic infarction results in coagulative necrosis {28669641} and this is distinct from tumour-type necrosis {32561208}. Extensive necrosis in the setting of a low-grade RCC appears to be associated with a more favourable prognosis {28669641}. Tumour-type necrosis (known as granular necrosis) has been shown to be an independently significant predictor of a less favourable outcome in CCRCC {32561208; 30129659}.

Sarcomatoid differentiation results in biphasic tumours with varying amounts of fibrosarcoma-like, pleomorphic, undifferentiated, or unclassified morphology {24025520; 3791160}. Heterologous differentiation such as chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, or rhabdomyosarcoma is rare {24025520; 3791160}.

Rhabdoid differentiation is characterized by solid sheets or discohesive single round to polygonal cells with globular cytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions and eccentric large pleomorphic nuclei. Not uncommonly, sarcomatoid and rhabdoid differentiation coexist. Both these types of differentiation are associated with a poor outcome, with a 15–22% 5-year cancer-specific survival for tumours with sarcomatoid differentiation {24025520; 27215555}. With rhabdoid differentiation, cancer-specific mortality of 40–50% and a median survival time of 8–31 months have been reported {24025520; 26017874}.
