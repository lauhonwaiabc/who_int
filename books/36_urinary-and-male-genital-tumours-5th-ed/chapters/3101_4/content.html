3.1.0.1 Renal cell tumours: Introduction

Epidemiology

The incidence and mortality rates of renal cell tumours have been increasing in many countries, across different levels of socioeconomic development (see #22000Table 2.01).

Incidence

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for 2% of all cancers globally and is the cause of 2% of cancer deaths {32494314}. In 2020, there were 271 249 new cases in men (age-standardized rate of 6.1 cases per 100 000 population) and 160 039 cases in women (age-standardized rate of 3.2 cases per 100 000 population) {33538338}. The M:F ratio was 1.24:1 in 1990 and 1.58:1 in 2017 {33005476}. Kidney cancer currently ranks as the seventh most common cancer in men and the tenth most common in women {30081855}. The incidence is higher in high-income countries than in low-income countries, with the highest rates in Belarus (age-standardized rate of 16.8 cases per 100 000 population) in 2018, followed by Latvia, Lithuania, and the Czech Republic [[Ferlay J, Ervik M, Lam F, et al. Global Cancer Observatory: Cancer Today [Internet]. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. Available from: https://gco.iarc.fr/today.]]. Elevated rates of kidney cancer are also found in northern and eastern Europe, North America, and Australia. Low rates are reported in Africa and eastern Asia {33005476}.

Mortality

Globally, deaths from kidney cancer increased from 68 140 in 1990 to 138 530 in 2017 {33005476}, with those in male patients exceeding those in female patients: in 2017, the total number of deaths was 89 620 in male patients and 48 910 in female patients {33005476}. Kidney cancer is the 16th most common cause of death from cancer worldwide. The case fatality rate is lower in high-income countries (overall mortality-to-incidence ratio: 0.4) than in low- and middle-income countries (0.5). Only 3.1% of the cases were diagnosed in Africa, but 5.7% of the deaths occurred in this region {33005476}.

Etiology
Obesity

Obesity is a risk factor for kidney cancer in both men and women {31766196; 21741761}. There is an association between baseline body mass index and renal cancer risk, with increased risk when raised body mass index is documented at age 50. Weight gain of ≥ 20 kg between the ages of 18 and 35 years or between the ages of 35 and 50 years is correlated with renal cancer risk {18544571}. The proportion of all cases of renal cancer attributable to being overweight or obese has been estimated at about 40% in the USA and up to 40% in European countries {19645011; 18280327}. The mechanisms by which obesity influences renal carcinogenesis are unclear, with chronic inflammation in adipose tissue and immune dysregulation potentially promoting carcinogenesis {29709261}. Renal cell proliferation may be caused by sex steroid hormones through direct endocrine receptor–mediated effects. Obesity associated with endocrine disorders, accompanied by decreased levels of sex hormone–binding globulin and progesterone, insulin resistance, and increased levels of growth factors such as IGF1, may be factors in renal carcinogenesis. No convincing association has been found between the ingestion of red and processed meat, poultry, or seafood and the risk of RCC {19505906; 19033572}.

Smoking

Cigarette smoking is causally related to renal cancer. Meta-analyses indicate that ever-smokers have a higher risk of renal cancer than never-smokers {21763961; 15523697}, with a dose-dependent increase in risk related to the number of cigarettes smoked per day, and that risk decreases in the 5-year period after smoking cessation. Epidemiological data on kidney cancer causation by smoking are often biased because cancer registries do not typically differentiate between RCCs and urothelial carcinomas of the renal pelvis, for which smoking is also a significant risk factor. In a study on smoking prevalence among 30 282 patients with renal cancer, 50.2% were current or former cigarette smokers. When correlated with histological types of RCC, the proportions of current or former smokers ranged from 38% in patients with chromophobe carcinoma to 61.9% in those with collecting duct / medullary carcinoma, suggesting that chromophobe carcinoma has a weaker association with smoking than do other RCCs {32357956}.

Hypertension

Hypertension or its treatment has been shown to be associated with risk of renal cancer {18048375; 30243799} independently of obesity, with an increased risk associated with hypertensive medication including diuretics. Acute kidney injury has been shown to correlate with papillary RCC {32213630}.

Acquired cystic disease

Patients on long-term haemodialysis due to end-stage renal disease develop renal cysts and have an increased risk of renal cancer (3–7%) {12028461}. They are 100 times more likely than people in the general population to develop renal cancer {12028461}. Some renal cancer types occur exclusively in end-stage renal disease. These tumours show tubular, papillary, tubulopapillary, microcystic, and solid patterns, with tumour cells having abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and large nuclei with conspicuous nucleoli, including intracytoplasmic lumina and intercellular spaces that impart a cribriform (sieve-like) appearance {16434887; 29851703; 32271189; 32604168}. In addition to these acquired cystic disease–associated RCCs, all other subtypes can also occur in end-stage renal disease.

Occupational exposure

A meta-analysis of the association between exposure to trichloroethylene (a solvent widely used as a metal degreaser and chemical additive) and clear cell renal cancer reported significant relative risks of 1.3 overall and 1.6 for high-exposure groups {20010212; 22163205}. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified trichloroethylene as Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans) on the basis of it causing kidney cancer. It is unlikely that coffee has a substantial effect {21406107} or that alcoholic drinks have an adverse effect on the risk of this cancer.

Genetic susceptibility

In this fifth-edition volume, the importance of genetic tumour syndromes in kidney neoplasia is emphasized in a dedicated chapter (section Chapter 14: Genetic tumour syndromes of the urinary and male genital tracts).

Classification

In this fifth edition, there is a new category of molecularly defined renal tumour entities. Tumour classification is a dynamic process, integrating multiple new areas of information based on enhanced molecular interrogations that have been conducted by numerous investigators since the publication of the previous edition. Attempts to classify RCC have traditionally been based on defining subtypes according to the predominant cytoplasmic or architectural features, with the consecutive identification of specific genotype–phenotype correlations (e.g. clear cell RCC, papillary RCC) or tumour location (e.g. collecting duct and renal medullary carcinomas), correlations with background renal disease (e.g. acquired cystic disease–associated RCC), resemblance of the tumours to embryological structures such as the metanephros (e.g. metanephric adenoma), or a specific hereditary background (e.g. hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC syndrome–associated RCC). The 2004 WHO classification first introduced tumour subtypes defined on the basis of a specific molecular alteration (e.g. translocation-associated RCC). In recent years, studies have shown that some of these molecularly defined tumours have an overly broad morphological spectrum without an unequivocal genotype–phenotype correlation. Therefore, molecular testing including next-generation sequencing is increasingly used to classify RCC, and it has especially broadened the differential diagnosis of tumours considered in the oncocytoma and chromophobe RCC category, which now include fumarate hydratase–deficient RCC, epithelioid angiomyolipoma, TFE3-rearranged and TFEB-altered RCCs, and eosinophilic solid and cystic RCC. Eosinophilic solid and cystic RCC can show TSC gene mutations or biallelic losses. Recent studies have identified many tumours whose cells have eosinophilic cytoplasm and oncocytic or chromophobe-like features in the RCC-NOS group, with somatic inactivating mutations of TSC2 or activating mutations of MTOR as the primary molecular alterations. However, TSC-associated and MTOR-associated neoplasms are a very heterogeneous group morphologically and immunohistochemically. To gain a better understanding of this heterogeneous group of oncocytic tumours, which are not classifiable as oncocytomas or chromophobe RCC, a category of unclassified oncocytic tumours has been introduced. This category of unclassified oncocytic tumours encompasses low-grade and high-grade oncocytic tumours because the behaviour of such tumours is poorly understood.

These developments demonstrate that some RCC subtypes represent completely different neoplasms, with differences in morphology, pathogenesis, molecular events, and prognosis, rather than purely morphologically defined subtypes. In contrast to brain tumours, it is not yet time for RCCs to undergo a purely molecular classification. And in contrast to previous editions of the WHO classification of urinary and male genital tumours, in this edition the editorial board has decided to first divide RCC into morphologically and molecularly defined RCCs. Molecularly defined RCCs include TFE3-rearranged RCCs, ALK-rearranged RCCs, TFEB-rearranged and TFEB-amplified RCC, ELOC (formerly TCEB1)-mutated RCC, SMARCB1 (INI1)-deficient RCC, and others.

RCCs with prominent leiomyomatous stroma frequently harbour mutations in TSC1, TSC2, MTOR, and/or ELOC (TCEB1). These tumours may have considerable morphological overlap with clear cell RCCs and clear cell papillary tumours, supporting the hypothesis that ELOC (TCEB1)-mutated RCCs are identical and indistinguishable from a subset of hereditary tuberous sclerosis–associated RCCs, originally described as “renal angiomyoadenomatous tumour–like RCC” or “tuberous sclerosis–associated papillary-type RCC”. A clear diagnosis of ELOC (TCEB1)-mutated RCC can only be made by complex molecular analyses. Therefore, it has been decided that this entity be put into the category of molecularly defined RCCs. In contrast, although almost all metanephric adenomas, adenofibromas, and metanephric stromal tumours are defined by BRAF p.V600E alterations, they are still regarded as morphologically defined neoplasms.

One important change in the fifth edition is the definition of papillary RCC. In 2016, it was hypothesized that type 2 papillary RCCs include different tumour entities. Since then, the essential and desirable criteria useful for the differential diagnosis of fumarate hydratase–deficient RCC, eosinophilic solid and cystic RCC, and translocation RCC have been further developed. Whereas the former type 1 papillary RCC is now regarded as the classic morphology of papillary RCC, the diagnostic criteria for type 2 papillary RCC need to be re-evaluated. Another change in the fifth edition is the name change from “clear cell papillary renal cell carcinoma” to “clear cell papillary renal cell tumour” (see section Clear cell papillary renal cell tumour), which was introduced in the fourth edition of the WHO classification. The name change was made because there are no reports of metastatic events for this indolent tumour entity.

The categories of nephroblastic tumours described in this edition of the WHO classification of urogenital tumours are also included in the WHO classification of paediatric tumours.

Morphological data still remain of clinical relevance in the treatment of cancer patients. For example, sarcomatoid differentiation, which has been recognized as a feature of aggressiveness, allows the selection of tailored therapy (immunotherapy). Immune checkpoint inhibitors are under consideration for the first-line treatment of patients with sarcomatoid RCC.

At present, molecular profiles do not influence the care of patients with kidney cancer in the setting of either surgery or systemic therapy. However, emerging data from clinical trials of immuno-oncology agents used in combination with or compared against VEGF inhibitors suggest that distinct gene expression signatures reflecting a prominence of angiogenesis or immune infiltration correlate with the response to therapy (CheckMate 214, JAVELIN Renal 101, and IMmotion151 studies {29562145; 33157048; 32895571}), and in the future this could support personalized therapy choices. The exact correlation between genomic alterations (including mutations and copy-number alterations) and features of the tumour microenvironment is under investigation {32895571; 32472114}. In terms of morphological features, the presence of sarcomatoid differentiation is associated with an increased benefit from immuno-oncology agents {33157048; 32873572}. It is worth noting that the evaluation of any potential biomarkers in renal cell cancer is complicated by prominent intratumoural heterogeneity evident both at the genetic level {29656894} and in transcriptomic features {25047176}.

Like in the fourth edition, there is an emerging body of knowledge in this fifth edition that is compelling but not sufficient for placing certain clinicopathologically and genomically distinct tumours into the formal classification of renal tumours. #28334Table 2.02 is a summary of tumours currently regarded as emerging entities. This table provides some information on how a few of these tumours may currently be classified; interestingly, all of them have an underlying, probably characteristic, molecular alteration.

Grading of RCC

Currently, nuclear grade is integrated in several prognostic tools for renal cancer, and (where applicable) it is required to be reported in nephrectomies by the International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) and other reporting guidelines and recommendations, such as those of the College of American Pathologists (CAP). In the fourth edition of this volume, the WHO Classification of Tumours / International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/ISUP) histological grading of renal cell tumour types was recommended specifically for clear cell RCC and papillary RCC; for grades 1–3, tumour grade is defined by the degree of nucleolar prominence, whereas grade 4 is defined by the presence of nuclear pleomorphism, tumour giant cells, and rhabdoid and sarcomatoid features. Although grading was not validated for other subtypes of RCC at the time those recommendations were made, the suggested approach was to report grading for all RCCs for completeness, with descriptions based on the WHO/ISUP grading criteria.

Since the publication of the previous edition and the continued description of newer subtypes of RCC and/or refinement of criteria of recently described entities, the process of validation of the WHO/ISUP grade in RCC subtypes is still in progress. Clinicians rely on nuclear grade; therefore, in #28412Table 2.03 we provide the state of understanding of WHO/ISUP grading in the context of published literature.

To date, only clear cell and papillary RCCs have adequate validated data for clinical applicability (category 1, #28412Table 2.03). Several papers have shown that the WHO/ISUP system is not applicable for chromophobe RCC (category 2). There are RCC subtypes where WHO/ISUP grading is potentially useful (category 3), so these tumour types should be graded and reported accordingly. Grading may be less relevant in certain RCCs where the nomenclature indicates aggressive behaviour (such as collecting duct carcinoma) (category 4), or where low-grade nuclear features are essential for accurate histological classification (category 6). For some other tumour types, data are still evolving, with clear instances where grading may be misleading (category 5) or where the significance of grading is unknown (category 7); these tumours might not be graded, or, if a grade is applied, a comment is added about the lack of known clinical significance of the grade. Morphologically, the category of other oncocytic tumours encompasses low-grade and high-grade oncocytic tumours, but the clinical behaviour of such tumours is not defined.

Other tumour types

In this edition of the WHO classification of urogenital tumours, the category of renal haematolymphoid neoplasms is addressed in section Chapter 11: Haematolymphoid tumours, and the neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the kidney are addressed in section Chapter 9: Neuroendocrine neoplasms, where these tumours are described for all the organs within the genitourinary tract. Similarly, metastases to the kidney are included in section Chapter 13.0: Metastases in the genitourinary system.

The mesenchymal tumours unique to the kidney are described in section Chapter 2: Tumours of the kidney, and section Chapter 10: Mesenchymal tumours deals with the other mesenchymal tumours that may occur in the kidney as well as in the other genitourinary organs (most notably, Ewing sarcoma and renal synovial sarcoma).

Increased knowledge about hereditary tumour syndromes and renal neoplasia is highlighted in section Chapter 14: Genetic tumour syndromes of the urinary and male genital tracts.
