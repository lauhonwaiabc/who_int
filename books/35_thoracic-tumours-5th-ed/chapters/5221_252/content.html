5.2.2.1 Carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumour of the thymus

Definition

Carcinoid/neuroendocrine tumour (NET) of the thymus is a neuroendocrine epithelial neoplasm of thymic origin with low-grade nuclear features; typical carcinoid (TC) has < 2 mitoses/2 mm2 and lacks necrosis, whereas atypical carcinoid (AC) has 2–10 mitoses/2 mm2 and/or foci of necrosis.

Related terminology

Acceptable: carcinoid tumour; atypical carcinoid.

Subtype(s)

Typical carcinoid (grade 1 NET); atypical carcinoid (grade 2/3 NET)

Localization

Carcinoid/NET of the thymus is localized in the anterior (prevascular) mediastinum.

Clinical features

About 50% of patients present with chest pain, cough, dyspnoea, or superior vena cava syndrome {11598168; 10884805; 10550713}. Paraneoplastic manifestations due to hormone production include Cushing syndrome (17–30% of adult and > 50% of childhood carcinoids) {11845907; 10550713; 11993687}, with or without cutaneous hyperpigmentation {8380112}; hypercalcaemia/hypophosphataemia due to production of PTHrP {7919739} or from primary hyperparathyroidism in the context of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) {9671073}; acromegaly {9579239}; and inappropriate production of antidiuretic hormone or atrial natriuretic peptide {8198356}. Carcinoid syndrome is exceedingly rare (< 1%) {10550713}.

The clinical presentation of ACs is indistinguishable from that of TCs and includes the same paraneoplastic manifestations. About 40–50% of patients already have mediastinal, cervical, or supraclavicular lymph node metastasis at presentation {11598168; 8045526; 10884805}. Invasion into adjacent organs (40–50%) or pleural or pericardial cavity (10%) is common {11598168; 8045526}. Sites of distant metastasis include lung, brain, lumbar spine, bone, liver, kidney, adrenals, skin, and soft tissues {11598168; 10884805; 10550713; 23110006}. Late recurrences occurring as late as 9 years after resection have been reported {11757339}.

Epidemiology

The average age at presentation for patients with typical carcinoids is 49 years {10550713}, with a marked male predominance. MEN1-associated thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) have all been carcinoids and occurred almost exclusively in adult men (age range: 31–66 years; mean: 44 years) {9064485; 27311764; 26070346}.

In the thymus, ACs are far more common than TCs, and they occur in a slightly older age group {11598168; 10884805; 23110006; 24764238; 31042566}. They usually occur in adults, with an average patient age of 48–55 years (range: 18–82 years) {11598168; 10884805; 10550713; 8045526}, but they have also been rarely observed in children {8380112; 10417976}. There is a strong male predominance, with the M:F ratio ranging from 2:1 to 7:1 {2196019; 11598168; 10884805; 10550713; 31042566}.

Etiology

About 25% of patients with thymic carcinoids have a family history of MEN1 {9681849}. Conversely, among MEN1 patients, thymic carcinoids occur in 8% of cases {12629087}. In MEN1, smoking has been reported to be a risk factor in males {12629087; 15713725; 19294466} but not females {22690831}.

Pathogenesis

TCs / grade 1 (G1) NETs show the lowest number of genetic alterations among thymic NETs. Recurrent aberrations include gains on chromosomes 1q, 5, 6q, 7q, 8q, 10, 11q, 12q, 13q, 18q, 20, 21q, and 22q and losses on chromosomes 1, 2p, 4p, 8, 10p, 11p, 15q, 17p, 18p, and 22q {15272285; 15890250; 24764238}. The gene locus of MEN1 on chromosome 11q13 is consistently unaltered {12629087; 15272285; 7912288; 9671073}. These alterations are different from those described in pulmonary TC. Moreover, copy-number alterations in gene loci of tumour suppressor and driver genes with reported frequent alterations in pulmonary NETs are very rare in thymic TC {9777940; 24670920; 31042566}. There are no published reports on specific gene mutations in TC.

ACs / grade 2 (G2) NETs show higher numbers of chromosomal alterations than TCs {24764238}, and the percentage of alterations shared between AC and TC is lower than the percentage shared between AC and large cell and small cell neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) {31042566}. A small study comparing histological and molecular features in primary and metastatic ACs {31042566} suggests that TC and AC belong to a spectrum of neoplasms that can further progress at least to intermediate-grade tumours with mitotic counts exceeding those allowed for AC (see also section Thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction, and section Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the thymus). Single cases with CTNNB1 gene mutations are on record {28451756}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most tumours are unencapsulated and either circumscribed or grossly invasive. The size ranges from 20 to 200 mm (mean: 80–100 mm) {8551758; 10884805; 10550713}. Cases associated with Cushing syndrome tend to be smaller (30–50 mm) due to earlier detection. They are grey-white and firm on cut section, can have a gritty consistency, and usually lack the characteristic lobulated growth pattern of thymomas. Oncocytic tumours may show a tan or brown cut surface. Calcifications are more frequent in thymic than extrathymic NETs {10884805}.

Histopathology

Tumour cells are uniform and polygonal, with relatively small, round nuclei; finely granular chromatin; and pale eosinophilic cytoplasm. Most tumours show trabecular and rosetting growth patterns, but several other patterns, such as festoons, solid nests, glandular structures, and nuclear palisades, are also common. A delicate endocrine tumour–type vasculature between the nests and trabeculae is characteristic. Lymphovascular invasion is frequent. There are several recognized patterns, including spindle cell {946757; 1646476; 10392634}, pigmented {615841; 10530558; 3024805}, with amyloid {946757}, oncocytic {10824919; 6748352}, mucinous {9850376; 7573690}, angiomatoid {10374770}, and sarcomatous (with fibrosarcomatous, myoid, osseous, or chondroid differentiation) {7908657; 2067996}. Calcifications are also frequent {10884805}.

In ACs, all architectural features of TCs can occur. Even small punctate area of necrosis (comedonecrosis) in an otherwise-typical carcinoid justify a diagnosis of AC. Compared with TCs, ACs more frequently show some degree of nuclear pleomorphism, including rare anaplastic cells {11598168}, a focal diffuse growth pattern (so-called lymphoma-like) {11598168; 10884805; 8045526}, or extensive desmoplastic stroma with single-cell filing of tumour cells {6285747}.

Immunohistochemistry

Both TC and AC show strong expression of keratins and neuroendocrine markers (e.g. synaptophysin and chromogranin) but are usually negative for TTF1.

Atypical carcinoid tumours with elevated mitotic counts

Similar to grade 3 (G3) NETs in the pancreas, cases with carcinoid-like morphology but mitotic counts above the threshold currently allowed for AC (> 10 mitoses/2 mm2) also appear in the lung and thymus and are probably more frequent in the thymus than in the lung {31042566; 28631159; 27879513; 30485860; 26960398}. Please see section Thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms: Introduction for more detailed information. In the current edition of the WHO classification, such tumours are still classified as large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), but the report should state that the histological appearance is that of a carcinoid tumour with increased mitotic counts, that such tumours appear to be distinct from conventional types of LCNEC, and that systemic management (e.g. the effectiveness of adjuvant therapies) may also differ.

Cytology

Characteristic cells lie in loose clusters or small strands showing indistinct cell borders. They are uniformly small and round to oval with scant cytoplasm, sometimes interspersed with larger cells with abundant granular cytoplasm {7762327}. ACs cannot be distinguished from TCs on the basis of cytological features.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • TC: primary thymic NET with low-grade nuclear features, neuroendocrine morphology (e.g. trabecular or rosetting), absence of any necrosis, and mitotic count of < 2 mitoses/2 mm2
  • AC: same as TC, but with comedonecrosis and/or mitotic count of 2–10 mitoses/2 mm2

Desirable:

  • Both TC and AC: strong expression of keratins and neuroendocrine markers (e.g. synaptophysin and chromogranin), usually negative for TTF1
  • Ki-67 can be useful in distinguishing carcinoids from high-grade LCNEC and small cell carcinoma, particularly in small crushed biopsies

Staging

TC and AC should be staged according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM system [[Brierley JD, Gospodarowicz MK, Wittekind C, editors. TNM classification of malignant tumours. 8th ed. Oxford (UK): Wiley-Blackwell; 2017.]] {25396314}.

Prognosis and prediction

There are few published data on the prognosis of thymic carcinoids. The available data suggest that the clinical course of TCs is similar to that of ACs {24764238; 31042566}. Reported 5-year overall survival rates of ACs vary in published series, from 20–70% {10884805; 10550713; 24764238} up to 80% {23286964; 11598168}, with a median survival time of 59 months {24764238; 31042566; 25308116}.

Surgery and postoperative radiotherapy improve the outcome {29049806; 30276969}. Published 5-year survival rates are in the range of 50–70% {10884805; 10550713; 24764238; 31042566; 25308116}, with a median survival time of 126 months {24764238}.
