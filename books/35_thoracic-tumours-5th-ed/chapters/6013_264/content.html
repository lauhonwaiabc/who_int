6.0.1.3 Yolk sac tumour of the mediastinum

Definition

Yolk sac tumour (YST) of the mediastinum is a malignant non-seminomatous germ cell tumour arising in the mediastinum, characterized by numerous patterns that recapitulate the yolk sac, allantois, and extraembryonic mesenchyme.

ICD-O coding

9071/3 Yolk sac tumour

ICD-11 coding

2C28.0 & XH09W7 Malignant germ cell neoplasms of heart, mediastinum, or non-mesothelioma of pleura & Yolk sac tumour

Related terminology

Not recommended: endodermal sinus tumour.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

YST of the mediastinum occurs in the anterior (prevascular) mediastinum or rarely in the posterior (paravertebral) mediastinum {31619931}.

Clinical features

Patients may present with chest pain, dyspnoea, chills, fever, and superior vena cava syndrome {12722093; 12518361; 9264353}. Serum level of AFP is almost always elevated.

There are no specific imaging features. FDG PET may detect tiny occult primary tumours or recurrences {27088388; 23801057}.

Epidemiology

After puberty, YST is restricted to men, with rare exceptions {12203871; 16281866}. Its incidence peaks in the third decade of life and declines thereafter {23683844}. Between 15 and 59 years, its incidence is 0.3 cases per 1 million per year {23683844}. Pure YST accounts for 2–12% of mediastinal germ cell tumours in adults, being the fourth commonest germ cell tumour, after seminomas, mixed germ cell tumours, and teratomas {9264351; 21610519; 18222228; 12518361}.

In young children, the epidemiology is different. YST has an incidence of 0.25 cases per 100 000 children (aged < 15 years) per year, being the most common malignant germ cell tumour before puberty, with a peak incidence at 1 year {14752882}. Up to the age of 5 years, YST is virtually the only malignant germ cell tumour of the mediastinum {11921289}. There is a strong female preponderance (70–80%) {14752882}. After the age of 6 years, pure YST is rare {14752882}.

Etiology

Klinefelter syndrome is a risk factor after puberty, for unknown reasons {16470792}.

Pathogenesis

An aberrant midline migration of primordial germ cells has been hypothesized {15426876; 17705807}, but origin from local embryonic stem cells is an alternative theory. Mediastinal YSTs in children aged < 8 years exhibit genetic losses at 1p, 4q, and 6q and gains at 1q, 3, 20q, and 20, but neither isochromosome 12p nor sex-chromosomal alterations {11753688; 11921289; 11528555}. In contrast, YSTs occurring after the age of 8 years show isochromosome 12p (in 60%), gain of chromosomes 21 and X (in 50% each), and loss of chromosome 13 (in 30%) {11921289}. Age-related genetic alterations are accompanied by distinct imprinting patterns {11585765; 15738984; 16001432} and gene expression profiles {18519683}.

Macroscopic appearance

Pure YSTs are solid and soft, and the cut surface is typically pale grey or grey-white, with a gelatinous or mucoid quality. After neoadjuvant treatment, haemorrhage and necrosis are common. Cyst formation may be treatment-related or may indicate a mixed germ cell tumour (YST and teratoma).

Histopathology

YST usually shows different histological patterns: microcystic (reticular), macrocystic, glandular-alveolar, endodermal sinus (pseudopapillary), myxomatous, hepatoid, enteric, polyvesicular-vitelline, solid, and spindle {13837288; 9264353; 17471115; 22261704}. The microcystic/reticular pattern is characterized by a loose network of cystic spaces and channels lined by flat or cuboidal cells with scant cytoplasm. In myxomatous areas, epithelial cells are in a mucinous stroma. The endodermal sinus pattern shows pseudopapillary structures and Schiller–Duval bodies. The polyvesicular-vitelline pattern is defined by cysts that are lined by cuboidal to flat tumour cells surrounded by a dense fibrous stroma. The solid pattern resembles embryonal carcinoma or seminoma, but cells are smaller and less pleomorphic than those of embryonal carcinoma and more pleomorphic than seminoma cells, and they typically retain the usual immunohistochemical profile of YST, although commonly with negative or weak and focal staining for AFP {22261704}. YST with a hepatoid pattern {2420222; 9331294} has cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm resembling fetal or adult liver {7139531}. The enteric and endometrioid patterns show glandular features resembling the fetal human gut and secretory endometrial glands, respectively {3661822; 2446292}. Syncytiotrophoblasts can occur in rare YSTs and do not change the diagnosis to mixed germ cell tumour {17471115}.

Sarcoma development in YST represents somatic-type malignancy; it is rare in pure mediastinal YST {17721191} but more common in mixed germ cell tumour {6090001; 12471871}. Associated, clonally related haematological malignancies (mainly acute leukaemias and myelodysplastic syndromes) {2158625; 2153216; 8069867; 10620634} occur only in adults and are commonest in mixed germ cell tumour with a YST component (58%) or pure YST (25%) {17471115; 10620634}.

Immunohistochemistry

YSTs are consistently immunoreactive for keratins (AE1/AE3), glypican-3 (GPC3) {17122513}, SALL4 {20410807; 19574883; 19365862}, and LIN28 {19578360; 21057460}, whereas they are consistently negative for OCT4, NANOG {12727846; 23893437}, SOX2 {20410807; 17527070; 17911410; 19369635}, and D2-40 {18045648; 17277761; 23893437}. Single syncytiotrophoblastic cells in rare YSTs are positive for β-hCG {9264353}.

YSTs may variably express AFP and PLAP in as many as 70% of cases, KIT (CD117) in as many as 43% {18045648}, and CD30 in as many as 11% {22261704; 21057460; 20410807; 19390421; 19574883; 9670832}, whereas < 25% of cases are EMA-positive in a minority of tumour cells {19574883; 18045648}.

Differential diagnosis

The typical morphological and molecular features of potential mimics of mediastinal YSTs are given in Table #14844.

Seminomas can resemble solid areas of YST. A pitfall is their common (80%) dot-like keratin expression {9264352}. Unlike YST, seminoma is typically positive for OCT4 and D2-40 and negative for GPC3.

Embryonal carcinoma shows larger and more atypical tumour cells, is less varied, and lacks microcystic areas and Schiller–Duval bodies. Unlike YSTs, embryonal carcinomas are positive for OCT4 and SOX2.

Choriocarcinoma is a biphasic tumour with haemorrhagic background. Pitfalls are the consistent shared immunoreactivity for SALL4 {19390421} and LIN28 {21631526} and rare occurrence of β-hCG–positive syncytiotrophoblasts in YST {9264353}.

Immature teratoma may mimic the varied growth patterns of YST and may focally express GPC3, AFP, SALL4, LIN28, HepPar-1, and CDX2 in endodermal components {24444105; 21288558; 17471115}. However, immature teratoma typically harbours CD56-positive and/or OCT4-positive neuroectodermal cells {21107090} or EMA-positive components.

Thymic carcinoma and YST may share a solid growth pattern and expression of KIT (CD117), and both may lack AFP expression {22261704; 14991904; 14762710; 18045648}. However, thymic carcinoma is often positive for CD5 {7519823} and negative for GPC3 {20410807}, SALL4 {22298374}, and LIN28 {21057460}.

Metastases of hepatocellular and hepatoid carcinomas and gonadal germ cell tumour to the mediastinum must be excluded {31320874}, because they may share expression of GPC3, AFP, CDX2, and HepPar-1 {12698156; 23893437}. Hepatocellular carcinoma is SALL4-negative {20182341}, and most metastatic carcinomas are negative for SALL4 and LIN28 {23893437; 24444105}, with rare exceptions {24525512; 21057460; 19390421}.

Cytology

Smears of YST show aggregates of medium-sized to large cells with variable nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and moderate to occasionally abundant cytoplasm, with a variable background of debris, mucoid material, or metachromatic material. Lymphoid cells and granulomas are typically absent. The presence of hyaline globules is typical of YST, but distinction between non-seminomatous germ cell tumours is often not reliable {12203871; 11842377}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnostic molecular pathology is usually not relevant. Rarely, identification of chromosome 12p alterations may help to exclude SALL4-positive OCT4-negative carcinomas {24525512} or identify somatic-type malignancy.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential:

  • A malignant germ cell tumour characterized by numerous patterns that recapitulates the yolk sac, allantois, and extra-embryonic mesenchyme
  • Growth pattern: usually multiple within a single tumour, including microcystic, macrocystic, glandular-alveolar, endodermal sinus, myxomatous, hepatoid, enteric, polyvesicular-vitelline, solid, and spindle cell

Desirable:

  • Schiller–Duval bodies are a hallmark of the disease but might be absent
  • Serology: AFP markedly increased
  • Coexpression of keratins, SALL4, glypican-3 (GPC3), and variably AFP
  • Negative staining for GATA3 and OCT4

Staging

There is no validated staging system for mediastinal germ cell tumours. Mediastinal YSTs almost always extend into adjacent structures, including the lung, while pleural dissemination is rare {12203871; 9264353}. Before puberty, pure and mixed mediastinal YSTs have a 30% risk for distant metastasis {11921289}, mainly to lung, lymph nodes, liver, bone, and brain {20514616; 22693072; 10673525; 22350540}. After puberty, 50% of mediastinal YSTs metastasize to lung or intrathoracic lymph nodes, while extrathoracic metastasis occurs in < 10% of cases {9264351; 9264353}.

Prognosis and prediction

The 5-year event-free and overall survival rates of all children aged < 15 years with malignant non-seminomatous mediastinal germ cell tumours are 83% and 87%, respectively, with no difference between YST and mixed germ cell tumours {10673525}. The most important favourable prognostic factor is complete resection of the primary lesion; following incomplete resection, the 5-year survival rate drops to 42% {10673525}. Primary brain metastasis is a poor prognostic sign {20514616; 22693072}.

After the age of 15 years, the 5-year overall survival rate in mediastinal non-seminomatous tumours is 45–54% {12176779; 20933444; 21610519; 24337455}. Lack of metastasis {21610519; 20933444; 9264351; 9264353}, completeness of resection and postoperative normalization of tumour markers {21610519; 18222229}, low preoperative β-hCG levels {20933444}, pure YST histology {20933444}, and complete tumour necrosis after chemotherapy {18222228} are favourable prognostic factors. Somatic-type malignancy and clonally related haematological malignancies are refractory to therapy and portend a poor prognosis {2158625; 2153216; 8069867; 10620634}.
