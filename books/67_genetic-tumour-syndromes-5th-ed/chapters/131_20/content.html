1.3.1 Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)

Definition

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a pathogenic variant in NF1 and characterized by a constellation of clinical criteria.

MIM numbering

162200 Neurofibromatosis type 1; NF1

601321 Neurofibromatosis–Noonan syndrome; NFNS

ICD-11 coding

LD2D.10 Neurofibromatosis type 1

Related terminology

Not recommended: von Recklinghausen disease; peripheral neurofibromatosis.

Subtype(s)

Mosaic neurofibromatosis type 1, including segmental neurofibromatosis type 1; spinal neurofibromatosis; neurofibromatosis–Noonan syndrome; 17q11.2 microdeletion syndrome

Localization

NF1 affects many different cell types and tissues.

Clinical features

Multiple café-au-lait macules are typically present at birth and increase in number during early childhood. Axillary and inguinal freckling occurs in > 80% and Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas) in > 90% of adults with NF1. Cutaneous neurofibromas (> 85% of adults with NF1) frequently develop during puberty {28230061}, whereas plexiform neurofibromas (PN) are probably congenital, arising in 30–50% of children with NF1 {28230061}. Individual PNs have variable growth rates but exhibit the greatest growth during infancy and childhood. A subset of PNs transforms into atypical neurofibroma / atypical neurofibromatous neoplasms of uncertain biological potential (ANNUBP), which can be premalignant lesions with increased risk of transformation into high-grade malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours (MPNSTs) {28551330;21987445}. Possible ANNUBP or MPNST should be considered in patients with growing nodular PNs, PNs in which an isolated portion exhibits disproportionate growth, or adults with continued PN growth.

Specific NF1-associated bone abnormalities, including severe scoliosis, sphenoid wing dysplasia, non-ossifying fibromas, decreased bone mineral density, and congenital tibial bowing, are encountered. Progressive tibial bowing can result in pathological fracture and pseudarthrosis {18248783}.

Many children exhibit learning disabilities, attention deficits, and problems with reciprocal social interactions {27760236}. Children with NF1 can also develop vasculopathies, especially of the renal and CNS circulation {34091747;35849956}. Macrocephaly, short stature, epilepsy, headache (including migraine), chronic pain, neuropathy, and sleep disturbances are overrepresented in people with NF1 {24065580;30006586;31010905}.

Fifteen per cent of children will develop an optic pathway glioma / pilocytic astrocytoma (OPG), which causes vision loss in 30–50% of affected individuals {28806346}. Low-grade gliomas (LGGs) are found in other locations, especially the brainstem {28330960;32300062}. Adults with NF1 are prone to malignant gliomas {32055852}. Brain MRIs in children show focal areas of high signal intensity on T2-weighted sequences, which tend to disappear with age and are sometimes difficult to differentiate from LGGs {30140579}, as well as larger grey matter and corpus callosum volumes {10690986}.

Other neoplasms observed at increased frequency are juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia, rhabdomyosarcoma, glomus tumours of the digits, gastrointestinal stromal tumours, phaeochromocytoma, breast cancer in female patients, and duodenal neuroendocrine tumours (NETs; somatostatinomas) {19410195}.

Epidemiology

NF1 is a common autosomal dominant disorder with a birth incidence of 1 per 3000 {25354145}.

Etiology

NF1 is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the NF1 gene, encoding neurofibromin (NF1) {2134734;1694727}. Half of those with NF1 have unaffected parents and represent de novo variants.

Pathogenesis

Neurofibromin (NF1) primarily functions as a RAS proto-oncogene GAP {8499945}. The EVH1 domain of SPRED1 (the gene mutated in Legius syndrome) binds to neurofibromin (NF1) on both sides of the GAP-related domain and recruits neurofibromin (NF1) to the plasma membrane where it accelerates RAS inactivation {26635368;27313208}. Neurofibromin (NF1) forms a dimer, which creates two functional states with respect to RAS regulation {34707296}.

Most NF1 clinical features, including café-au-lait macules, bony abnormalities, and benign tumours, result from loss of neurofibromin (NF1) function (biallelic NF1 inactivation), leading to increased RAS and RAS effector (MEK/ERK or AKT/mTOR) signalling. Atypical neurofibromas/ANNUBPs harbour additional genetic alterations, such as CDKN2A/CDKN2B and SMARCA2 loss (chromosome 9p) {21987445;30722027}. MPNSTs have a highly rearranged karyotype with near-universal gain of chromosome 8 {33591953} and frequent aberrations in the PRC2 complex and TP53 genes {25119042;25240281}. LGGs in NF1 are characterized by biallelic NF1 loss alone, although other variants are occasionally seen (e.g. in FGFR1) {33585982}. Alterations in CDKN2A/CDKN2B and/or ATRX are detected in some NF1 LGGs, where they are associated with anaplasia, increased proliferation, and more-aggressive tumour behaviour. High-grade gliomas in NF1 are also characterized by TP53, CDKN2A/CDKN2B, and ATRX variants {30531922}.

In both neurofibromas and OPGs, murine studies have revealed that non-neoplastic (stromal) cells with a heterozygous NF1 variant, including mast cells, macrophages, neurons, T cells, and microglia, are critical for tumorigenesis and continued growth {35589737;34040258;18984156}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearances of nerve sheath tumours in NF1 include small sessile or pedunculated growths of the skin (cutaneous neurofibromas), plaque-like thickening of the skin (diffuse neurofibroma), fusiform segmental expansions of individual peripheral nerve fascicles (intraneural neurofibromas), and multinodular/multifascicular nerve expansions (plexiform neurofibromas). Massive soft tissue neurofibromas are characterized by large neurofibromatous involvement of anatomical segments with infiltration of soft tissue and skeletal muscle. Fleshy masses and areas of necrosis and haemorrhage characterize MPNST. In general, CNS tumours in NF1 patients resemble their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Nerve sheath tumours in NF1 are predominantly neurofibromas, which are characterized by spindle cells with thin, wavy nuclei (neoplastic Schwann cells), immersed in a variably loose myxoid stroma. CD34 highlights the stromal component with a lattice-like pattern. Cells of the haematopoietic lineage, including histiocytes and mast cells are also found {28001089}. Nerve fibres are easily identifiable in intraneural subtypes, characterized by expansions of single (localized) or multiple (plexiform) nerve fascicles. PN is defined by a multinodular pattern of growth with involvement of multiple nerve fascicles, each surrounded by perineurium, and almost exclusively develops in patients with NF1. S100+ pseudomeissnerian bodies or corpuscles are a typical feature of diffuse and plexiform neurofibromas.

Recently proposed criteria for ANNUBPs are used to describe premalignant or worrisome changes in neurofibromas, including at least two of the following: cytological atypia, hypercellularity, loss of neurofibroma architecture (on H&E and/or CD34 staining), and a mitotic count of > 0.2 mitoses/mm2 and < 1.5 mitoses/mm2 (equating to > 1 mitosis/50 HPF and < 3 mitoses/10 HPF of 0.51 mm in diameter and 0.2 mm2 in area) {28551330}. Immunostaining may show p16 expression loss in ANNUBP tumour cells.

Most MPNSTs in NF1 are high-grade malignant spindle cell neoplasms with brisk mitotic activity and necrosis, which either develop in a pre-existing PN or arise de novo. A wide spectrum of heterologous differentiation (cartilaginous, osseous, rhabdomyoblastic, glandular) may be present. MPNST with prominent rhabdomyosarcomatous differentiation has been termed “malignant triton tumour”. Immunohistochemical analyses frequently demonstrate p16 and H3 p.Lys27 trimethylation loss {26990975}, as well as decreased or absent expression of Schwann cell markers (S100, SOX10).

Individuals with NF1 are also predisposed to a variety of glial neoplasms, with pilocytic astrocytomas being the predominant histological subtype. Gliomas that are difficult to classify with ambiguous features may also be encountered, as well as LGGs with morphological similarities to subependymal giant cell astrocytomas {29973652}. High-grade astrocytomas in NF1 are similarly aggressive to their sporadic counterparts, and frequently have loss of ATRX expression {30531922}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Although no mutation hotspots have been identified, a pathogenic NF1 variant is detected in 95% of people with NF1 {10862084}. Nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, and small insertions, small deletions, and small duplications all result in NF1 inactivation. In 4–5% of cases, a 17q11.2 microdeletion is identified, in which multiple genes are codeleted along with the NF1 gene. One of these deleted genes SUZ12, a component of the PRC2, has been implicated in MPNST development {25119042;25240281;25305755}.

To date, a number of NF1 genotype–phenotype correlates have been reported. Of these, the most striking involve individuals with the 17q11.2 microdeletion, a specific 3 bp deletion NM_001042492.3:c.2970_2972del p.(Met992del), or an NF1 missense variant affecting arginine at position 1809. Individuals with a 17q11.2 microdeletion have an earlier onset and more-severe burden of neurofibromas, more-severe cognitive disabilities, somatic overgrowth, dysmorphic features, and a higher risk of developing MPNST {34230207;34535841}. Individuals with the specific codon 992 or 1809 variants do not develop neurofibromas or OPGs {30190611;26178382}. In addition, patients with missense variants involving codons 844–848 appear to exhibit more-severe disease symptomatology, including a higher incidence of externally visible PN, symptomatic spinal nerve root neurofibromas, OPG, and MPNST {29290338}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: a clinical diagnosis of NF1 requires the presence of at least two of the following features:

  • Six or more café-au-lait macules (> 5 mm diameter in children, > 15 mm in adults)
  • Axillary/inguinal freckling
  • Two or more neurofibromas of any type or one PN
  • OPG
  • Two or more iris hamartomas (Lisch nodules) or two or more choroidal abnormalities
  • A distinctive bony abnormality (anterolateral bowing of the tibia, pseudarthrosis of a long bone, sphenoid dysplasia)
  • A heterozygous pathogenic NF1 variant with a variant allele fraction of 50% in apparently normal tissue
  • A parent with NF1 (by the above criteria)

These criteria were revised in 2021 {34012067}. Of note, there are other conditions with features that overlap with NF1, including Legius syndrome (MIM number: 611431), Noonan syndrome {26337637}, and constitutional mismatch repair deficiency {30415209}.

Staging

Staging for NF1-associated MPNSTs is identical to that utilized for the sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

NF1 is an autosomal dominant disorder, where affected parents harbour a 50% risk of disease transmission with each pregnancy. Individuals often have a shortened lifespan, mainly due to malignant disease (especially MPNST) and stroke {25354145;21694737;11283797}. In one large, population-based study {26926675}, the lifetime risk of malignancy in people with NF1 was 59.6% (vs 30.8% in the population without NF1). The lifetime risk of MPNST was 15.8%. There was a significantly increased risk of developing breast cancer in women, especially those < 40 years of age. When cancers of the nervous system are excluded, the 5-year survival of individuals with cancer and NF1 is significantly worse than that of those without NF1 (54.0% vs 67.5%) {26926675}. The standard mortality ratio for many complications is higher in women < 50 years of age {25354145}. Genetic counselling is recommended and includes the possibility of prenatal and/or preimplantation genetic testing.

MEK inhibitors are approved for use in children 2–18 years old with NF1 and symptomatic, inoperable PN {32187457}, and are currently being evaluated for adults with PN as well as children with LGG.
