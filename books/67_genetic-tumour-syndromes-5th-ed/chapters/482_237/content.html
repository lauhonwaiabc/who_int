4.8.2 Klinefelter syndrome

Definition

Klinefelter syndrome (KS) is a chromosome disorder caused by the presence of a germline (constitutional) additional sex chromosome resulting in the karyotype of 47,XXY and characterized by multiple congenital anomalies with predisposition to breast cancer and extragonadal germ cell tumours.

MIM numbering

None

ICD-11 coding

LD50.3 Klinefelter syndrome

Related terminology

Acceptable: 47,XXY syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Localization is as appropriate for each tumour type.

Clinical features

Owing to the variable expressivity of a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations in KS, affected individuals are diagnosed at various stages in life or remain undiagnosed. Phenotypes may include physical characteristics, cognitive delays and impairments, especially in language processing, azoospermia, signs of hypogonadism (which may be associated with psychosocial challenges in some cases), along with other features (see #38966Box 5.04) {30755791}. KS can present with tall stature, gynaecomastia, small testes, gynoid aspect of hips, sparse body hair, signs of androgen deficiency, and low serum testosterone coupled with elevated gonadotropins (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism), oligospermia with hyalinization and fibrosis of the seminiferous tubules, complete spermatogenic failure, Leydig cell insufficiency, and increased excretion of FSH {27644703}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of KS is estimated to be 152 per 100 000 men (1 in 658 men) {32506765;19804849}, with a median age at diagnosis of 27.5 years; earlier diagnosis can occur when characteristic phenotypes are present, such as tall stature, small testes, and gynaecomastia {32506765;23118429}. There is a maternal age effect as demonstrated by a 4-fold increase in prenatal occurrence of KS shown in mothers aged > 40 years compared with those < 24 years {12574191}.

An increased risk of breast cancer and extragonadal germ cell tumours is also a characteristic feature of KS. Epidemiological data on the absolute risk of male breast cancer is scant. However, KS patients are considered to have an increased likelihood of developing breast cancer, with the most convincing data supporting a risk about 20- to 30-fold higher than expected. This risk in men with KS, although elevated, is still considerably lower than that of women in the general population {21241366}. An increased incidence of mediastinal germ cell tumours has been reported in patients with KS, often leading to precocious puberty due to hCG-producing mediastinal germ cell tumours {1425792;16470792}. The biological mechanism of this association remains unclear, but may be secondary to genetic factors on the additional X chromosome {15172357;10655070}.

Etiology

The classic form of KS, present in 80–90% of cases, is defined by a 47,XXY karyotype and arises owing to maternal or paternal meiotic non-disjunction. Related aneuploidies – including 48,XXXY or 48,XXYY, a structurally abnormal X chromosome (e.g. 47,i[Xq],Y), or mosaicism with a normal cell line (47,XXY/46,XY) – constitute the other 10–20%. Mosaicism arises from either non-disjunction in an early mitotic division of the developing 46,XY zygote or from loss of one of the X chromosomes of a 47,XXY conception due to anaphase lagging {27644703}.

Pathogenesis

KS cases with the non-mosaic karyotype 47,XXY arise from segregation errors in parental meiosis. The occurrence of maternal versus paternal meiotic non-disjunction appears equally distributed in KS, and can involve errors of meiosis I or II in female patients but only of the second meiotic division in male patients. Phenotypes in KS are influenced by the type of additional X chromosome (including pattern of X inactivation), the effect of hypogonadism, or both. Features related to the additional X chromosome manifest before puberty with congenital malformations, tall stature, and small testes. Features due to testosterone deficiency present at puberty or during adulthood are particularly sparse body and facial hair, reduced muscle mass, gynaecomastia, and eunuchoid skeleton as well as impaired sexual desire, erectile function, and well-being. Of note, the presence of more than two copies of the X chromosome can lead to more-severe and progressive worsening of symptoms {27644703}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Histological findings in the testes of patients with KS include germ cell loss, leading to irregular distribution of germ cells {25105890}. Testicular cords (prepubertal) and tubules (postpubertal) show decreased fertility index, in most cases < 30%, and alternating tubules with and without germ cells. The two waves of germ cell proliferation occurring in the so-called minipuberty (3–4 months of age) and about 4 years of age do not occur in patients with 47,XXY KS. Germ cells progressively decrease in number, and in some cases disappear completely, from the second year of life onward. At the beginning of adolescence, testes increase in volume exceeding 3 ml with the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular axis. The expected germ cell proliferation occurs only in some tubules, which may show a few spermatocytes. In most patients, with the progress of puberty, spermatogonia disappear completely and Sertoli cells degenerate. At about 12 to 14 years of age, there is hyalinization of tubules and a progressive increase of Leydig cells. The final stage is that of a hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. In adults, there is usually a nodular pattern of Leydig cell hyperplasia surrounded by ghosts of seminiferous tubules.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Karyotype analysis of a peripheral blood specimen is the gold standard to document the presence of an additional copy of the X chromosome or structural X chromosome abnormalities, as well as identify cases of mosaicism with a normal cell line.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: confirmation of germline presence of an additional copy of the X chromosome or structural X chromosome abnormalities.

Desirable: a history of male breast cancer and/or extragonadal germ cell tumours (often producing hCG).

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

KS is associated with an increased morbidity resulting in loss of approximately 2 years in lifespan as well as increased mortality from various sources including breast cancer. When present, hypogonadism can lead to body composition changes and a risk of developing metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes. Boys with KS may benefit from speech therapy and special education. Testosterone replacement therapy is used mainly to alleviate acute and long-term consequences of hypogonadism and treat or prevent the frequent comorbidity {23118429}.

KS is the strongest risk factor for the development of male breast carcinoma. Presentation is usually a painless lump, but > 40% of individuals present with stage III or IV disease {20193984}. Outcomes are similar to those in women, and intervention includes mastectomy with axillary clearance or sentinel node biopsy. The majority of male breast carcinomas (~90%) are hormone receptor–positive, which influences choice of adjuvant therapy.
