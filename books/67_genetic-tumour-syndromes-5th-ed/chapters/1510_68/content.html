Definition

Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is an inherited cancer-prone condition caused by germline pathogenic variants in STK11 (LKB1), and characterized by mucocutaneous melanin pigmentation and gastrointestinal polyposis. Patients with PJS have a high lifetime risk of gastrointestinal and extraintestinal cancers, including pancreatic, breast, and gonadal tumours.

Localization

Polyps in PJS typically occur in the small intestine, present in 95% of patients with PJS. About 25% of patients have polyps in the colon and stomach {26721304}. The latter figure may represent an underestimate as small bowel polyps are more likely to come to clinical prominence.

Clinical features

The clinical diagnostic criteria for PJS are summarized in #38785Box 2.07. Presenting symptoms include abdominal pain, intestinal bleeding, anaemia, and intussusception, which typically manifest in the first two decades of life {17311531}. If present, the characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation allows diagnosis of asymptomatic patients in familial cases, but the characteristic PJS polyps constitute the main clinical hallmark. PJS is associated with a moderate or high risk of a range of malignancies with an overall lifetime risk of any cancer of 71–85% by the age of 70 years (#38786Table 2.29) {16707622;11113065;3587280}. Well-documented extraintestinal tumours include carcinoma of the breast (risk of 32–54% at 65–70 years of age), pancreas (risk of 11–36% at 65–70 years of age), and otherwise rare gonadal lesions including sex cord tumours with annular tubules of the ovary (risk as high as 21% at 70 years of age) and Sertoli cell tumours of the testis (risk as high as 9% at 70 years of age).

Epidemiology

PJS has an estimated incidence of 1 per 50 000 to 1 per 200 000 births.

Etiology

In PJS, a germline pathogenic variant affecting STK11, a tumour suppressor gene encoding a serine/threonine kinase, can be found in > 90% of patients. Most germline alterations are represented by point mutations and small intragenic deletions, but some larger deletions involving a few exons have been reported {17924967}. Splice variants have also been reported in the minor intron of STK11 (LKB1) {15608654}, which has a tumour suppressor effect that leads to an increase in intestinal polyps and eventually cancer {28965864}.

Pathogenesis

Peutz–Jeghers polyps bear a striking resemblance to sporadic prolapse polyps and it has been suggested that mucosal prolapse is central to the pathogenesis of Peutz–Jeghers polyps. The direct precursor to gastrointestinal cancer in patients with PJS remains unknown. Peutz–Jeghers polyps are probably an epiphenomenon to the cancer-prone condition and not obligate malignant precursors. Indeed, dysplasia in a Peutz–Jeghers polyp is exceedingly rare. In normal colonic crypts from patients with PJS, a protracted clonal evolution has been shown, allowing a greater number of variants to be retained over time, potentially explaining the increased risk of colorectal cancer {16344569;21940722}. Although STK11 (formerly LKB1) has been shown to regulate the intracellular energy sensor AMPK, how these genetic defects provoke benign hamartoma formation or mucocutaneous pigmentation and drive cancer progression remains unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearance of PJS polyps is not distinctive.

Histopathology

Full-blown colorectal Peutz–Jeghers polyps have a characteristic histology with villous architecture and a distinct arborizing smooth muscle core. Epithelial misplacement due to prolapse is relatively common in Peutz–Jeghers polyps and may extend into the serosa, thus mimicking a well-differentiated invasive neoplasm {16344569}. Overdiagnosis is a known pitfall in this setting. Gastric Peutz–Jeghers polyps more often lack specific histology and are frequently not readily distinguishable from gastric juvenile polyps or sporadic hyperplastic polyps. Small syndromic polyps also lack distinctive features, instead mimicking mucosal prolapse or regenerative hyperplasia depending on the site. Without knowledge of the clinical context, one should therefore be cautious establishing a new diagnosis of PJS based on gastric polyps alone {26721304}.

There are few data on breast cancer phenotypes in PJS, but the very limited data suggest that, akin to cancers in BRCA2 carriers, breast cancers in PJS are probably of intermediate-grade to high-grade, ER-positive and PR-positive, and ERBB2-negative {30689838}.

Regarding the histopathology of pancreatic tumours arising in the context of PJS, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms are the most common {16682259;35714671}, with a recent addition of intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm {36056133}. They are well-established precursors of pancreatic adenocarcinoma.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A germline variant in the tumour suppressor gene STK11 encoding a serine/threonine kinase can be found in > 90% of patients with PJS. With modern techniques including copy-number analyses, the majority of patients can be linked to causative STK11 germline variants. There is no evidence for genetic heterogeneity (i.e. a second locus causing PJS).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Clinical diagnostic criteria for PJS are listed in #38785Box 2.07. See also #38784Table 2.30.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

For patients who meet the clinical criteria for PJS or with a pathogenic variant in STK11, recommend referral to a specialized team and encourage participation in any available clinical trials. Clinical genetic testing is recommended for any patient meeting the clinical criteria or with a family history of PJS. Germline pathogenic variants in STK11 are identified in most. Small bowel intussusception was previously a major source of mortality and morbidity in PJS, but this can be more effectively managed by push enteroscopy with so-called clean sweep polypectomy and treated by surgery. Small bowel polypectomy should be performed for all polyps causing symptoms and polyps > 10 mm, to prevent polyp-related complications. The prognosis for patients with PJS is now mainly determined by the risk of malignancy, and an increased cancer mortality has been shown in PJS {21205875}. Patients with PJS should be surveilled (#38786Table 2.29) to prevent gastrointestinal complications and cancer {25645574}, but limited data exist regarding the efficacy of the various screening modalities in PJS.

Nevertheless, in the pancreas, given the association with cancer precursors (e.g. intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms, intraductal tubulopapillary neoplasm), a specific screening strategy for early diagnosis should be adopted.
