4.7 Helicases

Helicases are dynamic proteins that move along the DNA or RNA backbone, separating two hybridized strands as required for access for replication, transcription, translation, recombination, or repair. The human genome encodes 95 helicases, 64 for RNA, and 31 for DNA.

Four syndromes in the current classification are related to these processes.

Bloom syndrome results from loss of function of BLM, a RECQ helicase, critical for maintenance of genomic integrity and resolving stalled replication forks. It is this genome instability that leads to cancer predisposition, with increased chromosome instability, excessive homologous recombination and an increased number of sister chromatid exchanges {1518822;14263770;4140506}.

Werner syndrome is caused by loss of the ubiquitously expressed WRN protein, which has both helicase and exonuclease activities and plays a key role in DNA metabolism, including DNA replication, recombination, and repair, telomere maintenance, and gene expression {27859906;26984941;26993153}. The resulting genomic instability produces a predisposition to cancer.

Rothmund–Thomson syndrome is due to pathogenic variants in either RECQL4, associated with increased risk of osteosarcoma, or in ANAPC1, a component of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) {31303264;20301415}. Both APC1 and RECQL4 proteins are involved in DNA replication and repair.

DDX41-related haematological tumour predisposition syndrome results in loss of an ATP-dependent RNA helicase involved in various aspects of RNA metabolism, which results in a predisposition to haematolymphoid neoplasms.
