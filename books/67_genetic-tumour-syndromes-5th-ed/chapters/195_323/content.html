1.9.5 Hereditary thrombocytopenia and haematological cancer predisposition syndrome (RUNX1)

Definition

Hereditary thrombocytopenia and haematological cancer predisposition syndrome is a heterogeneous condition exemplified by RUNX1 familial platelet disorder with associated myeloid malignancy (RUNX1-FPDMM). RUNX1-FPDMM is an autosomal dominant cancer predisposition disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in the RUNX1 gene, characterized by prolonged bleeding and/or easy bruising with an increased risk of developing a haematological neoplasm.

MIM numbering

601399 Platelet disorder, familial, with associated myeloid malignancy; FPDMM

ICD-11 coding

3B64.01 Hereditary thrombocytopenia

Related terminology

Acceptable: RUNX1-familial platelet disorder.

Not recommended: familial thrombocytopenia; inherited thrombocytopenia.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Neoplasms associated with RUNX1-FPDMM usually involve the bone marrow.

Clinical features

The primary feature is persistent and unexplained mild to moderate thrombocytopenia. Associated qualitative platelet dysfunction leads to heavy and prolonged bleeding with or without a history of easy bruising. The associated bleeding features are seen in about 90% of cases. A minority of individuals require blood transfusions {10508512;28637618}. Rare individuals show overlapping phenotypic features of thrombocytopenia and myelodysplasia due to microdeletions of chromosome 21 encompassing RUNX1 {19679353;18487507}. The penetrance of germline RUNX1 pathogenic variants is largely unknown. Skin manifestations (e.g. eczema, psoriasis) have been reported in about 50% of patients {33661592}.

Between 20% and 50% of patients develop myeloid neoplasms, primarily acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) and myelodysplastic neoplasm (MDS). Some studies have reported an association with T- and B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma {26316320}.

Epidemiology

It is estimated that about 44% of individuals with RUNX1-FPDMM will develop AML or MDS, with a median age of onset of 33 years {32315381;25311743}. AML associated with RUNX1-FPDMM shows a male predominance among affected individuals {20722699}.

Etiology

The syndrome is caused by a pathogenic / likely pathogenic (P/LP) germline RUNX1 variant, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, insertion, or deletion, and, rarely, exon-spanning deletions and duplications.

Non-RUNX1 inherited thrombocytopenia is associated with other genetic alterations, such as sequence variants in the 5′ untranslated region of ANKRD26 (ANKRD26-related thrombocytopenia) {32618208} and variants in DDX41 {34473945}, ETV6 {32406789}, GATA1 {36231035}, and GATA2 {31069978}. Genetic causes of nearly half the cases of familial thrombocytopenia are unknown.

Pathogenesis

RUNX1 is part of the core-binding factor (CBF), a transcription factor complex comprising a DNA-binding CBFα subunit (RUNX1, RUNX2, or RUNX3) and a non–DNA binding heterodimerization partner CBFβ subunit (encoded by the CBFB gene). RUNX1 is a master transcriptional regulator of adult haematopoiesis expressed by all haematopoietic lineages. The molecular pathogenesis of RUNX1-FPDMM mainly involves loss-of-function missense variants throughout the RUNX1 gene, with P/LP variants being mostly clustered in the runt-homology domain {34233450}. Splice variants leading to reduced expression of RUNX1 proteins have been identified, and large deletions are also common.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

Peripheral blood smear evaluation typically shows a mild to moderate decrease in the number of platelets, which are generally of normal size and granulation {28659335}.

Myeloid neoplasms arising in individuals with P/LP germline RUNX1 variants show recurrent histopathological features, although none are pathognomonic {34028637}. The bone marrow is often hypocellular. Megakaryocytes are normal or slightly decreased in number but often have abnormal morphology characterized by small size and nuclear hypolobation {28659335;30600763;28059092}. Morphological changes in megakaryocytes may be sufficient to mimic the megakaryocytic dysplasia observed in MDS. However, a thorough evaluation of dysplastic changes in other lineages, blast percentage, and testing for additional clonal genetic abnormalities must be performed before making a diagnosis of MDS {28659335}. In contrast, the bone marrow findings in immune thrombocytopenic purpura, which is often a differential diagnosis, includes frequent hypercellularity and increased numbers of megakaryocytes, which are usually unremarkable in morphology {28659335;30600763}.

Immunophenotypic abnormalities of CD34-positive haematopoietic stem cells can be detected by flow cytometry in patients with germline RUNX1 variants without MDS/AML {26800764;28659335}.

Cytology

See Histopathology, above.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Many individuals are suspected to have a P/LP germline RUNX1 variant during workup for a haematological malignancy. Variant analysis using a multigene panel on skin fibroblast culture is needed to confirm the germline origin of the RUNX1 variant {29365323}. Once detected, the variants should be curated using standard criteria to evaluate the risk of myeloid malignancy {31648317;35764482}.

A subset of individuals has exon-spanning deletions or duplications, which may not be detected by amplicon-based targeted next-generation sequencing techniques, so alternative techniques for copy-number analysis, such as SNP arrays, must be considered {31723833}. These patients tend to have clonal haematopoiesis {26316320;26492932}. Genetic counselling and screening of family members should also be considered.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: detection of a P/LP germline RUNX1 variant.

Desirable: detection of P/LP germline RUNX1 variant in other family members; personal or family history of thrombocytopenia, prolonged bleeding, easy bruising, and/or myeloid neoplasia or acute leukaemia.

Prognosis and prediction

Individuals with RUNX1-FPDMM have a 25–50% lifetime risk of developing a haematological malignancy {32208489}. The prognosis for patients who develop MDS or AML is generally poor {26884589}.
