1.8.1 Tuberous sclerosis (TSC1, TSC2)

Definition

Tuberous sclerosis is a group of autosomal dominant disorders caused by a germline pathogenic variant of TSC1 on 9q34.13 or TSC2 on 16p13.3 and characterized by hamartomas and benign neoplastic lesions that affect the CNS and various non-neural tissues.

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Tuberous sclerosis 1; tuberous sclerosis 2

Localization

Tuberous sclerosis affects particularly the brain and extraneural sites including the skin, heart, lungs, kidneys, and eyes.

Clinical features

See #37757Table 2.39.

Neurological symptoms are among the most frequently observed and serious manifestations of tuberous sclerosis. The most common initial signs are focal seizures and epileptic spasms (in 80–90% of cases). Neuropsychiatric manifestations are common including a combination of behavioural, psychiatric, and intellectual disorders that may occur in the majority of patients (> 60%) including autism spectrum (40–50%), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; 30–50%), and depressive and anxiety disorders (30–60%) {2039135;20331728;20301399}.

CNS lesions in tuberous sclerosis include cortical dysplasias (tubers and white matter glioneuronal hamartomas), subependymal nodules, and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas {30976976;35963265}.

Cardiac rhabdomyomas are often a presenting feature in newborns and infants aged < 2 years, and more than half of cardiac rhabdomyomas are associated with tuberous sclerosis.

Cutaneous manifestations include hypomelanotic nodules, facial angiofibromas, and shagreen patches. Ungual (or subungual) fibromas often develop in childhood.

Renal angiomyolipomas develop by the age of 10 years in as many as 80% of people with tuberous sclerosis. Renal cysts are present in as many as 20% of affected individuals, but polycystic kidney disease only occurs in 3–5%.

Lymphangioleiomyomatosis severely impairs lung function and may be fatal; it is present in as many as 40% of women with tuberous sclerosis. All the phenotypic features of tuberous sclerosis can also occur sporadically. Sporadic angiomyolipomas are typically solitary, whereas tuberous sclerosis–associated angiomyolipomas are often multiple or bilateral.

Epidemiology

The incidence of tuberous sclerosis complex has been estimated between 1:6000 and 1:10 000 live births. Recent data from the United Kingdom estimated the incidence at 1:5800 live births, and a study in Germany using the most recent diagnostic criteria estimated the incidence rate from 1:6760 to 1:13 520 live births {34399110;2039137;30016967}.

Etiology

Tuberous sclerosis is caused by a pathogenic variant of TSC1 (~20% of cases) or TSC2 (~80% of cases) {10205261;18722871;17005952;28222202;28968464}.

The TSC1 gene

TSC1 maps to chromosome 9q34.13 {2889832} and contains 23 exons using transcript NM_000368.5 {9242607}, 21 of which carry protein-coding information (exons 3–23).

Gene sequence variants: Most common variants in the TSC1 gene are small deletions and nonsense variants (each accounting for ~30% of all variants in the gene) {11030407;10227394;10205261}. Virtually all variants are inactivating, leading to loss-of-function effects on the encoded protein {10227394}. Large deletions of the TSC1 gene are rare.

Gene expression: The TSC1-encoded protein, hamartin, has a molecular weight of 130 kDa. Hamartin is strongly expressed in brain, kidney, and heart, all of which are tissues frequently affected in tuberous sclerosis {10349994}. Its pattern of expression overlaps with that of tuberin, the product of the TSC2 gene.

The TSC2 gene

TSC2 maps to chromosome 16p13.3 {1303246} and contains 42 exons (NM_000548.5), with exons 2–42 encoding the functional protein.

Gene sequence variants: The spectrum of TSC2 pathogenic variants is wider than that for TSC1; it includes large deletions and missense variants, and (less frequently) splice-junction variants {9863590;11112665;10205261}. Exons 16, 33, and 40 have the highest number of sequence variants. Large deletions in TSC2 may extend into the adjacent PKD1 gene, with a resulting phenotype of tuberous sclerosis and polycystic kidney disease {17185137;10227394}. Multiple studies of genotype–phenotype correlations have demonstrated that TSC2 variants are associated with a more-severe phenotype overall than TSC1 variants {28222202;28968464}.

Gene expression: TSC2 encodes a large transcript, which shows widespread expression in many tissues, including the brain and other organs affected in tuberous sclerosis. Alternatively spliced mRNAs have been reported {8519695}. A portion of the 180 kDa protein product tuberin has substantial homology with the catalytic domain of RAP1GAP, a member of the RAS family.

Like in other tumour suppressor gene syndromes, somatic inactivation of the remaining wildtype allele for the TSC1 or TSC2 locus has been described. This usually occurs through a second somatic inactivating variant, loss of the wildtype allele, or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity exposing the germline variant, and it has been reported in kidney and cardiac lesions associated with tuberous sclerosis, as well as in subependymal giant cell astrocytomas {15624760}. However, there is conflicting evidence of whether a so-called second hit is required for cortical tuber formation, raising the possibility that some lesions in tuberous sclerosis may be due to haploinsufficiency {11468687;16912167}. Recent studies have predominantly found activating mTOR sequence variants in focal cortical dysplasias, but rarely TSC1 and TSC2 variants as well {31444548;28215400}. In recent studies, loss of TSC1 in periventricular zone neuronal stem cells was sufficient to cause aberrant migration and giant-cell phenotype, supportive of the two-hit hypothesis for tuber formation {22068588;21828270}.

Most of the cases in which no sequence variant is identified (~15% of patients with tuberous sclerosis) {11112665;10205261} are found to be somatic mosaics {26540169}.

Inheritance and genetic heterogeneity

Most tuberous sclerosis cases (~60%) are sporadic, indicating a high rate of de novo mutations {2918523}. In affected kindreds, the disease follows an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, with high penetrance but considerable phenotypic variability {7670658}.

Pathogenesis

Pathogenic variants in TSC1 or TSC2 cause hyperactivation of mTOR signalling and consequent deregulation of cell growth resulting in the development of hamartomas and tumours in various organ systems. Tuberin, hamartin, and TBC1D7 form a heteromeric protein complex (known as TSC) that functions as a signalling node that integrates growth factor and stress signals from the upstream PI3K/AKT pathway and transmits signals downstream to coordinate multiple cellular processes, including cell proliferation and cell size {26893383;2889832;1303246;9809973}. The complex negatively regulates the mTOR pathway {16288294;12172555;12271141}. Disruption of TSC causes upregulation of the mTOR pathway and increases proliferation and cell growth through two effector molecules: 4EBP1 and S6K1 {16288294;12271141}.

The understanding of the basic mechanism of mTOR pathway activation in tuberous sclerosis lesions has led to the use of mTOR inhibitors in the treatment of manifestations of tuberous sclerosis. mTOR inhibitors (rapamycin or everolimus) have been shown to be effective in reducing seizure frequency in children with refractory epilepsy {23730262;30169322}. Additionally, several associated tumours (renal angiomyolipomas, subependymal giant cell astrocytomas, and lymphangioleiomyomatosis) show marked size reduction and tumour-progression control in response to treatment with mTOR inhibitors {23158522;25456370;27351628;27409709}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of a germline pathogenic sequence variant in TSC1 or TSC2 is sufficient for the diagnosis or prediction of tuberous sclerosis complex regardless of clinical findings {34399110}, and occurs in 75–90% of patients {34399110}. Individuals with tuberous sclerosis complex who meet the diagnostic criteria but in whom a pathogenic variant cannot be detected are usually referred to as “no mutation identified”. In over half of such individuals, mosaic or intronic variants in either TSC1 or TSC2 have been detected {20165957;25927202;26540169;30255984}.

Clinical diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria for tuberous sclerosis were revised in 2021 at the International Tuberous Sclerosis Complex Consensus Conference and are based on genetic testing and/or clinical manifestations {34399110}. The molecular diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis complex is established in a proband by the identification of a heterozygous pathogenic gene variant in either TSC1 or TSC2 by molecular genetic testing, regardless of clinical findings. Antenatal diagnosis by sequence variant analysis is possible when parents or other family members are known to be affected {32623496}.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Tuberous sclerosis tends to shorten lifespan slightly (as compared with lifespan in a White control population) {1861550}. The most common causes of death in the second decade of life are brain tumours and status epilepticus, followed by renal abnormalities {24053982;27935023}. In patients aged > 40 years, mortality is most commonly associated with renal abnormalities (i.e. cystic disease or neoplasm) or lymphangioleiomyomatosis.

Requirements for diagnosis:

Definite tuberous sclerosis: Two major features or one major feature with two minor features.

Possible tuberous sclerosis: Either one major feature or at least two minor features.

Genetic diagnosis: A pathogenic variant in TSC1 or TSC2 is diagnostic of tuberous sclerosis (most tuberous sclerosis–causing variants are sequence variants that clearly prevent TSC1 or TSC2 protein production; some variants compatible with protein production, e.g. some missense changes, are well established as disease causing; other variant types should be considered with caution).

CNS

Cortical tubers

Disorganized cortex with disrupted cortical lamination; dysmorphic, markedly enlarged neurons; balloon cells (also known as giant cells); dense fibrillary gliosis; calcification of blood vessel walls and/or parenchyma; myelin loss

Mixed glioneuronal immunophenotype is typical of balloon cells:

·   glial markers: vimentin, GFAP, S100

·   neuronal markers: neurofilaments, class III β-tubulin, MAP2, connexin 26, connexin 32
 

White matter glioneuronal heterotopias and radial migration lanes

Linear or flame-shaped bands radiating from the periventricular zone to the subcortical white matter; dysplastic glioneuronal cells including balloon cells

As for cortical tubers
 

Subependymal nodule (SEN)

Classically located in the lateral ventricles at the thalamostriatal sulcus (frontal portion of the lateral ventricle)

Multiple contiguous SENs along the ventricular surface are known as candle guttering

Histologically identical to SEGAs (below) and considered to be their precursor lesion

As for SEGAs
 

Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA)

Circumscribed, moderately cellular tumours composed of a varied glial phenotype including polygonal cells with abundant, glassy cytoplasm to smaller spindle cells and gemistocyte-like cells arranged in sweeping fascicles, sheets, or ependymal-like perivascular arrangements

Giant pyramidal-like cells with a ganglionic-like appearance (without Nissl substance)

Rich vascular stroma with frequent hyalinized vessels and infiltration by mast cells and lymphocytes; parenchymal or vascular calcifications

Mitotic figures are rare, as is necrosis or microvascular proliferation

Mixed glioneuronal immunophenotype:

·   glial markers: GFAP, S100, variable OLIG2

·   neuronal markers: synaptophysin, NeuN, neurofilaments, class III β-tubulin, MAP2

Rare cases have BRAF p.Val600Glu variants

Specific DNA methylation class

Kidney

Angiomyolipoma

Usually well-circumscribed but not encapsulated solid masses, with cystic areas due to haemorrhage in larger tumours; tumours arise in the kidney without site predilection

Composed of adipose tissue and spindle and epithelioid smooth muscle cells with abnormal thick-walled blood vessels intimately admixed

Lipophages and giant cells can be seen, as well as cytological atypia mimicking lipoblasts with scalloped nuclei and multivacuolated cytoplasm or hibernoma-like features

When the smooth muscle component predominates, tumour cells are predominantly spindle shaped and arranged in bundles or with a lymphangioleiomyomatosis pattern

Immunoexpression of the melanogenesis markers HMB45 and melan-A, and cathepsin K is seen in most cases

Heart

Rhabdomyoma

Mostly intramural ventricular tumours but may also occur in the atria; intracavitary pedunculated or sessile masses may occur

Well-demarcated, unencapsulated nodules, easily distinguished from the normal myocardium

Composed of enlarged swollen myocytes with clear cytoplasm; strands of cytoplasm are connected to the so-called spider-cell feature of the peripheral cell membrane

Immunoexpression of markers of striated muscle cells: myoglobin, desmin, actin, and vimentin

Lung

Lymphangioleiomyomatosis

Composed of cytologically bland anastomosing lymphatic channels lined by a layer of flattened lymphatic endothelial cells; collagen layers or spindle cells resembling smooth muscle cells are present between channels

Lining cells are positive for CD31 and ERG, and with D2-40, and the stromal spindle cells express vimentin, desmin, and actin, as well as PR, keratin, and HMB45

Eye

Retinal astrocytic hamartomas

Well-circumscribed, slightly elevated lesions arising in the inner retina; most commonly along the arcades, and less commonly in locations adjacent to the optic nerve and in the periphery

Two main histopathological patterns: one is composed of fascicles of spindle-shaped astrocytes with cytologically bland nuclei; the second, also termed giant cell astrocytoma of the retina, resembles subependymal giant cell astrocytoma

Calcification and necrosis are common

Glial differentiation with GFAP

Retinal giant cell astrocytoma shows both glial and neuronal immunohistochemical markers
