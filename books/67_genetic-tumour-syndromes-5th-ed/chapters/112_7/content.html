1.1.2 Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (RET)

Definition

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by activating pathogenic variants in the RET proto-oncogene.

MIM numbering

171400 Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type IIA; MEN2A

162300 Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type IIB; MEN2B

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.0 Multiple polyglandular tumours

Related terminology

Acceptable: MEN2 syndrome, MEN2A, MEN2B; familial medullary thyroid carcinoma.

Not recommended: Sipple syndrome; multiple endocrine neoplasia type 3 (MEN3); Gorlin syndrome; Wagenmann–Froboese syndrome; mucosal neuroma syndrome; Farndon syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type 2A; multiple endocrine neoplasia, type 2B (formerly MEN3)

Localization

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), the hallmark of this syndrome, involves the thyroid gland, and specifically arises most often at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the lateral lobes. The disease begins as microscopic proliferations (classified as nodular C-cell hyperplasia) in this same location, usually bilaterally, and progresses to multifocal invasive carcinoma.

Phaeochromocytoma arises in the adrenal medulla and is often bilateral and multifocal. Extra-adrenal paragangliomas are not usually seen in this disorder.

Parathyroid proliferations arise in the parathyroid glands and are bilateral in 70% of patients.

Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis can be identified anywhere on the skin but most commonly involves the interscapular back.

Hirschsprung disease and oral-intestinal ganglioneuromas are manifestations of abnormal development of the myenteric and submucosal ganglionic plexus of the gastrointestinal tract. Loss of function leading to Hirschsprung disease most often affects the rectosigmoid colon. Ganglioneuromas can occur anywhere from the lips to the anus.

Clinical features

The disorder has several subtypes. MEN2A is the classic form, characterized by the development of MTC often associated with phaeochromocytoma and/or parathyroid proliferations. MEN2B (originally called MEN3) is a rare subtype characterized by highly aggressive early-onset MTC associated with a high risk of phaeochromocytoma, oral and intestinal ganglioneuromas, and a marfanoid body habitus. MEN2B is not associated with parathyroid disease.

Isolated MTC can occur. Formerly known as familial MTC, it is recognized as a subtype of MEN2A by the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines {25810047}. Other subtypes include MEN2A with cutaneous lichen amyloidosis and MEN2A with Hirschsprung disease.

Patients with MEN2 usually present with signs and symptoms of MTC including neck mass(es) sometimes causing pain, hoarseness, coughing, dysphagia, and/or dyspnoea. Occasional patients experience diarrhoea and flushing caused by calcitonin excess. Rarer presentations are due to phaeochromocytoma, which may cause hypertension, headache, tachycardia, sweating, tremors, and/or anxiety. Patients with Hirschsprung disease present in childhood with constipation, diarrhoea, vomiting, and/or abdominal distention {24972642}. Patients with MEN2B have a marfanoid habitus and ganglioneuromas may be seen on oral examination.

Hirschsprung disease affects the gastrointestinal tract in 7% of MEN2A patients with variants involving exon 10 {20979234;24972642}.

Mucosal neuromas in MEN2B can involve the oropharynx, lips, and eyelids with the addition of thickened corneal nerves, and ganglioneuromatosis of the gastrointestinal tract with neuroma or diffuse ganglioneuromatosis {4957444;5660196;11356339;8880581}.

Increasingly MEN2 is being diagnosed early owing to genetic screening of known kindreds.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of MEN2 is estimated to be about 1 per 30 000 population {26494384;25810047;30349395}. The incidence of MEN2A is about 1 per 1 973 500 individuals and MEN2B is much lower, at 1 per 38 750 000 individuals. The M:F ratio is about 1:1 but some studies have shown a slight female predominance. Geographical heterogeneity of RET variants has been documented {34757920}, probably due to founder effects in different populations {27809725}.

Etiology

Monoallelic gain-of-function pathogenic germline variants of RET induce constitutive activation of the RET receptor and are responsible for MEN2. In any given patient with one of the associated disorders, a negative family history does not exclude germline disease, as the disorder can skip generations, be caused by de novo pathogenic variants (16%) {40128946}, and be misdiagnosed.

MEN2B is more often due to de novo germline variants.

Pathogenesis

The RET proto-oncogene on 10q11.21 was discovered in 1985 owing to its ability to transform NIH-3T3 cells {2992805}. Activating RET proto-oncogene germline variants were reported to cause MEN2A a few years later {8099202}, followed by the detection of germline variants in MEN2B and familial MTC patients {7824936;7906866}.

The RET gene encodes a tyrosine kinase receptor. Physiologically, the RET receptor is activated by GDNF family ligands bound to GFRαs and forms dimers, recruited to lipid rafts {32816064}. Specific tyrosine residues within the tyrosine kinase domain of RET are autophosphorylated after RET dimer formation, which in turn activates MAPK, PI3K, JAK/STAT, PKA, and PKC {32816064}. Intracellular RET signalling is needed for the development of the enteric nervous system, kidney, and urinary tract {32816064}.

There are clear correlations between genotype and phenotype. MEN2A variants are mainly found in cysteines of codons 609, 611, 618, 620 (exon 10), or 634 (exon 11) of the extracellular domain leading to bisulfide-bonded homodimers and constitutive activation of RET (protein reference sequence NP_066124.1, corresponding to transcript variant 2 [NM_020975.6]) {7907913;8918855;21552134}. NM_020975.6:c.1900T>C p.(Cys634Arg) is the most frequent pathogenic variant and is associated with the classic triad of MTC, phaeochromocytoma, and hyperparathyroidism. Familial MTC is characterized by sequence variants in exons 8, 13, 14, and 15 that do not affect cysteines, or by variants in codons 609–620. The NM_020975.6:c.2647G>A p.(Ala883Thr) RET variant has induced MTC in a family in a homozygous setting only. In about 2% of patients with MEN2, no variants in RET can be detected and therefore it is still unclear whether other genes can lead to MEN2 {20516206}.

There are also correlations between genotype and aggressiveness of MTC that allowed the ATA to classify pathogenic sequence variants of the RET gene into three different categories (highest, high, and moderate) based on the risk of aggressive MTC {25810047}. In MEN2A, the closer to the cell membrane the mutant cysteine residue lies, the higher the transforming ability {18936155;20979234}. The highest-risk category includes patients with MEN2B and the NM_020975.6:c.2753T>C p.(Met918Thr) RET variant, while the high-risk category includes patients with RET variants at codon 634, i.e. NM_020975.6:c.1901G>T p.(Cys634Phe), NM_020975.6:c.1900T>G p.(Cys634Gly), NM_020975.6:c.1900T>C p.(Cys634Arg), NM_020975.6:c.1900T>A p.(Cys634Ser), NM_020975.6:c.1902C>G p.(Cys634Trp), or NM_020975.6:c.1901G>A p.(Cys634Tyr); and/or the NM_020975.6:c.2647_2648delinsTT p.(Ala883Phe) RET variant. The moderate-risk category includes patients with all other listed pathogenic RET variants. The common RET variants and their related risk of aggressive MTC and development of phaeochromocytoma and hyperparathyroidism are described in #37173Table 2.03. All reported RET sequence variants are deposited in the multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 RET proto-oncogene database {19177457}.

The vast majority of patients with MEN2B carry the RET NM_020975.6:c.2753T>C p.(Met918Thr) variant in the kinase domain leading to constitutive autophosphorylation. RET NM_020975.6:c.2647_2648delinsTT p.(Ala883Phe) is found in 2–3% of patients with MEN2B {9360560}.

Usually, biallelic loss-of-function germline sequence variants of RET lead to a non-functional RET receptor and are a cause of Hirschsprung disease. However, the association of Hirschsprung disease with MEN2 implicates activation of this oncogene as interference with normal development of the enteric plexuses.

Macroscopic appearance

MTC in this disorder is typically bilateral and multicentric. The cut surface of the tumours is white to yellow and homogeneous {33492588}.

Phaeochromocytomas have a tan-white to dusky haemorrhagic appearance. The adrenals from patients with MEN2 can contain two or more separate phaeochromocytomas. The interposed adrenal medulla is expanded, and this may be apparent grossly {23478233;25379023}. Patients with longstanding MEN2 can develop multiple tumours with a spectrum of degenerative changes.

Histopathology

Medullary thyroid carcinoma

MEN2-associated MTC may be histologically indistinguishable from sporadic MTC, but familial cases are usually multifocal, bilateral, and associated with C-cell hyperplasia {7285384}. Complete sampling of prophylactically removed thyroids is important to identify medullary thyroid microcarcinomas. The junction of the upper and middle thirds of the lateral lobes has a high density of C cells and is a common location to identify microcarcinomas.

Histological patterns of MTC include solid, follicular (tubular/glandular), papillary, spindled, oxyphilic, paraganglioma-like, insular, angiosarcoma-like, and pleomorphic/anaplastic {6840715;3068650;31727331;26293669;33687689;6999920}; rare melanotic, squamous, giant cell, clear cell, and small cell patterns exist {2705868;30424779;8838338;2917321}. Amyloid, seen in as many as 75–90% of MTCs, is deposition of a calcitonin precursor {26304852}. Calcification and mucin production may also be seen {6687422}. MTCs are graded based on the International Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma Grading System based on necrosis, mitotic activity, and Ki-67 index {34731032}; small cell features, vascular invasion, or stromal desmoplasia may also predict more-aggressive behaviour. Mixed follicular/MTC and mixed papillary/MTC can occur in MEN2 {9174630;8495962;32660411}. MTC must be distinguished from follicular cell–derived thyroid carcinomas as well as from other neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) {33687689} based on immunoreactivity for low-molecular-weight keratin, INSM1, chromogranin A, synaptophysin, calcitonin, CGRP, TTF1, and monoclonal CEA; MTC is negative for thyroglobulin and monoclonal PAX8 (clone MRQ-50) {31912298}.

Progression of C-cell hyperplasia to neoplasia is a characteristic feature of MEN2-associated MTC. The name “hyperplasia” may not be appropriate, as the C-cell proliferations in this setting are clonal {33492588}. Definitions of C-cell hyperplasia are controversial, and with the ready availability of genetic testing and recommendations that all patients with MTC be offered this counselling and testing, identification of the presence or absence of C-cell hyperplasia increasingly lacks clinical relevance {30694510}. Furthermore, physiological or reactive/secondary C-cell hyperplasia can be seen in hypercalcaemia, hyperparathyroidism, increasing age, hypergastrinaemia, chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, and adjacent non-medullary thyroid tumours {8989229;6175219;3899904;16840909;8200646}.

Medullary thyroid microcarcinoma measures ≤ 10 mm. The distinction of medullary thyroid microcarcinoma from C-cell hyperplasia can be difficult. Helpful features include loss of organoid arrangement, extension beyond the basement membrane, desmoplastic stromal reaction, and surrounding infiltrating tumour cells {11688458}; however, these features may be difficult to recognize {23478233}. Even very small medullary microcarcinomas can behave aggressively {22399508} and metastases are reported in tumours < 2 mm. Hereditary medullary thyroid microcarcinomas are more often multifocal and more often have positive nodal status compared with sporadic medullary microcarcinomas {22399508}. In a SEER Program database (1988–2007) study of 310 medullary thyroid microcarcinomas, 37% (65 of 176) with any lymph nodes removed had metastases, including 23% of tumours ≤ 5 mm {21717441}.

Phaeochromocytoma

MEN2-associated phaeochromocytomas may be indistinguishable from sporadic forms; however, they are often bilateral and associated with adrenal medullary hyperplasia, defined as the medulla making up more than one third of the thickness of the adrenal gland or extending beyond its normal location in the apex and body (i.e. involving the tail of the adrenal) {23478233;24476517}. Progression of adrenal medullary hyperplasia to phaeochromocytomas is a characteristic of MEN2-associated phaeochromocytoma. In MEN2, the term microphaeochromocytoma has replaced “nodular hyperplasia” as this is a clonal process. Phaeochromocytomas in MEN2 generally have a low risk of aggressive behaviour and produce both norepinephrine and epinephrine. Composite phaeochromocytomas can also occur in MEN2 {27704704;28760150}.

Other pathologies

Parathyroid proliferations associated with hyperparathyroidism can be seen in 15–30% of individuals with MEN2A but are not seen in MEN2B. They should be classified as adenomas rather than hyperplasia when there is multiglandular disease that is more likely to be multiple neoplasms involving single or multiple glands.

Hirschsprung disease is characterized by a complete absence of ganglion cells in the myenteric and submucosal plexus; this most often involves the rectosigmoid colon {20979234;24972642}.

Mucosal neuromas in MEN2B that involve the oropharynx, lips, and eyelids are characterized by disorganized bundles of nerves; in contrast, ganglioneuromatosis of the gastrointestinal tract features admixtures of ganglion cells {1063979}.

Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis, thought to be due to repeated scratching due to intense pruritus {8757765;12864791;29420094}, is histologically characterized by globular deposits of amyloid in the papillary dermis with overlying epidermal hyperkeratosis and acanthosis.

Cytology

Cytology may be performed in MTC; it is not usually performed in the setting of MEN2 but bilateral lesions should suggest this diagnosis. Biopsy of adrenal lesions is not indicated given the risk of hypertensive crisis.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of MEN2 is confirmed by the identification of a germline variant in the RET gene that is known to be pathogenic. It is recommended that germline DNA testing be performed on any patient with MTC regardless of age, lack of associated disorders, or lack of family history.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: documented pathogenic variant of the RET gene; AND/OR MTC associated with any other feature of MEN2.

Desirable: C-cell hyperplasia; adrenal medullary hyperplasia; multifocal MTC or phaeochromocytoma.

Staging

Malignant neoplasms associated with this syndrome generally follow cancer staging suggested by Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis in MEN2 is determined by MTC, and much less or not at all by phaeochromocytoma, parathyroid hyperplasia, or other manifestations of MEN2B.

The prognosis for MTC can be predicted by a grading system based on the Ki-67 proliferation index, mitotic count, and presence or absence of necrosis {34731032;32452872;32313184}. MTC prognosis for patients with MEN2 is superior to that of patients with sporadic MTC, with 10-year survival rates depending on disease stage, currently at 100%, 93%, 71%, and 21% for stage I, II, III, and IV, respectively {9578814}. This is probably the result of screening, early detection, and prophylactic surgery in this patient group. It has been firmly established that the various RET variants confer a range of risk profiles to individuals and families carrying these variants, with the p.Met918 MEN2B variant carrying the highest risk, followed by the p.Ala883 MEN2B and p.Cys634 MEN2A variants, responsible for a high-risk profile, and then finally followed by p.Cys609, p.Cys611, p.Cys618, and p.Cys620 variants, which give a moderate risk. Still other RET variants result in an MTC-only phenotype, including p.Gly533, p.Glu768, p.Leu790, p.Val804, and p.Ser891 {18062802;18248648;29142004;24561444}. An additional two RET sequence variants have been described as modifiers that result in a more-aggressive clinical course {23416954}. The risk profile has led to recommendations for prophylactic thyroidectomy in variant carriers in MEN2 families, with surgery performed before the age of 1 year for the highest-risk group of MEN2B patients and before the age of 5 years for those in the high-risk group of codon 634 variants in MEN2A. For those in the moderate-risk group, surgery may be postponed until the age of 10 years {28609830;25810047}. Apart from thyroid gland resection, central neck lymph node dissection is also performed, but lateral neck dissection is reserved for cases in which there would be suspicion of lymph node metastases, which in general is not the case in prophylactic surgery patients. C-cell hyperplasia is always found in thyroids of RET variant carriers, and the occurrence of small MTC is not uncommon. Apart from surgical guidelines on the basis of genetic risk status, biochemical screening for calcitonin levels plays an important role in the timing of surgery and postoperative follow-up. The latter is only necessary if an MTC has been found, as for C-cell hyperplasia no follow-up is indicated. In fact, postoperative biochemical cure is associated with very high 10-year survival rates, whereas short calcitonin doubling times are associated with poor prognosis, except for the rare cases in which an MTC no longer produces calcitonin on the basis of dedifferentiation.

Phaeochromocytoma development in patients with MEN2 is also determined by genotype, with the lifetime risk of phaeochromocytoma being almost 90% by the age of 80 years in patients with RET Cys634 sequence variants and 100% in patients with MEN2B with the Met918 sequence variants {23416954}. In contrast, the overall risk of phaeochromocytoma in patients with MEN2 is 40% and some RET variants, as mentioned above, give no risk at all of phaeochromocytoma {26494388}. Phaeochromocytoma is usually not the presenting tumour in patients with MEN2, although every patient needs to be screened for its presence in order to avoid life-threatening crises at thyroid surgery. In addition, biochemical screening for urinary metanephrines is imperative in the clinical follow-up of MEN2 patients {25810047}. Fewer than 5% of MEN2-related phaeochromocytomas give rise to metastasis {7595173}.

Parathyroid disorders are also related to the specific genetic defect in RET, occurring most frequently with NM_020975.6:c.1900T>C p.(Cys634Arg) variants {7907913;7641404}. Screening for hyperparathyroidism is indicated from the age of 11 years for the highest-risk and high-risk RET variants, and from the age of 16 years for the moderate-risk category {25810047}. In MEN2B, no parathyroid disease has been described.
