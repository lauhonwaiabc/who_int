0.4 Guidelines for the reporting of sequence variants in tumours

Introduction

Variation in the sequence of the human genome is the basis of heritable (germline or constitutional) disorders and may lead to a predisposition to non-heritable (somatic) disorders such as cancer. However, in both cases, there might also be environmental factors that are required to trigger appearance of the disease phenotype.

Documenting variation in our genomes is important for research and clinical care. Accuracy in the description of DNA variants is therefore essential {33252176;26920083;28122645}. The diagnosis and management of tumours increasingly depends on the demonstration of simple sequence variants (colloquially known as mutations), and other genetic alterations, such as translocations and gene fusions, that drive their causation. The discovery of such tumour-associated sequence variants not only facilitates the diagnosis of tumour types that are difficult to classify based on histomorphology and immunohistochemistry alone but can also be of prognostic and therapeutic value. To unify the description of such variants and to make new discoveries available for clinicians and pathologists, the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) developed a nomenclature for the standardized and unambiguous description of sequence variants {26919400;26931183;12938082;17251329}. This nomenclature is now maintained under the auspices of the Human Genome Organization (HUGO) {39702242} and has become the universally accepted nomenclature for describing all sequence variants, whether obtained from sequencing entire genomes, exomes, or panels of candidate genes. The resulting sequencing reports provide the basis for clinical interpretation of variants, leading to consensus recommendations for molecular pathology {25741868} and for clinical oncology {27993330}.

Biologists and clinicians around the world have applied these guidelines to report new discoveries in DNA sequence variation. However, there is evidence that full compliance with reporting standards is not universal and a recent analysis of reporting of variants in the histone H3.3 genes highlights these problems {33779999}.

In the production of this volume, we have endeavoured to comply with internationally accepted standards. However, we have had to acknowledge that there are longstanding reports of sequence variants in formats that are not standards compliant. We have attempted to convert all legacy variant descriptions into a standards-compliant format.

The following sections detail the issues that are relevant to producing standards-compliant sequence variant descriptions.

Gene symbols, names, and IDs

Every human gene is assigned a symbol, a name, and an ID by the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC, https://www.genenames.org/) {32747822}. Genes should always be referred to by their HGNC symbol to avoid confusion with genes that might have similar descriptions that are in common use or that have been used in the past. Gene symbols should always be written in italics, for example HRAS.

Every gene is assigned an ID by HGNC, and that ID is stable and is never changed. In this context, it is important to note that gene symbols and gene names may change over time. For example, the HGNC webpage for the HRAS gene (https://www.genenames.org/data/gene-symbol-report/#!/hgnc_id/HGNC:5173) shows that the symbol for this gene was previously HRAS1. In addition, the gene has had two previous names. When a gene symbol is first mentioned, its HGNC ID, which is HGNC:5173 for the HRAS gene, should also be reported.

Gene product names

Although HGNC assigns approved names to all genes, it might be convenient to refer to the gene product using a name that is shorter than the approved gene name. An additional problem is that some gene products might be known by several commonly used names. To mitigate possible confusion when referring to gene products, it has been proposed {33408252} that the HGNC gene symbol be used, but without italics to highlight the distinction between gene and gene product. Care must be taken regarding the appropriate use of italics to avoid confusion regarding whether the string of characters refers to the gene or to its product, namely, HRAS and HRAS. It should be noted that the commonly used gene product name is the same as the HGNC-assigned gene name.

Sequence variant descriptions

The HGVS Sequence Variant Nomenclature guidelines (https://hgvs-nomenclature.org/) {26931183;39702242} are perceived by some as being arcane and overcomplicated. However, correct implementation of the guidelines is essential to ensure accurate reporting of sequence variants, which then facilitates data exchange. It is worth noting that the term “variant” is used, not “mutation”, as the latter term tends to imply that the DNA sequence change is deleterious. The more neutral term “variant” is used to describe any DNA sequence change, deleterious or not.

The Sequence Variant Nomenclature guidelines are subdivided into logical sections and examples are provided within each section. A simple summary of the guidelines (https://hgvs-nomenclature.org/stable/background/simple/) describes the core concepts.

Reference sequences

Sequence variants are reported as changes relative to a reference sequence. It is vital that appropriate reference sequences be chosen for the variant that is being described. Guidance is provided regarding reference sequences (https://hgvs-nomenclature.org/stable/background/refseq/) and only sequences that are deemed to be valid may be used.

For variants described at the genome/chromosome level, chromosome reference sequences are required, and genome build GRCh38 is preferred. A valid reference sequence ID must always be included, such as NC_000017.11; “GRCh38” and “chr17” are not valid identifiers.

For variants described at the transcript level, the MANE Select transcript {35388217} for the gene in question should be used if it exists for that gene. For some genes, there may also be a MANE Plus Clinical transcript, which may be more appropriate for variant reporting in specific instances. A MANE Select transcript exists for most protein-coding genes across the genome. If no MANE Select transcript exists for your gene of interest, please select some other appropriate transcript.

Variants must always be described at the genome (g.), transcript (c.), and protein (p.) levels. A p. description must never be presented without the corresponding c. description.

A software tool (https://variantvalidator.org/service/gene2trans/) is available to help you select appropriate transcripts, MANE Select, or otherwise, for all human genes.

Locus Reference Genomic (LRG) gene and transcript sequences {20398331;24285302} are now obsolete and are no longer being actively supported by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and the European Molecular Biology Laboratory's European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI). No new LRG records will be created and both NCBI and EMBL-EBI recommend the use of MANE transcripts for clinical reporting {35388217}.

Validating sequence variant descriptions

It is critically important that variant descriptions be validated with respect to syntax and data content. The Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD) HGVS variant description syntax checker (https://lovd.nl/HGVS/) ensures correct syntax. If the syntax is correct, it is able to pass the variant description directly to VariantValidator {28967166;39358598} (https://variantvalidator.org/) to validate the data content of the description.

Where the author is confident of the description syntax, individual variants may be directly validated using the VariantValidator interactive validation tool (https://variantvalidator.org/service/validate/). A batch validation tool (https://variantvalidator.org/service/validate/batch/) is also provided to support validation of multiple variant descriptions. Full information is provided (https://github.com/openvar/VV_databases/blob/master/markdown/instructions.md) regarding how to use the batch tool and how to interpret any warning or error messages that might be generated. VariantValidator also provides an Application Programming Interface (API) (https://rest.variantvalidator.org/) for integration into diagnostic pipelines.

Gene fusions

Some types of tumour arise from gene fusions, such as between the BCAM and AKT2 genes, which is specific and unique to high-grade serous ovarian cancer. Until relatively recently, there was little agreement regarding how gene fusions should be formally described, leading to confusion on occasion. There are now formal guidelines from HGNC {34615987} for fusion genes that specify that a double colon (::) should be used as the separator between the two HGNC gene symbols, for example BCAM::AKT2. Note that the colons themselves are not written in italic type.

Where the precise breakpoint between fused genes has been determined by DNA sequence analysis, the breakpoint should also be reported using the standard HGVS Sequence Variant Nomenclature.

Gene fusions may result in the creation of adjoined transcripts for which there is an appropriate HGVS nomenclature recommendation (https://hgvs-nomenclature.org/stable/recommendations/RNA/adjoined_transcript/).
