0.5 Introduction to haematolymphoid genetic disorders

Genetic predisposition is assumed for a variety of haematolymphoid neoplasms, a subset of which is part of the spectrum of several monogenic tumour predisposition syndromes discussed in the present publication. Such monogenic predisposition can occur with or without syndromic features such as malformations, dysmorphisms, or developmental delay. Moreover, tumour predisposition can be restricted to the haematolymphoid system or affect other organ systems. Correspondingly, the presentation of these monogenic germline predispositions with haematolymphoid neoplasms follows the overall structure of the present issue. Examples include the RAS/MAPK pathway (e.g. Noonan syndrome), the mTOR pathway (e.g. activated PI3K-delta syndrome), transcription factors (e.g. CEBPA, RUNX1, ETV6, GATA2, PAX5, and IKZF family alterations), cell-cycle and apoptosis pathways (e.g. Li–Fraumeni syndrome, autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome [ALPS]), DNA repair and genomic instability pathways (e.g. constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome, MBD4, Fanconi anaemia, DDX41, ATM, NBN, BLM), and disorders of telomere maintenance or chromosomal aneuploidy syndromes (e.g. Down syndrome).

The applied pathway-based hierarchical classification leaves few emerging or rare germline tumour syndromes with predisposition to haematolymphatic neoplasms currently unmapped. One example is the SAMD9-related haematological tumour predisposition syndrome (MIRAGE syndrome [myelodysplasia, infection, restriction of growth, adrenal hypoplasia, genital phenotypes, and enteropathy]; MIM 617053). There is evidence that proteins encoded by SAMD9 and its paralog SAMD9L (SAMD9-like) are involved in endosome fusion and that SAMD9 has a role in growth factor signal transduction, but data are not yet sufficient to classify it in the present scheme {27182967}.

Similarly, the broad field of inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) related to predisposition to haematolymphoid neoplasms could not be covered fully here. IEIs can contribute to lymphoma predisposition via direct tumorigenic mechanisms (e.g. genomic instability in the case of ATM mutation) or indirect mechanisms such as deficient immune surveillance leading to the emergence of (pre)malignant cells. Only some of the IEI-associated haematolymphoid neoplasms are covered in detail in this publication. For details of IEI classification, the reader is referred to the regular updates of the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) {35748970}. An overview of haematolymphoid neoplasms related to IEI, such as in BTK deficiency, is provided in #38446Table 1.02 as well as in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume of this series.

Several epidemiological studies point to an increased incidence of haematolymphoid neoplasms in first- and second-degree relatives of patients with haematolymphoid malignancies. Notably, familial clustering does not necessarily imply monogenic predisposition, and there is increasing evidence of polygenic risk. In addition, and particularly in lymphoid neoplasms, other reasons for familial clustering need to be taken into account. These include infection with and even germline transmission of oncogenic viruses (e.g. HTLV), germline predisposition to infections related to malignancy (e.g. sickle cell trait / malaria in Burkitt lymphoma, EBV infection in some IEIs), sharing of antigens triggering certain B-cell or T-cell receptors, and chemical or physical hazards. These factors influencing the risk for haematolymphoid neoplasms are discussed within specific sections covering these lymphoma types in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume of this series.
