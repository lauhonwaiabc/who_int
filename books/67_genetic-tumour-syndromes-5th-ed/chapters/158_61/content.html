1.5.8 Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1)

Definition

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants of MEN1.

MIM numbering

131100 Multiple endocrine neoplasia, type 1; MEN1

ICD-11 coding

2F7A.0 Multiple polyglandular tumours

Related terminology

Acceptable: multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 syndrome; multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1.

Not recommended: multiple endocrine adenomatosis type 1; familial Zollinger–Ellison syndrome; Wermer syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Patients with MEN1 manifest with multifocal endocrine neoplasms in the parathyroids (including ectopic or supernumerary parathyroid glands in the mediastinum), anterior pituitary, pancreas, and duodenum, and less commonly in the stomach, adrenal, thymus, and lungs. In addition, various non-endocrine lesions may occur in the skin, CNS, and soft tissues.

Clinical features

The cardinal components of MEN1 syndrome consist of primary hyperparathyroidism, pituitary adenomas / pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs) and duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumours. Other endocrine tumours that can occur in patients with MEN1 include bronchopulmonary NETs, thymic NETs, adrenocortical tumours, and rarely phaeochromocytomas {32130200}. Facial angiofibromas, collagenomas, lipomas, leiomyomas, and CNS tumours (e.g. meningiomas, ependymomas) as well as hibernomas and breast cancers have also been described {22723327;25099597;33564173}. Lipomas, angiofibromas, and collagenomas occur in 30%, 85%, and 70% of patients, respectively, by the age of 4 years {11739416}.

Primary hyperparathyroidism is the most common manifestation and occurs in virtually all patients with MEN1. Because the nature of multiglandular parathyroid involvement can be asymmetrical and asynchronous, most patients with MEN1 syndrome are not subjected to the more common restricted surgical procedure {21713580;22470073}. Prophylactic partial thymectomy is also considered by experienced surgeons, given mediastinal recurrence due to ectopic or supernumerary parathyroid glands in as many as 12% of cases, and the risk of thymic carcinoid {21713580;22735224;22470073}.

Duodenopancreatic NETs (functioning or non-functioning) are usually diagnosed owing to symptoms related to functioning NETs (e.g. gastrinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, VIPoma) or on imaging studies performed during the screening of non-functioning NETs in carriers of MEN1 pathogenic variants {30202829}. Conventional (e.g. CT, MRI, EUS) imaging studies are required in localizing small non-functioning NETs {30202829}. Functional imaging studies (e.g. 68Ga-DOTATATE/DOTATOC PET-CT, FDG PET) can help determine the extent of various NETs in patients with MEN1 {26242621;28263205;28790162;30202829;32971521}.

MEN1-associated PitNET can be suspected when patients develop symptoms related to pituitary hormone excess (e.g. galactorrhoea and amenorrhoea, acromegaly, Cushing disease) or when a non-functioning macrotumour causes visual field defects or intracranial mass-related symptoms. When an ectopic source of GHRH is suspected as the cause of acromegaly {3145190;8126126;22983831}, plasma GHRH over the threshold of 250–300 ng/L has been shown to have excellent specificity {22442262;23122576}.

MEN1-associated adrenal lesions can be non-functioning {12016472;17235589}, or can be seen in association with Cushing syndrome or primary aldosteronism {1639957;12016472;21415556;22084155}. In general, adrenal cortical carcinomas are rare but they are more common when compared with sporadic manifestations {22084155}.

Most paediatric-onset MEN1 manifestations are diagnosed after positive predictive genetic testing, while affected individuals are generally asymptomatic. In the absence of symptoms or hormone excess, biochemical screening is considered effective from the age of 5–10 years, and imaging studies (e.g. brain, thoracic, and abdominal MRI) are performed from 10–15 years {22723327;25594862;28674121;28530019;30339208}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is estimated to be between 3 and 20 per 100 000 population {33249439}. About 10% of patients have germline MEN1 pathogenic variants arising de novo, with no family history {9463336;23933118}. The mean age at time of diagnosis ranges between the fourth and fifth decades of life in various MEN1-associated manifestations {28132167;21950691;11836268;26126205;30637623}. Age-related penetrance and variable expression can occur {23933118;33249439}.

There is variable female prevalence recorded in MEN1 registries from Italy {28132167}, Japan {21950691}, and the French and Belgian Groupe d’étude des Tumeurs Endocrines {21551167}. No significant sex differences have been identified in MEN1-related primary hyperparathyroidism {21551167}. In addition, pulmonary NETs occurred equally in both sexes in a large series {24915123}. However, pancreatic NETs (PanNETs) {21551167} and thymic NETs {21551167;24915123;21950691} were found to have a male prevalence. In contrast to the exclusive male prevalence in the European databases {21551167;24915123}, the female prevalence of MEN1-associated thymic NETs was significantly higher in Japan {21950691}. PitNETs also show a female prevalence {11836268;21551167;26126205;30637623}.

The prevalence of primary hyperparathyroidism, gastroenteropancreatic NETs, and PitNETs is 93–94%, 53–59%, and 38–50%, respectively, in MEN1 registries {28132167;21950691;11836268;26126205}. The prevalence of insulinoma was higher in Japanese patients with MEN1 compared with those in western Europe {21950691}. Gastric enterochromaffin-like cell (ECL cell) NETs occur in 23–29% of patients {11344233;18270260}. Depending on the diagnostic criteria applied (either histologically confirmed or radiologically identified), bronchopulmonary NETs occur in about 5–23% of patients with MEN1 {24389729;24915123;26070346;26113081;28785839;33135721}. The estimated prevalence of thymic NETs is < 5% {24915123}. Adrenal lesions have been reported in 20–70% of patients with MEN1 {16549608;18426829;22084155}.

Etiology

Germline MEN1 pathogenic sequence variants, which are inherited as an autosomal dominant trait, are responsible for the development of MEN1 syndrome {23933118;33249439}.

Pathogenesis

Germline MEN1 pathogenic variants occur in the majority of patients with MEN1 and include a large variety of genetic abnormalities, including those leading to frameshift and nonsense variants, or large deletions, including those in intronic areas leading to splice-site defects {17879353;26767918;33249439}. These are accompanied by loss of the wildtype allele in > 90% of MEN1-driven tumours. The 610 amino acid protein product is called menin, for which the exact role in tumorigenesis remains to be established, although many roles have been proffered, including in epigenetic regulation of gene transcription, cell division and cell proliferation, cell motility, cytoskeletal structure, DNA repair, genomic stability, cell signalling, and transcriptional regulation, through the potential interaction with about 50 proteins {9103196;9215690;17879353;22829075;23652667;23850066;26560942;28899949;33249439}. Menin loss has shown correlations with various epigenetic aberrancies in MEN1-related tumours, such as loss of H3 p.Lys4me3 expression through inactivation of the KMT2 (MLL2) complex and gain of H3 p.Lys27me3 at specific genetic sites {25537453}, DNA hypermethylation, and histone acetylation {33249439}. In the absence of loss of heterozygosity at the MEN1 locus, epigenetic silencing via microRNAs may also contribute to the inactivation of the second allele {22761894;33249439}.

Macroscopic appearance

Patients with MEN1 often manifest multiglandular parathyroid disease. The individual parathyroid glands are variably involved, but all are grossly enlarged (> 6–8 mm) and increased in weight (> 40–60 mg). The cut section of parathyroid glands may show nodularity. In patients with MEN1 syndrome, PitNETs are grossly indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts, and are white to yellow soft tumours. Multiple contiguous PitNETs cannot be distinguished by the naked eye. MEN1-associated pancreatic disease typically shows multiple tan to yellow parenchymal tumour nodules with a homogeneous cut surface. Cystic changes can occur in glucagon-expressing PanNETs {20922407}. Duodenal NETs are often identified as multiple small nodules in the upper duodenum or at the margin of an ulcer. Multiple gastric nodules in the background of a hypertrophic mucosa should raise the possibility of MEN1-associated gastric NETs (type 2 gastric ECL-cell neuroendocrine tumorigenesis). Individual lung and thymic NETs are often indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. Multiple small pulmonary neuroendocrine microtumours (0.5–5 mm) can also be identified {26113081}. An adequate sampling of the non-tumorous duodenum, pancreas, stomach, and lung is often required since MEN1-associated tumours often show precursor lesions or associated microscopic tumours {23478233;26113081}. Bilateral adrenal cortical nodules {21266030;22084155} and rare phaeochromocytomas {22084155} can also occur in patients with MEN1, but individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

MEN1-related parathyroid disease

Multiglandular parathyroid disease is the hallmark of MEN1-related primary hyperparathyroidism {15490065;23933118;25592387}. Although the finding of multiple abnormal parathyroid glands generally results in a diagnosis of parathyroid hyperplasia, molecular data suggest that MEN1-related multiglandular parathyroid lesions are composed of multiple monoclonal proliferations, constituting multiple multiglandular microadenomas {26163537;2568586;32468209}. Unlike most sporadic parathyroid adenomas, MEN1-associated parathyroid proliferations lack the characteristic atrophic rim of non-lesional parathyroid tissue. Nuclear atypia, necrosis, and prominent mitotic figures are uncommon. Glands may show stromal fibrosis and even irregular borders that can mimic the morphological features associated with atypical parathyroid tumour, and reactive changes associated with previous manipulations {17021134;26163537;26177319;33740321}. Almost all MEN1-associated parathyroid neoplasms behave as benign tumours. Although parathyroid carcinoma has been reported in the setting of MEN1, benign local recurrence is much more common and it can be difficult, if not impossible, to separate benign local recurrence from carcinoma {32468209;22281890;25557532}. Therefore the diagnosis of carcinoma should be avoided unless there is unequivocal demonstration of invasion into adjacent organs, vascular invasion, lymphatic invasion or perineural invasion, and/or the presence of metastases {21455204;26163537;26177319}. The parathyroid pathology in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 is histologically indistinguishable from that in MEN1. Therefore, patients presenting with MEN1-like parathyroid disease but lacking germline MEN1 pathogenic variants should be tested for germline CDKN1B {23933118} and MAX variants {33367756}.

MEN1-related pituitary disease

At a morphological level, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts {18300794}. PIT1-lineage PitNETs are common in MEN1-related pituitary disease {34017065}; however, other PitNETs including those of TPIT and SF1 lineages can also occur {30215160;18300794;7479350;33249439}. Plurihormonal tumours have been reported frequently in a series {18300794}; however, among functional PIT1-lineage tumours, lactotroph tumours are the most frequent and are followed by somatotroph and mammosomatotroph tumours {30637623;26126205;11836268}. Additionally, immature PIT1-lineage PitNETs (formerly known as poorly differentiated PIT1-lineage adenomas) can occur in patients with MEN1. Multiple PitNETs of distinct cell lineages can also occur in patients with MEN1 {11059654;11081204;18300794;30215160}, and this occurrence was found to be significantly more frequent when compared with patients without MEN1 {18300794}. Somatotroph or mammosomatotroph hyperplasia is an uncommon finding in MEN1 syndrome, but when it occurs in this setting, a GHRH-producing PanNET should be excluded {2887080;18300794}. MEN1-associated PitNETs show increased S100-positive folliculostellate cells compared with their sporadic counterparts. The significance of this finding is unknown.

MEN1-related pancreatic disease

The hallmark of MEN1-related pancreatic disease is the presence of multifocal PanNETs (> 5 mm) or unifocal PanNETs arising in the background of neuroendocrine microtumours (also known as neuroendocrine microadenomatosis, each 0.5–5 mm) {16699310;24718881}. Glucagon is the most frequently identified hormone followed by PP, insulin, and somatostatin {24718881}. Glucagon-positive tumours may be cystic {20922407}. Rarely, other hormones (e.g. calcitonin, PTHrP) may be expressed {9920064;21483254}. Gastrin is virtually never expressed. In cases of a MEN1-related hypoglycaemic syndrome, insulin-cell tumours can be the source of the insulin excess. Very rarely a PanNET may express a non-pancreatic hormone (e.g. GHRH causing ectopic acromegaly) {16699310}. Since MEN1 inactivation can occur in about 40% of sporadic PanNETs {33459926}, loss of menin expression cannot be used to distinguish MEN1 syndrome in the absence of multifocal neuroendocrine microtumours. However, global loss of nuclear menin expression may help rationalize germline screening for MEN1-related pathogenesis especially when global loss of nuclear menin expression occurs in multiple PanNETs and microtumours [[Mete O, Hannah-Shmouni F, Kim R, et al. Inherited neuroendocrine neoplasms. In: Asa SL, La Rosa S, Mete O, editors. The spectrum of neuroendocrine neoplasia. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2021. pp. 409–59. Bookref]].

MEN1-related duodenal disease

Multifocal duodenal NETs that arise in the background of neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia are the hallmark of MEN1-related duodenal disease. Hyperplastic gastrin and somatostatin cells are typically identified in the crypts and the Brunner glands {15887103;24718881}. Multifocal gastrin-producing duodenal NETs account for the most common cause of Zollinger–Ellison syndrome {1678681;1968616;23478233;24718881} [[Mete O, Hannah-Shmouni F, Kim R, et al. Inherited neuroendocrine neoplasms. In: Asa SL, La Rosa S, Mete O, editors. The spectrum of neuroendocrine neoplasia. Cham (Switzerland): Springer; 2021. pp. 409–59. Bookref]]. In almost all patients with MEN1 with Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, the source of gastrin excess is multicentric duodenal NETs {1678681;17312377;17985091;1968616}. The tumours are usually < 10 mm and are therefore difficult to detect {18520436}. In as many as 80% of cases, the tumours give rise to large periduodenal and/or peripancreatic lymph node metastases, which were formerly misinterpreted as pancreatic gastrinoma primaries. The individual MEN1-related duodenal NETs resemble sporadic tumours; however, multifocal disease and the presence of synchronous neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia are clues to MEN1. Rare examples of MEN1-related duodenal NETs can be somatostatin-positive {15887103;24718881;23478233}.

MEN1-related gastric disease

An ECL-cell hyperplasia-to-neoplasia progression sequence due to autonomous gastrin secretion is the hallmark of MEN1-related gastric disease and defines the type 2 ECL-cell NET pathogenesis {23478233;25053544;25740317}. Affected patients show multifocal ECL-cell NETs arising in the background of ECL-cell hyperplasia in the oxyntic mucosa {23478233;25053544;25740317}.

MEN1-related bronchopulmonary and thymic disease

Most MEN1 syndrome–associated bronchopulmonary neuroendocrine neoplasms are well-differentiated epithelial NETs that show either low-grade (typical carcinoid) or intermediate-grade (atypical carcinoid) proliferative features; however, neuroendocrine carcinomas (small cell and large cell types) have rarely been reported {28785839}. Individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts. Evidence suggests that MEN1-related lung disease is enriched in multifocal neuroendocrine cell proliferations ranging from pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia to neuroendocrine microtumours (tumourlets, each 0.5–5 mm) and NETs (> 5 mm) {26113081;33641055;35597259}. Global loss of nuclear menin expression has been suggested to be helpful in the setting of multifocal pulmonary NETs and microtumours {33641055}. Diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia is the relevant differential diagnosis in this setting {34352379}. The distinction of MEN1-related thymic NETs from sporadic tumours may be difficult in the absence of relevant clinical history or synchronous manifestations of MEN1-related states. However, the application of menin immunohistochemistry may be helpful in young individuals. Ectopic ACTH-related Cushing syndrome can occur in MEN1-related thymic NETs {21550948;24452869;36520343}.

MEN1-related adrenal disease

Most MEN1-related adrenal lesions are benign cortical lesions that may be bilateral, and include adrenal cortical nodular disease (e.g. paediatric-onset macronodular cortical disease), cysts, and adrenal cortical adenomas. Adrenal cortical carcinomas may occur in just over 1% of patients with MEN1 {22084155}. Histology for carcinomas or other adrenocortical tumours is comparable with that for sporadic cases, and both sarcomatoid and myxoid variants of adrenocortical carcinoma have been described {31118348}. In addition to cortical lesions, phaeochromocytomas can occur in patients with MEN1, with a frequency of about 1% {23778871;25494863}.

Other MEN1-related lesions

Many other entities have been described to occur with increased frequency in patients with MEN1, although sufficient pathogenetic evidence has not been collected for all of these. This does not apply to cutaneous lesions, which have been firmly established as a component of MEN1. They include lipomas, angiofibromas, and collagenomas. These lesions are frequently multiple and the combined occurrence may give a clue to the presence of MEN1 syndrome in such patients {15531478}. Another group of tumours that occurs in MEN1 is CNS tumours, including meningiomas and spinal ependymomas {14871962;23562332;28238068}, but again histopathology does not differ from that of their sporadic counterparts. Furthermore, soft-tissue lesions have been reported to be associated with MEN1 syndrome, such as leiomyoma {11549605;15144407;18729310;32577074}, gastrointestinal stromal tumour {25022420}, deep-seated or visceral lipoma {25545711}, and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour {25678969}. Finally, an increased breast cancer risk and pathogenetic underpinning has been presented for the relationship between loss of the wildtype MEN1 allele and the occurrence of breast cancer in patients with MEN1 {25099597}. So far, follicular nodular disease and thyroid neoplasms do not seem to be part of the MEN1 spectrum, and occurrence of these has been attributed to chance {25572387}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual endocrine tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline MEN1 testing is offered to (1) an index patient with a clinical diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome (based on two or more MEN1 syndrome–related tumours), (2) an asymptomatic first-degree relative of a patient with a pathogenic MEN1 variant (typically as early as < 5 years of age), (3) symptomatic first-degree relatives (with at least one MEN1-related tumour) of a proband with a pathogenic MEN1 variant, (4) patients with primary hyperparathyroidism–related multiglandular parathyroid disease or parathyroid adenomas in young individuals (< 30 years of age), and (5) patients with gastrinoma or multifocal PanNETs at any age {33249439}.

In general, DNA sequence analysis (exons and splice-junction variants) and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification for duplication/deletion analysis (screening for larger alterations) are used in most clinical laboratories {33249439}. About 10% of patients with MEN1-related clinical states may test negative for pathogenic MEN1 variants {23933118}. A fraction of patients who test negative for MEN1 variants but who have MEN1-like states may harbour pathogenic germline CDKN1B variants (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 syndrome) or other CDKN variants {23933118;30990521}, as well as MAX variants (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 5 syndrome) {33367756}; however, the commonly used screening tools may also fail to capture some germline MEN1 variants as well as somatic mosaicism {33249439}. Therefore, whole-exome sequencing or whole-genome sequencing may help reduce the fraction of variant-negative patients on first-line diagnostic molecular analyses {33249439}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical diagnostic criteria: at least two MEN1 syndrome–associated tumours; OR familial diagnostic criteria: at least one MEN1 syndrome–associated tumour and a first-degree relative with a confirmed diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome; OR genetic diagnostic criteria: germline MEN1 variant.

Staging

Malignant tumours are staged using the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging systems that are applied to sporadic manifestations.

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with MEN1 are at increased risk of death, usually related to the disease and its complications {11038219;19949948;23645327;33249439}. Variant-negative patients with two out of the three main MEN1 manifestations generally have a different phenotype and clinical course with manifestations at a higher age and a normal life expectancy {27842554;32703679}. Some studies suggest the existence of high-risk MEN1 variants. Alterations affecting the JUND interacting domain may be associated with an increased risk of death {23376981}, and those affecting the FOXN3 interacting domain may be associated with an increased risk of aggressive PanNETs {25210877}. However, further validations are required. Nevertheless, the well-established causes of high morbidity are thymic NETs and duodenal and pancreatic NETs, along with rare cases of aggressive adrenal tumours {11038219;19949948;23645327;24915123;24389729}. Female sex, family history of MEN1, and recent diagnosis are associated with a lower risk of death {19949948}. At the tumour level, the prognostic factors are the same as those for sporadic tumours: tumour stage, tumour size, and tumour grade are important parameters {23645327}. MEN1-associated lung NETs are slow-growing tumours with a good prognosis {33135721}.
