3.2.5 Beckwith–Wiedemann spectrum (IGF2, CDKN1C)

Definition

Beckwith–Wiedemann spectrum (BWS) is an overgrowth and cancer predisposition syndrome caused by genetic or epigenetic defects at chromosome 11p15.5 and/or 11p15.4, with physical features occurring in infancy and early childhood.

MIM numbering

130650 Beckwith–Wiedemann Syndrome; BWS

ICD-11 coding

LD2C Overgrowth syndromes

Related terminology

Acceptable: Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Molecular subtypes include: KCNQ1OT1:transcription start site differentially methylated region; IC2 loss of methylation; H19/IGF2:IG differentially methylated region; IC1 gain of methylation; chromosome 11p15.5 paternal uniparental disomy; deletions or duplications of 11p15.5; pathogenic variants on the maternal allele in CDKN1C

Localization

Most malignant tumours associated with BWS occur in the abdomen and include kidney (nephroblastoma), liver (hepatoblastoma), and adrenal (neuroblastoma or adrenocortical carcinoma) tumours.

Clinical features

BWS comprises a constellation of features including macrosomia, hemihyperplasia, abdominal wall defects, macroglossia, visceromegaly, and predisposition to paediatric embryonal tumours, primarily nephroblastoma (frequently bilateral) and hepatoblastoma. Rarely, other malignancies occur, such as acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, haemangioma, melanoma, neuroblastoma, pancreatoblastoma, phaeochromocytoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma. Hepatic haemangiomas and pancreatomegaly with hyperinsulinaemia are also seen {30882989;23804593}.

BWS is considered a clinical spectrum, in which affected individuals may have many of these features or only one or two clinical features. Particularly in children with low levels of somatic mosaicism, the BWS phenotype can be subtle. Often, these children are diagnosed with BWS only after presenting with a tumour.

Differential diagnoses include other overgrowth syndromes such as Malan, Perlman, Simpson–Golabi–Behmel, Sotos, and Weaver syndromes, as well as PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum {28634246;28674120;29377879;30842651}.

Of the molecular subtypes, chromosome 11p15.5 paternal uniparental disomy and IC1 gain of methylation are associated with the highest childhood malignancy risk (mainly hepatoblastoma and nephroblastoma). Pathogenic variants in CDKN1C are associated with neuroblastoma. Children with IC2 loss of methylation have the lowest cancer risk {30842651;32451468}.

Epidemiology

BWS occurs in 1 in 10 000 to 1 in 13 700 births with one third being premature {23918458}. BWS is observed more frequently in multiple births, although usually only one infant is affected, and it is slightly more common in children born after assisted reproductive technology {28634246}. Analysis of BWS in Japanese patients revealed a significantly lower frequency of H19 differentially methylated region hypermethylation and a higher frequency of chromosome abnormality than in North American and European patients {17700627}.

Etiology

BWS is associated with aberrant expression of imprinted genes located at the 11p15 chromosomal region, including KCNQ1OT1, H19, CDKN1C, and IGF2. Imprinting of these genes results in only the paternal allele (KCNQ1OT1 and IGF2) or the maternal allele (H19 and CDKN1C) being normally expressed {31466347;26583054}.

The characteristic overgrowth seen in patients with BWS may be partially explained by inappropriate IGF2 expression of both parental alleles, or loss of expression of the CDKN1C maternal allele {29514882}. CDKN1C and IGF2 are protein-encoding genes. CDKN1C encodes a cell-cycle, cell-division, and growth regulator that acts as a tumour suppressor gene, while IGF2 encodes an insulin-like growth-promoting factor. The KCNQ1OT1 and H19 genes are transcribed but not translated and their RNA suppresses the expression of the maternal copy of IGF2 and the paternal copy of CDKN1C, respectively {32039119}.

Pathogenesis

Dysregulated expression of genes at the 11p15 chromosomal region may occur through a number of different epigenetic and genetic mechanisms {32039119;26583054}.

Chromosomal abnormalities detectable by karyotyping, such as duplications of the paternal 11q15 region and translocations or inversions of the maternal chromosome 11 region, are identified only in a minority (1–2%) of patients with BWS. Aberrant imprinting of KCNQ1OT1 or H19 can be detected in a large proportion of patients with BWS, with 50–60% of them exhibiting KCNQ1OT1 hypomethylation, H19 hypermethylation, or rarely KCNQ1OT1 and H19 microdeletions {29377879}.

Other mechanisms responsible for BWS include CDKN1C pathogenic variants (5–10% of BWS sporadic cases and 40% of families with autosomal dominant BWS) or more commonly lack of expression of the CDKN1C maternal allele due to abnormal imprinting. In 10–20% of patients, loss of CDKN1C expression and increased IGF2 expression occur as a result of paternal isodisomy of the 11q15 chromosome region. Paternal isodisomy may be extensive and may rarely involve the whole genome {30882989;23804593}. It is believed that the aberrant IGF2 and/or H19 expression contributes to the increased tumour risk in most patients with BWS {32412586}.

Genetic causes of BWS are often mosaic, and may require testing of tissues other than blood {32039119}.

Macroscopic appearance

Tumours have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Perilobar nephrogenic rests are seen in the kidneys of infants and young children with BWS and are considered precursor lesions for the development of Wilms tumour. Also, medullary ray nodules are seen in patients with Wilms tumour and BWS.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular analyses include methylation analysis at imprinting loci, screening for variants of CDKN1C, and copy-number analysis at 11p15.5 and/or 11p15.4. Owing to mosaicism, analyses of different tissues may be necessary to establish the diagnosis {32451468}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

See #38980Box 7.01 and #37710Table 7.01.

Staging

Staging of embryonal tumours is done similarly to that of the non-BWS counterparts and includes pretreatment extent of disease (PRETEXT) staging for hepatoblastoma and Children’s Oncology Group (COG) or International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) staging for Wilms tumour {29427028;30310143}.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis largely depends on clinical complications (such as tumours) and the quality of care. Cancer surveillance protocols and clinical guidelines have been developed and patients should be followed by a clinical team with knowledge of this condition {29377879;28674120}.

The clinical diagnosis is based on a clinical scoring system (see below). For a clinical diagnosis of classic BWS, a patient requires a score of ≥ 4. A score of ≥ 2 merits BWS molecular testing {29377879;32430359}.

Cardinal features (2 points per feature):

·   Macroglossia

·   Exomphalos

·   Lateralized overgrowth

·   Multifocal and/or bilateral Wilms tumour or nephroblastomatosis

·   Hyperinsulinism (lasting > 1 week and requiring escalated treatment)

·   Pathology findings: adrenal cortex cytomegaly, placental mesenchymal dysplasia, or endocrine cell hyperplasia of the pancreas (previously termed “pancreatic adenomatosis”)

Suggestive features (1 point per feature):

·   Birthweight > 2 standard deviations above the mean

·   Facial naevus simplex

·   Polyhydramnios and/or placentomegaly

·   Ear creases and/or pits

·   Transient hypoglycaemia (lasting < 1 week)

·   Typical BWS tumours (neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, unilateral Wilms tumour, hepatoblastoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, or phaeochromocytoma)

·   Nephromegaly and/or hepatomegaly

·   Umbilical hernia and/or diastasis recti
