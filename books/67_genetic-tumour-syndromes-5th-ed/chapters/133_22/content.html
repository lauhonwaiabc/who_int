1.3.3 Costello syndrome (HRAS)

Definition

Costello syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a pathogenic HRAS variant; clinical manifestations include characteristic facies, short stature, distinctive hand posture, severe feeding difficulty, and failure to thrive.

MIM numbering

218040 Costello syndrome; CSTLO

ICD-11 coding

LD2F.1Y Other specified syndromes with multiple structural anomalies, not of environmental origin

Related terminology

Acceptable: faciocutaneoskeletal syndrome.

Subtype(s)

Congenital myopathy with excess of muscle spindles

Localization

Young children often develop multiple papillomata, typically in the perinasal region and possibly in the perianal region {16010679}. Malignant tumours in children include rhabdomyosarcoma and neuroblastoma, which may arise at any site but are typically axial, including retroperitoneal, paravertebral, and head-and-neck primary sites {16010679}. Patients may develop urothelial carcinoma of the bladder {16010679}.

Clinical features

Costello syndrome includes distinct facial features, fine hair, and loose, soft skin with deep palmar and plantar creases; increased birth weight with subsequent feeding difficulties and failure to thrive after birth; congenital heart defects including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias; postnatal cerebellar overgrowth including Chiari 1 malformation; and developmental delay and intellectual disability. There is a 10–15% lifetime risk of malignancy, with no sex predilection {31222966}.

Epidemiology

Costello syndrome is a rare disorder with an estimated birth incidence of 1 per 500 000 [[CostelloKids (Internet). CostelloKids Support Group. Available from http://www.costellokids.com. Bookref]].

Etiology

Costello syndrome is caused by heterozygous germline pathogenic variants in the HRAS gene {16170316;16443854;16329078}. The majority of mutations are de novo, although rare reports describe paternal somatic mutations {19206176}. Gonadal mosaicism is suggested in parents of affected siblings {21834037}.

Pathogenesis

HRAS is a member of the RAS/MAPK pathway, where in normal conditions its gene product alternates between its active, signal-transmitting form and its inactive conformation, thus playing a key role in normal growth and development {18470943}. In Costello syndrome, pathogenic variants in HRAS result in continued activation, causing dysregulation of the RAS signalling pathway {22261753}.

HRAS sequence variants are common somatic mutations, identified in as many as 30% of human cancers {2547513} and occurring in a variety of different cancer types, including urothelial carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, and melanoma {32602003}. Identification of germline pathogenic variants in HRAS in Costello syndrome represented the first germline pathogenic variants identified in the RAS family of GTPases {16170316}; however, the specific variants seen in these sporadic tumours differ from those seen in Costello syndrome {16329078}.

Macroscopic appearance

Rhabdomyosarcomas and neuroblastomas arise at sites typical for these tumours in children and show expected macroscopic features. Urothelial carcinomas are often exophytic, papillary masses when identified on cystoscopy.

Histopathology

The histopathology of facial papillomata in Costello syndrome is infrequently discussed in the literature, but they are thought to arise secondary to irritation / frictional forces rather than being viral in etiology.

Rhabdomyosarcoma in Costello syndrome is of the embryonal subtype {9863604;10424828;16010679}. Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma is characterized by ovoid to spindled cells often arranged in a myxoid stroma. Tumour cellularity is often variable, with alternating regions of loose and dense cellularity. Rhabdomyoblastic differentiation may be present, ranging from cells with elongate tails of eosinophilic cytoplasm (strap cells) to cells showing terminal cytodifferentiation in the form of myotubes. The dense pattern of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma often lacks differentiation and may mimic alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma.

Neuroblastoma and ganglioneuroblastoma are also described in the setting of Costello syndrome {8213903;11045582;11131863}. Neuroblastomas are composed of neuroblasts (small, round cells with coarse chromatin) in a background of neuropil. Increasing differentiation may shows cells with varying degrees of ganglion cell differentiation as well as increasing quantities of neuropil. Ganglioneuroblastomas show increased amounts of Schwannian stroma, with associated regions of neuroblastoma.

Urothelial carcinoma in Costello syndrome {12187253;10449656;10678668;16010679} is often low-grade, with recurrent tumours, but reports including histopathological descriptions are rare.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Hotspot mutations resulting in Costello syndrome involve glycines in positions 12 and 13, with NM_005343.4:c.34G>A p.(Gly12Ser), NM_005343.4:c.35G>C p.(Gly12Ala), NM_005343.4:c.37G>T p.(Gly12Cys), and NM_005343.4:c.35_36delinsTT p.(Gly12Val) accounting for > 90% of cases of Costello syndrome {22261753}. Sequencing of HRAS exon 2 detects variants in 80–90% of patients tested, and sequencing can be expanded to all other coding exons and flanking intronic regions in the event of a negative result {22261753}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: characteristic facies and hand positioning, polyhydramnios, and large birth weight with failure to thrive and severe feeding difficulties after birth; identification of a pathogenic HRAS variant.

Desirable: presence of multiple facial papillomata, deep palmar and plantar creases, and cardiac abnormalities including cardiac hypertrophy and arrhythmias.

Staging

Staging is as for sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common malignancy in Costello syndrome, followed by neuroblastoma and urothelial carcinoma. The overall risk of malignancy in Costello syndrome is approximately 10–15% {16329078;16443854}. Some studies have suggested a genotype–phenotype correlation, with 7% of patients with the most common NM_005343.4:c.34G>A p.(Gly12Ser) variant developing malignant tumours and as many as 50% of patients with rare NM_005343.4:c.35G>C p.(Gly12Ala) or NM_005343.4:c.37G>T p.(Gly12Cys) variants developing malignant tumours. The role of screening tests is not well defined for patients with Costello syndrome, but patients and providers should be aware of the increased risk. Subsequent treatment should follow standard protocols.
