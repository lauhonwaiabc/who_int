1.5.4 Serrated polyposis (RNF43)

Definition

Serrated polyposis is a condition of largely unknown etiology, characterized by multiple serrated polyps in the large intestine and associated with an increased risk of colorectal carcinoma.

MIM numbering

617108 Sessile serrated polyposis cancer syndrome; SSPCS

ICD-11 coding

2E92.40 Polyposis syndrome

Related terminology

Not recommended: hyperplastic polyposis.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Serrated polyposis affects the large intestine but not the upper gastrointestinal tract or the small intestine {22490521}. Extracolonic manifestations have not been reported.

Clinical features

The clinical criteria for the diagnosis of serrated polyposis are presented in #37551Box 2.06.

The phenotype of serrated polyposis is heterogeneous, representing a continuum between patients who barely meet the clinical definition and patients with a high polyp burden and multiple large polyps, fulfilling both criteria {26603485;28400194}. About 25% of patients present with a type 1 phenotype (i.e. fulfilling only clinical criterion 1), 45% present with a type 2 phenotype (fulfilling only clinical criterion 2), and 30% have both phenotypes {26264224;26603485}. The median cumulative polyp number is most commonly 30–40, with a range as wide as 6–240 polyps and frequent pancolonic distribution {22490521;26264224;26603485;22510757}.

Epidemiology

There is an almost equal sex predilection. Most patients are diagnosed at 50–60 years of age, with some patients diagnosed in early adulthood {22490521;26264224;26603485;22525305}.

The highest reported prevalence in primary screening colonoscopies is 0.1% {26911398}.

Environmental factors associated with serrated polyposis include cigarette smoking and high body mass index. About one third of patients have at least one first-degree relative with colorectal carcinoma; 5% have a first-degree relative with serrated polyposis {26264224;26603485}. The first-degree relatives of patients with serrated polyposis have five times the incidence of colorectal carcinoma seen in the general population {22525305;20584785}.

Etiology

Germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in RNF43 have been reported in 2% of patients with serrated polyposis {27582512;29330307;27329244}.

Serrated polyposis can be a component of well-defined genetic syndromes: MUTYH-associated polyposis {19013464}, hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome caused by a duplication upstream of GREM1 {22561515}, and less commonly PTEN hamartoma tumour syndrome and juvenile polyposis. However, other polyp types or extraintestinal features are almost always present. Expert pathology review is important for a correct clinicopathological classification.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is largely unknown. A small subset of patients show autosomal dominant inheritance with germline pathogenic variants in RNF43 (17q22), a gene whose protein product is involved in the WNT signalling pathway {24512911;24308963}. Two variants have been reported: NM_017763.6:c.337C>T p.(Arg113*) and NM_017763.6:c.394C>T p.(Arg132*).

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic appearances mimic those of sporadic serrated polyps/lesions.

Histopathology

Any patient fulfilling at least one of the clinical criteria (see #37551Box 2.06) is diagnosed with serrated polyposis. Any subtype of serrated lesions/polyps (hyperplastic polyps, sessile serrated lesions with or without dysplasia, and traditional serrated adenomas) is included in the final count. The diagnosis may require more than one colonoscopy.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Serrated polyps in the setting of serrated polyposis have molecular characteristics similar to those of sporadic polyps {27499922}.

Approximately 50% of colorectal carcinomas in patients with serrated polyposis have a BRAF variant, < 5% have a KRAS variant, and 40% are MLH1 deficient {21641392;23211288}. This molecular phenotype suggests that only half of all serrated polyposis–associated colorectal carcinomas develop from serrated polyps via the serrated neoplasia pathway. The other half presumably follow the conventional adenoma–carcinoma pathway.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The clinical criteria for the diagnosis of serrated polyposis are presented in #37551Box 2.06. See also #37556Table 2.25.

Staging

Adenocarcinomas developed in the setting of serrated polyposis should be staged using the latest edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification {9355980} and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) cancer staging manual [[Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. Bookref]].

Prognosis and prediction

Once dysplastic, the sessile serrated lesions may develop relatively rapidly into cancer {26475632}. The cancer risk differs depending on the phenotype, first clinical presentation, and polyp histology. In two large retrospective multicentre cohort studies, colorectal carcinoma was diagnosed in 16% and 29% of patients, most of them before or at the time of serrated polyposis diagnosis {26264224;26603485}. Reported risk factors for colorectal carcinoma included the fulfilment of both clinical criteria for serrated polyposis, more than two sessile serrated lesions proximal to the splenic flexure, at least one serrated polyp with dysplasia, and at least one advanced conventional adenoma. In a large retrospective Spanish study, the cumulative incidence of colorectal carcinoma detected during surveillance was 3.1% and 6.4% at 3 years and 5 years, respectively {30068004}.

Criterion 1: At least five serrated polyps proximal to the rectum all being ≥ 5 mm, with two or more ≥ 10 mm

Criterion 2: More than 20 serrated polyps of any size distributed throughout the large bowel, with at least five proximal to the rectum

Any histological subtype of serrated polyp/lesion (hyperplastic polyp, sessile serrated lesion without or with dysplasia, traditional serrated adenoma) is included in the final count; the polyp count is cumulative over multiple colonoscopies
