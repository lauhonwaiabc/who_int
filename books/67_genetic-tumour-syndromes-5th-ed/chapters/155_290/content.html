1.5.5 WT1-related tumour predisposition syndrome (WT1)

Definition

WT1-related tumour predisposition syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder with variable presentation of Wilms tumour, gonadoblastoma, genitourinary anomalies, and impaired renal function associated with germline pathogenic variants in WT1.

MIM numbering

194080 Denys–Drash Syndrome; DDS

136680 Frasier Syndrome

256370 Nephrotic syndrome, type 4; NPHS4

ICD-11 coding

LD2A.Y Other specified malformative disorders of sex development

Related terminology

Acceptable: Denys–Drash syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Tumours of the kidney and gonads are typical.

Clinical features

This is an overlapping group of disorders involving variable degrees of the following:

  • genital abnormalities including ambiguous genitalia or pseudohermaphroditism; most patients are (46,XY) phenotypic females but some are (46,XX) with variable female or male genitalia;
  • degenerative renal disease (hypertension, proteinuria, mesangial or focal glomerulosclerosis);
  • Wilms tumour or gonadal tumours; gonadal tumours are more characteristic of Frasier syndrome and are infrequently seen in Denys–Drash syndrome.

    • Nephrotic syndrome type 4 has been described as a rapidly progressing nephrotic syndrome in early childhood associated with WT1 mutation and characterized by isolated mesangial sclerosis or focal segmental glomerulosclerosis {9529364;11241056}. Although nephrotic syndrome type 4 does not require a diagnosis of Wilms tumour or other cancer, several cases are reported which later develop Wilms tumour; thus, there is overlap in these syndromes {9607189}. It is recommended that children who develop rapidly progressing nephrotic syndrome in early childhood be evaluated for germline WT1 variants.

    Epidemiology

    The overall population incidence is not known as most reports are small series or single cases. The majority of patients with Denys–Drash syndrome develop Wilms tumour. In the International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) series, 0.8% of Wilms tumours are associated with Denys–Drash syndrome {34638500}. No reported race- or ethnicity-specific predilection due to WT1 variants has been reported. Aside from the syndromes discussed here, WT1 germline variants also occur in a small percentage (< 5%) of patients with Wilms tumour without other syndromic features, including a lack of genitourinary anomalies {9398852;15483024}.

    Etiology

    WT1 localizes to chromosome 11p13. Coordinates are chr11:32,389,058-32,435,360(hg38); see #39000Table 2.26.

    Dominant negative WT1 variants in zinc finger domains that interfere with DNA binding and transcription activity were described initially, most commonly reported as missense variants in the zinc fingers encoded in exons 8 and 9 {1655284}, but additional studies described missense variants in other regions of WT1 including exons 1, 3, 6, and 7. Splice variants predominantly in exon 9 are characteristic of Frasier syndrome {9398852} and some patients have nonsense or frameshift loss-of-function variants. Although the disorder can be transmitted as an autosomal dominant syndrome, multiple families with variable penetrance are described {20150449;24161391}, and most cases are the result of de novo variants in WT1 in the proband, especially in those with severe phenotypes with renal failure or non-functional gonadal tissue.

    Pathogenesis

    The WT1 heterozygous deletion is responsible for impaired renal development, the persistence of intralobar rests, and a predisposition to Wilms tumour {1655284}. Ambiguous genitalia, or genitourinary anomalies also observed with missense and loss-of-function variants in WT1, can also be seen in WAGR syndrome (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, and a range of developmental delays) (see section WAGR syndrome). The molecular pathways involved in tumorigenesis are uncertain {36284226}.

    Macroscopic appearance

    Wilms tumours in WT1-related tumour predisposition syndrome are macroscopically similar to other (sporadic) Wilms tumours.

    Histopathology

    Wilms tumours are most commonly of mixed histological type. However, 36% of Wilms tumours in Denys–Drash syndrome included in the SIOP series had stromal-type histology {34638500}.

    Gonadal tumours in Frasier syndrome are typically gonadoblastoma and/or dysgerminoma.

    Cytology

    Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

    Diagnostic molecular pathology

    A molecular diagnosis through sequencing of a blood or saliva sample reveals a germline sequence variant in WT1, usually in codons that encode zinc finger domains in exon 8 or 9, or a splice variant in exon 9 or 10 in WT1. Splice variants in exon 9 result in the loss of three amino acids (Lys, Tyr, Ser) and are associated with a Frasier phenotype. Not all individuals with a WT1 variant will have the complete Denys–Drash or Frasier phenotype, as WT1 germline variants have been observed in children with Wilms tumour without major genitourinary disorders, although less severe anomalies such as cryptorchidism or hypospadias have been reported {9817285}.

    Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

    Essential: WT1 germline pathogenic sequence variant; genital abnormalities; degenerative renal disease; predisposition to Wilms tumour (Denys–Drash syndrome), or gonadoblastoma (Denys–Drash or Frasier syndrome).

    Staging

    Wilms tumours when they develop are staged similarly to non-syndromic Wilms tumours. Most associated tumours are small and non-metastatic, and 29% are bilateral (stage 5) {34638500}. Likewise, gonadal tumours are staged similarly to non-syndromic tumours.

    Prognosis and prediction

    Survival of Wilms tumour is similar to that for non-syndromic Wilms tumours, although it has been suggested in a retrospective series that tumours associated with Denys–Drash syndrome with stromal histology are less likely to respond to presurgical chemotherapy {34638500}. Progressive renal failure with end-stage renal failure is characteristic of all of the WT1-related syndromes, such that long-term survival is influenced by renal failure and the need for long-term dialysis and/or renal transplant {31807296}. For patients with WT1 germline pathogenic variants and pseudohermaphroditism with gonadal dysgenesis, prophylactic removal of gonadal tissue is recommended to prevent gonadal cancers {32352694;25623218}. Consideration of prophylactic nephrectomy is advised in WT1 disorders where end-stage renal disease and renal failure have occurred in early childhood {30450273}.
