1.3.4 Noonan syndrome (various genes)

Definition

Noonan syndrome is a genetic disorder due to germline pathogenic variants in genes in the RAS/MAPK pathway, leading to widespread multiorgan system involvement, including specific tumour predisposition. Individuals with Noonan syndrome may also have specific cardiac anomalies, bleeding disorders, developmental delays / learning differences, short stature, and characteristic facial features.

Subtype(s)

Localization

The most common malignancy in Noonan syndrome is juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia (JMML), although other paediatric leukaemias (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, acute myeloid leukaemia) have also been reported. Solid malignancies occur during childhood albeit with a rarer incidence. These include, but are not limited to, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (ERMS), neuroblastoma, pilocytic astrocytomas, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumours, oligodendroglioma, and central giant cell granulomas {21500339;25742478;26855057}.

Clinical features

Individuals with Noonan syndrome exhibit multisystem involvement owing to variation in genes encoding signal transducers and regulators with a role in the RAS/MAPK pathway {20876176;23312968;36394128}. Cardiac anomalies, developmental delay, learning difficulties, short stature, renal anomalies, lymphatic malformations, bleeding difficulties, cryptorchidism, and a facial gestalt represent major features. Genotype–phenotype correlations exist, involving cardiac and ectodermal involvement, postnatal growth, and cognition. In particular, PTPN11 variants have been associated with pulmonic stenosis, while RAF1, LZTR1, RIT1, and MRAS variants more commonly occur in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. SOS1 and SOS2 variants have been associated with a lower prevalence of short stature and cognitive deficits and a higher occurrence of ectodermal anomalies {20876176;23312968}.

Individuals with Noonan syndrome are also at risk of cancer. In previous retrospective cohort studies, those with Noonan syndrome were eight times as likely to develop cancer as the general population {25742478} and had a 4% cancer risk by the age of 20 years {21500339}. Some variation is reported to be associated with increased cancer risk (e.g. at PTPN11 codon 61 or the NM_002834.5:c.218C>T p.(Thr73Ile) variant of PTPN11 and the NM_004985.5:c.173C>T p.(Thr58Ile) variant of KRAS), but how much so is unknown {15928039;16474405;25097206}. It is notable, however, that individuals with Noonan syndrome due to germline PTPN11 variants are at elevated risk of JMML-like myeloproliferation but only roughly 10% of these individuals progress to frank JMML {25420281;12717436;25564399}.

Note that some RASopathies (e.g. Costello syndrome [HRAS], Legius syndrome [SPRED1], cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome [BRAF, KRAS, MAP2K1, MAP2K2], CBL-associated Noonan syndrome–like disorder [CBL], Mazzanti syndrome [SHOC2, PPP1CB], and the MAPK1-associated RASopathy [MAPK1]) share significant phenotypic and oncogenic overlap with Noonan syndrome.

Epidemiology

Noonan syndrome has an estimated incidence of 1 case per 2500 live births. However, that estimate is based on subjective clinical assessment. The most common germline variants in Noonan syndrome are in PTPN11 (~50%), followed by SOS1 and LZTR1 (~15%) {20301303}.

Etiology

Noonan syndrome is due to germline pathogenic variants in genes involved in the RAS/MAPK pathway. These genes include BRAF, KRAS, LZTR1, MAP2K1, MRAS, NRAS, PTPN11, RAF1, RASA2, RIT1, RRAS, RRAS2, SOS1, SOS2, and SPRED2. These variants result in hyperactivation of the RAS/MAPK pathway, which is involved in nearly every facet of cellular programming and, notably, growth.

Pathogenesis

Noonan syndrome is genetically heterogeneous. It is largely inherited as a dominant disorder, although recessive forms have been described (i.e. Noonan syndrome caused by biallelic LZTR1 or SPRED2 inactivating variants).

The underlying molecular mechanism of Noonan syndrome is dysregulation of the RAS/MAPK signalling pathway, which is a critical regulatory pathway for cellular proliferation, migration, differentiation, and senescence {16051167;15731001}. RAS proteins are GTP/GDP-binding GTPases that signal through a number of effector signalling cascades, including the RAF/MEK/ERK protein kinase pathway. Signal transmission is started by activation of surface receptors by hormones, cytokines, and growth factors that recruit and initiate guanine nucleotide exchange factors, which then promote release of GDP from RAS, favouring the binding of GTP and activation of the GTPase, thus promoting downstream signalling. Pathogenic germline variants leading to RAS signalling dysregulation include those in RAS genes (KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS) along with modulators of receptors (CBL) or RAS (PTPN11, SOS1, SOS2, LZTR1, NF1, and SPRED2), as well as those in downstream signal transduction (RAF1, BRAF, MAP2K1, MAP2K2, and SHOC2) {21396583;36394128}.

Macroscopic appearance

Solid tumours related to Noonan syndrome have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

In JMML, bone marrow trephine biopsy reveals a hypercellular bone marrow, often with myeloid hyperplasia and rarely erythroid hyperplasia. Dysgranulopoiesis is often minimal and if identified includes pseudo–Pelger–Huët cells or cells with cytoplasmic hypogranularity. Megakaryocytes are frequently decreased in number with minimal dysplasia. Monocytes are often increased, but less so than in the peripheral blood. Blasts are increased, but below the threshold of 20%. Reticulin fibrosis may be present {8607451;9160658}.

Neuroblastoma is defined as having a background of detectable neurites with or without Homer Wright rosette formation. Conventional nuclear morphology is often described to have a salt-and-pepper appearance.

ERMS is variably cellular, ranging from stellate cells in abundant myxoid stroma to sheets of poorly differentiated, densely packed cells with round to slightly angulated nuclei {23765537}. ERMS may have prominent spindle cell morphology, although foci of typical ERMS morphology are usually present {8434703}. Typical ERMS shows alternating loose and dense cellularity, often with scattered differentiated rhabdomyoblasts. After therapy, differentiated and mature rhabdomyoblasts may persist {17006261}.

Pilocytic astrocytomas have low to moderate levels of cellularity with cells displaying a wide variety of morphological features. Nuclei are often round to elongated. Multinucleated cells with horseshoe-shaped nuclear clusters are often seen. Rosenthal fibres and eosinophilic granular bodies are common. A myxoid background with microcystic change is common, as are degenerative changes, calcifications, hyalinized vessels, and haemorrhage {25792358}.

Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumours display a multinodular intracortical growth pattern and the pathognomonic specific glioneuronal element, which is columns oriented perpendicularly to the cortical surface, formed by bundles of axons lined by small oligodendroglia-like cells. Between these columns, neurons with normal cytology appear to float in a mucoid matrix {3143922}.

Conventional oligodendrogliomas have uniformly round nuclei that are slightly larger than those of normal oligodendrocytes and show an increase in chromatin density or a delicate salt-and-pepper pattern. A distinct nuclear membrane is often apparent. In formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue, oligodendroglioma cells often appear as round with well-defined cell membranes and clear cytoplasm around the central spherical nucleus. This creates the typical honeycomb or fried-egg appearance.

Central giant cell granulomas display a solid, unencapsulated tumour with clustering of numerous multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells. The background is haemorrhagic and consists of vascular stroma with bland spindle cells {31950466}.

Cytology

ERMS reveals primitive round and spindled cells.

Smears of pilocytic astrocytoma reveal cells with long, fine, hair-like processes. The cells usually show uniform, round to spindled nuclei with minimal atypia. Rosenthal fibres, eosinophilic granular bodies, and glomeruloid vessels can be seen.

Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumours reveal a spread of uniform rounded cells, without substantial pleomorphism or mitotic activity. A variably mucinous or fibrillary matrix is sometimes identified.

Oligodendroglioma cells show uniform round nuclei and well-defined cytoplasm.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genetic analysis is recommended for assessment of PTPN11, KRAS, MRAS, MAP2K1, MAP2K2, NF1, NRAS, RASA2, RRAS, RRAS2, RIT1, SHOC2, PPP1CB, SPRED1, SPRED2, SOS1, SOS2, RAF1, BRAF, CBL, CDC42, and LZTR1 {25683281;36394128}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: germline pathogenic variant in gene(s) associated with the RAS signalling pathway and the classic phenotype suggestive of Noonan syndrome.

Desirable: correlation between the expected oncological subtype and Noonan syndrome–associated clinical phenotype.

Staging

Noonan syndrome–related malignancies are staged similarly to their sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

JMML is the most common malignancy in Noonan syndrome and confers poor overall survival if untreated, with a risk of transformation to an aggressive phenotype in about 33% of cases; however, patients with Noonan syndrome may demonstrate spontaneous remission of their myeloid neoplasm. Thus, treatment decisions (e.g. haematopoietic stem cell transplantation) need to be made carefully and with the inclusion of a JMML expert. Owing to the increased incidence of other malignancies in Noonan syndrome, patients and providers should be aware of this risk. If a malignancy develops, subsequent treatment should follow standard protocols.
