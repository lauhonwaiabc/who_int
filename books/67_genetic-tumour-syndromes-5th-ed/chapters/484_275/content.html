4.8.4 Down syndrome

Definition

Down syndrome (DS) is a chromosome disorder caused by the presence of a germline (constitutional) additional copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21) and characterized by multiple congenital anomalies and predisposition to myeloid proliferations, which manifest as transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM) and myeloid leukaemia of DS (ML-DS).

MIM numbering

190685 Down syndrome

ICD-11 coding

LD40.0 Complete trisomy 21

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Mosaic Down syndrome; translocation Down syndrome

Localization

TAM presents mainly in peripheral blood, usually with mild bone marrow involvement; severe cases affect the liver, spleen, pleura, and pericardium. ML-DS is predominantly detected in the bone marrow but can also affect the liver and spleen, and rarely the CNS {18182574;21849481}.

Clinical features

The DS phenotype is well characterized and involves manifestations affecting multiple body systems, most prominently musculoskeletal, neurological, and cardiovascular (#38383Box 5.05). Characteristic facial features comprise a flat profile with upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, and a protruding tongue. Individuals with DS are also predisposed to multiple health conditions, such as autoimmune diseases, early-onset Alzheimer disease, thyroid dysfunction, obstructive sleep apnoea, epilepsy, hearing and vision problems, recurrent infections, and the haematological disorders TAM and ML-DS {35490285}. The clinical presentation for mosaic trisomy 21 carriers is highly variable, ranging from being comparable to individuals with non-mosaic trisomy 21 to a nearly normal phenotype {25412855}.

TAM typically presents as peripheral leukocytosis with increased blasts during the first 7 days of life; it can be asymptomatic to life-threatening {24021668}. Severe disease manifests with hepatomegaly (40%), splenomegaly (30%), vesicopapular skin rash (11%), and pleural and/or pericardial effusion (9%) {29916557}. TAM may also transform into ML-DS.

Epidemiology

The frequency of trisomy 21 in the population is 1 in 700 to 1 in 1000 live births {31580536}. This estimation is influenced by multiple factors, such as maternal age at conception and the availability and accuracy of screening tests {25822844}. Among pregnancies with a diagnosis of DS confirmed by cytogenomic evaluation of chorionic villi samples or amniotic fluid, approximately 30–40% and 10–20%, respectively, will subsequently miscarry {9826896}; it is estimated that nearly 80% of conceptuses with trisomy 21 are lost across all clinically recognized pregnancies {6241455}.

TAM, the most common leukaemia in neonates, occurs in 25–30% of newborns with DS (GATA1 alterations present). Of these newborns with DS, 10% have clinical TAM and 15% have silent TAM (no clinical or haematological features) {24021668}. A history of TAM is found in approximately 30% of ML-DS patients, and the median age of onset of ML-DS is 1.6 years {28389462}. The incidence of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) is 150-fold higher in young children with DS compared with the overall paediatric population. Similarly, the incidence of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma in young children with DS is 40-fold higher than a comparable group of children without DS. Solid tumours are less common in individuals with DS of all ages compared with the general population {10675114}.

Etiology

DS is caused by the presence of a germline additional copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), most often (95%) resulting from non-disjunction of homologous chromosome 21 or its chromatid during meiotic division, although about 4% arise from translocations.

Pathogenesis

Advanced maternal age at conception is a major risk factor for trisomy 21 owing to non-disjunction of homologous chromosomes or chromatids during meiotic division. DS cases arise from segregation errors in both meiosis I and meiosis II during oogenesis and more rarely during spermatogenesis {19050929;6241455}.

DS is caused by gain of chromosome 21, particularly q-arm material as the p-arm of this acrocentric chromosome contains only repetitive regions and copies of ribosomal RNA genes. While approximately 95% of DS cases are due to the presence of three independent chromosome 21 copies, 4% are estimated to arise from translocations, most frequently unbalanced Robertsonian der(14;21)(q10;q10),+21 or +21,der(21;21)(q10;q10), and 1% are due to mosaicism with a normal cell line from a mitotic error after zygote formation {8733049}. Partial gain of chromosome 21 is rare and the phenotype varies according to the region triplicated {19002211}. The recurrence risk for parents with an affected child can be 1–100% and is influenced by the structural form of the additional chromosome 21 {35490285}. Three independent copies of chromosome 21 gives a recurrence risk of 1% until the mother’s age exceeds 40 years, when the risk increases according to advanced maternal age. A parental carrier of a Robertsonian translocation involving another chromosome such as t(14;21)(q10;q10) has an empirically defined recurrence risk for maternal carriers of 10–15% and for paternal carriers of 2–5%, while a Robertsonian t(21;21)(q10;q10) carrier parent has a theoretical 100% recurrence risk.

For most TAM cases, application of a sensitive testing method to neonatal peripheral blood demonstrates the presence of hemizygous or heterozygous somatic variants in exon 2 or 3 of GATA1 at chromosome Xp11.23 {24021668;12586620;18182574}. These somatic variants encode a truncated GATA1 protein (GATA1s) lacking the N-terminal activation domain {12172547}, which is leukaemogenic only in trisomy 21 long-term haematopoietic stem cells {16783379;34244384}. Identical GATA1 variants are present in ML-DS cases {24056718;31303423;14656875}. ML-DS evolves from pre-existing TAM clones through acquisition of additional alterations, usually in cohesion components, epigenetic regulators, or tyrosine kinase signalling pathway genes {24056718;31303423}. Although ML-DS arises from the combination of trisomy 21, GATA1 variant, and secondary mutations in other genes, the exact mechanism of ML-DS development is unknown.

Macroscopic appearance

Not relevant

Histopathology

In TAM, peripheral blood blasts are seen in all symptomatic cases and frequently exceed bone marrow blasts; blast morphology is variable. In ML-DS, bone marrow blasts may be < 20% with morphology and immunophenotype similar to TAM with frequent reticulin fibrosis. Furthermore, some cases may present with a prominent proliferation of abnormal megakaryocytes {18182574;21849481;29916557}. For additional details of the histopathology, please refer to the sections on myeloid proliferations associated with Down syndrome and on B-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma in the Haematolymphoid tumours volume of this series.

Cytology

Blood film and bone marrow aspirate cytology are used for diagnosis. Solid organ cytology is not relevant.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Prenatal screening to assess DS risk traditionally involved various combinations of maternal serum and amniotic fluid biochemical analyte measurement with ultrasonography {35490285}. More recently, non-invasive prenatal screening has allowed the evaluation of cell-free placental DNA in maternal blood, with higher sensitivity and specificity {28397325}. Screening results positive for an increased risk of trisomy 21 in a fetus should be confirmed with a diagnostic genetic test.

Cytogenomic analysis of a prenatal (chorionic villi samples, amniotic fluid), cord blood, or postnatal peripheral blood specimen is the gold standard to confirm the presence and the structure of an additional copy of chromosome 21 associated with DS. Chromosomal microarray analysis will identify gain of the q-arm of chromosome 21; however, this testing does not provide structural information to inform parental recurrence risk.

In an individual with DS, the diagnosis of TAM requires detection of a clone with a pathogenic variant in exon 2 or 3 of GATA1 in peripheral blood {29916557;14656875;21715302}. DNA sequencing of GATA1 is recommended as soon as possible after birth as peripheral blood blast percentage often falls rapidly and can affect testing sensitivity {29916557}. Similar GATA1 testing should be performed in all cases of suspected ML-DS, in combination with a myeloid gene panel that includes genes commonly altered in ML-DS {24056718;31303423}. Low blasts in the bone marrow may also cause false negative results. AML in children with DS who are > 4 years of age is rare and may represent an unrelated AML {18059480}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: confirmation of germline trisomy 21 (see #38384Table 5.30); for TAM: peripheral blood leukocytosis with increased blasts; for ML-DS: myeloid neoplasm with persistent increased peripheral blood and/or bone marrow blasts (may be < 20%); for TAM or ML-DS: detection of exon 2 or 3 GATA1 sequence variant (GATA1 exon 2 or 3 sequencing should be performed in all cases with peripheral blood blasts > 10%).

Desirable: determination of the structure of the additional copy of chromosome 21 for recurrence risk assessment; for ML-DS: mutation profiling and detection of sequence variants in other genes, for example cohesin complex, EZH2, KANSL1, and/or JAK3.

Prognosis and prediction

Data from global and national registries have demonstrated that the life expectancy for individuals with DS has improved over time {40342505;26651430}. Clinical care guidance for individuals and their families in whom a diagnosis of DS is established by chromosome analysis or suspected by prenatal screening is provided by the American Academy of Pediatrics {35490285}.

TAM resolves in 2–3 months without treatment in > 90% of patients. However, approximately 20% of clinical TAM cases later develop into ML-DS {18182574;21849481}. No clinical, haematological, or molecular features reliably predict which TAM cases will progress to ML-DS {18182574;21849481;24021668;29916557}. Risk factors for early death in severe TAM include preterm delivery, ascites, leukocyte count > 100 × 109/L, hepatomegaly, and coagulopathy {18182574;21849481;29916557}.

ML-DS prognosis is better than AML prognosis in children without DS, with event-free and overall survival rates of 87–89% and 89–90%, respectively, despite lower chemotherapy doses {28389462;28400376}. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is reserved for relapse and is rarely performed. DS-associated acute lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma has an inferior outcome compared with patients without DS, mostly related to frequent relapse, treatment-related complications, and lower frequency of favourable cytogenetics {24222333}.
