4.2.3 Fanconi anaemia (FANC genes)

Definition

Fanconi anaemia (FA) is a clinically and genetically heterogeneous disorder caused by biallelic germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in the FA/BRCA DNA double strand–break repair pathway, resulting in chromosomal breakage and hypersensitivity to DNA crosslinking agents. Characteristic clinical features include developmental abnormalities in major organ systems, early-onset bone marrow failure, endocrine anomalies, and a high predisposition to cancer.

Localization

Manifestations of FA may develop in all organs and tissues.

Clinical features

FA is characterized by a range of physical abnormalities, progressive bone marrow failure (FA-BMF), and an increased cancer risk. Classic features include short stature, skeletal abnormalities (abnormal thumbs, absent radii, hip dysplasia), skin hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation, abnormal facies (triangular face, microcephaly, microphthalmia), abnormal kidneys, and decreased fertility {2985019;12393516}, although the absence of these features does not exclude the diagnosis of FA. The most common neoplasms associated with FA are myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (HNSCC), squamous cell carcinoma of the vulva and uterine cervix, Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma), and medulloblastoma {12393516;20507306;25307146}. Children with cancers arising in the setting of specific FA subtypes may have a family history of breast, ovarian, and pancreatic cancer in the maternal and/or paternal lineages, caused by heterozygous carrier status for pathogenic variants in BRCA2, PALB2, or more rarely other FA genes.

Haematological conditions in patients with Fanconi anaemia

A recently proposed classification {34196171} distinguishes between the following five categories:

  • FA-BMF: normal blast percentage and normal cytogenetics in a patient with cytopenia; FA-BMF ranges from mild to severe;
  • FA with aberration of indeterminate potential (FA-AIP): normal blast percentage and AIP; AIPs are clonal aberrations, such as duplication of 1q, that remain stable over a prolonged period of time without transformation;
  • FA-MDS without excess blasts: normal blast percentage and clonal aberrations unambiguously associated with transformation (−7, −7q, +3q, complex);
  • FA-MDS with excess blasts: increased blast percentage in bone marrow ≥ 5% to 20% or in peripheral blood ≥ 2% to < 20% (but bone marrow and peripheral blood < 20% blasts);
  • FA-AML: increased blast percentage in bone marrow or peripheral blood ≥ 20%.

    Patients with FA have a greatly increased risk of dysplasia of the squamous epithelium lining the oropharynx and progression to squamous cell carcinoma {12525204;15331448;18831513;22504776}. FA-associated HNSCC frequently occurs as multifocal disease, preceded by oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMD) presenting as lichenoid keratosis or leukoplakia {25662766;26276748;29481998}. Epithelial cells isolated from FA-associated OPMD demonstrate a higher frequency of micronuclei and other nuclear aberrations (DNA damage markers), as well as variants involving the FAT1, TP53, and NOTCH2 genes involved in oral carcinogenesis {29481998}. FA-associated OPMDs have a higher risk of malignant transformation than those in the general population {29481998}. As such, FA-associated OPMDs must be routinely biopsied and kept under close surveillance, even if they display a low-risk clinical or histological appearance. New studies suggest that brush biopsies play a role in oral cancer surveillance {34854575;35884529}.

    CNS tumours in patients with Fanconi anaemia

    The predominant CNS tumour manifestation of FA is medulloblastoma, which is most often the sonic hedgehog–activated molecular subtype and frequently also TP53 mutant {2985019;14559878;14670928;14707715;16825431;17200671;19373780;23698033;26064523;26657402;28453743;29753700}. Individual examples of patients with FA with other CNS embryonal tumours or glioblastoma have also been reported {19530235;26740091}.

    Other childhood cancers in patients with Fanconi anaemia

    Beyond the aforementioned tumour associations, children with FA complementation groups D1 or N (due to biallelic germline variants in BRCA2 and PALB2, respectively) also may develop Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma), neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and acute lymphoblastic leukaemia {15689453;16410081;25381700;34597127}.

    Epidemiology

    FA is most often an autosomal recessive disorder resulting from homozygous or compound heterozygous germline variants in the 21 different FANC genes. The two exceptions are complementation group B (FANCB), which is X-linked, and complementation group R (RAD51), which is autosomal dominant. The estimated carrier frequency of a heterozygous germline variant in one of the FANC genes is 1 in 100–200 individuals, while the approximate incidence of FA syndrome is 1 case per 130 000 births {21739583}. Some ethnic groups, such as Ashkenazi Jews, Afrikaners, and Tunisians, have the highest carrier frequency (1 in 80–90 individuals) owing to founder effects and isolation {12525534;33679882}. FANC-A is the most common type, accounting for 65% of all FA cases, followed by FANC-C (12%), and FANC-G (8%) {29254745}. According to a recent study enrolling 421 patients for all FA subgroups combined, the cancer-specific standardized incidence ratio for childhood myeloid neoplasms was 445 (95% CI 272–687) {34597127}. By age 18 years, 10.6% of patients with FA have developed some type of cancer {34597127}. The risk of solid malignancies among patients with FA increases with age, and it is estimated that one in three develop a solid malignancy by 40 years of age {12393424}. The increased risk of HNSCC in patients with FA has been estimated at 500–800 fold {12525204;15331448;18831513;22504776;26567103}. The relative risk for squamous cell carcinoma of the vulva among FA patients is approximately 3000-fold higher than in the general population {23558727}.

    Etiology

    FA can be caused by germline transmission of deleterious mutations in at least 21 different genes, which result in a mostly similar clinical phenotype but with some differences, including variable tumour predisposition. Depending on the specific causative gene, FA is differentiated into complementation groups A through W (e.g. complementation group A is caused by biallelic germline mutation in the FANCA gene). Increased risk of the different haematological manifestations and HNSCC among FA patients has not been linked to any specific FA genotype to date {32480311}. However, only two FA complementation groups have been associated with an increased risk of medulloblastoma, Wilms tumour, neuroblastoma, and acute lymphoblastic leukaemia: D1 and N, which result from biallelic germline mutations in the BRCA2 and PALB2 genes, respectively.

    Increased risk of HNSCC among patients with FA occurs independently of exposure to tobacco and alcohol {15331448;26567103;21734703;22922648}. Oral HPV prevalence is higher in patients with FA compared with their unaffected family members and unrelated controls according to one study {33803570}. However, the etiological role for HPV in FA-associated HNSCC remains unproven {15735012;23558727}. After allogeneic haemopoietic stem cell transplantation, patients with FA have a higher risk of HNSCC compared with non-transplanted FA patients, which has been attributed to both the conditioning regimen and the occurrence of chronic oral graft-versus-host disease {8547667;22504776}.

    Pathogenesis

    The FANC genes encode proteins involved in the homologous recombination of DNA double-strand breaks and in the repair of DNA crosslinks. Deleterious mutation of FANC genes is causative of FA, resulting in impaired homologous recombination and crosslink repair, which drives chromosomal aberrations, such as amplifications, deletions, and translocations, that promote tumorigenesis {16675878;17476307;26593718}.

    Cancers arising in the setting of FA are genomically characterized by a large number of intrachromosomal breaks, which can be visualized by copy-number analysis as several chromosomes having multiple segmental gains and losses {28453743;29753700}. A distinct mutation signature has also been observed in cancers arising in the setting of FA, featuring elevated numbers of large (> 3 bp) insertions and deletions with overlapping microhomology at breakpoint junctions characteristic of homologous recombination deficiency {29753700;36450981}. There is also an enrichment of structural variants, including unbalanced translocations and fold-back inversions {36450981}.

    Macroscopic appearance

    Solid tumours related to FA have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

    Histopathology

    FA-BMF mimics myelodysplastic neoplasms with low blasts or severe aplastic anaemia. Dysgranulopoiesis and dysmegakaryopoiesis are histological indicators of progression to MDS {20023263;29998978}.

    The histological features of FA-associated HNSCC are mostly similar to those of their sporadic counterparts. FA-associated HNSCC is often poorly differentiated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and has been reported to exhibit a high proportion of cancer stem cells {21138479;25340704}.

    Medulloblastomas arising in the setting of FA are usually of the large cell / anaplastic histological type, or they can have desmoplastic/nodular histology with superimposed anaplasia {29753700}.

    Cytology

    Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

    Diagnostic molecular pathology

    Diagnosis of FA has traditionally employed chromosomal breakage analysis performed on patient-derived cultures of either leukocytes from a peripheral blood sample or dermal fibroblasts from a skin biopsy after treatment with an agent that induces DNA interstrand crosslinks, such as diepoxybutane (DEB) or mitomycin C. Some laboratories employ an alternative cell-cycle analysis methodology assessing for G2 phase prolongation/arrest in stimulated leukocyte cultures {3592130}. Notably, somatically acquired reversion mutations have been reported to occur within haematopoietic stem cells in patients with FA, resulting in mosaicism within the circulating leukocyte population, which may potentially confound diagnostic testing utilizing leukocyte cultures in these rare patients {33984160}. Mutation analysis of a germline DNA sample to assess for pathogenic germline mutations or deletions in the FANC genes is a complementary diagnostic methodology that can identify the causative gene abnormality.

    Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

    Essential: positive chromosomal breakage analysis after exposure of leukocyte or fibroblast cultures to crosslinking agents (e.g. DEB test).

    Desirable: biallelic pathogenic germline mutations in a FANC gene, except FANCB and RAD51 (FANCR) requiring a monoallelic germline variant.

    Staging

    Staging is as for sporadic counterparts.

    Prognosis and prediction

    Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is highly efficacious for patients with FA-BMF using matched related and unrelated donors, and outcomes with haploidentical donors have improved remarkably {24144640;33656536}. Current preparative regimens for haematopoietic stem cell transplantation have eliminated the use of radiation and employ low doses of alkylating chemotherapy to lower risks of secondary malignancies and organ toxicity. Treatment of patients with FA-MDS with excess blasts or FA-AML remains a challenge and the outcome is poor, often requiring initiation of transplant regimens during aplasia from induction chemotherapy {27020090}.

    FA-associated HNSCCs have an aggressive biological behaviour characterized by poor survival and high relapse rates {26484938;32480311}. Surgery is the primary treatment of choice for FA-associated HNSCC given the high toxicity associated with radiation and chemotherapy in this patient population {26484938;32480311}.

    Medulloblastomas arising in the setting of FA have a dismal prognosis {29753700}. This is probably a reflection of both the aggressive biology of these tumours and the fact that these patients cannot tolerate the high doses of genotoxic chemotherapies used to treat medulloblastoma as standard.

    Patients with FA have a dramatically increased risk of developing secondary neoplasms after radiation or chemotherapy, predominantly therapy-associated MDS and AML.

    ·   Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma)

    ·   acute lymphoblastic leukaemia
