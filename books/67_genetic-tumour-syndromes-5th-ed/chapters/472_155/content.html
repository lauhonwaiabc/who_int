4.7.2 Werner syndrome (WRN)

Definition

Werner syndrome (WS) is an autosomal recessive tumour syndrome caused by germline pathogenic loss-of-function variants affecting WRN and characterized by genetic instability, leading to multiple features consistent with accelerated premature ageing and tumour formation with elevated risk of thyroid carcinomas, melanoma, meningiomas, and sarcomas of soft tissue and bone.

MIM numbering

277700 Werner syndrome; WRN

ICD-11 coding

LD2B Syndromes with premature ageing appearance as a major feature

Related terminology

Acceptable: progeria of the adult.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Location is as appropriate for tumour type.

Clinical features

Patients with WS develop a prematurely aged appearance beginning in the second decade of life, with an increased risk of developing cancer and other age-associated non-neoplastic diseases (#37524Box 5.02).

The most consistent clinical findings, first seen after the age of 10 years, include bilateral cataracts, skin changes resembling scleroderma, short stature with absence of the pubertal growth spurt, and premature greying and loss of scalp hair. Less consistent findings, especially in younger patients, include diabetes mellitus, hypogonadism, osteoporosis {5327241;15734684;33087645;33155383;35343623}, soft-tissue calcification {33260264} [[Hatamochi A. Dermatological features and collagen metabolism in Werner syndrome. In: Goto M, Miller RW, editors. From premature gray hair to helicase–Werner syndrome: Implications for aging and cancer. Tokyo (Japan): Japan Scientific Societies Press; 2001. pp. 51–9. Bookref]], premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, a high-pitched, squeaky, or hoarse voice, and flat feet. Scoring systems developed from clinical findings and family history can identify patients likely to have WS, where the diagnosis can now be confirmed by molecular testing (#37524Box 5.02) {20301687;22817610}. Additional information on WS can be found in Oshima et al. {20301687} and at the University of Washington Werner Syndrome International Registry website (https://dlmp.uw.edu/research-center/werner/).

The elevated cancer risk in WS is type selective and includes both epithelial and non-epithelial neoplasms. Two thirds of reports of neoplasia in WS are, in decreasing frequency, thyroid epithelial neoplasms (most commonly thyroid follicular carcinoma), acral lentiginous melanoma, meningioma, soft tissue sarcomas, haematological malignancies and preleukaemic disorders, and primary bone neoplasms (#37525Table 5.24). These neoplasms all occur at significantly elevated risks, except leukaemias, at 8.9-fold to 53.5-fold higher in patients with WS versus population controls {23573208}. Many other neoplasms, including common adult epithelial malignancies and less-frequent neoplasms, such as astrocytoma and phaeochromocytoma, have been reported in patients with WS, although none is clearly at elevated risk versus population control data {23573208}.

Unusual features of neoplasia in WS include early age at diagnosis, atypical sites (e.g. osteosarcoma of the patella), and especially multiple neoplasms; about 22% of patients with WS (42 of 189) were found to have one to four additional concurrent or sequential neoplasms, often of different types and at different sites {23573208}. The most common causes of death in WS are cancer and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Epidemiology

Worldwide frequency estimates for WS are 1:22 000 to 1:1 000 000, with population frequency strongly influenced by local pathogenic founder variants and consanguinity [[Schellenberg GD, Miki T, Yu CE, et al. Werner syndrome. In: Valle D, Beaudet AL, Vogelstein B, et al., editors. Scriver’s online metabolic and molecular bases of inherited disease. 8th ed. New York (NY): McGraw-Hill; 2011. Bookref]]. Known pathogenic WRN variants occur in large population genomic datasets at a higher carrier frequency than suggested by these estimates (2.12%; 191 of 9019 individuals) {27859906}. WS is probably underdiagnosed in the absence of early, well-defined developmental features, and owing to a variable, slowly developing clinical phenotype {20301687}.

Etiology

WS is caused by biallelic pathogenic loss-of-function variants in WRN at 8p12, leading to loss of the ubiquitously expressed WRN protein and its helicase and exonuclease activities {27859906} (#37525Table 5.24).

Pathogenesis

The WRN protein has both helicase and exonuclease activities and interacts with many other proteins to play multiple important roles in DNA metabolism (see #37525Table 5.24). Loss of these functions causes genomic instability by perturbing DNA replication, recombination, and repair, telomere maintenance, and gene expression. Genetic instability and altered gene expression can drive cell loss, dysfunction, and senescence in many cell lineages and tissues. The resulting atrophic or hyperplastic changes probably contribute to the cellular and clinical phenotype of WS {26984941;26993153}. Pathogenic variants in WRN include those generating premature stop codons, insertions/deletions causing frameshifts, multiexon deletions and duplications, or splicing site alterations leading to exon skipping or new exon generation {27667302;20301687}.

Macroscopic appearance

Multisystem non-neoplastic pathology is common in patients with WS coming to autopsy, involving most notably the skin and cardiovascular system {27559010}.

Histopathology

The histopathological spectrum of neoplasia in WS {23573208} overlaps with, but is distinct from, neoplasia risk in the related RECQ helicase deficiency syndromes, Bloom syndrome and Rothmund–Thomson syndrome (see section Bloom syndrome and section Rothmund–Thomson syndrome).

Thyroid neoplasms are most often follicular thyroid carcinomas, followed by papillary or anaplastic thyroid carcinomas. For unknown reasons, thyroid neoplasms and melanomas are significantly underrepresented in patients with WS outside of Japan. The risk of melanoma is confined almost exclusively to acral lentiginous melanoma of the palms and soles, or in mucosa lining the nasal cavity, oesophagus, and vulva. Soft tissue sarcomas documented in WS include undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, and leiomyosarcoma. Meningiomas are the third most common neoplasm and have been documented in patients worldwide. There are several reports of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours, pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and undifferentiated spindle cell sarcoma. Osteosarcoma is the dominant primary malignant bone tumour in WS, together with rare reports of chondrosarcoma or osteochondroma. Osteosarcomas may display osteoblastic or fibroblastic differentiation and be extraskeletal in location. Leukaemias cover the full spectrum of acute myelogenous leukaemias, as well as reports of atypical leukaemias and preleukaemic marrow disorders. Rare lymphoid neoplasms (T-cell leukaemia, plasmacytoma) have also been reported. The elevated risk of marrow-associated neoplasia may reflect the progressive accumulation of bone marrow genetic damage in WS patients {10811130}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

A diagnosis of WS can be established by clinical criteria alone (#37524Box 5.02). However, molecular testing for biallelic loss-of-function variants in WRN or altered protein expression {20301687} may be especially helpful in young individuals and others in whom the WS clinical phenotype may be incompletely penetrant or developed. The detection of pathogenic WRN variants also distinguish WS from atypical WS, a phenocopy caused by variants in the unrelated LMNA gene {26993153}. Additional information on WS-associated WRN variants and on genetic testing can be found in Oshima et al. {20301687} and on the Genetic Testing Registry website of the US National Institutes of Health (NIH; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gtr/conditions/C0043119/).

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: all four major cardinal signs (bilateral ocular cataracts, premature greying and/or thinning of scalp hair, characteristic dermatological pathology, and short stature) plus two or more additional findings; OR biallelic pathogenic WRN variants (confirmed diagnosis); alternatively: ≤ 3 cardinal signs AND additional signs (if present) (suspected diagnosis).

 

See #37524Box 5.02 and #37525Table 5.24.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Cells from patients with WS, and presumably their neoplasms, are hypersensitive to many classes of DNA-damaging anticancer therapies. Conventional doses of these agents may lead in some instances to treatment-related toxicity or death {16019564;30146558}.
