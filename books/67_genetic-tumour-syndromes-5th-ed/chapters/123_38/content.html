Definition

Sturge–Weber syndrome (SWS) is a congenital neurocutaneous condition due to a somatic mosaic activating sequence variant, most often in GNAQ, affecting small blood vessels and capillaries. There is no evidence that it is hereditary.

Related terminology

Localization

The vascular anomaly (capillary malformation, CM) that characterizes SWS can impact as many as three organ systems, including the eye (choroid, episclera, conjunctiva, anterior segment) {27257371;31313748}, skin {15295918;33175124}, and brain (leptomeninges) {23907585;16505293;21712663}.

Clinical features

Choroid CM (diffuse choroidal haemangioma) occurs in 20–70% of SWS patients {15763200;1287171}. Patients may experience blurry, distorted, or declined vision related to choroidal thickening and secondary retinal and retinal pigment epithelium changes from exudation {24658736;29403070;12689885}.

Conjunctival and episcleral CMs appear as red, dilated, tortuous, and elevated vessels {27257371;15763200;1287171}.

Anterior segment abnormalities of the eyes in SWS include iris heterochromia and malformations in the anterior chamber angle {7523256}. Anterior chamber angle malformations, increased episcleral venous pressure related to CM, and accelerated ageing of the trabecular meshwork are some of the proposed mechanisms for the pathogenesis of glaucoma in SWS {27257371;34847393}.

Cutaneous CM appears as discolouration of the skin, ranging from pink to red to deep purple. It can appear flat, but with time may develop vascular ectasias producing a nodular appearance and superficial blebbing {33175124}. The characteristic SWS cutaneous lesion, facial CM (port-wine stain), is usually unilateral and follows the distribution of the first branch of the trigeminal nerve and the embryonic vasculature in this area. SWS-associated CMs less frequently involve the trunk and extremities.

Leptomeningeal CM most commonly involves the parietal and occipital lobes and is best diagnosed by MRI {23907585}. Venous stasis within the malformation may lead to hypoxia and ischaemia marked by dystrophic calcification and cortical necrosis. Neurological manifestations are variable and include headaches, hemiparesis/hemiplegia, visual field deficits (if involving the occipital cortex), recurrent stroke-like episodes / transient ischaemic attacks, and seizures of varying degrees of severity {21712663;3598114;7769179}. Cognitive effects are highly variable ranging from unaffected to mild learning disabilities to severe cognitive impairments. Behavioural problems have been reported including attention problems and mood issues {23000106}.

SWS is associated with endocrine dysfunction, including central hypothyroidism and an increased risk of growth hormone deficiency {16551788}. This presents as childhood growth below the fifth percentile or height significantly below the mean parental height.

Epidemiology

The incidence of SWS is estimated at 1 in 20 000–50 000 births. There is no sex, race, or ethnicity predilection {26564078}.

Etiology

The congenital vascular anomalies characterizing SWS are due to a gain-of-function postzygotic somatic sequence variant in GNAQ at chromosome 9q21.2. Approximately 90% of patients with SWS have a specific GNAQ sequence variant, NM_002072.5:c.548G>A p.(Arg183Gln), which was found in affected tissues at a mosaicism level of 1–18% {23656586}. This sequence variant leads to activation of downstream MAPK signalling, resulting in vascular anomalies {23656586}. The p.Arg183Gln alteration has also been detected in skin tissue from patients with non-syndromic port-wine stains. Recently, a mosaic activating NM_002067.5:c.547C>T p.(Arg183Cys) sequence variant in GNA11, a gene located at chromosome 19p13.3 that is highly homologous to GNAQ, was identified in a limited number of GNAQ p.Arg183Gln-negative cases of SWS; the GNA11-altered cases may have an atypical clinical presentation {32771470;31838126;35715928;31726051}.

Pathogenesis

SWS is thought to result from aberrant vessel growth and malformations in the skin, eye, and brain due to the effects of somatic mosaic GNAQ sequence variants on the cephalic neural crest during the first trimester {23656586}. It is hypothesized that the GNAQ sequence variant may affect the G protein–coupled receptor endothelin, which has important roles in vasculogenesis {23656586}. The embryonic stage of development during which the sequence variant occurs may govern whether the malformations are syndromic or not {23656586}; non-syndromic port-wine stains may represent a late origin of the somatic GNAQ sequence variant in vascular endothelial cells, while gene alterations in SWS may occur earlier in development in progenitor cells that are precursors to a larger variety of cell types and tissues, resulting in a syndromic phenotype.

Macroscopic appearance

Choroid CM appears as a diffusely thickened choroid with variably sized blood-filled vascular channels separated by thin septa, associated with dilated draining vortex veins. Port-wine skin and brain lesions are rarely examined (see Clinical features, above).

Histopathology

CMs in the choroid, episclera, and conjunctiva appear as unencapsulated collections of thin-walled, variably sized vascular channels. Secondary changes include retinal pigment epithelial alteration, subretinal fibrosis, serous (exudative) retinal detachment, and sequelae of paediatric glaucoma (buphthalmos) {34847393;820013}.

See the Skin tumours, Eye and orbit tumours, and Central nervous system tumours volumes for a full description of haemangiomas at those sites. CMs are histologically well circumscribed, showing sinusoidal congested vessels (caverns) without intervening arteries, capillaries, or veins. The vessels comprise a single layer of attenuated endothelium and fibrotic walls, without arterial or venous features and without smooth muscle cells. The vessels are arranged back to back with little or no interposed organ tissue. At a young age with early lesions, such as the classic facial port-wine stain, only minimal vascular changes are present, but over time in some patients there may be hyalinization, calcification, cholesterol crystals, and microhaemorrhages {28552149;21095470;26055853}.

Cytology

Diagnostic molecular pathology

GNAQ and, if necessary, GNA11 evaluation is performed using the affected tissue to identify a somatic heterozygous pathogenic sequence variant consistent with the diagnosis of SWS (although not specific for SWS) {23656586}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: two of three diagnostic criteria: cutaneous facial CM, increased intraocular pressure, and leptomeningeal CM; individuals with only leptomeningeal angiomatosis are considered to have type 3 SWS.

Desirable: demonstration of a pathogenic somatic sequence variant in GNAQ or GNA11 in affected tissue.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis and life expectancy for patients with SWS is markedly variable and depends on the location of the lesions and their functional impact. The extent of facial cutaneous CM may be predictive of neurological disability {28711177;30853154;33677229}. Bilateral brain involvement and early onset of intractable seizures before 1 year of life are clinical features associated with worse outcomes. Intellectual disability occurs in approximately half of individuals with SWS and is reportedly severe in a third of patients {8216540;7645596}. Screening and earlier detection of lesions may be beneficial to patients. Published guidelines for surveillance are available {33175124;30020536;29803545}.
