1.1.3 Juvenile polyposis syndrome (BMPR1A, SMAD4)

Definition

Juvenile polyposis syndrome (JPS) is an autosomal dominant tumour syndrome caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in SMAD4 or BMPR1A, characterized by multiple juvenile polyps and predisposition to colorectal cancer.

MIM numbering

174900 Juvenile polyposis syndrome, infantile form

174900 Polyposis, juvenile intestinal

175050 Juvenile polyposis / hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia syndrome

ICD-11 coding

2B90.Y Other specified malignant neoplasms of colon (includes Juvenile polyposis of infancy & Juvenile gastrointestinal polyposis)

Related terminology

Acceptable: generalized juvenile polyposis; juvenile polyposis coli; juvenile polyposis of infancy; juvenile polyposis of the stomach; familial juvenile polyposis; combined juvenile polyposis / hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu) syndrome.

Not recommended: chromosome 10q22.3-q23.2 deletion syndrome; hamartomatous gastrointestinal polyposis.

Subtype(s)

Colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis; juvenile polyposis of infancy

Localization

The polyps in JPS predominantly arise throughout the colon, ranging in number from 1 to > 100. Gastric and small intestinal polyps, respectively, are found in as many as 85% and 14–33% of patients {3024956;25390638;10764709;26721304}.

Clinical features

Patients usually present with gastrointestinal bleeding, manifesting as haematochezia. Melaena, prolapsed rectal polyp, passage of tissue through the anus, intussusception, abdominal pain, and anaemia are also common. Juvenile polyposis can present as either colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis, or as juvenile polyposis of infancy, a subtype with severe symptoms {16685657;18510548;1091175}.

JPS is associated with a 34-fold increased relative risk of colorectal cancer and a cumulative risk of colorectal cancer of 39–68% by the age of 60 years {17303595;25645574}. The mean age at colon cancer diagnosis is 44 years (range 15–68 years). Gastric, duodenal, and pancreatic cancers are also reported in JPS, but no formal risk analysis for these malignancies has been reported {9869523}. Gastric cancer lifetime risk ranges from 10% to 30% {25645574}. The median patient age of upper gastrointestinal carcinoma is 58 years (range 21–73 years) {25645574}. Gastric and small bowel carcinomas together are estimated to occur at about one fifth the frequency of colorectal cancer in patients with JPS {17303595}.

SMAD4 variant carriers often have more-severe gastric polyposis and a higher risk of gastric cancer {25389115;28340255;12417513;12136244;16152648;17873119}, and SMAD4 variants are also associated with hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia {15031030}, whereas carriers of BMPR1A variants are more likely to have cardiac defects. For individuals without a variant in SMAD4 or BMPR1A, a different phenotype including younger age at diagnosis, lower likelihood of family history, and decreased need for colectomy has been reported {33097490}.

Epidemiology

The incidence has been estimated to be 1 case per 100 000–160 000 person-years in European populations. As many as half of all new cases arise in patients with no related family history {17311531;25645574}.

Etiology

A germline variant in SMAD4 or BMPR1A is identified in 50–60% of patients with JPS who have colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis {18823382;18178612;17873119;11381269;9582123}. Most variants are point mutations or small base-pair deletions, 5–15% of variants are larger deletions of one or more exons or the entire SMAD4 or BMPR1A gene, and about 10% of variants involve the gene promoter {18178612;17873119;18823382}. Juvenile polyposis of infancy is associated with contiguous deletion of the BMPR1A and PTEN genes at chromosome 10q23 {16685657;18510548;1091175;21834858}. Variants in ENG have been found in patients fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for JPS, but the role of ENG remains unclear {27146957;17204053}. Either a family history of polyps, cancer, and extraintestinal findings, or a minimum of three to five juvenile polyps is important to ascertain before embarking on genetic testing {27146957} (see #37422Box 2.01).

Pathogenesis

Gastrointestinal cancer in JPS arises from adenomatous epithelium within the juvenile polyp. Neoplasia may develop through a landscaper mechanism, in which it is postulated that neoplastic transformation of the epithelium is the result of an abnormal stromal environment {9616081;15017003}. In contrast, homozygous SMAD4 deletion has been shown to be limited to the epithelium of juvenile polyps from patients with germline SMAD4 variants, suggesting that SMAD4 acts as a gatekeeper in JPS pathogenesis {10811127;20682711}, although these hypotheses are not mutually exclusive.

Macroscopic appearance

Juvenile polyps are typically spherical with a smooth surface due to erosion.

Histopathology

Small juvenile polyps are indistinguishable from sporadic juvenile polyps. They have an eroded surface, abundant oedematous stroma with inflammatory cells, and cystically dilated glands with reactive epithelium. In the multilobulated or atypical variety, the lobules may be either rounded or finger like. There is a relative increase in the amount of epithelium over stroma. Glands show more budding and branching but less cystic change than in the classic solitary polyp {2853131}. Foci of dysplasia are regularly seen, particularly in atypical or multilobulated juvenile polyps {2853131;21412070}.

Gastric juvenile polyps show irregular hyperplastic glands mostly lined by foveolar epithelium and abundant oedematous stroma. Dysplasia is seen in about 15% of these polyps, which can show intestinal or pyloric gland differentiation {25390638}. It is difficult to reliably differentiate between gastric hyperplastic, juvenile, and Peutz–Jeghers polyps on the basis of histological features alone. It is therefore advisable to use more-inclusive terminology, such as “hamartomatous polyp, NOS”, and to recommend additional endoscopic examination with sampling of intestinal polyps and genetic testing in equivocal cases {20924281;26721304}.

By immunohistochemistry, loss of SMAD4 expression can be found in about half of all colonic and gastric juvenile polyps from patients with a germline SMAD4 variant, and it is indicative of somatic inactivation of the SMAD4 wildtype allele. In contrast, loss of SMAD4 expression is never seen in sporadic juvenile polyps or juvenile polyps from BMPR1A variant carriers. Loss of epithelial SMAD4 expression in the polyps of individuals with JPS is therefore specific for an underlying SMAD4 germline variant and can serve as a first screening for an underlying genetic defect. Loss of SMAD4 is not required for polyp formation or obligatory for neoplastic progression of a juvenile polyp {20682711;28340255}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Pathogenic germline variants in SMAD4 or BMPR1A can be demonstrated using next-generation sequencing.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The diagnostic criteria for JPS are summarized in #37422Box 2.01. Phenotypic subtypes of JPS and associated genetic defects are presented in #37423Table 2.05. See also #37454Table 2.06.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Patients with juvenile polyposis of infancy can have a shortened life expectancy and usually require early colectomy, because of severe diarrhoea, anaemia, and hypoalbuminaemia. Patients with colorectal or generalized juvenile polyposis are mainly at risk of colorectal and upper gastrointestinal cancer. Treatment should focus on preventing malignancy by close surveillance. Prophylactic colorectal and/or gastric surgery should be considered for patients who have a polyp burden that cannot be managed endoscopically, polyps with high-grade dysplasia, or a strong family history of gastrointestinal cancer {22171123;25645574}. Recent reports also suggest efficacy of sirolimus in decreasing polyp burden in individuals with both JPS and juvenile polyposis of infancy {31366686;34476273}.
