1.5.6 WAGR syndrome (WT1)

Definition

WAGR syndrome is a contiguous gene syndrome caused by de novo germline (constitutional) deletion at chromosome band 11p13 resulting in a predisposition to Wilms tumour, the presence at birth of Aniridia, Genitourinary anomalies, and Range of developmental delays (previously called mental Retardation). The genes underlying WAGR syndrome include WT1 deletion predisposing to abnormal development of the genitourinary system and Wilms tumour, and PAX6 deletion predisposing to abnormal ocular development.

Related terminology

Not recommended: Wilms, aniridia, genitourinary abnormalities, mental retardation.

Subtype(s)

WAGRO syndrome (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, a range of developmental delays, and obesity); chromosome 11p14.1-p13 deletion containing WT1, PAX6, and BDNF.

Localization

WAGR syndrome affects the eye, including abnormal development of the iris, cornea, and optic nerve; the kidney and renal system, including small glomeruli, glomerulosclerosis, and duplicated ureters; the gonads, including cryptorchidism, hypospadias, ambiguous genitalia, streak ovaries, and gonadoblastoma; and the CNS, including a range of developmental delays, autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), hypotonia, and seizures.

Clinical features

WAGR syndrome is often diagnosed at birth, based on the finding of aniridia – which is in most patients the initial observation that triggers the diagnosis – and confirmed by either traditional cytogenetics or microarray demonstrating deletion at 11p13 including both the PAX6 and WT1 genes. Aniridia is a panocular disorder and in addition to affecting iris development it can cause foveal hypoplasia, cataracts, lens dislocation, and optic nerve coloboma or hypoplasia {19218613}. The severity of ocular effects varies among patients. Wilms tumours (nephroblastomas) occur at an earlier age than in patients without WAGR syndrome (22 vs 39 months). Wilms tumours are often bilateral (17–37%) and of a low stage at presentation. Survivors of Wilms tumour have a high incidence of proteinuria in the second decade of life often leading to renal failure {14673045}.

WAGRO syndrome includes the physical features of WAGR syndrome, and in addition haploinsufficiency of BDNF, resulting in altered energy balance, hyperphagia, and obesity {11807873;18753648}.

Epidemiology

The National Wilms Tumor Study (NWTS) reported that 64 among 8553 or 0.75% of patients with Wilms tumour had features of WAGR syndrome. Of the patients with WAGR syndrome, 42% were female {14673045}.

Etiology

WAGR syndrome is caused by a de novo interstitial deletion of chromosome 11p13 including PAX6 and WT1.

Pathogenesis

The PAX6 hemizygous deletion results in abnormal ocular development and affected infants have aniridia and may also have increased ocular pressure resulting in glaucoma.

The WT1 heterozygous deletion is responsible for impaired renal development, persistence of intralobar rests, and a predisposition to Wilms tumour. Ambiguous genitalia, or genitourinary anomalies also observed with missense and loss-of-function variants in WT1, can also be seen in WAGR syndrome.

Histopathology

Histologically, WAGR syndrome is associated with stromal features observed in approximately 50% of cases and mixed Wilms tumours, with no anaplastic cases in a recent large European series {33146894}. Intralobar nephrogenic rests are a very frequent finding, observed in as many as 80% of cases {14673045;16199712}. Glomeruli in patients with WAGR syndrome are small (110 ± 37 microns, compared with 125 ± 18.5 microns in controls), a feature which may contribute to renal failure {17533022}.

Cytology

Not relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Diagnosis is by high-resolution cytogenetics or microarray demonstration of interstitial deletion at chromosome 11p13, with breakpoints and size of deletion varying among patients, including PAX6 and WT1. In an infant with aniridia, if a contiguous deletion is not detected, then molecular analysis for point mutation or deletion involving PAX6 can indicate sporadic aniridia, which substantially reduces the subsequent risk of Wilms tumour.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: characteristic physical features including aniridia or related eye anomalies; chromosome 11p13 heterozygous deletion containing both the PAX6 and WT1 genes demonstrated by karyotype, FISH, or chromosome microarray.

Staging

Wilms tumours are staged similarly to non-WAGR tumours, according to Children’s Oncology Group (COG) criteria if initial surgery, or International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) criteria if treated initially with chemotherapy. New approaches to staging may include stratification with molecular markers including loss of heterozygosity at 1p and 16q {33394739}.

Prognosis and prediction

Wilms tumours in WAGR syndrome as reported by the NWTS have typically favourable histology, are low stage metastatic, and have a high 4-year survival, reported as 95%. As in other Wilms tumour predisposition syndromes, presurgical treatment with chemotherapy is recommended and is effective to maximize nephron sparing {32459384}. Given the high risk of Wilms tumour, there are guidelines that recommend screening for Wilms tumour from the time of WAGR syndrome diagnosis by serial abdominal ultrasound initiated from diagnosis in infancy and repeated every 3–4 months until the age of 8 years {28674120}.

Long-term survival of patients with WAGR syndrome is influenced by the Wilms tumour diagnosis as well as the increased incidence of renal disease and failure {14673045}.
