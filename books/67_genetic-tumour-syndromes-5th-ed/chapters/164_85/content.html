1.6.4 Multiple osteochondromas (EXT1, EXT2)

Definition

Multiple osteochondromas is an autosomal dominant condition in which patients develop multiple benign cartilage-capped neoplasms at the bone projection. It is caused by variants in EXT1 or EXT2.

MIM numbering

133700 Exostoses, multiple, type I; EXT1

133701 Exostoses, multiple, type II; EXT2

ICD-11 coding

LD24.20 Multiple osteochondromas

Related terminology

Acceptable: hereditary multiple exostoses; multiple hereditary exostoses; osteochondromatosis.

Subtype(s)

Rarely, multiple osteochondromas is part of a contiguous gene deletion syndrome: Langer–Giedion syndrome (or trichorhinophalangeal syndrome type II) or Potocki–Shaffer syndrome (proximal 11p deletion syndrome, DEFECT11, 11p11.2 contiguous gene deletion syndrome).

Localization

Osteochondromas are located in bones that develop by enchondral ossification, especially the long bones of the extremities, predominantly around the knee, but flat bones like the ribs and pelvis may also be affected.

Clinical features

Osteochondromas begin to develop and increase in size in the first two decades of life, ceasing to grow when the growth plates close at the end of puberty. They are pedunculated or sessile (broad base) and can vary widely in size. The majority are asymptomatic. The number of osteochondromas may vary significantly within and between families. In addition, a variety of orthopaedic deformities can be found, like deformities of the forearm (shortening of the ulna with resultant bowing of the radius; 39–60%) {8027127}, inequality in limb length (10–50%), angulation of the knee (8–33%), deformity of the ankle (2–54%) {8027127;225330}, and disproportionate short stature (37–44%) {7702095;9272707}. Other complications include bursa formation, arthritis, and impingement on adjacent tendons, nerves, vessels, or spinal cord {7702095}. As documented, 84% of patients with multiple osteochondromas report frequent pain {15832158}, all of whom experience a profound impact on quality of life {22637207}. Patients with multiple osteochondromas may also show abnormal scar formation {17414021} and malformed and/or displaced teeth with abnormal enamel {18654627;21499719}. Symptomatic spinal involvement is rare {29016429}. Approximately 70–75% of patients with the condition have a positive family history {19810120}.

The most important complication of multiple osteochondromas, estimated to occur in 0.5–5% of patients, is progression towards secondary peripheral atypical cartilaginous tumour (appendicular skeleton) / chondrosarcoma grade 1 (axial skeleton, scapula, skull base, and pelvis) (ACT/CS1), or – rarely – to high-grade chondrosarcoma {7702095;9272707;8027127}. About 75.2% occur between the ages of 20 and 40 years and 56.2% at the pelvis and proximal femur {30591865}. Growth after puberty, increase in pain, or a thickness > 20 mm of the cartilaginous cap in adults should raise suspicion of secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma. Occasionally, patients with multiple osteochondromas also develop central ACT/CS1 (~3.6%) {25568391}.

In Langer–Giedion syndrome, craniofacial dysmorphism and a range of developmental delays are seen in addition to multiple osteochondromas {6496574}. Patients with Potocki–Shaffer syndrome present with enlarged parietal foramina, multiple osteochondromas, and sometimes craniofacial dysostosis and a range of developmental delays {8644736}.

Imaging

The diagnosis of multiple osteochondromas is confirmed by conventional radiographs. The size of the cartilaginous cap can be well established with T2-weighted MRI and CT to evaluate possible malignant transformation {10671608;20392983}. A cartilage cap > 20 mm should be regarded with caution. The role of 18F-FDG PET needs to be further established {16954943}. Annual MRI screening of the affected bones, especially between 20 and 40 years of age, seems beneficial to discover potential malignant transformation at an early stage {30591865;20233460}.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of multiple osteochondromas is about 1 case per 50 000 people within the general population {8027127}, although this may be an underestimation. There may be a slight male predilection {7702095;9272707}.

Etiology

Multiple osteochondromas is genetically heterogeneous and two genes, EXT1 at 8q24.11 {7550340} and EXT2 at 11p11.2 {8782816;8894688}, have been isolated. EXT1 is composed of 11 exons and spans approximately 350 kb of genomic DNA {9119404}. The transcript (NM_000127.3) has a coding region of 2241 bp {7550340}. The EXT2 gene spans 108 kb and contains 16 exons, for which the MANE Select transcript NM_207122.2 encodes a 718 amino acid protein with substantial similarity to the EXT1 gene product, especially in the C-terminal region {8782816;8894688}. The EXT genes have orthologues in various species.

With the currently used methods, germline inactivating EXT sequence variants or gross deletions are detected in > 90% of patients with multiple osteochondromas. EXT1 sequence variants are detected in about 65% of cases and EXT2 sequence variants in about 35% {19810120}. Inactivating sequence variants (nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants) represent the majority (75–80%) of variants causing multiple osteochondromas {19810120}. No hotspot sequence variants have been identified; sequence variants in EXT1 are spread over the different exons, whereas sequence variants in EXT2 more frequently affect the N-terminus. In the remaining cases of multiple osteochondromas, other alterations, such as deep-intronic or promoter sequence variants, inversions, translocations, or somatic mosaicism, may be the underlying cause {19810120;21280143;23341036;14556251}.

Osteochondromas develop upon biallelic inactivation of an EXT gene {10441575;20813973}; thus, a second somatic hit is required to inactivate the other allele in a chondrocyte within the growth plate {20080592} or in a mesenchymal progenitor cell from the perichondrium {23458899}.

Both EXT genes are also involved in a contiguous gene deletion syndrome. In Langer–Giedion syndrome, the 8q24 deletion includes the TRPS1 gene in addition to EXT1 {7550340;10615131}. In Potocki–Shaffer syndrome, patients carry an 11p12-p11.2 deletion that includes ALX4 and PHF21A, in addition to EXT2 {33126574}.

Pathogenesis

EXT1 and EXT2 mRNA is ubiquitously expressed {7550340;8782816;8894688}. Loss of heterozygosity, aneuploidy, or other changes can occur in the cartilaginous cap of osteochondroma, inactivating the second allele in at least a subset of the cells at the somatic level {20813973;10502322;23821404}. Somatic compound heterozygous sequence variants in EXT1 or EXT2 have been reported, but other mechanisms have been postulated {20418910}. The gene products, EXT1 and EXT2, are endoplasmic reticulum–localized type II transmembrane glycoproteins that form a Golgi-localized hetero-oligomeric complex that catalyses heparan sulfate polymerization {9756849;10639137;10545594}. Heparan sulfate signalling is involved in multiple signalling pathways, and EXT variants therefore affect hedgehog {15177029}, BMP {29277722}, FGFR3 {29545125}, and WNT/β-catenin pathway {29545125} signalling. BMP signalling in particular has been shown to be crucial for osteochondroma development in mice, and inhibitors of BMP signalling (e.g. palovarotene) are currently in clinical trials for the prevention of osteochondroma formation in children with multiple osteochondromas {28768899;29120519;28445472}.

Macroscopic appearance

Gross specimens of the individual tumours are similar to sporadic osteochondromas.

Histopathology

The tumours are histopathologically identical to their sporadic counterparts. Malignant transformation leads to a secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 in the vast majority of cases. High-grade chondrosarcoma is rare (< 10%) {12782876}. Osteosarcoma, spindle cell sarcoma, and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma are extremely rare {10458834;11896078;19734846}. On histology alone, it is hard to distinguish osteochondroma from secondary peripheral ACT/CS1 in the setting of multiple osteochondromas. Certain clinical and radiographic features, including cartilaginous cap thickness, need to be evaluated to diagnose malignant transformation {22555180}. Osteochondromas must be distinguished from metachondromatosis and from dysplasia epiphysialis hemimelica (Trevor disease), which are not related to EXT1 and EXT2 variants. Clinical and radiological features and family history allow distinction.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

The diagnosis of multiple osteochondromas is made on the basis of clinical and radiological data, so molecular analysis for germline variants in EXT1 or EXT2 is not mandatory for the diagnosis. Moreover, to date, a germline heterozygous EXT1 or EXT2 variant has been detected in 70–95% of patients {19810120}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: radiologically at least two osteochondromas of the juxtaepiphysial region of long bones; positive family history and/or a proven germline variant in one of the EXT genes.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Intrafamilial variability in clinical expression of the disease has been described, and at the moment no strong prognostic factor predicting evolution of the disease (i.e. number of osteochondromas in adult age, deformities) is known. Variants in EXT1 seem associated with a more-severe phenotype and shorter stature than EXT2 variants {15446535;16879194;17589361;17676624}, whereas patients without a disease-causing EXT variant usually have a mild phenotype. Additional (genetic) factors contribute to the final clinical presentation {22037484}. Male patients seem to be characterized by a more-severe clinical presentation than female patients. Because there is no evidence for the presence of any risk or protective factor related to malignant transformation, an adequate follow-up is required for all patients.
