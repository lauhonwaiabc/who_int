4.1.3 Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)

Definition

Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in one of the four mismatch repair genes, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. It causes a broad range of tumours in childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood, including haematological malignancies; brain tumours; colorectal, endometrial, and other Lynch syndrome–associated cancers; and multiple colorectal polyps often including high-grade dysplastic adenomas.

Localization

Cancers can arise in the haematopoietic system, CNS (mainly brain), colon, rectum, stomach, small bowel, renal pelvis and/or ureter, bladder, endometrium, ovary, kidney (Wilms tumour), adrenal gland (neuroblastoma), breast (phyllodes tumour), muscle / soft tissue (different types of sarcoma), liver (hepatic adenoma), and skin (basal cell carcinoma, melanoma) {24737826;33945292;26318770;29785566}. Multiple, mainly adenomatous, polyps arise in the large bowel and also in the small intestine and stomach {24737826;25307252;26729549;21376568;21182953}. CMMRD is also associated with several non-neoplastic features, the most frequent of which is café-au-lait macules {18709565}.

Clinical features

CMMRD predisposes individuals to a broad spectrum of malignancies with an age of onset usually from childhood until young adult age (0.5–38 years; mean age < 10 years) {24737826;33945292}. These include predominantly malignant brain tumours, with high-grade gliomas being the most prevalent; haematological malignancies, among which T-cell lymphoblastic lymphomas are the most common; colorectal and other Lynch syndrome–associated cancers; and also embryonal malignancies, sarcomas, and other cancers. Individuals with CMMRD develop multiple colorectal adenomas, often with high-grade dysplasia {24737826;25307252;26729549;21376568;33945292} (see section Lynch syndrome). Most patients with CMMRD also have neurofibromatosis type 1–like features (mainly café-au-lait macules), and/or other hyperpigmented and hypopigmented skin areas {9927033;9927034;18709565;27779754}, and/or multiple developmental venous anomalies (also known as cerebral venous angiomas) in separate regions of the brain {30166433;32642664}. Other less-frequent non-malignant features include non–therapy induced cavernoma, agenesis of the corpus callosum, one or more pilomatrixomas, and paediatric systemic lupus erythematosus {24737826;23629955;22692065;31501241;30013564;21182953;22837778}.

A 3-point scoring system has been proposed for the suspected diagnosis of CMMRD in paediatric or young adult patients with cancer based on the type of tumour and additional features, including non-malignant features, consanguinity of parents, and family history {24737826}. Mainly brain tumours, but also other CMMRD-associated tumours, frequently have somatic POLE and POLD1 exonuclease domain proofreading mutations and are ultramutated tumours (tumour mutation burden > 100 mutations per Mb) {25642631;27001570;29056344;33355208;39420201}. Hence, a tumour mutation burden that is unusually high for a paediatric tumour should raise the suspicion of CMMRD, in particular if the mutation signature indicates MMR deficiency (Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer [COSMIC] signatures SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26, or SBS44). Furthermore, a paediatric tumour in a family with Lynch syndrome should raise the suspicion of CMMRD.

Epidemiology

Based on the prevalence of heterozygous MMR gene pathogenic variants {27799157}, the estimated birth incidence of CMMRD in children of unrelated parents is 1 in 1 million and may be as many as 110 times higher if parents are consanguineous {30415209}. CMMRD is reported to account for 1.0–2.7% of adolescents and young adults (age ≤ 25 years) with colorectal cancer {32585361;26195711}. The prevalence of CMMRD in paediatric high-grade gliomas may be as much as 23% and even higher (39%) in countries where parental consanguinity is high {26293621}. As many as 10% of paediatric patients with T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma may have CMMRD {34964038}.

Etiology

CMMRD is an autosomal recessive syndrome caused by biallelic germline inactivation of one of the four main mismatch repair genes; this can be the result either of two different pathogenic variants present in trans (compound heterozygous) or of the same pathogenic variant present on both alleles (homozygous). In approximately 60% of cases, CMMRD is caused by germline biallelic PMS2 pathogenic variants, in approximately 20–30% by MSH6 biallelic pathogenic variants, and in another approximately 10–20% by either MLH1or MSH2 biallelic pathogenic variants {24737826;26318770;33945292}. CMMRD pathogenic variants are listed in the International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours (InSiGHT) database (https://www.insight-database.org) {32613597}. It appears that hypomorphic MMR variants may be considered pathogenic for CMMRD, but the risk for heterozygous carriers is not high enough for the variant to be considered pathogenic for Lynch syndrome. Recognized subtypes depend on the gene affected, as follows:

  • mismatch repair cancer syndrome 1 is caused by variants in MLH1 at chromosome 3p22.2;
  • mismatch repair cancer syndrome 2 is caused by variants in MSH2 at chromosome 2p21-p16.3;
  • mismatch repair cancer syndrome 3 is caused by variants in MSH6 at chromosome 2p16.3;
  • mismatch repair cancer syndrome 4 is caused by variants in PMS2 at chromosome 7p22.1.

CMMRD is closely related to Lynch syndrome (see section Lynch syndrome) and attenuated forms of CMMRD may clinically overlap with Lynch syndrome. A CMMRD-like phenotype may result also from POLE proofreading deficiency due to a constitutive POLE pathogenic variant {34816535}; digenic inheritance of a heterozygous germline pathogenic variant of PMS2 in combination with a germline heterozygous pathogenic variant either of POLE or of POLD1 may also result in a CMMRD-like phenotype {34967012;35260767;36291559}.

Pathogenesis

Germline inactivation of both alleles due to biallelic pathogenic variants of a given MMR gene results in loss of MMR function in all somatic cells, which are consequently deficient for mismatch repair (dMMR), and all somatic cells usually lose expression of the respective MMR protein. Hence, nuclear expression loss of the affected MMR protein in CMMRD-associated tumours is typically not restricted to neoplastic cells, but is also detectable by immunohistochemistry in non-neoplastic surrounding tissue and in tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes. This should be taken into account when testing for dMMR in paediatric or young adult patients with cancer and should not be interpreted as a failure of proper staining. Defective MMR leads to unrepaired base mismatches producing an increase in the point mutation rate (hypermutant); this occurs especially within stretches of DNA containing short (one to five nucleotides) tandem-repeat sequence motifs termed microsatellites, for which functioning MMR is required to maintain stability of their lengths {25707793}; thus, dMMR can manifest in CMMRD-associated tumours with microsatellite instability (MSI) due to variation in the lengths of these microsatellites. However, probably owing to a high frequency of somatic POLE or POLD1 exonuclease domain proofreading mutations in many CMMRD-associated tumours, MSI testing using the techniques developed for Lynch syndrome may fail to reveal MSI in CMMRD-associated tumours {19156169;21204794;12549480;17440981;33355208}. Nevertheless, assessment of germline MSI in blood leukocytes (i.e. non-neoplastic tissue) is possible in CMMRD and is a diagnostic hallmark of this cancer predisposition syndrome {23483711;26116798;30740824;31494577;33355208;36586540}. The dMMR cells have abnormal regulation of apoptosis and therefore a relative growth advantage that contributes to tumour formation {10097137;20102395}.

Macroscopic appearance

Macroscopic appearances of bowel adenomas and cancers, as well the other Lynch syndrome–associated cancers, haematological and brain malignancies, and benign neoplasms, are indistinguishable from those of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

The colorectal polyps are mostly adenomas, often with high-grade dysplasia. The colorectal cancers in CMMRD are similar to those in Lynch syndrome with tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, Crohn-like peritumoural lymphocytic aggregates, and sometimes with poor differentiation or a mucinous pattern with a pushing edge {11159189;29906313;18283560;23435936}. The histological findings of other CMMRD-associated carcinomas are generally similar to those in Lynch syndrome. However, the glioblastomas arising in the setting of CMMRD often have severe nuclear pleomorphism and/or bizarre multinucleated giant cells {32642664}; these features of a paediatric high-grade glioma or glioblastoma should raise suspicion of possible CMMRD.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline loss of MMR usually results in loss of expression of the respective MMR protein as detectable by immunohistochemistry and is typically observed in both neoplastic and all morphologically normal cells. MSI tested by the techniques developed for Lynch syndrome may detect MSI in CMMRD-associated tumours, but may fail to reveal MSI in some CMMRD-associated tumours, particularly brain neoplasms {19156169;21204794;12549480;17440981;33355208}. CMMRD-associated tumours are usually either hypermutated or ultramutated with a very high tumour mutation burden, and display mutation signatures indicative of MMR deficiency or combined POLE and POLD1 proofreading inactivation and MMR deficiency (COSMIC signatures SBS6, SBS14, SBS15, SBS20, SBS21, SBS26, or SBS44) {25642631;27001570;29056344;33355208}. These mutation signatures can also be found in tumours of patients with Lynch syndrome, or in severe POLE proofreading deficiency {34816535} and digenic inheritance of both heterozygous PMS2 and either POLE or POLD1 proofreading pathogenic variants {34967012;35260767;36291559}, and hence should be interpreted with caution.

Owing to the limitations inherent to genetic testing, ancillary tests assessing germline MMR deficiency can be used to confirm CMMRD in cases where genetic testing renders an inconclusive result because it either detects one or more variants of uncertain significance or fails to detect biallelic pathogenic variants. Several of these ancillary tests have been developed to assess germline dMMR in DNA from (non-neoplastic) blood leukocytes {23483711;26116798;30740824;31494577;33355208}. Some of them have limitations {23483711}, but most of them are highly reliable and confirm or exclude CMMRD with (nearly) 100% sensitivity and specificity {26116798;30740824;31494577;33355208}. However, currently there is limited availability and approval of these tests outside specialized laboratories. Immunohistochemical staining of the MMR proteins has been proposed as an ancillary diagnostic test {33622763}, but considerable care should be taken to avoid any false positive results. To confirm CMMRD by immunohistochemistry, loss of MMR expression in all cells of the investigated tissue (neoplastic and non-neoplastic cells) should be observed. Consequently, there is no internal positive staining control. Hence, it is important that an appropriate external positive staining control (from a different patient) is simultaneously processed. It is noteworthy that PMS2 may be expressed at very low levels in some tissues, which may lead to a false negative staining interpretation in all cells of a tissue that is not related to CMMRD.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: identification of two causative germline pathogenic variants in trans (homozygous or compound heterozygous) in one of the four relevant MMR genes (MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, MLH1) confirms the diagnosis of CMMRD; in cases where genetic testing renders an inconclusive result, a validated ancillary test assessing germline MMR deficiency can be used to confirm CMMRD.

Desirable: immunohistochemical staining of all four MMR proteins in tumour tissue in any suspected CMMRD-related tumour to determine the expression in neoplastic and in non-neoplastic cells, including tumour-infiltrating leukocytes.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

CMMRD-associated tumours are frequently hypermutated or ultramutated, resulting in high neoantigen formation {27001570}. There is growing evidence for the clinical efficacy of immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors, for treatment of patients with CMMRD-associated tumours {34992263;35235414;27001570}. Survivors of a CMMRD-associated tumour have a high risk of further malignancies and need intensive surveillance mainly for intestinal tract cancers and brain tumours {24556086;28572265}, which improves clinical outcome for the patients {33945292;36411031}.
