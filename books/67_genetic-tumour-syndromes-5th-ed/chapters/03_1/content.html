0.3 Introduction to the WHO Classification of Genetic Tumour Syndromes

The definitive classification of specific tumour types underpins the diagnosis and care of individual patients, as well as research into tumour causation, prevention, diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment. The understanding of tumour formation at a molecular level is now at a point where it needs to be integrated into diagnosis, including the increasing importance of tumour predisposition syndrome recognition to patients and their families. The WHO Classification of Tumours represents the taxonomy of cancer, providing the definitions of individual tumours and the international standards for their diagnosis. After a series of meetings and discussions, the WHO Classification of Tumours editorial board agreed that a formalized hierarchical classification system for inherited tumour susceptibility (predisposition) syndromes would advance the field and would build on existing list-based classifications of inherited disease. The classification has evolved over the last year in many ways, but it has become clear that tumour predisposition syndromes can be categorized into a relatively small number of major mechanisms, which now form the chapters in this volume. They are often identified by their gene defect, and testing for these alterations is increasingly important for their diagnosis.

Certain facts are emphasized by the classification. It is notable that, while alterations of growth factor receptors and their signalling pathways are well represented, there are no common syndromes from alterations of their ligands. There are also a number of relatively common syndromes associated with defects in DNA repair. In both cases, recognition of the molecular abnormalities involved can be used to predict response to treatment of either the syndromes or their associated tumours (e.g. BRCA1 syndrome – PARP inhibitors). One other observation is that there is a lot that remains unknown, not simply in terms of the existence of tumour predisposition syndromes, but also about how given genetic alterations lead to the development of tumours. The relatively equal spread of genes associated with such syndromes across the genome is also striking.

Organizational structure

Hierarchical systems ideally have multiple options under each level: that is, multiple families/genes under each category and multiple syndrome names under genes. In some cases, there can be fairly direct 1:1 correlations between syndromes and genes, although there are some syndromes that involve the same genes, but have different names or expressions of the disease depending on the specific DNA alteration present or the ability to affect multiple pathways. We have therefore organized this taxonomy of genetic tumour syndromes according to the major mechanism affected, the molecular pathway involved, the syndrome, and finally the individual genes in which pathogenic sequence variants lead to tumour development. The pathway approach has largely resolved one issue, in that there is an imperfect correlation between diagnosable clinical syndromes and genes. Some syndromes are caused by multiple genetic variants, or even by the alteration of genes physically close to one another in the genome.

We hope that this will be helpful to those involved in the diagnosis of genetic tumour syndromes, including pathologists faced with tumours that might indicate the presence of a genetic disorder, and genetics experts needing a concise classification of these syndromes.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Each volume in the fifth edition has a chapter on genetic tumour syndromes, and we have chosen to collect all of these together, suitably revised, in this volume, adding several syndromes new to the classification where their recognition is of scientific and clinical importance. However, it is the nature of classifications to evolve with new evidence, and as this taxonomy becomes established, it is likely that more will be added.

There was a particular issue for the many syndromes resulting in leukaemia or lymphoma, and a separate introduction has been produced for these to highlight the issues involved for haematolymphoid neoplasia genetic predisposition syndromes.

Terminology

One subject on which the editorial board had considerable discussion was the relative merits of calling these familial, hereditary, or genetic tumour syndromes. Because some syndromes are more commonly due to de novo or postzygotic mutation, and some only have benign tumours, we decided to use “genetic tumour predisposition syndromes” as the most appropriate generic term. “Predisposition” is used as this highlights the fact that some affected individuals may not develop tumours, but have the gene alteration, or other signs and symptoms of the disorder.

Another point of discussion was usage of the term “germline” for pathogenic sequence variants rather than “constitutional”. Some experts do prefer the word “constitutional”, but it seems that the former is largely preferred in PubMed and changing this would have caused confusion. The translation of “constitutional” into some languages can also cause problems, and the difficulties of conflating this with consanguinity, with appropriate sensitivity, was an important consideration here.

As this volume will be used by non-experts, a brief glossary of terms that come up frequently and can be misunderstood is included (see #39003Table 1.01). Some are used loosely in general parlance and this should prove helpful.

Nomenclature

The names of syndromes are largely arbitrary, and it is noted that eponymous names are slowly changing towards a gene-based system. We have chosen to take a pragmatic approach and base the nomenclature on common usage, listing alternative terminology as necessary. In general, this reflects the relative ease of clinical features versus genetic analysis for diagnosis. The diagnostic approach is for the syndrome, not the tumour, and some tumour types occur in multiple syndromes. Equally, variants at different points in the same gene can produce very different syndromes (e.g. in APC, producing both familial adenomatous polyposis and gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of the stomach [GAPPS]).

There are still some areas of potential confusion, with multiple names used for the same syndrome. One example is hereditary gastric and breast cancer syndrome (see section Hereditary gastric and breast cancer syndrome), an autosomal dominant cancer predisposition syndrome with inactivating germline CDH1 or CTNNA1 variants in families or individuals characterized by diffuse gastric cancer and/or lobular breast cancer, occasionally with cleft lip/palate. This has also been called hereditary diffuse gastric cancer syndrome, hereditary lobular breast cancer, diffuse gastric and lobular breast cancer syndrome, diffuse gastric cancer syndrome / lobular breast cancer syndrome, CDH1-related tumour susceptibility syndrome, and cadherin-related tumour susceptibility syndrome. We have listed alternative terminology as acceptable, or not recommended, in line with best practice.

Other syndromes have similar names for different syndromes, for example Carney complex and Carney triad. These are two different syndromes. Knowledge of the molecular associations of the succinate dehydrogenase–deficient tumour syndromes has recently expanded (see section SDH-deficient tumour syndromes). While previously defined clinically, the term “Carney triad” is now defined by dual clinical and molecular criteria as a syndromic succinate dehydrogenase–deficient tumour syndrome where no germline succinate dehydrogenase mutation has been identified with current methodologies. Older terminology is now discouraged in favour of terminology based on identification of the mutated gene {30877234}. Carney complex (see section Carney complex (PRKAR1A, PDE8B, PDE11A)) is an autosomal dominant syndrome characterized by myxomas, endocrinopathy, and pigmented skin lesions; 70% of patients have inactivation of PRKAR1A, which encodes the type 1α regulatory (R1α) subunit of PKA.

Term

Definition

Allele

Different versions of the same gene or variant

Constitutional

A sequence variant present in all the body’s cells, including the germ cells, that can therefore be passed on to offspring

Gene

The basic unit of inheritance, containing the information encoding specific peptides/proteins or RNAs

Germline

In this context, used similarly to “constitutional”, although usually regarded as defining the germ cells – the sperm and oocytes that pass on genetic material to offspring

Mosaic(ism)

A genetic change present in some but not all cells in the body, caused by postzygotic mutation and resulting in multiple genotypes within the individual affected

Penetrance

The proportion of individuals carrying a particular variant (or allele) of a gene (the genotype) that also expresses an associated trait (the phenotype)

Pathogenic

A genomic variant is called pathogenic if there is a direct link between the change and a particular condition or syndrome

Postzygotic

A change in the genome that is acquired during the lifespan of an individual, instead of being inherited (see “Mosaic(ism)”)

Sequence

The sequence of nucleotide bases in DNA

Somatic

The cells in the body other than sperm and oocytes (germ cells) and their gonadal progenitors

Syndrome

A set of medical signs and symptoms (and pathological abnormalities) which are correlated with each other and often associated with a particular disease or disorder

Taxonomy

An ordered arrangement of groups or categories, used to indicate relationships

Variant

A genetic variant is a permanent change in the DNA sequence
