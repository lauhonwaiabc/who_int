1.6 Hedgehog signalling pathway

The hedgehog pathway, discovered in Drosophila melanogaster, comprises three hedgehog molecules, two membrane receptors (PTCH1 and PTCH2), an activation molecule (SMO), and three downstream transcription factors (GLI1, GLI2, and GLI3). These have roles in organ development, but in adults also regulate functions in the brain, skin, and gonads {36932157;36766700}. Naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome) includes basal cell carcinomas, ovarian fibromas, and medulloblastoma, and results from inactivating germline sequence variants in the human homologue of the Drosophila segment polarity patched gene (PTCH1) {8681379;8658145}, SUFU, the product of which binds GLI molecules {25403219}, and exceptionally GPR161 (an orphan G protein–coupled receptor involved in the hedgehog pathway, whose ligand is unknown) {31609649}.

SMO (Curry–Jones) syndrome is caused by a postzygotic gain-of-function mutation in the gene SMO. SMO is a membrane-spanning G protein–coupled receptor normally maintained in an inactive state by binding to the hedgehog receptors PTCH1 and PTCH2. Hedgehog binding to its receptor disrupts the latter’s interaction with SMO, releasing the inhibition. The NM_005631.5:c.1234C>T p.(Leu412Phe) substitution in SMO locates to a pivot region in transmembrane helix 5 that appears crucial for transducing the PTCH-mediated inhibitory signal, leading to constitutive SMO activation in the presence of PTCH and cell proliferation {24859340;25759020}.

ELP1 is a scaffold protein, mutation of which leads to medulloblastoma. Loss of PTCH1 in these tumours has been noted and is thought to be part of their pathogenesis {32296180}.

Mutation of the EXT1 and EXT2 genes, which encode glycosyltransferases, an enzyme family involved in heparan sulfate synthesis, leads to multiple osteochondromas {31211456;31853203}.
