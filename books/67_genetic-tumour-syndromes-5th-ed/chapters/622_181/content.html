6.2.2 SMARCB1- and LZTR1- related schwannomatosis (SMARCB1, LZTR1)

Definition

SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis (previously known as schwannomatosis) is a disorder caused by pathogenic variants in SMARCB1 or LZTR1 (both at chromosome 22q11) and is characterized by multiple schwannomas (spinal, cutaneous, and cranial) and, less commonly, meningiomas (cranial and spinal). Since the underlying germline pathogenic variant is not NF2, these schwannomatoses are also referred to as non–NF2-related schwannomatosis.

Related terminology

Acceptable: schwannomatosis; non–NF2-related schwannomatosis.

Subtype(s)

SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis 1; LZTR1-related schwannomatosis 2; other 22q-related schwannomatosis; schwannomatosis not elsewhere classified (NEC) (individuals in whom no pathogenic variant was identified by molecular analysis of blood and tumours)

Localization

Tumours associated with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis affect the central and peripheral nervous systems, with predilections for paraspinal and peripheral nerves, as well as (less commonly) the meninges.

Clinical features

Clinical diagnostic criteria

The original clinical diagnostic criteria for schwannomatosis were established in 2005 {15955931} and were recently revised {35674741}. The updated criteria incorporated hybrid neurofibroma/schwannoma into the criteria and revised the nomenclature, proposing subtypes of schwannomatosis based on germline alterations: SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis, LZTR1-related schwannomatosis, other 22q-related schwannomatosis (in cases with multiple schwannomas with the same molecular findings at chromosome 22q, including alterations of the DGCR8 gene). In a subset of schwannomatosis patients, despite adequate analysis no alteration can be found; these cases can be designated as schwannomatosis NEC. The inclusion of molecular analysis as an essential part of the diagnosis aims at improving the distinction between NF2-related schwannomatosis (in particular early or mosaic forms) and the above forms of schwannomatosis {21451418;1484939;9207339;23401320}.

Schwannomas

Patients with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis have multiple schwannomas in spinal nerve roots, and less commonly in cranial nerves, including unilaterally in the vestibular nerve (the latter especially in patients with LZTR1-related schwannomatosis). The tumours have a segmental distribution in about 30% of patients, presumed to be the result of genetic mosaicism {15955931;22927469;25480913;22105938}. Unlike in NF2-related schwannomatosis, severe chronic pain associated with the tumours is characteristic of the disease, while neurological deficits and polyneuropathy are uncommon {22927469}.

Meningiomas

Some patients (5%) with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis develop a meningioma {25857641}. Patients with SMARCB1 germline variants may have multiple meningiomas, with the falx cerebri being a preferential location for cranial meningiomas {19582488;20930055;22038540}.

Other tumours

Other tumours associated with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis are rare. The occurrence of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours has been reported {18285426;22082606;31240424}. Also, tumours reported in SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis include leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma, adrenal cell carcinoma with a molecular profile similar to that of the schwannomas, and mosaic immunostaining for the SMARCB1 protein {24525513;31240424;26001331;26799435}.

Epidemiology

SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis has an estimated annual incidence of 1.25–2.5 cases per 100 000 population {10636128;9647170}.

Etiology

SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis is often caused by mutation of SMARCB1 or LZTR1, both located at chromosome 22q11. The great majority of SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis cases are sporadic, with only about 15% of patients having a positive family history {8780094;9647170}. In the familial form, the disease displays an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance, with incomplete penetrance {12821741}. In 2007, the SMARCB1 gene at chromosome 22q11 was identified as a familial schwannomatosis-predisposing gene {17357086}. In 2014, the LZTR1 gene was identified as a second causative gene in schwannomatosis {24362817}. In 2020, the DGCR8 gene (which maps very close to LZTR1 at chromosome 22q11) was identified as the predisposing gene in a family {31805011}; this form is currently included in the 22q-related schwannomatosis category. Coffin–Siris syndrome and Noonan syndrome are genetic syndromes that can be caused by SMARCB1 and LZTR1 alterations, respectively, and while generally not associated with schwannomatosis, exceptions have been reported {26364901;35840934}.

Pathogenesis

According to the tumour suppressor gene model, both copies of the SMARCB1 or LZTR1 gene are inactivated in the tumours of patients with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis. In addition, there is inactivation of both copies (by mutation and deletion) of the NF2 gene, located distal to SMARCB1 and LZTR1 in the chromosome 22q12.2 region {18647326;18285426;18072270;20729918;25008767;25335493;24362817;25480913}. A four-hit, three-step model of tumorigenesis has been proposed for SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis: first, an (inherited) SMARCB1 (or LZTR1) germline variant occurs (hit 1); next, loss of the other chromosome 22 follows, with the wildtype copy of SMARCB1 (and LZTR1) and one copy of NF2 (hits two and three); finally, a somatic variant of the remaining copy of the NF2 gene occurs (hit four) {23401320;18072270}.

The SMARCB1 gene

SMARCB1 is located in the chromosome 22q11.23 region and contains nine exons spanning 50 kb of genomic DNA {9671307}. The SMARCB1 protein is a core subunit of mammalian SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling complexes. These act as master regulators of transcription, using ATP for sliding the nucleosomes along the DNA helix {21654818}. Unlike rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 1 (see section Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome), in SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis, most germline variants in SMARCB1 are missense and splice site, which are predicted to result in the synthesis of an altered SMARCB1 protein with modified activity {24933152}. The mosaic staining pattern seen in many schwannomatosis-associated schwannomas suggests the absence of SMARCB1 protein in part of the tumour cells {18422762}. In the rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome, SMARCB1 germline variants are nonsense or frameshift and involve exons 2 and 9. In contrast to schwannomatosis, in malignant rhabdoid tumours of the kidney and atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumours of the CNS, there is total loss of SMARCB1 expression in tumour cells. Although there are distinct genotype–phenotype correlations in these two syndromes, overlaps have been reported. In a few families the affected individuals inherited a SMARCB1 variant and developed schwannomatosis or a rhabdoid tumour {22082606;21108436;19124645}. However, the schwannomas in these families (as well as the rhabdoid tumours) displayed total loss of SMARCB1 protein expression {22082606;19124645}.

The LZTR1 gene

The LZTR1 gene is situated proximal to SMARCB1 in the chromosome 22q11.21 region and contains 21 exons, spanning 17 kb of genomic DNA {24362817}. It has been suggested that LZTR1 may function as a substrate adaptor in cullin-3 ubiquitin ligase complexes, binding to cullin-3 and targeting substrates for ubiquitination {23917401}, and it may function as a negative modulator of RAS activity {30442762;30481304}. The reported LZTR1 germline variants in schwannomatosis include missense and splice-site as well as frameshift and nonsense variants, and they are found along the entire coding sequence of the gene, affecting the functionally important domains of the LZTR1 protein {25008767;25335493;24362817;25480913}. Immunostaining of LZTR1-associated schwannomas with an LZTR1-specific antibody demonstrates absent or reduced expression of the protein, consistent with its function as a tumour suppressor {25335493}. Germline LZTR1 variants were found in patients with schwannomatosis who had a unilateral vestibular schwannoma {25480913;27856782}. Because these patients phenotypically resemble patients with mosaic neurofibromatosis type 2, this causes the misdiagnosis with neurofibromatosis type 2 in a small number of cases (1–2%) {29909380}.

Macroscopic appearance

Schwannomas in schwannomatosis are identical to their non-schwannomatosis counterparts.

Histopathology

The histopathology of schwannomatosis-related tumours largely overlaps with that of their non-schwannomatosis counterparts. Most (70%) of the schwannomas associated with schwannomatosis are hybrid schwannoma/neurofibroma tumours that are sometimes misdiagnosed as neurofibromas or malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours {22927469}.

Immunohistochemically, almost all schwannomas of patients with familial schwannomatosis show a mosaic pattern of staining for SMARCB1 {18422762}. This mosaic staining is also frequently seen in the schwannomas of patients with sporadic schwannomatosis (55–100% of cases) and in schwannomatosis related to neurofibromatosis type 2, but is rare in solitary, sporadic schwannomas {17357086;18422762;28365909}. What exactly causes this absence of SMARCB1 staining in a subset of the tumour cells is unknown at present; the synthesis of a mutant SMARCB1 protein is not a prerequisite because mosaic expression of SMARCB1 is also noted in tumours from patients lacking a germline variant in SMARCB1 (patients with an LZTR1 variant or patients with no variants found in either SMARCB1 or LZTR1) {28365909}.

Cytology

The cytological features of tumour cells from schwannomatosis-associated tumours are indistinguishable from those of sporadic (non-schwannomatosis) tumours.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In patients with SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis, SMARCB1 appears to be involved as a causative gene in about 50% of familial cases but only in < 10% of sporadic cases {18647326;18285426;21255467;24933152}. In patients without SMARCB1 germline variants, LZTR1 pathogenic variants have been found in about 40% of familial cases and 25% of sporadic cases {25008767;25335493;25480913}. The fact that most schwannomatosis cases cannot be explained by the involvement of SMARCB1 or LZTR1 suggests the existence of additional causative genes {25008767}. Indeed, the DGCR8 gene (22q11.21) was recently identified as the predisposing gene in a family with multinodular goitre and schwannomatosis {31805011}. Patients with the same chromosome 22q alterations in multiple tumours (without SMARCB1 or LZTR1 variants) are grouped into the diagnostic category of other 22q-related schwannomatosis. Patients with schwannomatosis typically have somatically acquired NF2 variants in their schwannomas, but no germline NF2 variants {9399891;12975302;12821741}.

Staging

Not relevant

Prognosis and prediction

Life expectancy in SMARCB1- and LZTR1-related schwannomatosis is near normal (76.9 years); it is higher than the mean life expectancy of patients with NF2-related schwannomatosis (66.2 years) {29909380}.

Diagnostic criteria for schwannomatosis with SMARCB1 or LZTR1 variants:

 

At least one pathologically confirmed schwannoma or hybrid nerve sheath tumour AND SMARCB1 or LZTR1 pathogenic variant in unaffected tissue

 

OR

 

A shared SMARCB1 or LZTR1 variant in two schwannomas or hybrid nerve sheath tumours

Diagnostic criteria for 22q-related schwannomatosis:

 

An individual who does NOT meet criteria for neurofibromatosis type 2 (schwannomatosis related to neurofibromatosis type 2), SMARCB1-related schwannomatosis or LZTR1-related schwannomatosis, does NOT have DGCR8 pathogenic variant

 

AND has both of the following molecular features:

 

 

Loss of heterozygosity of the same chromosome 22q markers in two schwannomas or hybrid nerve sheath tumours

 

 

AND

 

 

A different NF2 pathogenic variant in each tumour, which cannot be detected in unaffected tissues
