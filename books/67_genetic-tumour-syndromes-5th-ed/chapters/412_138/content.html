Definition

Muir–Torre Syndrome (MTS) is a rare autosomal dominant tumour predisposition disorder defined by the association of cutaneous lesions (predominantly sebaceous neoplasms, occasionally keratoacanthomas) and visceral malignancies associated with Lynch syndrome (LS), in the absence of other predisposing factors. MTS is most often associated with LS, caused by pathogenic variants affecting the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2. A similar phenotype to MTS can be seen in patients with inherited MUTYH pathogenic variants.

Localization

Patients with MTS usually present with skin tumours, but may also have visceral tumours of the colorectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, ovary, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, urinary tract, kidney, brain, and prostate.

Clinical features

Patients with MTS present with cutaneous manifestations which can appear before (in 22%), concurrent with (6%), or after (56%) the diagnosis of visceral malignancy {10534628}. The cutaneous manifestations include well-differentiated sebaceous neoplasms, mainly sebaceous adenoma but also sebaceoma, and, less frequently, sebaceous carcinoma (but not sebaceous hyperplasia) and keratoacanthoma {3728878;26892655;18551751;26826402}. Sebaceous neoplasms outside the head-and-neck region, multiple sebaceous neoplasms, and family history are strong indicators {18551751;33201015;7601953}. Cystic keratoacanthomas, sometimes multiple and possibly showing sebaceous differentiation, in younger patients at sites without chronic sun exposure can also be a feature, but these have been less frequently reported.

Visceral malignancies associated with LS vary according to which MMR gene is affected and include tumours of the colorectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, ovary, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, urinary tract, kidney, brain, and prostate {26657901;27261338;18398828}. Neoplasms can develop at any age, but often arise in young people. Some develop multiple tumours.

Epidemiology

The population incidence of MTS has not yet been determined, but the syndrome has been identified in approximately 5–9% of individuals from families with LS {26826402;16826164;18270343}.

Etiology

The primary cause of MTS is a germline pathogenic variant affecting a DNA MMR gene, i.e. MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2; MMR gene–specific risks are set out in the Prospective Lynch Syndromes Database (PLSD), available at https://plsd.eu {26657901;27261338;28754778;29046738}. Some individuals have mutations involving adjacent genes, such as EPCAM (TACSTD1), which affects the neighbouring MSH2 gene, or LRRFIP2, which affects MLH1 {23408351;23264089;21309036;21712435}. Abnormalities of MSH2 are strongly associated with the MTS phenotype, with MSH2 pathogenic variants in 90% of cases. Most of the remaining cases harbour MLH1 abnormalities. An isolated deficiency of MSH6 or PMS2 has been documented {26826402}. This strongly contrasts with LS, where germline deficits of MLH1 and MSH2 have roughly equal incidence. The phenotype of sebaceous neoplasia and visceral malignancy can also be seen in patients with autosomal recessive MUTYH-associated adenomatous polyposis (familial adenomatous polyposis 2; MIM number: 608456).

Several environmental and lifestyle factors have been identified as modifiers of LS. Cigarette smoking, increased body mass index, and alcohol consumption are associated with increased risk, whereas acetylsalicylic acid, ibuprofen, multivitamin and calcium supplements, hormone replacement therapy (but not oral contraceptive pill use), and increasing parity in women are associated with a reduction in cancer risk {20145170;21559014;26282643;26109217}.

Pathogenesis

The four MMR genes encode proteins which form complexes that detect and repair errors in base pairing during DNA replication {33005611}. MLH1 dimerizes with PMS2, while MSH2 dimerizes with MSH6 {25595178}. Germline alteration in one of the MMR genes followed by a second somatic mutation event in the remaining wildtype allele {18551751} leads to cells becoming mismatch repair deficient (dMMR). This deficiency has several important consequences. Firstly, dMMR cells escape the normal control of apoptosis and gain a relative growth advantage {20102395}. Secondly, mismatch repair deficiency leads to an increase in the mutation rate, especially within repetitive stretches of DNA called microsatellites; this manifests as microsatellite instability (MSI). Thirdly, mismatch repair deficiency may also lead to abnormal mismatch repair protein expression, which is identifiable by immunohistochemistry.

Macroscopic appearance

Solid tumours related to MTS have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

The histopathological appearance is related to the tumour type and is not distinctive.

Immunohistochemistry for the mismatch repair proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6) is a common first step to screen for mismatch repair deficiency. The presence of all four proteins suggests microsatellite stability. Loss of nuclear staining for any of the proteins indicates dMMR and suggests the most likely involved gene and the need for additional testing. Immunohistochemically, approximately one half to two thirds of unselected sebaceous neoplasms are deficient in MMR proteins {25006859;26826402;18551751}. The rates of loss vary; in the head-and-neck region, adenomas show 30–40% loss, whereas loss in carcinomas in this region is rare. Outside the head-and-neck region, both adenomas and carcinomas commonly show loss (in > 80% of cases). The interpretation of MMR protein immunohistochemistry is typically straightforward, but it should always be performed with adequate internal control staining. Patchy intact staining can occur owing to uneven antibody diffusion, variable fixation, or tissue hypoxia {12055083;12697826}. Cytoplasmic staining has been described with EPCAM and MSH2 deletions {27896849;34055602}.

PCR amplification of discrete microsatellite loci in tumour-extracted DNA is a functional assessment of MSI and strongly correlates with immunohistochemistry in LS-associated tumours {23556052;18556767;12713593;11859205}. Nevertheless, the sensitivity and specificity of MMR immunohistochemistry in predicting the underlying gene defect is 85% and 48%, respectively, in sebaceous neoplasms, compared with the sensitivity and specificity values of 92–94% and 88–100%, respectively, in colorectal tumours {23212176;24603434}. The value of MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI testing is low in periocular sebaceous neoplasms, specifically in sebaceous carcinoma. It is considerably enhanced by testing more than one tumour from the same family and/or individual, especially if the tumours are rare.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Molecular genetic studies on the tumour and blood DNA can directly evaluate MMR gene status {25043945}. Since it is established that somatic MMR gene mutations are seen in sebaceous tumours, germline testing for confirmation is preferred {27287813}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

The possibility of MTS should be considered in all patients with cutaneous sebaceous neoplasia. Testing for germline mutation should be discussed when there is additionally a personal or family history of MTS associated visceral malignancy. Testing for germline mutation should also be discussed for patients with more predictive cutaneous findings, including sebaceous neoplasms outside the head-and-neck region, cystic sebaceous neoplasms, neoplasms with overlapping sebaceous/keratoacanthoma morphology, multiple sebaceous neoplasms, or loss of MMR staining by immunohistochemistry. Although routine MTS and LS screening is not recommended for periocular sebaceous carcinoma, the Mayo MTS risk score guidelines may be applicable to periocular sebaceous neoplasms {31797796;24603434;33005611}. Patients with score 2 or higher should be evaluated by MMR immunohistochemistry on colon or endometrial cancer or, when available, by molecular genetic testing {24603434}.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

The visceral malignancies of MTS and LS are generally found to have less-aggressive behaviour than their sporadic counterparts {7601953}.
