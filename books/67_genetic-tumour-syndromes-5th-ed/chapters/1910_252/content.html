1.9.10 Familial chordoma (TBXT)

Definition

Familial chordoma is a rare bone cancer thought to arise from remnants of the notochord and almost exclusively occurring in the axial skeleton and skull base. It has an autosomal dominant mode of inheritance caused largely by the germline duplication of the TBXT gene.

MIM numbering

215400 Chordoma, susceptibility to; CHDM

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

Conventional familial chordoma; chondroid familial chordoma; dedifferentiated familial chordoma

Localization

In keeping with the notochordal derivation, chordomas are located throughout the axial skeleton, but with familial chordoma showing a strong predominance in the spheno-occipital region compared with the mobile spine and sacrococcygeal bones {11227920}.

Clinical features

As a consequence of the proximity to the spinal cord or brain, the local bone destruction and compression and invasion of adjacent structures caused by the expansile growth of chordomas result in cauda equina or coccydynia. Other neurological symptoms include paraesthesia or visual disturbances if tumours are located in the skull base. On imaging, chordomas present as lobulated masses with a so-called collar-button appearance on sagittal scan and a dumbbell or mushroom appearance in the axial plane {10450844}. Most chordomas are heterogeneous and isointense with low to intermediate signal intensity in T1-weighted sequences {8888395}. On T2-weighted imaging, chordomas show a high signal intensity {1442442}.

Epidemiology

Chordomas occur in approximately 0.1 per 100 000 individuals in the United States {23504991;32559743} with the vast majority occurring sporadically. The incidence of familial chordoma is not known but only 10 families have been reported since 1958 {10077147;16645110;9475610;26722267;1179231}. Familial chordoma has a slight female predilection.

Etiology

A germline duplication of the TBXT gene, located at chromosome 6q27, which encodes the transcription factor brachyury, has been identified as a major susceptibility locus in many chordoma families. Brachyury expression is intimately linked to notochord development and has been robustly implicated in chordoma pathogenesis {19801981;21171078;22847733;30664779}. In both the familial and sporadic settings, the identification of a common missense SNP (dbSNP ID: rs2305089, NC_000006.12:g.166165782C>T) in the DNA-binding domain of TBXT has been strongly associated with disease risk in multiple White population cohorts and in an Iranian cohort, but not in Chinese individuals {23064415;24990759;34837714;34465320;24232574}. Other rare germline variants, with less significant statistical association than rs2305089, have also been identified {24990759}.

Pathogenesis

Functional genomics experiments demonstrate that chordoma cells are exquisitely sensitive to loss of brachyury expression, resulting in reduced proliferation, senescence, and cell death {21171078;22847733;30664779;33521702}. Brachyury expression is controlled through an autoregulatory feedback loop that involves it binding to a superenhancer region in proximity to the gene {33521702}.

Macroscopic appearance

Grossly, conventional chordomas have a lobular architecture with an infiltrative growth pattern resulting in the destruction of bone and often invading into the surrounding soft tissue. The tumour cells secrete copious amounts of extracellular matrix, which imparts a myxoid/jelly-like consistency to the tumour. Haemorrhage and necrosis are common, especially in the larger tumours. Chondroid chordoma is less gelatinous and is more firm, resembling cartilage.

Histopathology

Chordoma cells are epithelioid and show abundant vacuolated cytoplasm. The cells are arranged in cords and strands (from which chordoma gets its name) with hyperchromatic nuclei and an abundant surrounding myxoid matrix. The diagnostic hallmark is the nuclear positivity for brachyury protein using immunohistochemistry {16538613}.

Dedifferentiation in a chordoma implies the transformation of the more conventional component to a higher-grade tumour. This may take on the form of a fibrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, or an undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma and loss of brachyury expression.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Recurrent germline duplication of TBXT is detected {19801981}. Rare sequence variants in the PI3K signalling pathway and sequence variants in PBRM1 are also seen {33536423}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: two or more chordomas in one family.

Desirable: young age at diagnosis (generally < 20 years); location in the skull base, or the presence of multiple primary chordomas; identification of a germline duplication of TBXT is supportive of the diagnosis and is a prompt for screening of at-risk relatives.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

The specific risk for developing chordoma in the setting of familial chordoma has not been accurately quantified but is thought to be extremely high, necessitating long-term surveillance of first-degree family members {32559743}.
