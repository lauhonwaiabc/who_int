3.2.1 Retinoblastoma syndrome (RB1)

Definition

Retinoblastoma syndrome is an autosomal dominant cancer predisposition syndrome linked to a germline (constitutional) RB1 heterozygous pathogenic variant and the development of retinoblastoma as well as other extraocular primary malignancies.

MIM numbering

180200 Retinoblastoma; RB1

ICD-11 coding

2D02.2 Retinoblastoma

Related terminology

Acceptable: heritable retinoblastoma; familial retinoblastoma.

Subtype(s)

Heritable unilateral and bilateral retinoblastoma; trilateral retinoblastoma – bilateral with primary intracranial tumour (pineal or suprasellar tumour); non-ocular second primary malignancies (SPMs)

Localization

Retinoblastoma develops in the retina of one or both eyes. In retinoblastoma syndrome, bilateral tumours usually occur and present as multicentric retinal tumours. In about 15% of patients, the syndrome is associated with unilateral disease, thus supporting the need for molecular testing for RB1 and not relying solely on a clinical diagnosis {29056300}. Synchronous or metachronous involvement by malignant intracranial pineal or suprasellar tumours may develop in about 3.5% of these patients. Co-occurrence of intracranial malignant tumours and retinoblastoma is referred to as trilateral retinoblastoma {29056300}. In contrast to intraocular retinoblastoma, which has an excellent cure rate if treated before extraocular spread {24589388;25126964}, pineoblastoma is mostly fatal and surveillance is critical {24589388;25126964}. Patients may develop SPM starting in their second or third decade of life, with an overall risk of 0.5–1% per year of life {9727521}. The most frequent secondary malignancies are rhabdomyosarcoma, osteosarcoma, other soft tissue sarcomas, endometrial adenocarcinoma, lung carcinoma, and skin melanoma {25185089;31622129}.

Clinical features

A white pupillary reflex (leukocoria) is the most common presentation (63% of cases) for intraocular retinoblastoma and results from the retinal tumour {32105305}. Strabismus is the second most common presentation (10% of cases), followed by the combination of leukocoria and strabismus {34592118}. In advanced retinoblastoma, there may be iris heterochromia (neovascularization of the iris), glaucoma with buphthalmos, pseudohypopyon, hyphaema, and aseptic orbital cellulitis {26023180}. In tumours with extraocular manifestations, extension proptosis or a cauliflower-like mass arising from the front of the eye may be present. Proptosis presentation is more common in low- and middle-income countries compared with high-income countries. Children from low- and middle-income countries are older at presentation, with more advanced disease and a lower percentage of familial retinoblastoma, probably secondary to not reaching childbearing age; however, more studies are necessary to ascertain the cause of this difference {32105305}. Diagnosis is made clinically to confirm retinal tumours and exclude bilateral disease and is achieved by examining both eyes with dilated pupils under anaesthesia. Ultrasound is used to aid in the diagnosis to identify calcifications, a constant finding in these tumours.

Retinoblastoma is one of very few tumours that are not biopsied to confirm diagnosis before treatment is started. The contraindication to biopsy is to avoid extraocular spread of tumour. In the past 5 years, liquid biopsy approaches for molecular diagnosis have been described for the detection of circulating tumour DNA; however, there is currently no clinical implementation of these approaches {29049475;32633890;34298378}.

MRI of the orbits and brain is performed during the initial diagnosis of intraocular retinoblastoma to exclude extraocular extension and intracranial tumours. In patients < 12 months of age and especially those with the hereditary form, radiation (including radiography, CT, and external beam radiation therapy [EBRT]) should be avoided to curtail the lifetime risk of developing late-onset second cancers {35451226}.

Intraocular unilateral disease is usually treated by intra-arterial chemotherapy or enucleation with secondary consolidation by local treatments (laser, cryotherapy, or intravitreous chemotherapy) depending on the group and stage of the tumour in the eye. Systemic chemotherapy is usually employed in bilateral retinoblastoma with varying treatment regimens. Generally, six cycles of three chemotherapeutics with local consolidations throughout treatment are administered. Extraocular retinoblastoma including trilateral retinoblastoma requires intensive multimodal therapy, including intensive chemotherapy, and often requires autologous haematopoietic cell rescue, surgery, and radiotherapy {35820112;32187454}.

Epidemiology

Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignant tumour in children and is the most common primary malignant intraocular neoplasm {19704035}. There is no ethnicity or sex predilection. The incidence of retinoblastoma is reported as 1 in 16 000–18 000 live births {24100372;18621794;19704035} with a median age of 24 months at diagnosis for unilateral disease and 12 months for bilateral disease. The incidence of trilateral retinoblastoma is 3.5% among all hereditary retinoblastoma patients {26374932}.

The incidence, type, and location of SPMs in hereditary retinoblastoma are influenced by the type of retinoblastoma treatment the patient received. EBRT is especially associated with a 36% cumulative incidence of sarcoma in the irradiation field {15800318;30031154}. Chemotherapy plus EBRT further increases the risk of SPMs {25185089}.

Etiology

Retinoblastoma syndrome is caused by a germline RB1 pathogenic variant (first hit) followed by a somatic alteration (second hit), and originates from developing retinal cells, probably cone progenitors {25252974;30213853}. Inactivation of the RB1 gene can also be due to promoter methylation, insertion of LINE-1 elements, or chromothripsis on chromosome 13 {24509483;33670346;38213835}.

Pathogenesis

Although an inactivating RB1 variant is required for initiation, it may not be independently sufficient for malignant transformation, as retinocytomas also have biallelic RB1 variants {18211953}. Germline pathogenic RB1 variants are heterogeneous. Low- and regular-penetrance alterations show variation of phenotypic expression for familial retinoblastoma and SPMs {32434110}. Patients with somatic mutational mosaicism more often have a milder phenotype, delayed presentation, and unilateral retinoblastoma compared with patients with non-mosaic mutations {19280657}. Large deletions on the q arm of chromosome 13, including RB1 and neighbouring genes, cause a rare genetic disorder referred to as 13q deletion syndrome. Depending on the breakpoints and the size of the deletion, these patients may show neurodevelopmental delay, characteristic facial features, organ malformation, and retinoblastoma {21505449;24858910}. The origin of intracranial (pineal) retinoblastoma, somatic or germline associated, is probably different to that of other pineal tumours based on divergent DNA methylation profiles, and the common characteristics differ between intracranial and ocular retinoblastoma (e.g. chromosome 16 loss and 1q gain) {31820118;31768671}.

Macroscopic appearance

Intraocular tumours arise from the retina, are tan-white, friable with areas of necrosis and calcification, and most often invade the subretinal space (exophytic growth), the vitreous (endophytic growth), or a mixture of both. There may be vitreous tumour seeds and invasion into the optic nerve more often in endophytic tumours, and invasion into the choroid and sclera with possible extraocular extension in exophytic tumours [[Folberg R, Chévez-Barrios P, Lin AY, Milman T. Tumors of the eye and ocular adnexa. Rockville (MD): American Registry of Pathology; 2021. (AFIP atlas of tumor and non-tumor pathology, series 5; fascicle 3). Bookref]].

Intracranial tumours are poorly demarcated, soft, and friable. Adequacy of surgical resection of intracranial tumours may be defined based on postoperative imaging studies, and the surgical specimen is representative of the amount of tumour resected.

Histopathology

Retinoblastoma is composed of poorly differentiated neuroblastic (retinoblast) cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm. Apoptosis and mitotic figures are common in the solid tumour alternating with geographical areas of necrosis and dystrophic calcification. Varying degrees of differentiation may be seen, from extreme differentiation forming fleurettes (photoreceptor-like cells in a bouquet of flowers–type arrangement) lacking mitosis or apoptosis, to a moderately differentiated rosette-type arrangement (Flexner–Wintersteiner [most typical for retinoblastoma] or Homer Wright type), to poorly differentiated tumour. High-risk histopathological features for metastasis include invasion of the postlaminar optic nerve, ≥ 3 mm of tumour invasion into the choroid, and scleral invasion or extraocular extension. Concomitant > 3 mm of peripapillary choroidal invasion with postlaminar optic nerve invasion of > 1.5 mm has the highest risk for CNS recurrence {31539297}.

The histological features of the intracranial tumour in trilateral retinoblastoma are identical to those of retinoblastoma.

Cytology

Cytology is not clinically relevant. See Clinical features for diagnosis of intraocular retinoblastoma.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Identification of the specific RB1 gene variant and genetic counselling should be considered for all retinoblastoma patients, regardless of laterality or presence of trilateral disease. Inactivation of RB1 can result from small or large DNA alterations, complex rearrangements such as chromothripsis, loss of heterozygosity, and promoter methylation {20301625;24509483;33670346;12541220}. Increasingly, next-generation sequencing technologies are used to analyse the exons of RB1 and determine complex rearrangements {27155049;33670346}. RB1 RNA analysis may identify pathogenic splice variants caused by deep-intronic mutation {24688104}. In a recent study, patients with RB1 germline variants had a spectrum of alteration types: 37% nonsense, 20% frameshift, 21% splice, 9% large indel, 5% missense, 7% chromosomal deletion, and 1% promoter. Additionally, 10% of these patients exhibited mosaicism {24688104}. Methods such as next-generation sequencing and PCR-based assays can detect mosaic RB1 variants or promoter methylation {19280657;27155049;29851531}. In addition, capture-based targeted sequencing and relative haplotype dosage analysis of maternal plasma have been used to evaluate fetal RB1 status for paternal or de novo sequence variants; in a small study, the prenatal diagnosis was 100% concordant with the postdiagnostic result {33143217}. RB1 sequence variants are routinely evaluated in tumour tissue from enucleated eyes and in peripheral blood in most countries. However, there is a < 5% risk of missing low-level somatic mosaicism with current testing technologies on peripheral blood.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: histopathological confirmation of a tumour type associated with the syndrome; degree of differentiation for the intracranial tumours and association with meningeal involvement should be included.

Desirable: demonstration of the presence of germline pathogenic variants in RB1.

Staging

The International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB) groups eyes by tumour size and other features (groups A to E) and the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stages tumours T1 to T4 {15763190} [[Mallipatna AC, Gallie BL, Chévez-Barrios P, et al. Retinoblastoma. In: Amin MB, Edge SB, Greene FL, et al., editors. AJCC cancer staging manual. 8th ed. New York (NY): Springer; 2017. pp. 819–31. Bookref]], and new in the eighth edition is the hereditary classification system (cTMNH) for retinoblastoma. The hereditary category includes HX (unknown or insufficient evidence of a germline pathogenic RB1 gene variant), H0 (normal RB1 alleles in blood tested), and H1 (bilateral retinoblastoma, trilateral retinoblastoma, patient with family history of retinoblastoma, or molecular definition of germline pathogenic RB1 gene variant).

Prognosis and prediction

Unilateral and bilateral retinoblastoma syndrome prognosis depends on the stage at diagnosis (intraocular versus extraocular) and the presence or absence of high-risk features of metastatic disease {31539297}. Trilateral retinoblastoma has a poor lifelong prognosis. Prognosis is related to age at presentation, with children < 6 years of age having the worst outcomes (~6% survival) {32187454}. The risk of developing an SPM for retinoblastoma syndrome is 0.5–1% per year of life; the mortality of patients with SPM with retinoblastoma syndrome remains about 50% {31904159;19351917}.

For survivors, the current screening recommendations include annual history and physical examination with a focus on bony structures and radiotherapy field, and regular skin examination {32422154}. Because each tumour type presents at different ages – osteosarcomas in the first and second decades of life, soft tissue sarcomas 10–50 years after retinoblastoma diagnosis, and epithelial tumours and melanoma after the second decade of life – the screen should focus on these possible tumours. Patients may develop more than one SPM and surveillance continues throughout their life.
