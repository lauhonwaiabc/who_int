1.5.1 Familial adenomatous polyposis (APC)

Definition

Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline (constitutional) pathogenic variants in APC and characterized by gastrointestinal polyposis and cancers.

MIM numbering

175100 Familial adenomatous polyposis 1; FAP1

ICD-11 coding

2E92.40 & XH1CV4 Polyposis syndrome & Adenomatous polyposis coli

Related terminology

Acceptable: adenomatous polyposis coli.

Not recommended: Gardner syndrome; Turcot syndrome; familial adenomatous polyposis 1.

Subtype(s)

Attenuated familial adenomatous polyposis (AFAP)

Localization

Classic FAP is characterized by the development during adolescence of hundreds of colorectal adenomas, a small proportion of which progress to colorectal adenocarcinoma. Most patients also develop gastric and duodenal polyps, leading to an increased risk of duodenal adenocarcinoma. Desmoid tumours occur in about 10% of patients with FAP, mostly in the small bowel mesentery, abdominal wall, or extremities, and less frequently in the head and neck, mostly arising in soft tissue {975005;8154779}, but also the maxillary sinus, nasopharynx, and oral cavity.

Less-frequent extraintestinal malignancies are hepatoblastoma and cancers of the thyroid, biliary tree, pancreas, and CNS. Frequent benign extraintestinal features are osteomas (often in the mandible), dental abnormalities (supernumerary teeth and odontomas), and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium {26315524}.

Clinical features

Classic FAP is defined by the presence of ≥ 100 adenomatous polyps in the colorectum. Some patients may go on to develop thousands of polyps. The onset of colorectal adenomatous polyps usually occurs in the second decade of life. If colectomy is not performed, patients have a near 100% risk of colorectal adenocarcinoma by the age of 45 years {18194984}.

AFAP is distinguished from classic FAP by fewer (0–100) colorectal adenomas and a somewhat reduced risk (of 80%) and later onset (at a mean age of 56 years) of colorectal cancer {16461775;20105204}.

Almost all patients with FAP develop duodenal adenomas, mostly in the periampullary region and distal duodenum. Small bowel polyps and cancer typically present a decade later than colonic polyps and cancer. About 4–10% of patients develop duodenal adenocarcinoma {15951555}. More than 60% of patients with FAP develop gastric polyps, which are mainly benign fundic gland polyps (FGPs), but also adenomas {26721304}.

Desmoid tumours occur in about 10% of patients with FAP, mostly in the small intestinal mesentery, sometimes in the abdominal wall or extremities, but significantly less frequently in the head and neck, mostly arising in soft tissue {975005;8154779}, including in the maxillary sinus, nasopharynx, and oral cavity. The risk of desmoid fibromatosis in FAP is increased by clinical features such as prior surgery and certain types of APC variants {27387679}. Although desmoid tumours have no metastatic potential, they cause severe morbidity and mortality in a substantial proportion of the approximately 10% of patients with FAP who develop these lesions {10732754;9014661}.

The presence of benign extragastrointestinal features is variable, but almost all patients with FAP have some on close inspection {18194984}. Some benign extraintestinal manifestations can be used as a clinical marker for asymptomatic carriers in families with FAP. In addition, FAP has been associated with a slightly increased risk of thyroid carcinoma, hepatobiliary tree tumours, childhood hepatoblastoma, adrenocortical adenomas and carcinomas, and brain tumours, particularly medulloblastoma [[Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, OMIM® (Internet). Gardner syndrome is considered by many to be an obsolete term used to describe extracolonic features that are now known to be present in most FAP patients.

Turcot syndrome is not considered a recommended term since most cases of Turcot syndrome are due to constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome.

However, given the phenotypic variability of FAP, several other polyposis conditions can have near identical features. These other conditions include MUTYH-associated polyposis, polymerase proofreading–associated polyposis, AXIN2-associated polyposis, NTHL1-associated polyposis, hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome, constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome, and multiple polyps associated with variants in other genes (e.g. MSH3, BUB1B, and FAN1) {27476653}, although the number of colorectal polyps is usually lower in these other syndromes and there may be a different pattern of inheritance and other extracolonic features to provide diagnostic clues. Another group of patients with a phenotype mimicking FAP that have multiple colonic adenomas, usually without extracolonic features or a strong family history, may have a polygenic form of polyposis. A small number of cases have undetectable APC variants and may be regarded as presumed FAP if typical clinical features are present and molecular evidence of the other conditions is absent.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is 1 in 8000–10 000. There is no sex predilection and FAP accounts for < 1% of all colorectal cancers {18194984}.

Etiology

FAP is a Mendelian autosomal dominant syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants in the APC gene (chromosome 5q22.2) that result in reduced expression of or absent APC protein. The severity of disease varies with the position of the variant in the APC gene. Inherited APC variants located in or around the variant cluster region (around codon / amino acid 1309) are associated with the highest number of adenomas (thousands – severe polyposis) and the greatest risk of cancer at a younger age (~28 years), whereas variants outside this region are mostly associated with many hundreds of adenomas and a cancer risk for patients in their forties {10470088;12057910}.

Inherited variants that cause AFAP (0–100 colorectal adenomas) are located nearer to the N-terminus of the APC gene or within the alternatively spliced section of exon 9 (9a), and these patients develop fewer adenomatous polyps at a later age with a cancer risk also at a later age (patients in their fifties) {20105204}. Desmoid tumours are associated with germline variants in APC involving codons 1310–2011 in the mid to C-terminal portion of the encoded protein {26179480}.

APC acts as a classic tumour suppressor gene, and a tumour phenotype arises when the non-mutant allele is spontaneously lost or mutated by a somatic event (second hit). When the inherited variant is in the variant cluster region, the second somatic variant may be either a point mutation or a complete deletion or loss of heterozygosity of the second allele. For inherited variants outside of the variant cluster region, the second somatic hit is usually a point mutation in the mutation/variant cluster region; and loss of heterozygosity has not been observed with inherited variants outside the mutation/variant cluster region. In AFAP, second hits are only observed in the variant cluster region. Therefore, for a colorectal adenoma/cancer to develop, at least one variant must be within the mutation/variant cluster region. Interestingly, patients with germline deletions of the entire APC gene do not always exhibit a low number (< 100) of colorectal adenomas.

In about 20–30% of FAP cases, there is no known family history of the condition, and most of these cases probably represent either de novo variants or cases of the autosomal recessive syndrome MUTYH-associated polyposis, or other polyposis syndromes. Despite the variations in phenotype, it is very rare for any carrier of a pathogenic APC variant not to develop multiple bowel polyps and cancer if preventive measures are not taken. Mosaic de novo APC variant carriers also occur, and they typically have milder and/or localized polyposis {17604324}.

Pathogenesis

The classic APC protein isoform is 2843 amino acids long (several isoforms exist as a result of alternative splicing), and it is centrally involved in regulating WNT signalling. Normal APC restrains colorectal epithelial cell proliferation through its role as a regulator of the WNT signalling pathway by acting as a key scaffold protein that binds critical components (including AXIN2 and phosphorylating enzymes) that tag (by phosphorylation and subsequently ubiquitination) the WNT effector protein β-catenin for proteasomal destruction. With reduced expression or absence of APC protein, the loss of critical APC functions (especially its binding of the AXIN2 and β-catenin proteins) results in greatly impaired degradation of β-catenin with upregulation of WNT signalling, effectively rendering the WNT pathway constitutively activated {10733430;23589686}. Adenomas arise owing to somatically acquired second hits to the non-mutant APC allele. The adenomas subsequently progress in a very similar way to sporadic adenomas of the large bowel, through variants in genes such as KRAS, SMAD4, and TP53 among others {8861899}.

Macroscopic appearance

The mucosa of the resected colon may display hundreds of polyps, which are mostly polypoid or villous adenomas. Most patients with FAP also develop duodenal adenomas with similar appearances to their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

The large bowel polyps are almost always classic adenomas of varying type (tubular, tubulovillous, or villous), grade (low or high), and size; they are similar to sporadic adenomas in appearance. The same features are observed in duodenal adenomas. However, characteristic of FAP is the frequent presence of monocryptal adenomas and oligocryptal adenomas (microadenomas) in otherwise normal-looking colorectal mucosa, including in the stalk mucosa of resected larger adenomatous polyps.

Most gastric polyps (80%) are FGPs. FGPs in FAP are often multiple and may cause fundic gland polyposis. Low-grade dysplasia has been described in nearly 40% of FAP-associated FGPs, but high-grade dysplasia and malignant transformation are rare {26721304}. About 20% of gastric polyps are adenomas, mostly foveolar-type adenomas (17%), some pyloric gland adenomas (3%), and rarely intestinal-type adenomas {26721304}.

The main differential diagnosis for gastric adenocarcinoma and proximal polyposis of the stomach (GAPPS) is fundic gland polyposis in the setting of FAP (classic and attenuated forms). The unique sparing of the antrum in patients with GAPPS, and the absence of a colonic polyposis phenotype, is an important clinical feature that distinguishes GAPPS from (attenuated) FAP. Prolonged use of proton-pump inhibitors may be the cause of FGPs/polyposis without dysplasia and should be considered in the differential diagnosis {27634363}.

Desmoid tumours appear as bland, indolent, invasive fibroblastic tumours of connective tissue that expand relentlessly with infiltration into adjacent connective tissues and hence are difficult to excise completely (sometimes called aggressive fibromatosis). Immunohistochemistry reveals nuclear expression of β-catenin.

FAP-associated thyroid tumours are cribriform morular thyroid carcinomas, which are characteristic for this syndrome {18948764;20966648;21455198;29239041;34019236;29785019}. The cribriform component is positive for TTF1, strongly positive for nuclear β-catenin, and negative for thyroglobulin {21455198;29239041}. PAX8 is absent in half of these tumours and focal in the others {34019236}. Morulae are positive for CK5 and CD5, but negative for p63, p40, TTF1, and PAX8 {29239041;34019236}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

About 80–90% of sporadic colorectal cancers carry acquired (second hit) variants in the APC gene, and this is considered to be the adenoma-initiating event {8861899}. Although APC has other functions (including regulation of cell polarity, cell–cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and spindle formation), its WNT pathway regulatory function of controlling intracellular levels of β-catenin is key to its effects on driving adenoma development {9065401;8861899}. FAP-related desmoid tumours are caused by APC rather than CTNNB1 variants, the latter being more common in the sporadic counterparts; nevertheless, they also show nuclear β-catenin immunoreactivity secondary to WNT pathway activation {22419028}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: identification of a germline pathogenic variant in APC in a patient with colorectal polyposis.

Desirable: presence of > 100 colorectal adenomas is indicative of a probable diagnosis of classic FAP; presence of 0–100 colorectal adenomas is indicative of a probable diagnosis of attenuated FAP; presence of > 1000 colorectal adenomas is indicative of a probable diagnosis of severe variant FAP.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Approaches to the management of FAP cases are guided by their clinical presentation and severity. Colorectal screening by endoscopy and chemoprevention are in use {28674119;22122775;26315524}. Colectomy is often required to prevent the development of colorectal adenocarcinoma. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is indicated for FAP patients aged 25–30 years and is guided by the Spigelman stage of duodenal polyposis {2571019;15951555}. Duodenal polyposis is treated endoscopically for as long as possible, but surgery is often required. In addition, screening for extraintestinal manifestations is recommended by some, including for desmoid tumours {28674119;25645574}.

People with FAP have a 3.35-fold elevated risk of dying compared with the general population {8223060}. The main causes of death are upper gastrointestinal malignancy, perioperative complications, desmoid tumours, and suicide {8223060;10499482}.
