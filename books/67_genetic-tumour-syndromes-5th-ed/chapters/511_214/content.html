5.1.1 Dyskeratosis congenita (DKC1, TERT, TERC, TINF2, others)

Definition

Dyskeratosis congenita is an inherited syndrome caused by defective telomere maintenance, characterized by tongue leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, abnormal skin pigmentation, and high rates of bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, cancers, and other complications.

Related terminology

Subtype(s)

Autosomal dominant dyskeratosis congenita (DKCA); autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB); X-linked dyskeratosis congenita (DKCX); Zinsser–Engman–Cole syndrome; Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome; Revesz syndrome; Coats plus syndrome

Localization

The disease involves the skin as hyperpigmentation, the nail as dystrophy, and oral mucosa as leukoplakia.

Clinical features

Dysplastic fingernails and toenails, oral leukoplakia, and lacy, reticular skin pigmentation define the mucocutaneous phenotype of dyskeratosis congenita but most patients have additional manifestations, including bone marrow failure. Oral leukoplakia is most commonly seen on the tongue. Signs of dystrophic nails, typically longitudinal ridging, are seen at the ages of 5–13 years {19327580}. Reticular hyperpigmentation is typically fine, with a lace-like pattern frequently on the face, upper trunk, and upper arms.

Other mucocutaneous findings include adermatoglyphia (no finger prints), palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, early greying, scalp or eyelash hair loss, and epiphora (watering eyes). Bone marrow failure is seen in the second decade of life in 90% of cases. Patients have an increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck, upper aerodigestive tract cancers (oesophageal carcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma), and haematological malignancy, including myelodysplasia, acute myelogenous leukaemia, and lymphoma.

Coats plus syndrome (cerebroretinal microangiopathy with calcifications and cysts 1) is a subtype of dyskeratosis congenita with bilateral Coats disease of the retina, together with bone marrow failure, CNS calcifications, leukodystrophy, poor bone healing, vascular telangiectasis, and gastrointestinal bleeding. It is caused by germline variants in telomere capping proteins, primarily biallelic CTC1.

Epidemiology

The prevalence is not known but estimated at approximately 1 per 1 000 000. However, the expansion of the phenotypic spectrum to include familial pulmonary fibrosis due to pathogenic germline variants in telomere biology genes and the recognition of a spectrum of telomere biology disorders suggests the prevalence is probably higher.

Etiology

Dyskeratosis congenita is caused by germline variants in genes important in telomere biology. Inheritance is most commonly X-linked recessive (DKC1). Other modes of inheritance include autosomal dominant (TERC, or TINF2 mutation), autosomal recessive (NOP10, NHP2, WRAP53, CTC1), and autosomal dominant or recessive (TERT). De novo / sporadic cases can occur with TINF2 {30047419;33482595}. In total, disease-causing variants have so far been reported in at least 18 genes regulating telomere maintenance (e.g. DKC1, TERC, TERT, NOP10, NHP2, ACD, TINF2, POT1, CTC1, STN1, WRAP53, RTEL1, PARN, NAF1, and ZCCHC8) {36485133} (see #38694Table 6.01 for a fuller list).

Pathogenesis

Telomeres consist of (TTAGGG)n repeats and a protein complex at chromosome ends essential for chromosomal stability. Telomerase adds repeat nucleotides to the 3′ end of DNA after replication, to prevent shortening of telomeres with each cell division. A number of genes have been implicated in dyskeratosis congenita (reviewed in {36151328;36485133;35929966;33482595}). DKC1, TERC, TERT, NOP10, and NHP2 encode components of the telomerase complex responsible for telomere maintenance. For telomere protection, TINF2 and ACD encode components of the shelterin complex. For recruitment and docking of telomerase onto the telomere CTC1 encodes part of the CST complex. WRAP53 is involved in telomere trafficking. RTEL1 and STN1 are involved in telomere replication. PARN is involved in the control of mRNA stability {36151328}.

Macroscopic appearance

Solid tumours related to dyskeratosis congenita have the same appearance as their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Cutaneous findings are nonspecific, comprising epidermal atrophy, dermal telangiectasia, and pigment incontinence {26089061}.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Clinical diagnosis may be confirmed by telomere-length testing. Testing of peripheral blood leukocytes from patients with dyskeratosis congenita shows the telomere lengths to be below the first percentile {22058220;17468339;33709208}. Sequencing of affected genes may be helpful in confirming the diagnosis.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of short telomeres in peripheral blood; clinical findings in the skin, nail, or mouth.

Desirable: demonstration of pathogenic variants in affected genes, such as DKC1, TERC, TERT, and others (see #38694Table 6.01).

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis varies depending on both genetic and environmental factors. Patients with a mild form of the disease can present with one or more clinical features, whereas others can develop bone marrow failure by 20 years of age. By 40 years of age, 40% of patients experience bone marrow failure.
