4.5.3 Nijmegen breakage syndrome (NBN)

Definition

Nijmegen breakage syndrome (NBS) is an autosomal recessive tumour syndrome caused by hypomorphic sequence variants affecting NBN and characterized by progressive microcephaly, early growth retardation, intellectual disability, immunodeficiency, cellular sensitivity to ionizing radiation, and an increased risk of malignancy, primarily lymphomas and other solid tumours.

MIM numbering

ICD-11 coding

4A01.31 DNA repair defects other than combined T-cell or B-cell immunodeficiencies

Related terminology

Not recommended: Nijmegen breakage syndrome 1.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Lymphoma is the most common tumour seen in patients with NBS, of both B- and T-cell origin {18788073;26271390}. Other solid tumours that have been reported include medulloblastoma, gliomas, and soft tissue sarcomas.

Clinical features

The cardinal characteristics of patients with NBS are disproportionate progressive microcephaly observed from birth that influences dysmorphic craniofacial features, growth deficits until early childhood (2–3 years) that mildly improve with age, increased risk of malignancy, premature ovarian failure in female patients, and immunodeficiency marked by recurrent infections {10799436;20301355}. Additional manifestations may include developmental delay, decline in intellectual ability, absent or delayed prepubertal growth spurts in later stages of childhood and adolescence, skin abnormalities, and other congenital malformations {10799436;15279809;22373003}.

The high predisposition to developing cancer occurs at early ages, with an estimated 70% risk of malignancy; some individuals developed multiple primary malignancies {35454905}. T-cell malignancies (T-lymphoblastic leukaemia and T-lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma are the most common {18788073;26271390;35454905}. Medulloblastomas, rhabdomyosarcoma, and other solid tumours have been reported.

Epidemiology

No reliable estimates of worldwide prevalence of NBS exist, but it probably occurs in approximately 1:100 000 live births, most commonly in Eastern European populations owing to a founder variant in NBN {20301355}. A recurrent frameshift NBN variant NM_002485.5:c.657_661del p.(K219Nfs*16) is responsible for a large proportion of the pathogenic alleles {20301355;9590180}, with an allele fraction of 0.0003969 in European (non-Finnish) populations per the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v3.1.2; gnomad.broadinstitute.org).

Etiology

NBS is caused by biallelic hypomorphic variants of the NBN gene (at 8q21.3), which encodes the NBN protein. The first report of pathogenic variants in the NBN gene in patients with NBS dates to 1998 {9590180} including the founder variant described above.

Pathogenesis

NBN is a member of the MRE11/RAD50/NBN double strand–break repair complex, often referred to as the MRN complex {9590180}. As a non-catalytic component of the MRN complex, NBN is involved in ATM-dependent DNA damage signalling regulation, thereby affecting critical processes, such as DNA damage–induced apoptosis and cell cycle–checkpoint activation, as well as the replication stress response {15279770;35164800}. Pathogenic sequence variants affect exons 6–10 of NBN, including the most common founder variant, resulting in truncated proteins with partial NBN activity. This partial activity leads to defects in the biological processes outlined above and promotes cellular transformation and cancer development {11279524}. Of note, cells from one patient with biallelic potential loss-of-function sequence variants demonstrated absent NBN protein by immunoblot and radiosensitivity by colony survival assay {25677497}. Individuals with heterozygous variants in NBN may have an increased risk of cancer in later adulthood {36346689}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance of tumours arising in patients with NBS resembles that of the corresponding sporadic tumours in the same organs.

Histopathology

The histopathological findings for tumours arising in patients with NBS are similar to those in unaffected individuals.

Cytology

The cytological findings of lymphomas and leukaemias in patients with NBS are similar to those of the sporadic counterparts, and cytological specimens may be used for molecular pathology analyses.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Targeted evaluation for the recurrent NM_002485.5:c.657_661del p.(Lys219Asnfs*16) founder sequence variant in NBN may be performed first, as many patients are homozygous for this variant, with full gene sequencing when necessary to detect other pathogenic alterations (predominantly frameshift with few missense variants). Large exon or multiexon deletions or duplications involving NBN are rare in NBS {20301355}. Quantitative radiation sensitivity of lymphoblastoid cell lines from patients is sometimes used as a clinical assay but is not specific to NBS.

Seventy-four per cent of patients with NBS also present with non-malignant, clonal rearrangements typically involving chromosomes 7 and 14 and the IG and/or TR genes in peripheral blood lymphocytes, which can precede the development of a lymphoid malignancy {20167538;35454905}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of biallelic germline (constitutional) pathogenic sequence variants in NBN.

Desirable: clinical features of the disorder, including microcephaly, progressive growth retardation, and variable developmental delay.

Staging

Staging is as for sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

Treatment of tumours in patients with NBS is related to their stage. Patients with NBS are more likely to have treatment-related toxicities, particularly pulmonary disease. Radiation therapy should be avoided. A consensus guideline for treatment of tumours in patients with NBS was recently published {35454905}. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation is recommended after first remission with evidence for improved long-term survival {35454905;35767752}.
