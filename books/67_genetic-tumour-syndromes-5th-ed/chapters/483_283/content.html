4.8.3 Turner syndrome

Definition

Turner syndrome (TS) is a sex chromosome disorder caused by the germline (constitutional) complete or partial absence of a second sex chromosome resulting in the karyotype of 45,X in non-mosaic cases and generally characterized by multiple congenital anomalies and infertility.

MIM numbering

None

ICD-11 coding

LD50.0 Turner syndrome

Related terminology

Acceptable: 45,X syndrome; Ullrich–Turner syndrome.

Not recommended: 45,XO syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Location is as appropriate for tumour type.

Clinical features

The most common phenotypes of individuals with TS across diverse populations are short stature, cubitus valgus, and low posterior hairline {31854143}. Additional clinical findings include congenital heart disease (bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation of the aorta and aortic stenosis), webbed posterior neck, narrow maxilla, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, congenital lymphoedema, horseshoe kidney, excessive pigmented naevi, hypoplastic nails, short fourth metacarpals, hearing loss, amenorrhoea and infertility secondary to ovarian dysgenesis, endocrine disorders such as type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, and autoimmune disorders {31213699}. Intelligence is generally normal for these individuals, but there is a higher prevalence of specific learning disabilities including decrease in visual-spatial and/or visual-perceptual abilities and sexual difficulties compared with the general population {7606849;31213699}.

Although the overall cancer incidence is not increased in TS {33112259}, the occurrence of cancer is different from the general population. Large TS cohort data showed that women with TS seem to be at an increased risk of skin neoplasms, CNS tumours, and colorectal cancers, but are at a decreased risk of breast cancer {18282803}. There is an increased risk of gonadal cancers.

Epidemiology

The prevalence of TS is estimated to be 1 per 2000–3000 women {16849410;6307025}. The median age at diagnosis is about 15 years; many women with TS are not diagnosed until adulthood and some are never diagnosed {16849410}.

Approximately 25–35% of patients with Turner syndrome and Y chromosome material develop gonadoblastomas {25535833;27052295}.

Etiology

In a large study, data showed that less than half of patients with TS syndrome have a 45,X karyotype. Others are mosaic for 45,X and another cell lineage (such as 45,X/46,XX; 45,X/47,XXX), while the rest are distributed between 46,X,i(Xq) and other structural abnormalities of the X chromosome {18282803}. In addition, about 3% of women present with 45,X/46,XY {31213699}.

The absence of part or all of the X chromosome is thought to lead to global changes in the genome including methylation and transcriptional changes {33112259}. The TS genome is globally hypomethylated with few regions of hypermethylation compared with normal controls. In addition, RNA expression changes have been reported in both the X chromosome and the autosomes. It has been postulated that genes escaping X inactivation are probable candidates to explain part of the TS phenotype. So far, SHOX (encoding the SHOX protein) has been implicated in the growth deficit associated with TS {27194967}. Furthermore, another gene on the X chromosome TIMP1 (encoding the TIMP1 protein) seems, together with TIMP3 (a gene on the autosome), to explain the increased risk of bicuspid aortic valve and aortic dilation in women with TS {30281655}. Other genes, such as KDM5C, which participates in the transcriptional repression of neuronal genes, contribute to the neurocognitive profile of TS {27687697}.

Pathogenesis

TS cases with the non-mosaic karyotype 45,X arise from segregation errors in meiosis as well as postfertilization loss of a sex chromosome unrelated to meiotic non-disjunction. While it is possible to determine the parental origin of the missing sex chromosome, the stage of the meiotic error is difficult to establish owing to the absence of the sex chromosome that is mis-segregating. Studies of apparently non-mosaic TS cases have shown, contrary to what occurs in autosomal aneuploidies, that about 75% of cases are caused by loss of the paternal chromosome {16647844}.

In 45,X women, ovarian tissue is replaced with streak gonads that are devoid of germ cells. Therefore, with few exceptions, TS individuals are infertile. It has been reported that the loss of germ cells involves a block early in meiosis and the majority of 45,X oocytes arrest shortly after entering meiosis {3757617}. Although successful pregnancies have been observed in TS, this has been speculated to be due to the presence of mosaicism for a normal cell line in the gonads. This, in turn, may explain the discrepancy between the increased number of miscarriages of conceptuses with 45,X karyotype and the survival of others to adulthood.

The presence of Y chromosome material in mosaic TS individuals is associated with an increased risk of gonadal cancer, such as gonadoblastoma and dysgerminoma {29248931;36305565}. However, patients with 45,X/46,XY mosaicism are heterogeneous in their clinical manifestations ranging from normal female to normal male to varying severity of genital anomalies, and it has been shown that the increased risk of gonadal germ cell neoplasm is associated with marked genital anomalies and ambiguous genitalia. Therefore, in 45,X/46,XY women, without signs of virilization, tumour risk is low. However, since the gonadal tissue is often a non-functional streak, preservation is not necessary {27032613;21508138}.

Most patients with gonadoblastoma feature a female phenotype. However, their gonads carry Y chromosome material and show either a dysgenetic/dysplastic testicular histology (20%) or streak gonad appearance (20%) {4193741}. Since the genetic male developmental pathway is faulty, or androgen effects are deficient, SOX9 expression is insufficient to support normal formation of seminiferous cords and Sertoli cells, leading to predominance of FOXL2 {18348162;21682576}, causing a delay in germ cell maturation, with expression of OCT3/4 and TSPY, which combined with KIT/KITLG signalling seems to promote neoplastic transformation.

Macroscopic appearance

Gonadoblastomas range from soft to firm, are brown, yellow, or grey in appearance, and measure as much as 80 mm. They often show multifocal calcification. If they progress to invasive germ cell tumours, they show soft, grey nodules {25129544;31805291}. Other tumours show features similar to those of their sporadic counterparts.

Histopathology

Gonadoblastoma is an in situ form of malignant germ cell tumour consisting of germ cell neoplasia in situ / seminoma / dysgerminoma cells, and incompletely differentiated sex cord cells reminiscent of Sertoli/granulosa cells, covered in more detail in the Paediatric tumours volume of this series [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Paediatric tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 7). https://publications.iarc.who.int/608. Bookref]]. Other tumours show features similar to those of their sporadic counterparts.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Karyotype analysis of a peripheral blood specimen is the gold standard to document the presence of an additional copy of the X chromosome or structural X chromosome abnormalities as well as identify cases of mosaicism with a normal cell line. FISH can also be useful in detecting Y chromosome material or low-level mosaicism of another cell line.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: confirmation of the absence of one sex chromosome or mosaicism for another cell line and/or structural X chromosome abnormalities.

Staging

Gonadoblastomas are regarded as an in situ malignancy, so are regarded by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classification and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) as pTis.

Prognosis and prediction

Morbidity and mortality may be increased in individuals with TS compared with the general population {31213699}, mostly due to endocrine diseases including autoimmunity (diabetes and/or thyroiditis) and osteoporosis and associated bone fragility. In addition, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, respiratory (mainly pneumonia), neurological, urogenital, and musculoskeletal disorders contribute to the excess mortality in individuals with TS and therefore clinical management is best accomplished with a multidisciplinary medical team.

Guidelines for (1) diagnostic and genetic issues, (2) growth and development during childhood and adolescence, (3) congenital and acquired cardiovascular disease, (4) transition and adult care, and (5) other comorbidities and neurocognitive issues were produced in 2016 by a consensus group and based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system {28705803}. Gonadectomy was recommended in all individuals with Y chromosome material identified by karyotyping, though this is being re-evaluated based on recent studies {36305565}.
