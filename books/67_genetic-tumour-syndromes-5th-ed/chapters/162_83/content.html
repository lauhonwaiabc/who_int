1.6.2 SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome (SMO)

Definition

SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome is a mosaic disorder of excessive hedgehog signalling defined by the presence of a postzygotic gain-of-function variant in the SMO gene, the progeny cells from which have populated more than one tissue site, characterized by the risk of developing patchy skin lesions, polysyndactyly, diverse cerebral malformations, unicoronal craniosynostosis, iris colobomas, microphthalmia, and intestinal malrotation with smooth muscle hamartomas or neoplasms.

MIM numbering

601707 Curry–Jones syndrome; CRJS

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Acceptable: Curry–Jones syndrome.

Not recommended: Happle–Tinschert syndrome; segmental naevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome.

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

The localization of tumours depends on the tissue distribution of cells harbouring the mosaic SMO mutation. Additional somatic mutations are probably necessary to drive clonal tumorigenesis {33846763;35135164}. Basaloid follicular hamartoma, trichoblastoma, and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) arise in the skin; medulloblastoma in the cerebellum; and intestinal hamartoma in the small and large bowel.

Clinical features

The organs or systems most commonly affected are the skin, eyes, craniofacial area, brain, bowel, and limbs (see #37323Box 2.10 for details). Asymmetrical involvement is frequent {18709310;18798318;27236920;31120550}. Associated medulloblastoma may be discovered incidentally or present with cerebellar signs; basaloid follicular hamartoma, trichoblastoma, and BCC may present with visible skin lesions {35972041}; and intestinal hamartoma presents as discrete fibrotic masses in the retroperitoneum, mesentery, and wall of the small or large bowel {18798318;28386950}.

Imaging

Medulloblastoma is typically diagnosed using MRI of the brain. Intestinal hamartoma may be detectable using conventional radiography or MRI with contrast.

Epidemiology

No epidemiological data are available on SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome, currently reported in only about 20 cases worldwide.

Etiology

Unknown

Pathogenesis

SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome is caused by a postzygotic gain-of-function mutation in the SMO gene. All recognized cases to date have harboured the identical pathogenic sequence variant NM_005631.5:c.1234C>T p.(Leu412Phe). SMO is a membrane-spanning G protein–coupled receptor normally maintained in an inactive state by binding to the hedgehog receptors PTCH1 and PTCH2 in their unliganded state. Hedgehog binding to its receptor disrupts the latter’s interaction with SMO, releasing the inhibition. The p.(Leu412Phe) substitution in SMO locates to a pivot region in transmembrane helix 5 that appears crucial for transducing the PTCH-mediated inhibitory signal, leading to constitutive SMO activation in the presence of PTCH {24859340;25759020}. Increased cell proliferation associated with SMO-p.(Leu412Phe) has been observed in mouse ameloblast-lineage cells {24859340}.

Macroscopic appearance

Based on current limited evidence, the macroscopic appearance of most tumours (basaloid follicular hamartoma, trichoblastoma, BCC, medulloblastoma) appears similar to that of their sporadic counterparts. No sporadic equivalent to intestinal hamartoma is known; these present as 5–50 mm diameter, fibrotic, firm-textured, smooth, white masses with homogeneous cut surfaces {18798318}.

Histopathology

For most tumours associated with SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome (basaloid follicular hamartoma, trichoblastoma, BCC, medulloblastoma), it is unclear if there are any morphological distinctions from their sporadic counterparts. Intestinal hamartoma exhibits smooth muscle bundles associated with intermyenteric ganglion cells (smooth muscle hamartoma); gastrointestinal leiomyomas and myofibromas may also be encountered {18798318;28386950;31120550}.

Differential diagnosis must distinguish (1) sporadically occurring tumours harbouring activating SMO mutations (which have been previously described in BCC, ameloblastoma, meningioma, and medulloblastoma {27236920}), but where there is no clinical or molecular evidence of more extensive mosaicism; (2) patients who are germline heterozygous or homozygous for any other SMO variant that is not constitutively activating.

Cytology

Cytologically, individual tumours are indistinguishable from their sporadic counterparts.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Confirmation of diagnosis requires (1) the presence of the SMO c.1234C>T sequence variants in tumour tissue, and (2) identification of the same variant either in a separate tumour or in non-tumorous tissue (to distinguish from sporadic tumours). Although the variant may be detectable using dideoxy-sequencing in relatively pure tumour tissue, deep massively parallel sequencing is recommended to enhance variant-detection sensitivity to about 1% when investigating non-tumorous tissue {35135164}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

See #37323Box 2.10 and #39037Table 2.35.

Staging

Staging is as for sporadic counterparts.

Prognosis and prediction

The specific prognosis for tumours associated with SMO-related Curry–Jones syndrome is not reported, but successful treatment of medulloblastomas has been recorded in several publications {31120550;18798318;31825089}.

Recommendations for surveillance have included annual (or more frequent) brain MRI scans to detect medulloblastoma and skin examination for early detection of BCCs {35135164}.

Essential:

 

Identification of the SMO NM_005631.5:c.1234C>T nucleotide transition in samples from more than one tissue site

 

OR

 

Identification of SMO NM_005631.5:c.1234C>T in a single tissue sample, accompanied by at least one desirable feature involving a different organ or different part of the same organ

Desirable:

One or more of:

 

1. Swirling epidermal involvement often following Blaschko lines, with macules and/or papules, hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation, hypertrichosis and/or atrophoderma, palmar or plantar pits

 

2. Ocular malformations (coloboma, cataract, microphthalmia)
 

3. Abnormal skull (unilateral craniosynostosis, macrocephaly) or dentition
 

4. Brain malformations (partial agenesis of corpus callosum, hemimegalencephaly, polymicrogyria) that can be associated with developmental delay and intellectual disability
 

5. Medulloblastoma
 

6. Intestinal involvement (dysmotility, malrotation, pseudo-obstruction, hamartomas, leiomyomas, myofibromas)
 

7. Limb shortening, polydactyly and/or syndactyly
