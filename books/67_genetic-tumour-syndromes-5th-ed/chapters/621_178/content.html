6.2.1 Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome (SMARCB1, SMARCA4)

Definition

Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome is a disorder characterized by a markedly increased risk of developing malignant rhabdoid tumour (MRT) and/or its related CNS counterpart, atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour (AT/RT), due to germline loss or inactivation of SMARCB1 (rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 1, RTPS1), or rarely of SMARCA4 (rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 2, RTPS2), in which ovarian small cell carcinoma of the ovary of hypercalcaemic type (SCCOHT) is common.

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

Not recommended: rhabdoid predisposition syndrome; familial posterior fossa brain tumour syndrome of infancy.

Subtype(s)

Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 1, associated with alterations in SMARCB1; rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 2, associated with alterations in SMARCA4

Localization

RTPS1 and RTPS2 can affect both the nervous system and extraneural organs/tissues. RTPS1 most commonly involves the CNS followed by the kidney and soft tissues. RTPS2 involves the gynaecological system (mainly ovary), with much rarer CNS presentations.

Clinical features

Individuals with RTPS1 often present with AT/RT. AT/RTs generally occur in early childhood but are occasionally found in adults {15779233}. Patients with germline sequence variants or copy-number alterations of SMARCB1 may develop isolated AT/RTs or AT/RTs with synchronous or metachronous renal and/or extrarenal MRTs {9892189;12016529}. By far the most frequent extra-CNS presentation in RTPS1 is MRT of the kidney. A pathogenic germline SMARCB1 variant may be suspected in any young child with a rhabdoid tumour of any localization, while bilateral renal MRT is pathognomonic of RTPS. MRTs may also originate in the head-and-neck region, paraspinal soft tissues, heart, mediastinum, abdominal cavity, liver, pelvis, and extremities {10078913;23816299;35565313}. Children surviving a primary MRT can develop a second primary in a different location {23154773}. Individuals with RTPS2 often present with SCCOHT, a rare highly aggressive ovarian tumour in adolescents and young adults {24752781;32156746} [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Female genital tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2020. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 4). https://publications.iarc.who.int/592. Bookref]].

Median age of tumour onset in both RTPS1 and RTPS2 is younger than in patients with sporadic tumours. Patients with RTPS1 are more likely to demonstrate multiple synchronous or metachronous tumours. Approximately one third of children with RTPS1 present in the first year of life with synchronous or multifocal tumours {33692948}. Presentation for RTPS2 has been less well characterized. To date no other distinguishing clinical features are evident in patients with RTPS1 or RTPS2, compared with sporadic rhabdoid tumours. The exceptions are those patients with velocardiofacial syndrome or so-called “distal chromosome 22q deletions” who have germline deletions that include SMARCB1 {17541642}. Isolated cases of patients with rhabdoid tumours and other genomic disorders, as well as loss of expression of SMARCB1 (INI1) and/or SMARCA4 (BRG1) in syndromic and rare non-syndromic malignant brain tumours, have also been reported {17603804;19938088;23510391;35446794;28608987;26364901}.

Epidemiology

Germline SMARCB1 alterations (RTPS1) are estimated to occur in as many as 35% of all patients with AT/RTs and as many as 21% in extracranial MRTs {33249395;16459991;21108436}. Individuals with a germline variant are more likely to present with a tumour in the first months of life or even congenitally {35565313}. Second primary tumours may arise 15 years or more after the primary diagnosis {30393974;35678633}, suggesting that lifetime surveillance is required. The epidemiological features of germline SMARCA4 alterations (RTPS2) are less well characterized. However, data from small case series suggest a significantly higher proportion of germline alterations in RTPS2, as well as evidence of a younger age of onset for SCCOHT patients (median age of 24 years, range of 14 months to 71 years) than in sporadic cases {31954538;25060813}.

Etiology

RTPS1 and RTPS2 are caused by pathogenic germline variants in SMARCB1 or SMARCA4, respectively (see #37381Table 7.04). The underlying etiology of these changes have been more extensively characterized for SMARCB1 where de novo germline alterations can occur during oogenesis/spermatogenesis, or postzygotically during the early stages of embryogenesis (mosaic) {21108436;25060813;16261613;10521299}. Tumours in rare families with multiple affected children and no parental SMARCB1 alteration are probably due to gonadal mosaicism {28722703}. In some families, apparently unaffected carriers may develop schwannomas in the fourth or fifth decades of life. Germline variants in both SMARCB1 and SMARCA4 have also been shown to contribute to other genetic syndromes, such as Coffin–Siris syndrome, associated with dysmorphic features and intellectual disability, although the disease-causing variants are typically missense variants that are dominant negative or lead to a gain of function.

Pathogenesis

Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 1 (SMARCB1)

The SMARCB1 gene was the first subunit of the SWI/SNF complex found to be altered in cancer. This gene is located at chromosome region 22q11.23 and contains nine exons spanning 50 kb of genomic DNA {9671307}. Alternative splicing of exon 2 results in two transcripts and two proteins with lengths of 385 and 376 amino acid residues, respectively. The so-called SNF5 homology domain in the second half of the protein harbours highly conserved structural motifs through which SMARCB1 interacts with other proteins {19234532}. The SMARCB1 protein is a core subunit of mammalian SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling complexes, which regulates gene expression via ATP-mediated nucleosomal remodelling {21654818}. The SMARCB1 protein functions as a tumour suppressor via repression of CCND1 gene expression, induction of the CDKN2A gene, and hypophosphorylation of RB1, resulting in G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest {12149641;19305156}. Loss of SMARCB1 leads to activation of EZH2, a histone lysine methyl transferase and catalytic component of the PRC2 complex, resulting in increased H3 p.Lys27me3 marks associated with repression of polycomb gene targets. The Hippo signalling pathway is involved in the detrimental effects of SMARCB1 deficiency, and its main effector (YAP1) is overexpressed in AT/RT {24892285;20951942}.

SMARCB1 tumorigenesis follows the Knudson two-hit hypothesis. One copy of the SMARCB1 gene is inactivated by a nonsense or frameshift variant, deletion, or other structural variation affecting the SMARCB1 locus, or, rarely, missense or splicing alterations, whereas the second event is typically due to a chromosomal deletion or loss of heterozygosity {9671307;9892189}. With ongoing advances in molecular genotyping technology, additional rarer mechanisms of genomic variation have been identified, including retrotransposon insertion {33896072;34231212}. The types of SMARCB1 alterations observed in sporadic MRTs are similar to the spectrum of germline alterations reported to date. However, single-base deletions in exon 9 occur most often in AT/RTs in patients without detectable germline alterations {16459991;21108436;19124645}. In rhabdoid tumours, biallelic loss-of-function events result in total loss of SMARCB1 expression in tumour cells {15105654}. This contrasts with the presence of missense and splice-site SMARCB1 variants and the mosaic SMARCB1 expression in schwannomas seen in patients with schwannomatosis {24933152}.

RTPS1 follows an autosomal dominant inheritance, but because of high mortality and morbidity for patients with rhabdoid tumour, familial inheritance of the predisposition is generally rare and most cases occur de novo {10521299;10739763}. It has been reported that as many as 35% of patients with rhabdoid tumour carry a germline SMARCB1 alteration as the first hit {21208904;21108436}. However, a recent study demonstrated a lower incidence of germline alterations suggesting prior studies may have been biased by inclusion of patients with multiple primary tumours, virtually all of whom have a germline alteration in SMARCB1 {32105509}. A few families have been reported in which the affected individuals inherited a SMARCB1 variant and developed SMARCB1-mutated schwannomatosis or a rhabdoid tumour {22082606;21108436;19124645}. In these families, the rhabdoid tumours as well as the schwannomas displayed total loss of SMARCB1 protein expression {22082606;19124645}. A patient with co-occurrence of rhabdoid tumour and schwannomas is on record {29779243}.

Transcriptome and DNA methylation profiling separate SMARCB1-altered AT/RTs into three molecular groups, which by consensus have been designated as AT/RT-TYR, AT/RT-SHH and AT/RT-MYC {31889194}. The much less common SMARCA4-altered AT/RT constitutes another molecular group {33331994}. Molecular groups show differences in patient age, localization, and survival. Recent data indicate a further molecular and clinical heterogeneity in the AT/RT-SHH group, identifying three subgroups (AT/RT-SHH-1a, AT/RT-SHH-1b, and AT/RT-SHH-2), with some evidence of increased frequency of germline alterations in the AT/RT-SHH-2 subgroup {35501487} as well as SMARCA4-altered AT/RT {33331994}. Immunohistochemical and/or molecular techniques should be used to confirm the absence of nuclear SMARCB1 or SMARCA4 protein expression and/or SMARCB1 or SMARCA4 alterations.

SMARCB1 has also been identified as one of three schwannomatosis-predisposing genes. Approximately 15% of schwannomatosis cases demonstrate a family history and most cases are sporadic. This gene appears to be involved in about 50% of familial cases but in ≤ 10% of sporadic cases {18647326;18285426;21255467;24933152}. In contrast to RTPS1, schwannomatosis generally arises from missense and splice-site SMARCB1 germline variants that may result in variable protein expression, although rare families experiencing both tumour types have been reported {24933152;29779243}. SMARCB1 germline variants rarely predispose individuals to the development of multiple meningiomas {19582488;20930055;22038540}.

While a spectrum of CNS tumours including meningiomas, gliomas, and melanomas may show rhabdoid features, such so-called composite rhabdoid tumours generally retain nuclear SMARCB1 expression {15761491}, strongly suggesting that they do not contain the same genetic alterations as classic AT/RTs and are therefore unlikely to be part of RTPS1.

Methylation and immune profiling of extra-CNS MRT has identified both renal and extrarenal MRT subgroups, as well as shared molecular features with the AT/RT-MYC subgroup {31708418}. MRTs may also originate in the head and neck region, paraspinal soft tissues, heart, mediastinum, abdominal cavity, liver, pelvis, and extremities {10078913;23816299;35565313}. Children surviving a primary MRT can develop a second primary in a different location {23154773}. SMARCB1 alterations have also been identified in varying proportions of a variety of epithelial, myoepithelial, and mesenchymal adult-onset neoplasms, including myoepithelial carcinoma (10–40%), extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (20%), epithelioid schwannoma (40%), and epithelioid malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (70%), but to date these tumours have not been described in association with RTPS1 {15899790;32646614;29397238;22082606}.

Rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome 2 (SMARCA4)

The SMARCA4 gene, located at chromosome 19p13.2 and encoding a catalytic subunit of the SWI/SNF complex, was the second member of this complex reported in a cancer predisposition syndrome {25169151;20137775}. Germline and somatic alterations in SMARCA4 give rise to SCCOHT. While it may be reasonably assumed that many of the underlying mechanisms identified in the pathogenesis of SMARCB1 are relevant to SMARCA4, there have been limited studies to date. Nonsense and missense alterations of SMARCA4 have been described as potential primary inactivating changes, along with copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, in AT/RTs, as well as nonsense or frameshift variants in SCCOHT {25060813}. The incidence of germline alterations is high in SCCOHT, and familial presentations with both extraneural and CNS tumours due to SMARCA4 germline alterations have been reported {23775540}.

Other SWI/SNF genes

More recently, other genes encoding SWI/SNF subunits have also been identified as recurrently mutated in cancers and may be associated with other hereditary tumour predisposition syndromes (such as PBRM1 causing hereditary renal cell carcinoma and SMARCE1 causing multiple familial intracranial and spinal clear cell meningiomas) but these genes have not been associated with development of RTPS-defining tumours (e.g. AT/RT, MRT, SCCOHT) {25911086;23377182;25143307;33185983}. Collectively, 20% of all human cancers contain a SWI/SNF gene variant, and therefore it can reasonably be expected that the definition of RTPS may continue to evolve {24853101}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Immunohistochemical staining for SMARCB1 and SMARCA4 expression allows for identification of tumours associated with RTPS1 and RTPS2, respectively, although the loss of nuclear staining alone does not distinguish a sporadic from an RTPS-associated tumour.

Genetic testing including sequence and copy-number analysis is therefore strongly recommended to confirm suspected germline alterations. Demonstration of a germline SMARCB1 or SMARCA4 pathogenic variant in a patient with AT/RT, MRT, or SCCOHT is sufficient for the diagnosis of RTPS1 or RTPS2, respectively {20137775;25494491}. Patients with multiple MRTs, with both AT/RT and MRT, or with affected siblings or other relatives are almost certain to be affected by RTPS themselves.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: demonstration of a germline SMARCB1 or SMARCA4 pathogenic variant in a patient with AT/RT, MRT, or SCCOHT is sufficient for the diagnosis of RTPS1 or RTPS2, respectively.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Germline alterations in SMARCB1 have been reported to be associated with poorer outcomes in both AT/RT and extracranial rhabdoid tumours {32105509;33249395}. Another prospective therapeutic study identified an association with metastatic disease but not overall prognosis {33737307}. The precise impact of SMARCB1 germline variants on patient outcomes remains to be definitively established. An increasing number of AT/RT survivors with germline SMARCB1 variants are being reported, suggesting that other variables such as intensity or type of therapy as well as specific SMARCB1 genotypes may influence outcomes. Although the current evidence remains sparse, international consensus criteria for genetic testing and clinical screening have been published. This includes patients with tumour disease as well as relatives with a germline alteration not yet affected by tumours {33532948;29215836}.
