4.4.1 Xeroderma pigmentosum (various genes)

Definition

Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) is an autosomal, recessive disease due to deficient nucleotide excision repair of DNA damage, causing cellular hypersensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) radiation. It is characterized by skin sensitivity to sunlight, ocular photophobia, and early onset of freckle-like skin pigmentation followed by neoplastic changes in sun-exposed sites. There are seven complementation groups of XP (XPA–XPG), each caused by biallelic mutation of one of seven nucleotide excision repair genes or one XP variant form (XPV).

Localization

Tumours are found mainly on sun-exposed skin and eyes (see #38625Box 5.01). Haematological malignancies and brain tumours are also more frequent than in the general population.

Clinical features

Diagnosis is based on clinical features and confirmed by DNA sequencing and/or by tests of hypersensitivity to and DNA repair deficiency after UV radiation–induced damage in cultured cells {20301571}.

Skin: Approximately half of patients with XP have a history of acute sunburn after minimal sun exposure {21097776}. The other half have a history of almost-normal tanning. However, in all patients, numerous freckle-like hyperpigmented macules appear on sun-exposed skin, usually before the age of 2 years. Eyelid papillomas may be present. Basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma are common, as is melanoma in the UV radiation–exposed portions of the skin {21097776}. As in the general population, the distribution of keratinocytic carcinomas (basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas) is predominantly on sun-exposed sites, while that of melanomas is more uniformly spread over the body {8053698}.

Eyes: Ocular findings are limited to the anterior (UV radiation–exposed) structures. Photophobia may be associated with prominent conjunctival redness. UV irradiation of the eye may result in severe keratitis, leading to corneal opacification and vascularization. Patients may have loss of eyelashes, ectropion, entropion, or complete loss of the lids. Benign conjunctival inflammatory masses have been reported {23601806}.

Nervous system: Neurological abnormalities have been reported in approximately 25% of cases {21097776}. Onset may occur early in infancy (de Sanctis–Cacchione syndrome) or may be delayed until the second decade of life. An adult-onset form of neurodegeneration has been reported {29892709}. The neurological abnormalities may initially be mild (e.g. isolated hyporeflexia), but often lead to progressive sensorineural deafness (beginning with high-frequency hearing loss) {23365097}, mental retardation, microcephaly, spasticity, and/or seizures. In one study, the predominant neuropathological abnormality found at autopsy was loss of neurons, particularly in the cerebrum and cerebellum {24252196}.

Cancer: Among XP patients aged < 20 years, the risk of skin cancer (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma) in sun-exposed sites, including the anterior eye and the tip of the tongue, is > 1000 times the risk in the general population {21097776;21482201}. Multiple primary skin cancers are common. The reported median age of onset of non-melanoma skin cancer in XP in the USA is 9 years, i.e. 50 years earlier than in the general population.

Thanks to better photoprotection and therapeutic education, XP patients live longer now than in the past, but they appear to be at higher risk for development of internal (non-skin) cancers. The risks of CNS tumours (glioma, medulloblastoma, and astrocytoma) {21097776;3545087;9479081;10209947;35246173}, myelodysplasia and acute myeloid leukaemia {3545087;35246173;23278166;30914417;31439674}, gynaecological tumours (ovarian and uterine) {35246173;23278166}, and thyroid tumours {35246173;23278166;33155181} are several hundred times higher than in the general population and these tumours appear 50 years earlier {35246173}. The highest risk is for young (0–20 years) patients with XP (essentially XPC) with an odds ratio from 500 to 1300 for the American and the French cohorts, respectively {35246173}. The internal tumours in XP patients are caused by a very high level of mutation burden probably due to unrepaired endogenous purine DNA lesions {33203900}, in contrast to their UV-induced skin cancers, which are caused by pyrimidine DNA lesions {11289118}.

Early prevention and detection are necessary to protect these patients from lethal internal tumours.

Epidemiology

The incidence of XP is about 1 case per 1 million live births in Europe {18329345} and the USA, 1 per 22 000 in Japan {16905156}, and 1 per 5000 in the Comorian archipelago {21482201}. Patients (of all races) have been reported worldwide.

Etiology

XP has been associated with pathogenic variants in seven nucleotide excision repair genes – XPA, ERCC3 (XPB), XPC, ERCC2 (XPD), DDB2 (XPE), ERCC4 (XPF), and ERCC5 (XPG) {22217736} – and the POLH gene, coding the translesion DNA polymerase POLH (XP variant) {24130121}.

Pathogenesis

XPC and DDB2 (XPE) code for proteins that recognize bulky DNA lesions produced by UV radiation and some other DNA-damaging agents. Patients with XPC and XPE are proficient in transcription-coupled repair and deficient in global genome repair {19023283}, probably explaining their absence of neurological disorders. ERCC3 (XPB) and ERCC2 (XPD) are helicases necessary to open the double helix at the site of the lesion. XPA is able to confirm the position of the lesion on the damaged DNA strand, and ERCC4 (XPF) and ERCC5 (XPG) are endonucleases that cut the damaged strand at the 5′ and 3′ sites, respectively. Patients with XPA, XPB, XPD, and XPF and some with XPG are deficient in both transcription-coupled repair and global genome repair {19023283}. There is marked clinical and molecular heterogeneity in XP. Patients with XPC, XPE, or XPV do not have neurological involvement, whereas patients with XPA, XPB, and XPD and some with XPG may develop neurological abnormalities in addition to skin involvement. Patients with XPD may have one of at least five known clinical phenotypes: XP with skin disease, XP with neurological disease, XP–Cockayne syndrome complex, trichothiodystrophy (a disorder characterized by sulfur-deficient brittle hair {19681155}), and XP–trichothiodystrophy complex {22217736}.

Histopathology

Skin and other cancers occurring in XP are the same morphologically as they are in the general population.

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: XP is diagnosed based on clinical abnormalities of the skin and eyes as listed above, as well as family history.

Desirable: the clinical suspicion may be confirmed by specialized testing including cellular hypersensitivity to UV radiation and chromosomal breakage studies; DNA sequencing may be used to identify the specific gene complementation group and may require the identification of pathogenic variants of specific genes (DDB2, ERCC1, ERCC2, ERCC3, ERCC4, ERCC5, POLH, XPA, or XPC); cultured skin fibroblasts show hypersensitivity to killing by UV radiation.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Management of XP is based on early diagnosis, lifelong protection from UV radiation exposure, and early detection and treatment of skin neoplasms {24417420}. Adherence to photoprotection has the potential for improvement for at least a third of European XP patients {31183946}. Therapeutic education towards better photoprotection is necessary for patients and their families. Risk for development of internal tumours, such as CNS, thyroid, and gynaecological tumours, and haematological malignancies should be regularly evaluated according to the ethnicity of the patient with XP {35246173}. Gene therapy using corrected XP skin cells is still being investigated in vitro {26255934;24236034;22068429}.
