2.3.1 Hereditary tyrosinaemia type 1 (FAH)

Definition

Hereditary tyrosinaemia type 1 (HT1) is an inborn error of metabolism leading to inefficient breakdown of tyrosine. The disease is caused by germline pathogenic sequence variants in FAH and is associated with increased cancer risk, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma.

MIM numbering

276700 Tyrosinaemia type 1; TYRSN1

ICD-11 coding

None

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

HT1 is a multiorgan disease with its course dominated by hepatic manifestations, such as progressive liver failure, consequences of cirrhosis, and development of hepatic nodular lesions including childhood hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The neoplastic consequences of HT1 are concentrated in the liver {20301688}.

Clinical features

HT1 presents with a variable clinical picture even within affected families {25081276;33858731}. Liver disease predominates and typically manifests within the first 2 years of life. Patients present with progressive liver dysfunction, associated with diarrhoea, vomiting, failure to thrive, anaemia, bleeding, ascites, hepatosplenomegaly, hypoproteinaemia, and hypoglycaemia. Most children also have renal manifestations, such as tubular defects, glomerulosclerosis, or interstitial nephritis. Neurological involvement may progress to paralysis, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy may occur. Hyperplasia of pancreatic Langerhans islets has been reported, but there is no evidence of an increased frequency of neuroendocrine neoplasia. Abnormal liver imaging is present in about 75% of patients by ultrasound {25081276}.

Before the advent of nitisinone (2-[2-nitro-4-trifluoromethylbenzoyl]-1,3-cyclohexanedione, or NTBC) treatment, about 40% of patients were reported to develop HCC and 75% of explant livers from transplanted patients with HT1 were shown to carry HCC {173827;24852359}. However, HCC is rarely observed at first clinical presentation.

The treatment of choice is currently nitisinone, which inhibits 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, an upstream enzyme of the tyrosine degradation pathway, and thus blocks the accumulation of toxic metabolites. Early treatment with nitisinone, accompanied by dietary measures (low-tyrosine and low-phenylalanine diet), prevents acute and early presentation of HT1 and largely prevents HCC development, although rare cases of HCC after initiation of treatment have been described. Surveillance for HCC is still recommended in treated patients. Nitisinone treatment started in the 1990s, and thus long-term outcomes and cancer incidence of patients with HT1 who have been treated await comprehensive assessment {36295804;35698261}.

Follow-up with ultrasound of the liver every 6 months has been recommended, with MRI reserved for those in whom HCC is suspected {25081276}.

Epidemiology

HT1 is present worldwide with a quoted incidence of 1:125 000 in central Europe {25081276}. Increased presence in the French-Canadian population and in other ethnic populations and regions has been described {28755184;28755185;35536841;25681080}.

Etiology

HT1 is an autosomal recessive disorder due to the deficiency of FAH as a consequence of homozygous or compound heterozygous inactivating germline variants of FAH located at chromosome 15q25.1 {20301688}. A variety of pathogenic variants (> 90) have been described, including missense, nonsense, and frameshift alterations, and splicing defects and deletions {8076937;30954369}. Specific alterations are present at higher frequencies in certain populations owing to a founder effect, such as the NM_000137.4:c.1062+5G>A allele, which was previously known as IVS12+5G>A, that accounts for approximately 90% of all the disease-causing alleles in the French-Canadian population {25681080}.

Pathogenesis

Homozygous or compound heterozygous germline FAH pathogenic variants affecting FAH function lead to inefficient enzymatic degradation of fumarylacetoacetate into fumarate and acetone {27397503;35201733}. Consequently, reactive intermediates and succinylacetone build up. Succinylacetone is secreted into the urine but also inhibits porphobilinogen synthase, which may lead to acute porphyria crisis.

The accumulation of toxic metabolites leads to hepatocellular damage, cholestasis, and subsequently regeneration and fibrosis. HCC is seen as a consequence of severe hepatocellular damage due to the metabolic defect exerted by HT1, as sufficient treatment largely abolishes HCC risk {25081276}. Although frequent chromosomal breakage has been described in cultured fibroblast cells from a patient with HT1 {2156246}, there is currently no evidence for the cancer-initiating potential of HT1 caused by FAH variants independent of their metabolic consequences.

Macroscopic appearance

Liver explantation or resection specimens show various degrees of fibrosis (mostly cirrhosis) with numerous nodules of variable sizes and colours (yellow, tan, green) to be analysed for neoplastic nature and malignant transformation. Liver biopsies will reflect this setting.

Histopathology

Patients with HT1 who do not have adequate treatment with nitisinone will develop variable degrees of fatty change, severe cholestasis, trabecular disarray often with prominent pseudoglandular formation, and progressive perisinusoidal and septal fibrosis, eventually leading to micronodular, macronodular, or mixed cirrhosis {2536631;9507034}. The liver may show numerous nodular proliferations, presenting the spectrum of macroregenerative and dysplastic nodules and HCC. Evaluation and classification of nodular lesions relies on the criteria used in adult liver tumours. Subtle differences and some overlapping features can make it challenging to distinguish between macroregenerative and dysplastic nodules and between dysplastic nodules and well-differentiated HCC. Rare cases of hepatoblastoma have also been reported {20547648}.

The diagnosis of HCC is based on histology demonstrating accepted signs of malignancy (vascular or interstitial invasion, extrahepatic spread, true neocapsule formation, significant cytological or histological atypia including trabecular disarray and reticulin fibre loss). Immunohistochemistry (glutamine synthetase [GS], glypican-3 [GPC3], HSP70) and molecular pathology (thymic epithelial tumour promoter variants, other oncogenic variants) may support the diagnosis of HCC [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Paediatric tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2022. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 7). https://publications.iarc.who.int/608. Bookref]].

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

While not essential for clinical management, demonstration of FAH pathogenic variants by DNA sequence analysis may be helpful in some patients and their family members, particularly for atypical presentations, prenatal diagnosis, and reproductive counselling {27397503;35201733}. Targeted analysis for high-frequency variants in FAH in specific populations can also be considered, such as for individuals of French-Canadian ancestry. A US and Canadian consensus group concluded that molecular testing for FAH should be performed for confirmation of HT1, although treatment should not be delayed while waiting for the results {28771246}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: abnormal liver and kidney function, with elevated levels of succinylacetone in urine and/or blood; the diagnosis of HT1 is made by prenatal or neonatal diagnostic testing using blood or urine based on symptomatic presentation.

Desirable: variant analysis as a diagnostic tool is not routinely performed, except in cases where prenatal diagnosis is planned.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to HT1-induced HCC.

Prognosis and prediction

Some countries have newborn screening programmes in place for tyrosinaemia type 1 {35201733;36731915}, allowing for early treatment with nitisinone, which has greatly improved prognosis and symptoms {31667718;36295804}. Clinical outcomes are better the earlier treatment is initiated. Nitisinone treatment greatly reduces the risk and incidence of HT1-induced HCC, but rarely cases can occur even with nitisinone treatment {35698261}. Nitisinone treatment started in the 1990s, and thus data on long-term outcomes and cancer are still sparse, though it seems that the protection afforded by this treatment is not complete in all cases {31667718;35698261}. Delay in initiating nitisinone treatment and persistently abnormal AFP levels are recognized risk factors for late-onset HCC {35698261}. For early detection and assessment of nodular hepatic lesions, surveillance is recommended, even with successful nitisinone therapy, and the prognosis for HCC depends on the potential for surgical measures (i.e. partial hepatectomy, transplantation).

There is currently no molecular predictive diagnosis applicable to cases of HT1-induced HCC. Broad molecular testing may be indicated in selected cases of unresectable HCC to evaluate individualized systemic treatment approaches.
