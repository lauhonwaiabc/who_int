4.1.1 Lynch syndrome (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6)

Definition

Lynch syndrome (LS) is an autosomal dominant syndrome caused by a germline (constitutional) pathogenic variant affecting one of the four DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) and characterized by a high predisposition to a wide spectrum of cancers.

Related terminology

Not recommended: cancer family syndrome {5088221}; hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer.

Subtype(s)

MLH1 Lynch syndrome; MSH2 Lynch syndrome; MSH6 Lynch syndrome; PMS2 Lynch syndrome; Muir–Torre syndrome (MIM 158320) {7224741}; patients with Lynch syndrome due to co-inheritance of pathogenic variants affecting more than one MMR gene, for example MLH1 and MSH6, are covered under multilocus inherited neoplasia alleles syndrome {26659639}

Localization

Depending on which DNA MMR gene is involved, cancers occurring in LS can arise in the colon, rectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, renal pelvis and/or ureter, bladder, kidney, ovary, brain, or prostate. Sarcomas are also probably part of the LS spectrum {31310679;30918358;32783184;32659967;34798988}.

Clinical features

LS is characterized by predisposition to a wide variety of cancers. Tumours occurring in this setting can develop at any age, but often arise in young people. Some individuals with LS develop multiple tumours; others may develop no tumours at all. Family history alone has poor predictive value (both positive and negative), but, nonetheless, can be helpful and should be sought. Cases of LS due to de novo germline pathogenic variants are well described, as are LS mosaicism cases {32678338;23112559}.

The cancers that are due to LS include tumours of the colorectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, ovary, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, urinary tract (renal pelvis, ureter, and bladder), kidney, brain, and prostate, as well as sebaceous skin tumours. As detailed in #37373Table 5.01 and #37374Table 5.02, each of the four MMR genes, when affected by pathogenic variants, result in different cumulative incidences of cancers in different organs, varying by age and sex. Individuals with likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants of the MSH2 gene (path_MSH2) listed in the International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours (InSiGHT) database (http://insight-database.org/) – corresponding with likely pathogenic and pathogenic variants in ClinVar (the Clinical Genome Resource), as defined by the ClinGen Variant Classification Expert Panel (VCEP) – may contract any of the cancers described and have the highest incidence of cancer (#37373Table 5.01; #37374Table 5.02; and #37379Table 5.03) [[Nakken S, Hovig E, Møller P, editors. Prospective Lynch Syndrome Database (Internet). Edinburgh (UK): European Hereditary Tumour Group & Middlesex (UK): International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours; 2018. Available from https://www.lscarisk.org. Bookref]] {26657901;27261338;28754778;29046738}. After pathogenic MSH2 variants, the risk of cancer is next highest with pathogenic variants of MLH1, somewhat lower (and with later onset) when MSH6 is affected, and lower still with PMS2 variants. Patients with LS can develop any cancer, which may or may not be due to their LS.

Muir–Torre syndrome is the co-occurrence of sebaceous skin tumours (sebaceous adenoma, sebaceoma, sebaceous carcinoma, or keratoacanthoma) with any internal cancer {7601953}. Many patients with LS have such skin tumours {29542113} and therefore may be diagnosed with Muir–Torre syndrome, but not all patients with Muir–Torre syndrome have LS. Sebaceous skin tumours have been reported in some patients with MUTYH-associated polyposis due to recessive MUTYH variants (see section Muir–Torre syndrome).

Individuals with LS can and do develop tumours, including cancers, at sites other than those listed above, such as breast and thyroid, but this is not to say that LS has caused these to develop; they are no more common than in the general population. Rather they develop sporadically and, because of the individual having a pathogenic variant affecting an MMR gene, they are liable to lose MMR. Heterozygous pathogenic variants in the MMR genes, in particular MSH2, are found significantly enriched (OR 7.33, 95% CI 3.64–14.82, p > 0.001) in children/adolescents with cancers and hence contribute, albeit with reduced penetrance, to cancer risk in children/adolescents {35980168}. Individuals with LS do not develop large numbers of colorectal adenomas, unless they have some other predisposing condition {26659639}.

Individuals who inherit from each parent an MMR pathogenic variant affecting the same MMR gene have constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD) due to biallelic MMR gene variants (see section Constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome). They may develop multiple adenomas and at a very young age {26729549} and a broad spectrum of malignancies, predominantly haematological malignancies and brain tumours in addition to colorectal cancer (CRC) and other LS-associated cancers. Most patients with CMMRD also have neurofibromatosis type 1–like skin features (mainly café-au-lait macules) and/or a wide variety of other non-malignant features {24737826;30166433}. Turcot described a syndrome of multiple colorectal adenomas and brain cancer {7661930;13839882}, but most cases are actually due to CMMRD, and therefore Turcot syndrome is a clinically defined subtype of CMMRD. However, clinically similar cases due to familial adenomatous polyposis, caused by inherited APC variants, have also been reported as Turcot syndrome.

Although Warthin was the first to describe what is now known as LS, Lynch redescribed it in 1966, calling it “the cancer family syndrome”, and it was also redescribed by Boland [[Boland CR. Cancer family syndrome: A case report and literature review. Am J Dig Dis. 1978;23(5):S25–7. doi:10.1007/BF01072482. Bookref]] {3931868;5088221}. Before the genes responsible for LS were identified and before the full clinical spectrum of what is now called Lynch syndrome became apparent, families for genetic linkage studies were identified using the Amsterdam criteria. The term “hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer” was then coined as an umbrella term to help in the education of clinicians {14574154}, and the Bethesda guidelines for selection of tumours for diagnostic testing were developed. Both the Amsterdam criteria and the Bethesda guidelines suffer from poor sensitivity. The Amsterdam criteria suffer from overly stringent specificity (excluding many individuals from testing), and the Bethesda guidelines suffer from nonspecificity at the cost of sensitivity. Later, the term “hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer” was no longer recommended (because it was inaccurate and confusing), and LS was defined as being caused by a heterozygous germline pathogenic variant affecting an MMR gene {23408351}.

Epidemiology

Systematic testing of CRC cases shows that LS is the cause of CRC in approximately 1 in 30 cases (see #37373Table 5.01). From the weighted mean extrapolated penetrance to age 85 years of LS as CRC (0.25) and country-specific lifetime risks of CRC, the prevalence of LS in the general population can be estimated to be approximately 1 in 140 (95% CI: 1 in 95–200), which overlaps with the total prevalence estimate of Win et al., which was 1 in 279 (95% CI: 1 in 192–403) {27799157;28135145;25244061;28754778}.

Founder pathogenic variants causing LS have been found in many populations; for example, Finland has a higher prevalence of LS as a result of this effect {7584997;28466842;17039269;29288294;28369810}. In addition, certain variants are relatively more common owing to recurrent mutation at hotspots {10978353}.

Etiology

The primary cause of LS is a germline pathogenic variant affecting an MMR gene (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2). Some individuals with LS have alterations involving adjacent genes that affect or extend into an MMR gene; for example, 3′-end deletions in EPCAM affecting MSH2 or a deletion in LRRFIP2 due to a paracentric inversion affecting MLH1 {23408351;23264089;21309036;21712435}. Some individuals may have epigenetic (DNA methylation) variants affecting MLH1 or MSH2, some of which may be caused by rearrangements involving adjacent genes {19564652;20473912;23264089;21309036;21712435}.

InSiGHT curates a public database of MMR genes [[International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours Database (Internet). Leiden (Netherlands): Leiden University Medical Center; 2018. Available from: https://www.insight-database.org/. Bookref]], interprets genetic variants according to published criteria, and is recognized by ClinGen and the Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) as the sole definitive worldwide resource {24362816} [[International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours (Internet). Middlesex (UK): International Society for Gastrointestinal Hereditary Tumours; 2018. MMR gene variant classification criteria. Available from: https://www.insight-group.org/criteria/. Bookref]].

Importantly, genotype is linked to phenotype as the Prospective Lynch Syndromes Database (PLSD) only includes individuals who have a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant as defined by the Variant Interpretation Committee (VIC) / VCEP. Although other variant interpretation methods are in use, these are not recommended as they may lead to disparity in interpretation between different laboratories/countries, leading to clinical confusion and diminished patient safety.

A number of environmental and lifestyle factors have been identified as modifiers of LS. Cigarette smoking, increased body mass index, and alcohol consumption are associated with increased risk, whereas acetylsalicylic acid, ibuprofen, multivitamin and calcium supplements, hormone replacement therapy (but not oral contraceptive pill use), and increasing parity in women are associated with a reduction of cancer risk in LS {34543445;20145170;21559014;26282643;26109217}.

Pathogenesis

Cells do not lose MMR function unless both alleles of a given MMR gene are inactivated. Therefore, it is not until a normal cell in a person with LS acquires a somatic hit in the corresponding normal MMR allele that that cell becomes MMR deficient (dMMR). This deficiency has several important consequences. Firstly, dMMR cells escape normal control of apoptosis (some DNA mismatches can stimulate apoptosis) and gain a relative growth advantage, although this may be dependent on subsequent mutation of other genes {10097137;20102395}. Secondly, dMMR leads to an increase in the point mutation rate, especially within repetitive stretches of DNA known as microsatellites {25707793}; this manifests as microsatellite instability (MSI) {20102395;30157243}. Thirdly, dMMR usually leads to abnormal MMR protein expression, which is identifiable by immunohistochemistry {31310679;33829541}.

In the normal-appearing colonic mucosa of people with LS, dMMR is present in approximately 1 crypt per 1 cm2 (i.e. in ~10 000 crypts per individual) (similarly, dMMR endometrial glands are seen in LS patients) {26960970}. These colorectal crypts can lead to immediately invasive lesions with acquired variants in CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin, the signalling intermediate that activates the WNT pathway, as does inactivation of APC), and with flat rather than polypoid morphology; a proportion of such flat lesions evolving into CRC tend to occur more in patients with MLH1 variants, as CTNNB1 is near MLH1 at chromosome 3p22.1 so loss of heterozygosity can delete both genes together. Such flat neoplasms are thought to account for the interval cancers that occur between seemingly normal colonoscopies. Patients with LS can undoubtedly develop CRC also from classic (proficient MMR) WNT pathway–activated adenomas, but dMMR crypts can also acquire secondary APC pathogenic variants and become adenomatous and polypoid. Therefore, there are at least three pathways to CRC in LS: (1) dMMR crypts can give rise to flat lesions with CTNNB1 (rather than APC) and TP53 mutation, which develop into flat cancers; (2) dMMR crypts acquire secondary APC mutations and turn into adenomatous polypoid neoplasms; and (3) patients with LS can develop polypoid adenomas with primary APC mutation (as in the normal population), and these secondarily acquire MMR deficiency during progression (see #37527Fig. 5.XXX) {32141610;30027543;29424427}. It is important to note that rectal cancers with dMMR are usually due to LS, even though they do not harbour CTNNB1 pathogenic variants, suggesting that the pathway only occurs in the colon {10533476;27432916;11454429}.

A substantial proportion (~15%) of non-LS colonic cancers have MMR deficiency. Most are due to sporadic somatic biallelic hypermethylation of the MLH1 gene promoter, as part of the right-sided serrated pathway {21875327}. These tumours usually (in ~80–85% of cases) acquire the specific BRAF oncogene sequence variant NM_004333.6:c.1799T>A p.(Val600Glu), and tests for either BRAF mutation or MLH1 promoter hypermethylation can be used to distinguish them from LS CRCs. Sporadic CRCs may arise in LS patients along this other (MLH1 hypermethylation) pathway. The proportion of CRCs with sporadic MMR deficiency is age dependent (see #37373Table 5.01 and #37374Table 5.02) {25081898;22081473}. Some sporadic CRCs with MMR deficiency (MSI and/or abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry), previously regarded as “unexplained dMMR” if no germline MMR gene variant is found, are in fact due to double somatic MMR gene mutations {29723603;30877237}. In a proportion of these cases, this may be due to another predisposition to CRC from a hereditary condition affecting DNA repair, such as MUTYH-associated polyposis, or polymerase proofreading–associated polyposis caused by pathogenic missense variants in POLD1 and POLE {28577310}; because these tumours may have the characteristics of LS and may occur in individuals with personal and family histories consistent with LS, some authors use the term “Lynch-like syndrome” (although some find this term confusing) to describe such cases (see #37378Table 5.04) {29107668;26309352;26319271}.

Recently, recessive inheritance of variants in the MMR gene MSH3 has also been identified as a cause of adenomatous polyposis {27476653}. With increasing gene panel sequence testing or genome sequencing, individuals are also being found with pathogenic variants in more than one MMR gene (called digenic LS) {21195604;26659639}, but it is unclear whether this is more severe than LS due to one MMR variant.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross appearance is related to the tumour type and is not distinctive.

Histopathology

The typical histological features in LS-associated CRC and sporadic CRC with dMMR include the presence of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, Crohn-like peritumoural lymphocytic aggregate reaction, poor differentiation, mucinous and/or signet-ring cell features, or a medullary growth pattern with a pushing edge {11159189;29906313;18283560;23435936}. These features are identified in both sporadic cancers with dMMR and those that occur in the setting of LS. Although these histological findings are commonly seen, they are not specific enough by themselves to distinguish dMMR from MMR-proficient cases.

Limited data are available about non-colorectal LS-associated cancers. LS-associated endometrial cancers include both endometrioid and non-endometrioid subtypes (clear cell, mixed endometrioid and clear cell, undifferentiated, dedifferentiated carcinomas, and carcinosarcoma) {25229768;23765559}. LS-associated ovarian carcinomas tend to be endometrioid, undifferentiated, or clear cell {18469706}. Both the endometrial and ovarian carcinomas may show increased tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, but this feature is also seen in sporadic cancers. Most LS-associated gastric carcinomas are intestinal type {9355980;17267619;19900449}, < 13% are diffuse type, and mucinous carcinomas are very rare. The presence of intraepithelial lymphocytes is not described. LS-associated small bowel carcinomas {27258561} show frequently mucinous, signet-ring cell, or medullary differentiation, often in combination with tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and Crohn-like reaction, similar to ampullary carcinomas {27829276}. Other types of LS-associated biliary carcinomas do not have any distinguishing features {27829276}. Pancreatic carcinomas associated with LS can be either acinar cell {25058881} or medullary {10793075}.

Reflex testing of all patients with CRC and endometrial cancer (and requested testing of selected other types of LS-associated cancer) is recommended by a large number of professional organizations, based on either the presence of MSI or abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry in the tumour {31056702}. There is no consensus as to whether MMR immunohistochemistry or MSI testing is the preferred first test, and they can be used in combination (see #54004Fig. 5.XXX and #37380Table 5.05) [[National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Molecular testing strategies for Lynch syndrome in people with colorectal cancer: diagnostics guidance (DG27). London (UK): NICE; 2017 Feb. Available from: https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/dg27. Bookref]] {25244061;28895526;25081898}. In cases with a low tumour cell percentage or intense inflammatory reaction, MMR immunohistochemistry is the better option. Subsequent determination of MLH1 promoter hypermethylation can be applied to estimate the risk of sporadic CRC versus germline variants in patients. BRAF mutation analysis in colonic cancer (not rectal or endometrial cancer) is an alternative to MLH1 promoter hypermethylation testing. The use of larger targeted gene panels that combine microsatellite testing and mutation analyses is under investigation.

Immunohistochemistry for the MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6) is a common first step to screen CRCs and endometrial cancers for MMR deficiency. The nuclear presence of all four proteins suggests MMR proficiency. Loss or abnormality of nuclear staining for any of the MMR proteins indicates dMMR and suggests the most likely involved gene and the need for additional testing. Loss/abnormality of MSH2 alone or loss/abnormality of both MSH2 and MSH6 suggests a variant in MSH2; similarly, loss/abnormality of MLH1 alone or loss/abnormality of both MLH1 and PMS2 suggests an underlying variant or promoter hypermethylation in MLH1 (see #37380Table 5.05). Concomitant loss/abnormality of both MSH2 and MSH6 (or of both MLH1 and PMS2) reflects the heterodimeric binding of MSH2 with MSH6 (or of MLH1 with PMS2) in MMR complexes, such that loss of the first partner leads to relative instability and loss of the second {18157157}. Isolated loss/abnormality of MSH6 or PMS2 suggests a variant in MSH6 or PMS2, but may occasionally be caused also by missense mutation of the heterodimerization partner MSH2 or MLH1. The typical expression pattern of MMR proteins includes diffuse staining in the tumour nuclei and many non-neoplastic cells, including adjacent normal epithelial and stromal cells and lymphocytes (that act as internal controls for good tissue fixation) (see #54004Fig. 5.XXX and #37380Table 5.05). The example illustrated shows a tumour with loss of nuclear expression of MLH1 and PMS2 and intact staining for MSH2 and MSH6. This pattern could be seen either in a sporadic tumour (most commonly due to hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter) or in the setting of LS.

The interpretation of MMR protein immunohistochemistry is typically straightforward, but it should always be performed with adequate internal control staining. Some pitfalls and unusual patterns of expression can occur, but awareness will prevent misinterpretation. Patchy intact staining can occur owing to uneven antibody diffusion, variable fixation, or tissue hypoxia {12055083;12697826}. Cytoplasmic staining may occur, but it is considered abnormal and therefore deficient, because there is no staining in the nuclei {27896849}. Weak, patchy, nucleolar, or even absent MSH6 expression has been reported in a substantial number of rectal tumours after neoadjuvant treatment without MSI or MMR mutation confirmed by molecular testing {21107085;21334712}. Clonal loss of MSH6 expression is often due to a secondary (somatically acquired) mutation in the MSH6 coding mononucleotide tract {26099011;22918162}. Approximately 3–10% of LS tumours that have MMR deficiency with MSI show no abnormality on MMR immunohistochemistry (presumably because of pathogenic variants that disable protein function but leave the protein detectable by immunohistochemistry) {22086678}. Whatever method is used, external quality assurance is required to ensure accuracy of diagnosis {25012948;26752307;30856687}.

In individuals with CMMRD, expression loss of the inactivated MMR gene is usually not restricted to neoplastic cells, but is seen also in the surrounding normal mucosa cells as well as in tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes. This should be taken into account when testing for mismatch repair deficiency in paediatric or young adult patients with cancer and should not be interpreted as a failure of proper staining. Another peculiarity of CMMRD-associated brain but also other tumours is that MSI testing using the techniques developed for LS often will fail to reveal MSI in these tumours {21204794;12549480;17440981;33355208}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

LS results from autosomal dominant inheritance of a germline variant (or de novo mutation) affecting one of four DNA MMR genes: MSH2 (2p21-p16.3), MLH1 (3p22.2), MSH6 (2p16.3, only 300 kb from MSH2), and PMS2 (7p22.1) {9499183}. The spectrum of pathogenic MMR gene variants is very broad and includes shortened, missense, and splice variants (the latter at and outside the invariant dinucleotides of canonical splice sites), small in-frame and out-of-frame deletions and insertions, variants affecting the start/stop codons, intragenic copy-number variants (deletions or insertions of one or more exons), whole gene deletions, and complex genomic rearrangements that may affect other genes, such as a paracentric inversion including LRRFIP2 and MLH1 {21712435}. Epigenetic silencing of MSH2 and MLH1 can also be the cause of LS in some patients. MSH2 silencing mainly results from 3′-end deletions of the adjacent EPCAM (TACSTD1) gene, located upstream of MSH2, leading to EPCAM stop-codon loss, transcriptional read-through into the MSH2 gene, and subsequent hypermethylation of the MSH2 promoter in EPCAM-expressing tissues {23264089;21309036}. Patients with deletions of the 3′ (terminal) end of EPCAM that do not extend into MSH2 are at risk of gastrointestinal cancers but seemingly not of the non-gastrointestinal cancers characteristic of LS {21145788;21769135;23264089}. In contrast, patients with EPCAM deletions that extend into MSH2 have a phenotype indistinguishable from that of patients with pathogenic variants in MSH2. Germline hypermethylation of the MLH1 promoter, characterized by soma-wide allele-specific MLH1 promoter hypermethylation and transcriptional silencing, can also be a cause of LS, but this is, with very few reported exceptions, not heritable {19564652;20473912}.

Defective MMR in a tumour prevents recognition and repair of both single mispaired bases and insertions or deletions that occur during DNA replication within repetitive DNA sequences {10369691}. This latter consequence can be detected as MSI, which is defined as the presence of extra alleles at a microsatellite compared with normal DNA (from normal tissue or blood) from the same individual {10369691}. Microsatellites vary in their propensity to show instability, and therefore the frequency with which the same microsatellite is affected in different tumour types varies. Instability is best observed at mononucleotide repeats (e.g. AAAAAAAAA…), with this being more substantial than at dinucleotide repeats (e.g. CACACACA…) {30376427}. Like any laboratory test, MSI tests using a limited set (five or more) of microsatellite markers balance sensitivity against specificity {32791559}. Current markers used in colonic cancer MSI diagnosis are known to have reduced sensitivity at detecting MSI in non-colonic tumours, such as endometrial, ovarian, small bowel, or gastric cancers, and in tumours from patients with MSH6 mutation {30376427}. Therefore, a small number of LS-associated tumours may not appear to have MSI using this test (although they may be recognized by abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry).

Defective MMR generates specific dMMR-associated tumour mutation signatures (SBS6, SBS15, SBS21, SBS26, and SBS44), whereas combined POLE proofreading-defective variants together with dMMR generates mutation signature SBS14, and POLD1 proofreading-defective variants with dMMR generates mutation signature SBS20 in tumours {33513945;35197584;32025018;32025007}.

It is important to note that dMMR tumours do not always have abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry or test positive for MSI {10369691}. MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI testing of carcinomas at sites other than the colon, or due to germline variants in MSH6 or PMS2, or of adenomas has reduced sensitivity {31695176}. MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI tests must be interpreted taking into account the tumour type, the patient’s personal and family history, and other test results (e.g. BRAF status or MLH1 promoter hypermethylation status); a multidisciplinary approach is recommended.

The value of MMR immunohistochemistry and MSI testing is considerably enhanced by testing more than one tumour from the same family and/or individual, especially if the tumours are uncommon or rare (e.g. rectal cancers, colorectal adenomas, small bowel cancers, sebaceous skin tumours, and others) {30004261;34750021;31695176}. Consistent immunohistochemical abnormality of one MMR protein in more than one tumour is excellent evidence for the pathogenicity of a variant in that gene, which is important given the number of missense and other difficult-to-interpret variants that occur in LS {33829541;32917768}. MSI in rectal cancers is rare and strongly associated with LS; this can be exploited clinically because the finding therefore has an excellent positive predictive value for LS, and negative predictive value when not present. Similarly, dMMR is rare in adenomas outside of LS, providing good positive and negative predictive values {10595914}. Some CRCs due to MUTYH-associated polyposis or polymerase proofreading–associated polyposis may exhibit both MSI and abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry due to somatic MMR gene mutation; they therefore appear to be due to LS, but are “Lynch-like syndrome” cases (see #37378Table 5.04).

About 15% of sporadic colonic cancers have dMMR, shown by MSI and abnormal MMR immunohistochemistry, usually due to epigenetic silencing of MLH1 by promoter hypermethylation at both alleles. Therefore, unselected colonic cancers with MSI have a poor positive predictive value for LS, although lack of MSI has a good negative predictive value for LS. Because sporadic MLH1 promoter hypermethylation in colonic cancers is strongly age dependent, age is a useful discriminator in its interpretation (see #37373Table 5.01 #37374Table 5.02 and #37379Table 5.03). Further tests (BRAF mutation and MLH1 hypermethylation tests) are required for dMMR colonic cancers with loss of MLH1 on immunohistochemistry, to distinguish between LS and sporadic origin.

Somatic mutation of BRAF, usually the activating missense p.Val600Glu (or sometimes other codon 600 variants), occurs in about 80–85% of sporadic colonic cancers with dMMR, but not in those due to LS; therefore, such variants are highly predictive of the tumour being of sporadic origin and not due to LS. However, approximately 15–20% of sporadic colonic cancers do not harbour a BRAF variant, so the absence of any BRAF p.Val600 variant does not definitively diagnose LS, although it does indicate that LS is more likely. It should be noted that BRAF testing is of no use in tumours other than colonic cancers. Alternatively, detection of MLH1 gene promoter hypermethylation in colonic and endometrial tumours provides good, although not unequivocal, evidence that the tumour is sporadic in origin, for two reasons: (1) very occasional sporadic tumours do occur in LS, and (2) germline MLH1 promoter methylation can be found in a few patients with LS {19564652}. A good practice for testing of MLH1 promoter hypermethylation in tumours is to include testing of the patient’s normal DNA (e.g. from normal colonic mucosal tissue far away from the resected cancer to avoid nearby field effect MLH1 promoter methylation, or from blood), then acquired MLH1 promoter hypermethylation will be readily apparent when it occurs.

LS can be definitively diagnosed after tumour testing by germline MMR gene sequence analysis to identify the pathogenic germline variant. It can be useful to have samples from more than one individual in the family, because case segregation studies may be required in order to determine pathogenicity or whether an individual is a phenocopy. It may also assure the suspected LS diagnosis and call for more extensive analyses in families with LS in whom a pathogenic MMR gene variant escapes initial detection owing to its nature (e.g. deep-intronic pathogenic variant, complex genomic rearrangements) when using standard molecular genetic methods such as massively parallel sequencing (next-generation sequencing). In cases with very young onset (e.g. < 35 years), LS is likely even in the absence of a family history, although pathogenic variants in genes (e.g. BUB1 and BUB3) encoding mitotic spindle checkpoint proteins should be considered, especially in teenage patients. Tumours from such individuals do not exhibit MSI, nor do those (usually) from families with specific POLD1 and POLE proofreading pathogenic variants (in polymerase proofreading–associated polyposis), although the family histories may be Lynch-like. Rare, characteristic tumours, such as small bowel cancer, ureteric transitional cell carcinoma, or skin sebaceous adenoma/carcinoma, have a high positive predictive value for LS and are therefore clinically significant. Synchronous or metachronous bowel cancers are also clinically significant, as is the development of any two LS-related tumours (e.g. CRC and endometrial cancer).

As tumour sequencing becomes more widespread, data on specific pathogenic variants in cancer-associated genes, as well as mutation signatures and repetitive microsatellite sequence data, are becoming available in individual cases {31530574}. This will both guide treatment and diagnose more causes of hereditary predisposition, including both LS and “Lynch-like syndrome”, as well as multilocus inherited neoplasia alleles syndrome (multiple germline variants in more than one cancer-predisposing gene in the same patient) {26659639}. It is also likely that pathogenic variant spectra and/or signatures, being more sensitive and specific, will replace MSI testing. Although gene panel testing may remove the need for MMR immunohistochemistry as a way of selecting which genes to test, MMR immunohistochemistry will remain critically important in providing phenotypic data to enable the interpretation of genetic variants {26659639;23945592}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: identification of a germline monoallelic pathogenic variant affecting one of the four MMR genes (MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, MLH1) confirms a diagnosis of LS; this includes, for example, germline pathogenic deletions of the 3′ end of EPCAM associated with inactivation of the neighbouring MSH2 gene (or LRRFIP2 deletions/rearrangements with inactivation of the neighbouring MLH1).

Desirable: studies of a manifest tumour showing loss or abnormal expression of the MMR protein corresponding to the germline pathogenic variant on MMR immunohistochemistry, and high MSI on MSI testing (by PCR or next-generation sequencing), without evidence of sporadic events (hypermethylation, somatic mutation); increasing use of mutation spectra/signatures that indicate dMMR is likely; a suggested algorithm for CRC tumour testing for LS is shown in #54004Fig. 5.XXX.

Staging

Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) / American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging is applicable to individual malignant tumours.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis and prediction are as for sporadic counterparts (see #37374Table 5.02).

MMR deficiency causes frequent insertion and deletion mutation events. When these occur in the protein-coding regions of genes, they result in frameshifts, which in turn result in the expression of novel, antigenic frameshift peptides that stimulate the immune system {19957108;30027543}. Local immune suppression in such tumours enables their survival; therefore, immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD1/PDL1 blockade, which inhibit such suppression, are effective in dMMR tumours {26028255;34477921}.

Note: “Lynch-like syndrome (LLS)” is a term used to describe tumours with mismatch repair (MMR) deficiency (and therefore microsatellite instability and/or abnormal immunohistochemistry) due to somatic mutation of both alleles of the same MMR gene, occurring in the setting of other features suggestive of Lynch syndrome (LS), such as young age of onset, other LS-associated tumour types, and a related family history; patients with LLS may have heritable variants in other (often DNA repair or maintenance) genes that have predisposed them to somatic MMR gene mutation; these are important to recognize and are listed in the table. Synonyms for LLS include LS mimic, LS-like, and mutation-negative LS. Patients with LLS may represent a heterogeneous group. LLS has a mean age of onset similar to that of LS. At least 50–60% of LLS-associated colorectal cancers exhibit biallelic somatic inactivation of the MMR genes within the tumour through somatic mutations (nonsense, missense, or frameshift variants; splice-site deletions; gene deletions; or loss of heterozygosity). LS tumours may themselves have somatic variants in the same genes in which heritable pathogenic variants cause the syndromes that in turn give rise to LLS. The overarching term “familial colorectal cancer X (FCC-X)” has been used for familial colorectal cancer in which the tumours are microsatellite stable (i.e. do not have MMR deficiency). Mutation of LLS-associated genes can also be the cause of FCC-X. The potential value of multiple gene test panels and tumour DNA sequencing will be apparent.

Associated malignant tumours

Lynch syndrome–associated malignancies include cancers of the colorectum, endometrium, stomach, small bowel, ovary, gallbladder, hepatobiliary tract, pancreas, kidney, brain, prostate, skin (sebaceous carcinoma), urothelial-lined urinary tract including renal pelvis, ureter, and bladder, and probably sarcomas (to be confirmed)

Associated benign tumours

Colorectal adenomas, gastric and duodenal / small bowel adenomas and other polyps, urothelial papillomas of the urinary tract, cutaneous lesions (sebaceous adenomas and sebaceomas, but not sebaceous hyperplasia) and keratoacanthomas

Cancer-related inheritance

Autosomal dominant (with reduced penetrance)

Chromosome location

Germline pathogenic variants affecting a DNA mismatch repair gene:

MSH2 (2p21)

MLH1 (3p22.2)

MSH6 (2p16.3, only 300 kb from MSH2)

PMS2 (7p22.1)

Some individuals have substitution variants / deletions / inversions involving adjacent genes, such as EPCAM (2p21), which affects the neighbouring MSH2 gene, or LRRFIP2 (3p22.2), which affects neighbouring MLH1
