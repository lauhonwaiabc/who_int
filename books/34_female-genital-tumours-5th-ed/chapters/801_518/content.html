8.0.1 Tumours of the uterine cervix: Introduction

According to estimates published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 570 000 cases of cervical cancer and 311 000 deaths from the disease occurred in 2018. Cervical cancer was the fourth most common cancer in women globally (after breast, colorectal, and lung cancers) and one of the top three cancers in women aged < 45 years in 145 countries worldwide. The estimated age-standardized incidence rates vary considerably, with annual rates ranging from < 4 cases per 100 000 women in several countries in the Persian Gulf and northern/western Europe to > 40 cases per 100 000 women in several countries in eastern Africa. Cervical cancer was the leading cause of cancer-related death in women in the regions of sub-Saharan Africa (see #16238Fig. #16238) {30207593; 31812369}. China and India together contributed more than a third of the global cervical cancer burden, with 106 000 cases in China and 97 000 cases in India {30207593}.

The WHO global cervical cancer elimination initiative has the ambitious goals of scaling-up HPV vaccination of girls, screening for and treating precancerous lesions, and providing access to diagnosis and treatment of invasive cancers [[WHO. Draft: Global strategy towards eliminating cervical cancer as a public health problem. Geneva (Switzerland): WHO; 2020 Apr 5 [updated 2020 May 11, cited 2020 Jun 23]. Available from: https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/cervical-cancer/cervical-cancer-elimination-strategy-updated-11-may-2020.pdf?sfvrsn=b8690d1a_4]]. Modelling exercises have indicated that high HPV vaccination coverage of girls could have a substantial impact in reducing cervical cancer rates in most low- and middle-income countries over the course of this century, with a high uptake of screening necessary in countries with the highest burden {32007141}.

Cervical epithelial pathology is dominated by HPV infection and its neoplastic consequences, and HPV infection has traditionally been considered necessary for the development of cervical carcinoma {10451482}. However, it has become increasingly recognized that a significant proportion of cervical carcinomas, in particular adenocarcinomas, are not associated with HPV infection. Moreover, as in other anatomical sites such as the oropharynx and the vulva, HPV-independent cervical carcinomas are generally more aggressive than HPV-associated carcinomas, a property that is of increasing clinical importance. This edition of the WHO classification of tumours therefore differs from previous editions by dividing epithelial tumours and their precursors on the basis of their association (or lack thereof) with HPV infection. Importantly, this change produces a classification that will allow more accurate assessment of the role of HPV testing in cervical screening programmes, as well as the role of HPV vaccination.

Almost all cervical squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs) are HPV-associated. However, HPV-independent SCC, although rare, has been described {25675193}. For this reason, and to harmonize the classification across lower genital tract sites, squamous lesions are now subdivided into HPV-associated and HPV-independent categories. HPV-associated and HPV-independent SCCs cannot be reliably distinguished on the basis of morphological criteria alone; p16 immunostaining or HPV testing is required. Although the percentage of HPV-independent SCCs in the cervix is very low, and there is currently no difference in treatment between HPV-associated and HPV-independent tumours, it is recommended that the type of cervical SCC (HPV-associated or HPV-independent) be documented on the pathology report. Nevertheless, a morphological diagnosis without differentiating the two categories is an acceptable alternative where the facilities necessary to make this distinction are not available. There is no evidence that an HPV-independent precursor lesion exists, and squamous intraepithelial lesions are therefore grouped into a single, HPV-associated, category.

Recent comprehensive studies have demonstrated unequivocally that, although the majority of endocervical adenocarcinomas are associated with HPV infection, a significant minority are not {29135516; 29877920}. The new classification recognizes this by separating HPV-associated and HPV-independent adenocarcinomas, dividing the latter into specific gastric, clear cell, mesonephric, and endometrioid types. Adenocarcinoma in situ has similarly been divided into HPV-associated and HPV-independent groups. It is important to stress that the large majority of adenocarcinomas that appear to be endometrioid represent HPV-associated adenocarcinomas with mucin depletion and that these tumours should be classified as HPV-associated on the basis of the essential and desirable criteria listed, such as the typical morphological features and diffuse p16 immunopositivity. True endometrioid carcinoma should be diagnosed only when HPV-associated adenocarcinoma and other mimics (e.g. extension from an endometrial carcinoma) have been rigorously excluded.

Serous carcinoma of the cervix has been removed from the classification because there is no evidence that it occurs as a primary tumour. Adenocarcinoma NOS has also been removed, because pathologists are encouraged to assign all cervical adenocarcinomas to either the HPV-associated or one of the HPV-independent categories.

Adenofibroma is a rare and controversial entity that has been omitted from the current classification. The differential diagnosis predominantly includes benign endometrial or cervical polyps with unusual morphology (focal phyllodes-like architecture, increased stromal cellularity surrounding glands, intraglandular stromal protrusions) on the one hand and adenosarcoma with a low degree of stromal mitotic activity on the other {18941402}. These lesions are much more common than adenofibroma, and a diagnosis of adenofibroma should be made with caution; only in a hysterectomy specimen; and only when stromal cellularity, periglandular cuffing, and mitotic activity are absent {25118811; 26715175; 20179434}.

Another change in this edition is the update of the staging of cervical carcinoma to the FIGO 2018 staging system {30306584; 30656645}. This update contains major changes, including the assessment of stage IA disease on the basis of depth of stromal invasion only, the subdivision of stage IB into three size categories, and the inclusion of lymph node metastasis (identified by imaging or pathology) in stage III.

Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) of the cervix are included in section Neuroendocrine neoplasia, and lymphoid tumours in section Haematolymphoid proliferations and neoplasia. Mixed epithelial and mesenchymal tumours are included in this chapter, but mesenchymal tumours are now included in section Mesenchymal tumours of the lower genital tract, which classifies and discusses mesenchymal tumours of the lower genital tract as a whole. Similarly, melanocytic lesions are included in section Melanocytic lesions, and metastases to the cervix in section Metastasis. section Genetic tumour syndromes of the female genital tract includes tumours of the cervix that are associated with hereditary tumour syndromes, such as gastric-type adenocarcinoma in Peutz–Jeghers syndrome.

Certain mesenchymal tumours are almost always limited to one site and are dealt with in the chapter about the site where they occur most commonly; see section Tumours of the ovary and section Tumours of the uterine corpus. In addition, perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa) and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour have rarely been identified outside the uterus and are therefore dealt with in the chapter on the uterus – see section Perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa) and section Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour.
