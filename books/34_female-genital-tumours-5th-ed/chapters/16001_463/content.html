16.0.0.1 BRCA1/2-associated hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome

Definition

BRCA1/2-associated hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome is an autosomal dominant cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline mutations of the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, with increased risk for breast and ovarian cancers and other cancer types.

Subtype(s)

Localization

BRCA1/2-associated hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome is associated with carcinomas of the breast, ovary, fallopian tube, and peritoneum. Patients may also develop prostatic, pancreatic, and endometrial carcinomas {31492746}, as well as other types of cancer.

Clinical features

The clinical features are related to site-specific disease.

Epidemiology

Approximately 10% of ovarian cancer is associated with inherited germline mutations of the BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene {21324516}. Germline mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 are known to confer a high lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancers {17416853}, as well as of contralateral breast cancer {23628597; 25857409}. The number of women who carry pathogenic BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations is estimated to be 1 in 300–500 {25198398}. BRCA1 mutations markedly increase ovarian / fallopian tube cancer risk (40–60%), with an average patient age of 50–53 years at diagnosis. The risk of ovarian / fallopian tube cancer for individuals with BRCA2 mutations is lower (11–35%), and the age of diagnosis is slightly higher (55–58 years) {17148771}. According to data from the Consortium of Investigators of Modifiers of BRCA1/2 (CIMBA), breast and ovarian cancer risks vary by type and location of BRCA1/2 mutations {25857409}. The incidence rate for breast cancer is 46% (BRCA1) and 52% (BRCA2), and for ovarian cancer 12% (BRCA1) and 6% (BRCA2). The incidence of both cancers in the same patient is 5% (BRCA1) and 2% (BRCA2) {25857409}.

The prevalence of BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations shows substantial variation in terms of mutation type and frequency by geographical region and race/ethnicity. For example, the prevalence of mutations is markedly higher among Ashkenazi Jews (2.5%) than in the general population (0.1%) {9150153; 10739756}. Other geographical locations around the world, such as Norway, the Netherlands, and Iceland, also have a higher prevalence of specific harmful BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations (1.8%) {10995809}. The prevalence of specific harmful BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations may also vary among individual racial and ethnic groups in the USA, including African-American, Hispanic, Asian-American, and non-Hispanic white populations {19841585; 19241424}.

Etiology

Inactivation of the BRCA1/2 genes by germline mutations results in defects in the homologous recombination repair pathway responsible for the accurate repair of DNA breaks using the sister chromatid as a template.

Pathogenesis

BRCA1 is localized to chromosome 17q21 and contains 23 coding exons. It encodes a nuclear protein of 1863 amino acids. A RING finger domain at the N-terminus mediates interactions with other proteins. BRCA1 is often mutated in three domains: the RING finger domain, exons 11–13, and the BRCA1 C-terminal domain {22737296}. BRCA2 maps to chromosome 13q13.1 and consists of 27 coding exons. The BRCA2 protein includes 3418 amino acids. There is an association between mutations in the central part of BRCA2 (the ovarian cluster region) and an increase in the likelihood of ovarian carcinoma. The BRCA1 and BRCA2 proteins share no structural homology with any other protein {21336636}.

BRCA1/2 proteins play a critical role in maintaining genomic stability by promoting error-free DNA repair {16998501}. BRCA1 acts in cell-cycle checkpoint control and by binding with proteins that are involved in the repair of DNA damage, such as RAD51. BRCA2 regulates the function of RAD51-mediated recombination. Functional BRCA proteins are tumour suppressors, maintaining genome stability through repair of double-strand breaks by homologous recombination, cell division control, and cell growth regulation {15546503; 8589730}. Germline BRCA1/2 mutations predispose individuals to a two-hit biallelic inactivation, involving firstly the inherited pathogenic mutation of one BRCA allele and then the somatic inactivation of the second wildtype allele {1303261; 25830658; 31090900}, together with somatic mutation signatures, suggesting functional deficiency in homologous recombination {31090900}. DNA double-strand breaks, when left unrepaired, can result in genomic rearrangements and cell death.

Macroscopic appearance

Not clinically relevant

Histopathology

The most important neoplasm of the female genital tract is high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) of the ovary / fallopian tube / peritoneum. Carcinosarcomas are also associated. Other histological tumour types have been reported in BRCA mutation carriers (e.g. endometrioid, clear cell, and undifferentiated carcinomas), but the relation to biallelic inactivation has not been documented {11112219; 15073127}. A localized serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (or serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma with limited invasion or spread) in the distal fallopian tube is found in 5–10% {31360879} of risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy specimens if the tube is examined using the SEE-FIM (sectioning and extensively examining the fimbriated end) protocol {16434898}. Some BRCA1-associated HGSCs more frequently have SET (solid, endometrial-like, transitional) architectural patterns, high mitotic count, tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, and geographical necrosis or comedonecrosis (see section High-grade serous carcinoma of the ovary). BRCA2-associated HGSC tends to have architectural patterns similar to those of BRCA1-related cases, but less-pronounced tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes and necrosis {24577588; 26574845; 22193042}.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Germline BRCA1/2 mutations are present.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: germline BRCA1/2 mutations.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The 10-year survival of HGSC in carriers is comparable to that of non-carriers {26556769; 25398451}. Survival rates after 5 years decline, approximating those of non-carriers. In addition, mutation carriers have a higher initial sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy. Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors targeting DNA damage repair improve survival in patients with BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations {27908594; 29166829; 25481791}.
