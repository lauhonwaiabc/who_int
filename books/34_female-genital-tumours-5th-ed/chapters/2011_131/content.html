2.0.1.1 Endometriosis and derived tumours

Definition

Endometriosis is the presence of endometrial glandular and stromal elements at extrauterine sites, including the cervix.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

GA10 Endometriosis

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Endometriosis is usually seen in the pelvis, including the peritoneum and adnexae, and less commonly in the bowel (particularly in the rectosigmoid colon), ureter, and caesarean section scar. Endometriosis-related tumours almost always arise in the ovary, with the rectovaginal septum being the most common extraovarian site {30240947; 27992931; 17102045; 31311360; 30363647; 20869701}.

Clinical features

Patients most commonly present with infertility, dysmenorrhoea, pelvic pain, and dyspareunia (can be related to the menstrual cycle), or with symptoms related to adhesions or an abdominal mass (in cases of ovarian endometriotic cysts). Patients with endometriosis-related tumours usually present with abdominal pain or swelling, and serum CA125 may be elevated.

Epidemiology

Endometriosis affects approximately 10% of women of reproductive age {30908874}. Related neoplasms develop in about 1% of patients {24905724; 28718209}. The distribution and prevalence of endometriosis is summarized in #10580Table #10580.

Etiology

Most often, endometriosis results from transtubal displacement of endometrial tissue, with peritoneal implantation and persistence in susceptible women {31479615}; a minority of cases result from traumatic displacement of endometrial tissue (including due to surgery) or vascular dissemination. It has also been proposed that migratory stem cells of endometrial or bone marrow origin may contribute to the development of some endometriotic lesions {26375413}.

Pathogenesis

In patients with endometriosis, the eutopic endometrium shows altered biological features, which may explain the propensity for successful implantation in the peritoneal cavity {21620779}. Immunological dysfunction may also play a permissive role in the development and persistence of endometriotic lesions {30054239}. Most endometriosis-related neoplasms arise in the ovary, and it has been suggested that endometriotic cysts provide a microenvironmental milieu that potentiates neoplastic transformation, including high estrogen and iron levels, cytokine and chemokine production, and immune factors {30087267}. Ovarian and extraovarian endometriosis (particularly atypical endometriosis) can demonstrate molecular alterations typical of neoplasia, including somatic mutations and monoclonality, and the clonal relationship between endometriosis and associated cancers is well established {9563487; 20942669; 28489996; 22976498}. However, recent findings of canonical oncogenic mutations in KRAS, PTEN, PPP2R1A, or ARID1A, as well as upregulation of HNF1β in the epithelium of deep-infiltrating endometriosis and incisional endometriosis (which are rarely associated with neoplasia), and even in histologically normal endometrium, suggest that such changes may be inherent in endometrial tissue and not directly associated with either the development of endometriosis or neoplastic transformation {28489996; 31187483; 30428062; 29604057; 9563487; 20942669; 22976498}. Endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas in the ovary may be associated with Lynch syndrome {29241974}.

Macroscopic appearance

Endometriosis is most often an incidental microscopic finding, but it can form a mass as a result of cystic change, associated muscle hypertrophy, fibrosis, and/or congestion. If the endometriosis is visible, then punctate blue, brown, or red patches or nodules with a puckered surface are characteristic. Ovarian endometriotic cysts are often characterized by altered luminal blood (chocolate cyst). Polypoid endometriosis may present as an exophytic nodule, especially in mucosal sites such as the bowel (closely mimicking a neoplasm), but also at serosal sites {15104291}. Endometriosis-related tumours usually demonstrate solid and/or papillary areas.

Histopathology

Conventional endometriosis resembles normal endometrium, including glandular and stromal elements (in varying proportions), the latter with typical arterioles, often associated with haemorrhage. Both elements can show morphological changes associated with endogenous or exogenous hormonal effect {17592255; 31846535}. The stroma may be subtle (if atrophic) and is sometimes the only component, so-called stromal endometriosis, which often occurs in the peritoneum and less commonly in the cervix {19155237; 26444251; 2327550}. Stromal endometriosis may form serosal nodules (so-called micronodular endometriosis) that may raise concern for an endometrioid stromal sarcoma {11493993}. Associated smooth muscle hypertrophy may impart a circumscribed, leiomyoma-like or adenomyoma-like appearance around ligaments or in the bowel wall, with resulting distortion of the muscularis propria that can lead to obstruction or intussusception. The epithelium can show a range of metaplastic changes (ciliated, mucinous, hobnail, eosinophilic, squamous, and clear cell), sometimes associated with limited tufting {9720509}. So-called pink cells associated with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and smudgy nuclei may be seen and interpreted as reactive. In the appendix, the endometrioid epithelium may be replaced by intestinal-type epithelium, mimicking an appendiceal mucinous neoplasm {24451279}. The stroma can also be associated with a range of changes including myxoid or elastotic alteration, fibrosis, decidualization, associated pigmented histiocytes, bizarre cytological atypia, pseudoxanthomatous nodules, calcification, and (rarely) ossification {17592255}. Decidualization, if florid, especially in pregnant women, may mimic signet-ring cells. Adhesions, peritoneal inclusion cysts, and mesothelial hyperplasia can be seen, with mesothelial hyperplasia sometimes being sufficiently florid to mimic a neoplastic process {21421699; 24901399}. Polypoid endometriosis is characterized by an exophytic growth from a serosal surface or may protrude into a mucosal-lined or cystic cavity. The glandular and stromal elements may have conventional appearances but often closely resemble those of a eutopic endometrial polyp, including cystic glands and collagenous stroma {15104291; 26095917}. Endometriosis can show perineural and perivascular distribution, which may mimic invasion. Immunohistochemistry for CD10 or IFITM1 may be helpful in highlighting subtle lesions {11986349; 32436195}.

Atypical endometriosis has been recorded in 1.7–4.4% of endometriotic lesions, almost always in the ovary, and it has been proposed as a potential precursor in the development of endometriosis-related neoplasia {19087566; 9088954; 29241974}. There may be a localized proliferation of crowded glands lined by atypical epithelium resembling atypical hyperplasia or intraepithelial neoplasia in the endometrium. More commonly, atypical endometriosis is characterized by an alteration in the lining of endometriotic cysts, where the epithelium shows varying degrees of stratification, disorganization, and cytological atypia, often associated with metaplastic alterations. Concurrent inflammatory and degenerative changes are usually present. However, atypical endometriosis may be associated with, and can be in anatomical continuity with, an endometriosis-related neoplasm, and in some instances it has preceded the development of carcinoma in the ipsilateral ovary.

The majority of endometriosis-associated neoplasms are malignant, with endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas being most common {7418292; 31097114; 29241974; 27912770; 31479615; 22976498}. Benign and borderline seromucinous tumours seem to occur less frequently. Rarely, endometrioid stromal sarcoma, adenosarcoma, or carcinosarcoma may occur {22653341; 29241974}, whereas squamous cell carcinoma is uncommon {29530394}.

Cytology

Endometriosis can be suspected on FNA if tubular organoid groups of columnar cells are present with no or minimal cytological atypia. Occasionally, cells with apical cilia can be found. If cytological atypia is marked or the groups are not well organized, adenocarcinoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis {23529978; 28139898}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: benign endometrioid glands surrounded by endometrial stroma, with or without recent haemorrhage or haemosiderin.

Desirable: in the absence of epithelium, endometrial stromal cells can be highlighted by CD10 or IFITM1, if needed.

Staging

Associated neoplasms are staged according to the appropriate organ sites if applicable.

Prognosis and prediction

Endometriosis is clinically benign if not complicated by secondary neoplasms.
