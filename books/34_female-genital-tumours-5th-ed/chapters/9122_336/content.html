9.1.2.2 Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated, of the vagina

Definition

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), HPV-associated, is an HPV-associated squamous tumour with stromal invasion and/or exophytic expansile-type invasion. Coincident cervical or vulvar carcinoma, or a prior history of these tumours within 5 years, must be excluded.

ICD-O coding

8085/3 Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated

ICD-11 coding

2C71.2 & XH0EJ7 Squamous cell carcinoma of vagina & Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-positive

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

These tumours occur predominantly in the upper third of the vagina and are more common in the posterior wall {18980291; 11812092}.

Clinical features

Most cases affect patients in their seventh to ninth decade of life. Signs and symptoms include vaginal bleeding/discharge, postcoital bleeding, urinary symptoms, pelvic pain, and a foreign body sensation within the vagina {7295606}.

Epidemiology

In 2018, vaginal cancers accounted for < 1% of all female cancer cases worldwide, with an estimated 17 500 new cases that year {30207593}. Where measured (largely in higher-income countries), the estimated 5-year survival rate is 50–70%, with about 8000 vaginal cancer deaths per year worldwide {30207593; 30138307}. Most cases of vaginal cancer (78%) are linked to HPV infection {31862245}. The age-standardized rates of vaginal cancer are mainly < 1 case per 100 000 person-years, with elevated rates (1–3 cases per 100 000 person-years) seen among black women in several US states and in indigenous populations in the USA, Canada, and New Zealand. Vaginal cancer incidence trends appear rather stable [[Bray F, Colombet M, Mery L, et al., editors. Cancer incidence in five continents. Volume XI. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2017. (IARC Scientific Publication No. 166). http://ci5.iarc.fr/CI5-XI.]]. One possible explanation for this apparent stability may stem from the relative rarity of the disease in most populations, making it difficult to detect any change in temporal development. In the future, high-coverage HPV vaccination may counter a rising burden from these cancers, which is partly associated with population ageing {31862245}, but such an effect will be observed only after a few decades.

Etiology

This tumour is related to persistent HPV infection. The majority of cases have been associated with high-risk HPV types, and HPV16 is the most frequent type (61%) {25155250; 24785610}. Low-risk HPV types have occasionally been described {18500261}. Other risk factors are a prior history of and treatment for an anogenital cancer (especially of the cervix), smoking, and immunosuppression {16803507}.

Pathogenesis

See section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix.

Macroscopic appearance

Vaginal SCC displays a variety of gross patterns, from ulcerated plaques to polypoid lesions or strictures.

Histopathology

Vaginal SCC displays patterns similar to those of cervical SCC, namely, keratinizing, non-keratinizing, warty, basaloid, and papillary patterns {21166704; 21917071; 16535806; 6329128; 29257037}. Tumours with mixtures of patterns are common and morphology is not specific for the presence of HPV. See also section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated, of the uterine cervix.

Cytology

See section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated, of the uterine cervix.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: no previous (≤ 5 years) or concomitant SCC of cervix or vulva; infiltrating, angulated, irregularly sized and shaped nests, anastomosing cords, and solid sheets; nuclear pleomorphism and increased mitotic count; a desmoplastic or inflammatory stroma; overexpression of p16 as an acceptable surrogate marker of HPV association.

Desirable (in selected cases): HPV testing.

Staging

Like that of other gynaecological malignancies, the staging of vaginal carcinoma is based on the FIGO/TNM staging system.

Prognosis and prediction

Prognosis depends on patient age, tumour stage, and tumour size (< 4 cm vs ≥ 4 cm) {23488292; 16803507}. For high-stage disease, the prognosis is dismal, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 50% for all stages {30139838}. Surgical treatment is usually performed only in low-stage disease and for tumours located in the upper vagina, because of the proximity of lower vaginal tumours to critical organs. Concurrent chemoradiation and combinations of external radiotherapy, intracavitary irradiation, and platinum-based chemotherapy have been used in high-stage disease {25281493; 15850914}. In several studies, the presence of HPV DNA has been found to be an independent favourable prognostic factor for disease-free and overall survival, and one study found HPV16-positive tumours to be associated with better survival {22226684; 23402906; 21666483}. It is estimated that the use of prophylactic HPV vaccines may reduce the incidence of HPV-associated vaginal carcinoma by 60% {31360870}.
