10.1.2.1 Squamous intraepithelial lesions, HPV-associated, of the vulva

Definition

Squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) of the vulva (also known as vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia [VIN]), HPV-associated, are proliferations of squamous cells driven by HPV infection, showing maturation abnormalities and nuclear hyperchromasia that do not extend beyond the basement membrane.

ICD-O coding

8077/0 Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion

8077/2 High-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion

ICD-11 coding

GA13.1 & XN8JY Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia NOS & Human papillomavirus

Related terminology

Acceptable: low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (VIN 1); high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (VIN 2); high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (VIN 3); VIN 2 or 3 of usual type

Not recommended: moderate squamous dysplasia; severe squamous dysplasia; squamous cell carcinoma in situ; vulvar carcinoma in situ of usual type; Bowen disease; Bowenoid papulosis

Subtype(s)

Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 1; vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 2; vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 3

Localization

These lesions are localized in the labia majora, labia minora, vestibule, clitoris, vaginal introitus, and perineum. Multifocal and multicentric lesions (past, concomitant, or future high-grade SILs [HSILs] of the cervix, vagina, and/or anus {25987900}) are frequent. Multifocality is more frequent in immunocompromised women.

Clinical features

About 60% of patients present with pruritus and irritation. A significant percentage are asymptomatic.

Epidemiology

Most intraepithelial lesions diagnosed in the vulva are HSIL, HPV-associated. The worldwide incidence has risen in recent decades {10986677}, but a decline is expected with uptake of HPV vaccination {27541373}. The lesions usually affect younger women.

Etiology

High-risk HPVs are the cause of HSIL. HPV16 is identified in > 70% of cases. Other high-risk HPV types are identified less frequently {23886586}. Cigarette smoking and immunocompromise are epidemiologically associated with an increased risk of HSIL {28590952}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis is similar to that of other high-grade HPV-associated intraepithelial neoplasms; see section Squamous cell carcinoma, HPV-associated, of the uterine cervix.

Macroscopic appearance

The lesions present as white, erythematous, or pigmented macules, papules, or verrucous plaques, which can coalesce.

Histopathology

Features usually identified in HSIL include loss of maturation, nuclear hyperchromasia, high N:C ratios, cytological and architectural atypia, and mitoses. Extension into skin appendages is frequent and can mimic invasion {1941803}. Two morphological patterns of HSIL have been described: basaloid (undifferentiated) and warty (condylomatous, bowenoid), although overlap between the two patterns is frequent {28590952}. Basaloid HSIL shows undifferentiated cells with scant cytoplasm replacing the epidermis. Warty-type HSIL is characterized by epithelial thickening with papillomatosis, pleomorphism, koilocyte-like changes, and multinucleation. Rarely, HSIL can simulate differentiated VIN {29505429} or extramammary Paget disease {23020200}. Abundant stromal melanophages are frequently seen. Dermal amyloid deposits {25149547} and mucinous areas are occasionally identified {19238078}. Low-grade SIL (LSIL) is mainly limited to condyloma acuminata. Flat LSILs are rare in the vulva.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

In vulvar HSIL lesions, HPV DNA or mRNA can be detected {23886586} and p16 is overexpressed {15213596; 28590952}. p53 immunostaining usually shows a wildtype pattern, but accentuated p53 staining may be seen {29254796}. If p53 expression is increased, it typically spares the basal layer, with the strong expression confined to suprabasal cells, in contract with the basal staining seen in differentiated VIN.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: loss of maturation; nuclear hyperchromasia; high N:C ratios; cytological and architectural atypia; increased mitotic activity, with mitoses in the upper layers of the epidermis.

Desirable: p16 block-type immunohistochemistry.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

HSIL carries a significant but relatively low risk of progression to squamous cell carcinoma if untreated {16319258; 27864932}. Spontaneous regression is rare {22207153}. Squamous cell carcinomas arising in HSIL are frequently of the basaloid and warty subtypes, but keratinizing carcinomas are also common. Current treatment options include topical imiquimod, local excision, cidofovir, and photodynamic or laser therapy. Risk factors for recurrence include age > 50 years, positive surgical margins, large size, and immunosuppression {22867736; 29126556}.
