7.3.1.3 Gestational choriocarcinoma

Definition

Gestational choriocarcinoma is a malignant tumour composed of neoplastic villous syncytiotrophoblast, intermediate trophoblast, and cytotrophoblast.

ICD-O coding

9100/3 Choriocarcinoma NOS

ICD-11 coding

2C75.0 & XH8PK7 Malignant trophoblastic neoplasms of placenta & Choriocarcinoma NOS

Related terminology

None

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Gestational choriocarcinoma usually occurs in the uterus but rarely occurs at ectopic or metastatic sites, including fallopian tube, ovary, cervix, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, and gut {5441265; 7035113; 22838244; 20673583}.

Clinical features

Common presentations include vaginal bleeding or haemorrhage at ectopic or metastatic sites in association with a highly elevated serum hCG level, appearing weeks to several years after pregnancy {16310678; 20728069; 28254207; 28877059; 30306586}. Intraplacental choriocarcinoma is often asymptomatic until metastasis occurs within the patient or (rarely) in the infant {9688762; 14614885; 16175749; 27020699}. With hCG monitoring for molar pregnancies, the association with term pregnancy increases. The diagnosis of choriocarcinoma after term delivery is usually made 1–3 months after delivery {30407075}.

Epidemiology

Gestational choriocarcinoma is the most common gestational trophoblastic neoplasm. The reported annual incidence per 40 000 pregnancies ranges from 1 case in North America and Europe to 3.3 cases in Japan and 9.2 cases in south-eastern Asia {20728069; 30306586}. Incidence has declined in recent years {12972736; 3706279}. Patients are usually of reproductive age (average age: 29–31 years) {4333065; 21247618}, but rare cases occuring in teenagers and postmenopausal women have also been reported {4333065; 21247618}. Patients with intraplacental choriocarcinoma fall into a similar age group. Although intraplacental choriocarcinoma can occur in any trimester, most cases are diagnosed in the third trimester or postpartum, with a median gestational age of 37 weeks {27020699}; there is significant uncertainty regarding its incidence, as well as regarding its impact on obstetric morbidity and the development of metastatic maternal and/or neonatal choriocarcinoma {27020699}.

Etiology

Risk factors include prior complete mole, race, and advanced maternal age {20728069}. The risk is considered to be higher in women of Asian and Native American origin, as well as in African-Americans {20728069}.

Pathogenesis

Most choriocarcinomas are androgenetic XX and follow complete moles {20182630; 28877059}. Biparental cases may derive from intraplacental choriocarcinoma preceded by normal pregnancy {31357948} and rarely from familial complete moles {28877059}. Occasionally, androgenetic choriocarcinoma may arise from an occult complete mole coexisting with a non-molar pregnancy as a dispermic twin pregnancy or from intraplacental mosaicism {12202441; 28877059}. More than 50% of choriocarcinomas are preceded by a complete mole; other cases are preceded by spontaneous or induced abortion (25%), normal pregnancy (22.5%), or ectopic pregnancy (2.5%) {5347178; 6829636; 8737309; 8929267; 10892763; 16816073; 20728069; 2175931; 27251425}. It is estimated that 2–3% of complete moles and 0.5% of partial moles progress to choriocarcinoma {6829636; 8737309; 8929267; 10892763; 20728069; 2175931}.

Macroscopic appearance

Choriocarcinoma forms single or multiple dark-red masses with florid haemorrhage and necrosis {14614885; 16310678}. Deep myometrial invasion can cause uterine perforation {30407075}. Intraplacental choriocarcinoma may look inconspicuous macroscopically, mimic a placental infarct or thrombus, or form friable papillary to solid lesions in term placentas {12873198; 20736774; 27020699; 30306586}. Thorough examination of the entire term placenta, with 5 mm interval sections, is therefore recommended in order to identify intraplacental choriocarcinoma {30407075}.

Histopathology

Choriocarcinoma is characterized by infiltrative, destructive, solid aggregates of mononuclear villous cytotrophoblast and intermediate trophoblast rimmed by multinucleated syncytiotrophoblast {30407075}, with marked cytological atypia and brisk mitotic activity. There is often haemorrhage, necrosis, and lymphovascular permeation. Choriocarcinoma may coexist with epithelioid trophoblastic tumour and placental site trophoblastic tumour {12823059; 29897391}. The presence of chorionic villi is considered to be discordant with the diagnosis of choriocarcinoma. However, intraplacental choriocarcinoma is well recognized in term placentas, with aggregates of cytologically malignant trophoblast morphologically resembling choriocarcinoma extending from the chorionic villi into the intervillous space {16175749; 12873198; 20736774; 27020699; 28877059}. A transition between benign and malignant trophoblast has occasionally been identified, as have vascular invasion and invasion of villous stroma {27020699}. Similar intramolar or early choriocarcinoma coexisting with complete or invasive mole is increasingly recognized {26535984; 28877059; 30407075}. The molar villi are surrounded by a markedly atypical trophoblastic proliferation with a focally biphasic pattern resembling that of choriocarcinoma {30407075}.

Strong immunoreactivity for hCG, HSD3B1, hPL, inhibin, MCAM, SALL4, HLA-G, MUC4, and p63 in neoplastic syncytiotrophoblasts and in intermediate trophoblasts is helpful {19145204; 10202672; 18223326; 9831208; 19188816; 27068524}. The Ki-67 proliferation index is typically high (> 90%) {30407075}.

The differential diagnosis includes placental site trophoblastic tumour, epithelioid trophoblastic tumour, non-gestational choriocarcinoma of germ cell origin, and somatic carcinoma with trophoblastic differentiation, as well as trophoblastic proliferation in early gestation, complete mole, exaggerated placental site, and placental site nodule/plaque {19284380; 28118159; 28582346; 28877059; 30407075; 22469506; 30306586}. These lesions necessitate different treatment approaches, so accurate diagnosis is important (see #13189Table #13189). Genotyping is useful for differentiating gestational choriocarcinoma from non-gestational choriocarcinoma and hCG-producing malignancies at intrauterine and extrauterine sites.

Cytology

Choriocarcinoma cells may occur in cervical cytology and must be distinguished from malignant epithelial tumours of the cervix {26712464}.

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Genotyping, including microsatellite (short tandem repeat [STR]) analysis, is helpful for identifying the presence of unique paternal alleles of the index gestation, supporting the diagnosis of gestational choriocarcinoma versus non-gestational choriocarcinoma of germ cell origin and somatic carcinoma with trophoblast differentiation (see #13189Table #13189) {17942145; 24944881; 28161066; 28877059; 31421690; 31028360}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: characteristic biphasic pattern, with malignant mononuclear villous cytotrophoblast and intermediate trophoblast rimmed by multinucleated syncytiotrophoblast.

Desirable: immunohistochemistry can help in cases when the classic pattern is lacking, especially in small biopsy samples; genotyping, if available, can support the diagnosis of gestational choriocarcinoma.

Staging

The FIGO and Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM classifications are used for staging. The WHO risk factor scoring system with FIGO staging is presented in #13022Table #13022 {30306586}.

Prognosis and prediction

Chemotherapy yields an excellent response, with a cure rate approaching 100%, and relates to tumour stage and prognostic score {30306586}. Surgery is indicated when there is uncontrollable bleeding and for chemoresistant cases.
