9.1.2.1 Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the vagina

Definition

Squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) of the vagina, also known as vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VaIN), are proliferations of squamous cells driven by HPV infection, showing maturation abnormalities and/or viral cytopathic changes that do not extend beyond the basement membrane. They are divided into low-grade SILs (LSILs) and high-grade SILs (HSILs). See section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix for further discussion.

Related terminology

LSIL (condyloma / VaIN 1)

Acceptable: mild squamous dysplasia; koilocytic atypia; koilocytosis

HSIL (VaIN 2)

Acceptable: moderate squamous dysplasia

HSIL (VaIN 3)

Acceptable: severe squamous dysplasia; squamous cell carcinoma in situ

Subtype(s)

Vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 1; vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 2; vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia, grade 3

Localization

Vaginal SILs arise in the squamous mucosa of the vagina and are most common in the upper third and fornices.

Clinical features

Vaginal LSILs and HSILs (VaIN 2 or 3) are usually asymptomatic. They may be associated with foci of SIL in other locations, particularly in the cervix {29767857}.

Epidemiology

The exact prevalence of vaginal SIL is unknown. The mean patient age for LSIL is similar to that observed for cervical LSIL. About one third of women with HSIL (VaIN 2 or 3) have a history of previous cervical HSIL (CIN 2 or 3).

Etiology

LSIL is associated with both low-risk and high-risk HPV types. It is the morphological manifestation of a productive, transient HPV infection and therefore tends to regress. HSIL (VaIN 2 or 3) is associated exclusively with high-risk HPV types: HPV16 most often, followed by HPV45 {30561092}. It is the morphological manifestation of a transforming infection and therefore has greater potential to progress to invasive carcinoma than does LSIL. However, the risk of progression of vaginal HSIL is lower than that of cervical HSIL. See section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix for further details.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis mirrors that seen in the cervix; see section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix for further details.

Macroscopic appearance

Vaginal SILs are difficult to recognize with the naked eye and can be appreciated on colposcopy only after application of 3–5% acetic acid or half-strength Lugol’s iodine solution. Unlike cervical SIL, vaginal SIL is often multifocal and multicentric – i.e. associated with cervical SIL or (less often) vulvar HSIL (VIN 2 or 3).

Histopathology

Vaginal SIL is divided using the two-tiered grading system recommended by the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) standardization project {22742517}. LSIL corresponds with condyloma / VaIN 1, and HSIL corresponds with VaIN 2 or 3. The histological criteria mirror those described in the section on cervical SIL (see section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix). For illustrations, see section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix, as well.

The essential and desirable diagnostic criteria are as described in the section on cervical SILs (see section Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

LSIL typically regresses, although it may progress to HSIL (VaIN 2 or 3), particularly if associated with high-risk HPV infection. Localized HSIL (VaIN 3) responds well to ablative or excisional procedures {29185264}. Extensive, multifocal disease requires multiple ablation treatments, with or without intravaginal fluorouracil chemotherapy or 5% imiquimod cream. Vaginectomy/colpectomy is reserved as a last treatment resort. The risk of progression of vaginal HSIL (VaIN 3) to invasion in immunocompetent patients is about 5%, significantly lower than that of cervical HSIL (CIN 3). The risk of recurrence is significantly higher in immunocompromised patients {30161052}.
