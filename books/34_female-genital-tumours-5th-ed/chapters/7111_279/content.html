7.1.1.1 Complete hydatidiform mole

Definition

Complete hydatidiform mole (CHM) is an abnormal gestation with diffuse trophoblastic proliferation and villous hydrops without embryonic development. The presence of paternal-only (androgenetic diploid) genome is the fundamental etiology in most cases.

ICD-O coding

9100/0 Complete hydatidiform mole

ICD-11 coding

JA02.0 & XH8CX2 Complete hydatidiform mole & Hydatidiform mole NOS

Related terminology

Acceptable: complete molar pregnancy; complete mole

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Uterus and less commonly ectopic in the fallopian tube or ovary {24134929; 15086739}

Clinical features

Classic symptoms of CHM during the late first or early second trimester include vaginal bleeding, excessive uterine size, hyperemesis, hyperthyroidism, and pre-eclampsia due to markedly elevated serum β-hCG {26433668}. Patients undergoing routine first-trimester ultrasound are diagnosed earlier, with absence of fetal heart beat (missed abortion), and are often clinically asymptomatic {25969351; 26588240}. Ultrasound shows snowstorm appearance and absence of fetal development due to numerous cystic hypoechoic spaces in well-developed CHM {28287945}, but in cases examined in early pregnancy, molar disease may not be suspected sonographically.

Epidemiology

The incidence of hydatidiform moles shows a distinct geographical distribution, being highest in south-eastern Asia (3.8–13 cases per 1000 pregnancies), the Middle East (3.2–5.8 cases per 1000 pregnancies), and South America (4.6 cases per 1000 pregnancies) and lowest in western Europe and North America (0.8–1.5 cases per 1000 pregnancies) {29451409; 14602247; 26586414; 12972736}. The incidence rates of complete and partial moles are not separated in most studies, and although older cohorts showed predominance of CHM, a trend of increasing rates of partial mole has been observed {26777992; 29451409; 27258730}. Significant risk factors for CHM include maternal age of < 20 or > 40 years, Asian race, a history of prior hydatidiform mole, and genetics (recurrent familial CHM) {14602247; 26777992}. Diet and socioeconomic status have also been suggested as possible risk factors.

Etiology

Most CHMs are sporadic (with a diploid paternal-only genome and maternal-only mitochondrial DNA) and contain two paternal haploid chromosome sets of either monospermic/homozygous origin (80–90%) or dispermic/heterozygous origin (10–20%). Rare tetraploid sporadic CHMs have four paternal haploid chromosome sets {18300805; 8651859}. Familial biparental CHM is rare, representing 0.6–2.6% of all CHMs {28135560; 20171777}. Mutations in the maternal-effect genes NLRP7 (NALP7) on chromosome 19q13.4 and KHDC3L on chromosome 6q13 have been identified as causative events in familial biparental CHM {16462743; 17579354; 21885028; 29463882}.

Pathogenesis

CHM develops through global imprinting alteration in a preimplantation embryo due to de novo absence of the maternal genome (sporadic androgenetic CHM) or as a result of NLRP7 (NALP7) or KHDC3L mutations (familial biparental CHM). Overexpression of the paternal genome leads to trophoblastic hyperplasia and failure of fetal development {28135560}, although the exact mechanism by which this occurs is not yet fully understood.

Macroscopic appearance

The evacuation specimen in well-developed CHM is voluminous, with visibly enlarged, semitransparent, hydropic villi. Fetal parts are absent, except in cases with a coexisting normal twin gestation {15963812; 17664887}. Very early CHMs have minimal or no gross evidence of abnormal villi and are indistinguishable from non-molar products of conception.

Histopathology

Well-developed CHM is characterized by enlarged, hydropic villi with frequent cistern formation, as well as marked trophoblastic proliferation with abnormal distribution and loss of polarity. Significant cytological atypia and mitotic figures are common at the implantation site. In a very early CHM, the villi may not be enlarged but have an irregular, bulbous or polypoid appearance, with hypercellular myxoid stroma, prominent karyorrhexis, and trophoblast hyperplasia. No fetal or non-villous placental tissues are present, although rare cases with fetal vessels and red blood cells have been reported {9120723}. Immunohistochemically, p57 (KIP2) nuclear staining is absent in cytotrophoblast and villous stromal cells in both sporadic and familial biparental CHMs {24907943; 12514787}. The differential diagnosis includes partial hydatidiform mole, hydropic abortion, and non-molar early gestation that may show overlapping morphology with villous hydrops and trophoblastic hyperplasia but show retained p57 nuclear staining in cytotrophoblast and villous stromal cells. Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping provides precise diagnosis of sporadic CHM by identifying the absence of maternal genetic contribution (androgenetic-only genome) {20308840; 23887308; 28135560; 30500280}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: morphological features of well-developed CHM; in early CHM, absence of p57 expression in cytotrophoblast and/or villous stromal cells is needed to make the diagnosis.

Desirable: paternal-only genome on genotyping analysis.

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

CHM carries a 15–20% risk of persistent gestational trophoblastic disease and a 2–3% risk of gestational choriocarcinoma, regardless of gestational age at diagnosis {20673583; 22846466; 25969351}. Studies have shown a higher frequency of persistent gestational trophoblastic disease associated with heterozygous CHM than with homozygous cases {20208060; 26535984}. Because risk stratification for persistent gestational trophoblastic disease is not yet possible, all CHMs necessitate hCG surveillance.
