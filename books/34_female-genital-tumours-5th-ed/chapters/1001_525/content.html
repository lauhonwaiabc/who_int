10.0.1 Tumours of the vulva: Introduction

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is by far the most frequent malignant vulvar tumour. This edition of the WHO classification differs from previous editions by dividing SCCs on the basis of their association with HPV infection {23190170}. Approximately two thirds of vulvar SCCs arise via an HPV-independent pathway {23886586}; they behave more aggressively than HPV-associated carcinomas {28899974}. Although several morphological patterns are associated with each etiopathogenic subtype, the two categories cannot be confidently distinguished unless molecular HPV tests are used {28815579}. Block-type immunoreactivity for p16 is a reliable (although not perfect) surrogate of HPV-associated SCCs and can be assessed in most laboratories {26630231}. Although there are currently no differences in treatment between the two distinct etiopathogenic types, it is recommended that the type of SCC (HPV-associated or HPV-independent) be documented on the pathology report. Nevertheless, a morphological diagnosis without differentiating the two categories is an acceptable alternative where facilities to make this distinction are not available.

As in previous editions, the precursors of SCC are divided into HPV-associated and HPV-independent {23190170}. The HPV-associated lesions are designated as squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs), which is in keeping with the unified Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) standardization project recommendations {22820980}. Unlike in the cervix and vagina, flat low-grade SIL is rare in the vulva, and most HPV-associated vulvar precursors are high-grade SILs. The term “HPV-independent vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN)” is recommended for the HPV-independent precursors. Most lesions included in this category are morphologically classified as differentiated VIN, but there is some morphological overlap between differentiated VIN and high-grade SIL {29505429; 19730361}. Recently, two other precursors of HPV-independent SCC have been described: differentiated exophytic vulvar intraepithelial lesion and vulvar acanthosis with altered differentiation {15105653; 27834349; 29470358; 31097103}. These precursors have been included as subtypes of HPV-independent VIN {27834349}.

Condyloma acuminatum can arise in the cervix and vagina, but it is particularly frequent in the vulva and is therefore discussed in this chapter. Most seborrhoeic keratoses in young women represent subtypes of condyloma acuminatum {22583360} and should be diagnosed as such. However, a proportion of cases in older patients are not associated with HPV {24556611}, and the entity has been maintained in this chapter.

Other primary tumours (e.g. epidermal cyst or basal cell carcinoma) are identical to the same lesions at other cutaneous sites, and the reader is referred to the volume on skin tumours. Tumours originating in mammary-type glands {21169735; 27759697} and lesions derived from Bartholin glands are covered in this chapter, as are Paget disease and other rare primary vulvar adenocarcinomas.

Melanocytic lesions of the vulva are discussed in section Melanocytic lesions. A number of mesenchymal tumours occur in the vulva, and some are quite specific to this region. These are now included in section Mesenchymal tumours of the lower genital tract, which classifies and discusses mesenchymal tumours of the lower female genital tract as a whole. Similarly, neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) are covered in section Neuroendocrine neoplasia, haematolymphoid proliferations and neoplasia in section Haematolymphoid proliferations and neoplasia, and metastases in section Metastasis.
