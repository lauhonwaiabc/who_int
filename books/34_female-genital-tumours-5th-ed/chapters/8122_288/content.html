8.1.2.2 Squamous intraepithelial lesions of the uterine cervix

Definition

Squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs) of the uterine cervix, also known as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), are proliferations of squamous cells driven by HPV infection, showing maturation abnormalities and/or viral cytopathic changes that do not extend beyond the basement membrane. They are divided into low-grade SILs (LSILs) and high-grade SILs (HSILs).

Subtype(s)

Localization

SILs are located on the cervical squamous mucosa and most often arise in the transformation zone. HSIL, in particular, has a strong predilection for the squamocolumnar junction.

Clinical features

Cervical SILs are asymptomatic lesions identified through cytological screening and colposcopy or by visual inspection after 3–5% acetic acid application. Features such as acetowhite change, iodine negativity, mosaicism, and punctation may be identified colposcopically {29372143}. Colposcopically, atypical vessels in addition to major changes such as rapid acetowhitening and a coarse mosaic / punctuation can be appreciated {22914406}.

Epidemiology

The epidemiology of SIL is linked to that of HPV infections, with a peak incidence observed in young women and a declining rate in the ensuing decades: HPV DNA is detectable in as many as 80% of women in their early 20s but falls to about 5% among women in their sixth decade of life {22431528; 1328078}. In screened population in high-income countries, LSIL has a cross-sectional prevalence of 5–10% and the prevalence of HSIL is 0.5–1%. HSIL typically occurs at an older age than LSIL, although there is broad overlap and HSIL has been demonstrated within a year or two of HPV infection in adolescents. Importantly, the rate of HSIL (CIN 2) regression is higher in adolescents and young women than in older populations {29487049; 21099605}.

Etiology

Both LSIL and HSIL are, by definition, attributable to an HPV infection. More than 40 HPV types can infect the cervix, although 18 high-risk (HR) and about 6 low-risk (LR) types account for the majority of infections. Most clinically valid HR-HPV tests assess for the 14–18 most common HR types {9774574; 28403015}. The HR types HPV16 and HPV18 and the LR types HPV6 and HPV11 have been identified in a significant proportion of SILs {21991433; 30632704}. However, increased uptake of vaccination against these types is anticipated to reduce the frequency of these infections and consequently the incidence of SIL {30944092}.

LR-HPV types can cause both exophytic LSILs, known as condylomata acuminata (genital warts), and flat LSILs; however, the majority (80–90%) of flat LSILs are attributable to HR-HPV types. All HR-HPV–associated LSILs bear a risk of progression to HSIL and malignancy. While LR-HPV has rarely been associated with carcinoma {24076770}, in contrast, HSIL arises exclusively in the context of HR-HPV infection.

Whether HSILs develop from LSILs or evolve independently is controversial, and attempts to clarify this question are confounded by issues of interpretive variation and sampling. Importantly, HSIL can be identified in geographical continuity with LSIL; however, microdissection studies have shown that in most cases this reflects simultaneous infections with different HPV types rather than evolution of LSIL to HSIL {25353286}. Biologically, HSIL represents a clonal expansion of cells driven to proliferate by abnormal expression of the HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7.

Pathogenesis

The presence of HPV DNA is necessary but not sufficient for the development of SIL, because many HPV infections do not proceed to morphologically detectable lesions. LSIL occurs when HPV infection becomes productive in cells that have initiated maturation, whereas HSIL results from virally driven clonal expansion of cells throughout the epithelium.

Viral gene expression is coordinated with squamous differentiation in LSIL, whereas it is uncoupled from squamous differentiation in HSIL. Uncoordinated expression of the viral E6 and E7 proteins of HR-HPV types is key to the induction of genetic and other cellular abnormalities associated with the development of HSIL {27905473}. The E6 and E7 proteins exert their main effects on cell-cycle control proteins, in particular p53 and RB1 (pRB), respectively, and the interaction between E7 and RB1 is responsible for the marked upregulation of p16 protein expression in lesions infected with HR-HPVs. LR-HPV E7 proteins do not induce p16 overexpression, explaining the absence of block-type p16 positivity in lesions infected with LR-HPV types {11291057}.

LSILs are generally DNA-stable, and their enlarged nuclei are usually euploid or polyploid. HSILs, in contrast, more often exhibit aneuploidy than polyploidy, reflecting their increased genetic instability. They also demonstrate DNA integration and abnormalities of 1p and 3q more often than LSIL {14694518}. Hypermethylation of CpG islands in the promoter regions of tumour suppressor genes appears to be important for malignant progression, because this molecular feature is common in HSIL but rarely seen in LSIL {31067838; 31344234; 30098013}.

It has been suggested that HSILs may derive primarily from a specialized type of squamocolumnar junctional cell that resides between the transformation zone and the columnar epithelium and bears a unique immunohistochemical signature {22689991; 24076771}. However, the alternative view that they may arise from metaplastic epithelium also has its proponents {25081304}.

Macroscopic appearance

Most HR-HPV–associated cervical SILs are flat lesions that are not initially discernible with the naked eye from the background normal mucosa. Grossly, there is no way to differentiate between LSIL and HSIL, and these two lesions may coexist. They can be visualized macroscopically with the help of a colposcope and usually only after the application of acetic acid, which renders the lesions white (with HSIL usually appearing more acetowhite and for a longer time than LSIL). Importantly, colposcopic sampling has imperfect sensitivity, because flat SIL can sometimes be challenging to identify. Therefore, a negative biopsy result does not negate or override concern prompted by a cytologically detected lesion.

Histopathology

“LSIL” and “HSIL” are the preferred terminology in both tissue and cytology specimens from the cervix {22742517}. This is because of the improved reproducibility and enhanced biological relevance of the two-tiered LSIL/HSIL system versus the previous three-tiered CIN 1/2/3 system, as well as the clarity of tissue-agnostic terminology given the shared pathogenesis of HPV-driven SILs across anogenital anatomical sites {22742517; 7942772}. HSIL may be subdivided into HSIL (CIN 2) and HSIL (CIN 3), in particular in young women (aged < 30 years), because there is evidence that the former shows significantly higher regression rates {29487049}.

LSIL

LSIL is characterized by the lower third of the epithelium demonstrating a proliferation of basal/parabasal-like cells that may show mitotic activity, albeit usually without atypical mitoses, along with koilocytic atypia with clearly retained features of maturation/differentiation. Although the cells of the overlying two thirds demonstrate increased cytoplasm and loss of parabasal/basal morphology, nuclear enlargement persists such that the N:C ratio remains elevated. The constituent nuclei also demonstrate hyperchromasia and nuclear membrane irregularities, and they may show binucleation or multinucleation. Notably, these nuclei may be markedly atypical and may appear even in the top-most layers of the epithelium in LSIL, and this finding does not warrant a diagnosis of HSIL provided a significant amount of cytoplasm is still present. Many cells bear a well-defined halo-like vacuole around the nucleus. These haloes typically display an irregular, well-defined, dense border that helps distinguish them from glycogen-rich and reactive pseudohaloes {2171769}. Koilocytosis is usually most prominent in the upper third of the epithelium but may extend deeper.

LSIL must be differentiated from morphologically similar entities. Firstly, benign squamous epithelium with reactive or inflammatory features may mimic LSIL. LSIL overdiagnosis is a known problem in this context, with as many as 50% of biopsies diagnosed as LSIL on initial read failing to demonstrate HPV RNA and earning reclassification as benign/reactive on expert review {11255427; 29112014}. p16 immunohistochemistry has no utility at this diagnostic interface, because many LSILs are negative; however, direct HPV testing (e.g. HPV RNA in situ hybridization) may be informative {29112014}. Secondly, HSIL – in particular moderate HSIL (CIN 2) – may be challenging to distinguish from LSIL (CIN 1), and interobserver agreement is notoriously poor at this interface, even among experts {11255427}. In contrast with LSIL (CIN 1), moderate HSIL (CIN 2) shows extension of basal/parabasal morphology and mitotic activity beyond the lower epithelial third. However, diagnostic assessment may be complicated in tangential sections or cases with partial epithelial denudation. HSILs almost invariably exhibit block-type p16 staining, as do a significant proportion of LSILs. In the absence of block-type p16 staining, HSIL is extremely unlikely. It is helpful to use p16 immunohistochemistry in morphologically ambiguous cases when HSIL is suspected, especially in young women (aged < 30 years), because this should be downgraded in the absence of block-type p16 staining {22742517}.

HSIL

HSIL (CIN 2) demonstrates full-thickness nuclear abnormalities (hyperchromasia, coarse chromatin, irregular membranes), an increased N:C ratio, and mitotic activity extending into the lower two thirds of the epithelium, but with accumulation of cytoplasm in the uppermost cell layers. HSIL (CIN 3) shows full-thickness basal/parabasal-type atypia without an appreciable difference in maturation in the top-most versus the lower-most epithelial layers. Both HSIL (CIN 2) and HSIL (CIN 3) can show atypical mitotic figures peppered throughout the epithelium, including the superficial portions. Historically, pathologists attempted to identify a distinct lesion known as carcinoma in situ, but this term has since been removed from the lexicon because it is essentially encompassed by HSIL (CIN 3).

Notable patterns of HSIL include thin HSIL {30927371}, keratinizing HSIL, pleomorphic HSIL {28666641}, and papillary HSIL. Thin HSIL is usually < 10 cells thick. Keratinizing HSIL differs from conventional basaloid HSIL in that its cells show more superficial differentiation with prominent keratinization, but without the koilocytosis of LSIL. HSIL may have a papillary configuration when it lines endocervical papillae. However, papillary squamous cell carcinoma {16044054} may be diagnosed without definitive stromal invasion when there is a clinically visible lesion or the exophytic growth pattern is sufficient to indicate exophytic-type invasion.

Distinguishing between HSIL (CIN 2) and HSIL (CIN 3) can be challenging, with many CIN 2 cases being reclassified as CIN 3 on expert review {11255427}. Although errors at this diagnostic border have less clinical significance than those that occur at the LSIL (CIN 1) / HSIL (CIN 2) interface (because HSIL treatment is typically standardized irrespective of the “moderate” vs “severe” designation), there are some clinicians who consider treating HSIL (CIN 2) less aggressively in women who wish to preserve fertility. A more clinically relevant diagnostic dilemma occurs in cases that share features with atrophy and/or immature metaplasia. These benign changes can masquerade as HSIL and vice versa. The final differential diagnosis to consider for HSIL is squamous cell carcinoma. When there is expansive crypt involvement, with or without necrosis/keratinization, carcinoma should be excluded with additional evaluation {7962647}. When there is paradoxical maturation, carcinoma should be considered.

Ancillary testing

The tumour suppressor protein p16 is overexpressed in nearly all HSILs and some LSILs as a result of compensatory upregulation related to interference of the oncoprotein E7 of HR-HPV types with RB1 (pRB). Positivity is defined as strong block-type staining involving basal keratinocytes and extending beyond the lower third of the epithelium; some authors require at least 6 contiguous cells in horizontal extent, but this has not been well validated {25602791}. Application of p16 immunohistochemistry is recommended by the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) standardization project in three specific contexts: to aid in the distinction of HSIL from mimickers of precancer (immature metaplasia, atrophy, reparative changes, or tangential sectioning), to supplement morphological assessment for biopsy specimens interpreted as ≤ LSIL that are at high risk for missed high-grade disease based on the prior Pap or HPV testing result, and to inform the diagnosis of HSIL (CIN 2) versus LSIL in morphologically equivocal cases – block-type staining supports HSIL (CIN 2) {22742517}. However, it is critical to emphasize that overapplication of p16 must be avoided in biopsy specimens with morphological diagnoses of HSIL (CIN 3) or LSIL, because a significant proportion of morphologically unequivocal LSILs show block-type staining, which should not merit reclassification as HSIL (CIN 2) and which does not impart significantly increased risk {26541274; 25602791}. In general, pathologists should enlist p16 on a maximum of 20–40% of cervical biopsies to avoid HSIL (CIN 2) overdiagnosis, and some argue for an even lower utilization rate {29697437}.

Direct HPV testing may also have value in selected cases, in particular those with a differential of LSIL versus negative/reactive, because p16 has limited utility in this context. In such instances, HPV RNA in situ hybridization testing can prevent overdiagnosis of LSIL in morphologically equivocal cases, but its use should be restricted to morphologically challenging lesions to avoid unnecessary cost to patients {29112014}.

Cytology

LSIL is characterized by dysplastic cells with low N:C ratios and nuclei that are enlarged (> 3 times the size of an intermediate cell nucleus), hyperchromatic, and irregular. Binucleation and sharply punched-out perinuclear koilocytic haloes may be present. HSIL is characterized by dysplastic cells with high N:C ratios (> 1:1) and irregular nuclear contours, occurring singly, in sheets, or as crowded clusters. Nuclei are typically hyperchromatic but may be normochromic or hypochromic. Cytoplasm varies from delicate to dense or metaplastic. A large population-based study reported a 6.1% 5-year risk of CIN 3+ for women with HPV-associated LSIL on cytology and a 2.0% risk with HPV-independent LSIL {23519304}. For women with a Pap test diagnosis of HSIL, the 5-year risk of cancer was 6.6–6.8% {23519305}.

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

LSIL (condyloma / CIN 1)

Essential: full-thickness atypia with moderate to abundant cytoplasm in cells within the upper two thirds of the epithelium; basaloid morphology and significant mitotic activity should be restricted to the lower epithelial third.

Desirable: koilocytic atypia within the middle and surface cells (highly desirable).

HSIL (CIN 2)

Essential: full-thickness atypia characterized by basaloid cells and mitotic activity extending into the upper half to upper two thirds of the epithelium, but with retained koilocytic change on the surface.

HSIL (CIN 3)

Essential: full-thickness atypia wherein the base of the lesion is often indistinguishable from the surface; mitotic activity may be identified throughout the epithelium; the upper portions of the epithelium show a significantly higher N:C ratio than in LSIL and HSIL (CIN 2).

Staging

Not clinically relevant

Prognosis and prediction

The vast majority of LSILs (~90%) will regress after biopsy without additional intervention, typically within 1 year. However, about 10% of LSILs (most often cases driven by HPV16) are associated with the subsequent development of HSIL. These cases probably include an admixture of lesions with true progression and cases in which HSIL was not sampled at the time of original biopsy. Immunosuppression and smoking are both risk factors for LSIL progression. Efforts to identify biomarkers predictive of LSIL progression have thus far been unsuccessful. Furthermore, the potential therapeutic impact of biopsy on natural history, by removing or inducing regression in one quarter to one third of cases, confounds the development of any such marker for clinical use. Studies have shown that, despite its diagnostic value, p16 does not have reproducible predictive utility for identifying LSIL that will progress to HSIL {26541274; 25602791}. CK7 immunohistochemistry has shown some promise in this arena, but it is not recommended for clinical use because of the relatively low amplitude of risk increase associated with its positivity, as well as its failure to identify all cases with progression {27680604; 27515495}.

The capacity of HSIL to regress to normal or LSIL is less well established; estimated regression rates vary from 30% to 50% depending on patient age, lesion size, and HPV type but are confounded by the potential therapeutic impact of biopsy, which may be as large as 30%. HSIL (CIN 2) lesions show significantly higher regression rates, particularly in young women {29487049}. The risk of progression of untreated HSIL to cancer is estimated to be 0.5–1% per year, with an overall malignant progression rate of about 30% for HSIL (CIN 3) over a 30-year period {18407790}. HSIL detection occurs an average of two decades earlier than invasive carcinoma, suggesting a protracted window for development to malignancy. Limited data, derived primarily from an unethical clinical study conducted at the National Women’s Hospital in New Zealand between 1965 and 1974, suggest that approximately one third of inadequately treated HSIL (CIN 3) cases will progress to carcinoma over 30 years {18407790}.

Most patients with SIL can be cured by adequate treatment using cryotherapy, laser ablation, loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), or surgical conization {29126708}. Recurrence is predicted by the size of the lesion (which correlates with the completeness of the excision/ablation) and whether HSIL reaches the margins {17928267}. Testing for HPV DNA at 6–12 months after therapy, or even intraoperatively, is the best predictor of recurrent or residual disease {22266548; 23189989}.
