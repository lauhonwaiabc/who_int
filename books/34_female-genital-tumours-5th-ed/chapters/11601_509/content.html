1.16.0.1 Metastases to the ovary

Definition

Ovarian metastases are malignant tumours metastasizing to the ovary from extraovarian primary sites.

ICD-O coding

None

ICD-11 coding

2E05.0 Malignant neoplasm metastasis in ovary

Related terminology

Acceptable: metastatic carcinoma of the respective site involving ovary; Krukenberg tumour (metastatic signet-ring cell carcinoma, which may originate from many anatomical sites, most commonly stomach)

Subtype(s)

None

Localization

Ovarian metastases are commonly surface and cortex lesions or involve the ovarian hilum.

Clinical features

Clinical findings are often related to the primary tumour. However, a pelvic mass can be the first manifestation of disease in some patients {17184826; 27757533}. Bilateral ovarian involvement detected on imaging studies can be helpful in suggesting secondary involvement {30361868}. Low-grade mucinous neoplasms of appendix usually present with abundant jelly-like mucinous material in the abdomen (pseudomyxoma peritonei) and variably prominent mucoid nodules of peritoneal tumour, whereas metastatic high-grade carcinoma less often presents as pseudomyxoma peritonei {24300528; 9331286}. In most cases, the appendiceal primary tumours were detected concurrently with ovarian metastatic lesions, but the appendix may not be strikingly enlarged {9331286; 17895750}.

Epidemiology

The proportion of malignant neoplasms of the ovary that are of metastatic origin ranges from 3% to 30% {19823050; 3692671; 15047218; 12713997; 17184826; 25894432}. Ovary is the most common site of metastasis within the gynaecological tract {29750707}. Metastases in the ovaries can present synchronously or metachronously with the primary neoplasm. In some cases, an ovarian mass represents the first manifestation of disease from a clinically occult non-ovarian primary {27757533; 17184826}.

Colorectal adenocarcinoma is the primary tumour that metastasizes to the ovary most commonly {19823050; 25894432}. Breast cancer metastasis is uncommon in surgical specimens but common at autopsy {25894432; 15047218; 17184826}; ductal and lobular carcinomas, respectively, account for 75% and 25% of metastases. The incidence of occult breast cancer metastases in risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy specimens from patients with BRCA1/2 mutations was not higher than the incidence in a hospital-based breast cancer patient population {19440148}. Metastasis from appendiceal adenocarcinoma is less common than metastatic colorectal or breast carcinoma {28730323; 25894432}. Metastasis to the ovary from primary pancreatobiliary carcinoma is relatively uncommon {3692671; 16515581; 17184826}. Ovarian metastases of endocervical and endometrial adenocarcinoma are also uncommon. Other uncommon primaries include carcinomas of lung, urinary bladder, and kidney; cervical (and other rare primary site) squamous cell carcinoma; melanoma, carcinoid, non-gynaecological and gynaecological sarcomas, lymphoma, and mesothelioma {17452813}.

Etiology

The cause is related to the primary tumour type and location.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis depends on the primary tumour. Ovarian metastases can develop via direct invasion or via lymphovascular/haematogenous, transcoelomic/transperitoneal, or transtubal spread {31846521}. Some low-grade ovarian endometrioid tumours may be clonally related to synchronous low-stage/low-grade endometrial endometrioid carcinomas {26832770; 26832771}, suggesting the possibility of tumour cell exfoliation and transtubal spread from the endometrial tumour or its precursor {31846521}. Bilateral massive ovarian involvement (Krukenberg tumour) can result from transperitoneal or haematogenous spread of gastric carcinoma [[WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. Digestive system tumours. Lyon (France): International Agency for Research on Cancer; 2019. (WHO classification of tumours series, 5th ed.; vol. 1). https://publications.iarc.fr/579.]]. Colon cancer with ovarian metastases had a significantly higher frequency of KRAS, SMAD4 (DPC4), and NTRK1 mutations than did colon cancer without ovarian metastases {27875625}.

Macroscopic appearance

The gross features that favour metastasis include small tumour size (often < 10–13 cm), bilaterality, a nodular growth pattern, and the presence of tumour on the surface and/or in the superficial cortex of the ovary {12604884; 12826891; 18162780; 25894432}. In contrast, primary ovarian mucinous tumours are unilateral and large (> 10–13 cm) {18162780; 27757533; 30179792}. Not infrequently, however, metastases can be large, unilateral, and cystic, simulating a primary ovarian neoplasm. The presence of extraovarian metastatic disease favours secondary ovarian involvement {22075188; 30179792}.

Histopathology

Histological features that favour metastasis include an infiltrative growth pattern with stromal desmoplasia, a nodular growth pattern, involvement of the ovarian surface and superficial cortex, and hilar and lymphovascular space involvement {12604884}. In contrast, primary ovarian tumours lack these features and have a confluent glandular growth pattern. However, some metastatic carcinomas display growth patterns seen in the primary ovarian tumours (including confluent glandular growth) and display lower-grade component–appearing areas, simulating a primary ovarian benign or borderline tumour-like background lesion. Not infrequently, the morphological features in the metastases differ from those in the primary tumour {27757533}. The presence of signet-ring cells strongly favours metastatic disease, with rare exceptions {18670351; 17527072}.

Metastatic gastric carcinoma is frequently composed predominantly of signet-ring cells {6291740; 16538048}. Various other architectural patterns can also be seen, including glands, tubules, trabeculae, nests, sheets of cells, and intestinal-type glands {6263118; 17063077}. There is often a variably conspicuous stromal component that ranges from densely cellular to acellular (resembling a fibroma) or strikingly oedematous. The oedema tends to be central. Stromal luteinization is relatively common.

Metastatic colorectal carcinoma is typically composed of glands of moderate size, but the glands can range from small and tubular to large and cystically dilated {1595595; 3812871; 16434891; 18317225}. A characteristic pattern in which the epithelium is draped along the periphery of luminal eosinophilic necrotic material containing karyorrhectic debris (garland pattern with dirty necrosis) is often seen {16434891}. There may be simple individual glands or a complex architecture with cribriform growth. The epithelium is typically stratified and highly atypical and is usually not overtly mucinous. Stromal condensation and luteinization around the glands are common.

Not uncommonly, metastatic pancreatobiliary tumours display a component of, or are composed predominantly of, patterns resembling primary benign or borderline ovarian mucinous tumours. These patterns include well-differentiated mucinous glands with minimal nuclear atypia and areas of intracystic glandular infoldings / papillary architecture. The glands may be infiltrative within a desmoplastic stroma. Nuclear atypia can vary from minimal to marked {18043033; 18580314; 21263249; 2764222; 2294064; 30194204}. Rarely, pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma can metastasize to the ovary {18724247}.

Metastases from low-grade mucinous neoplasms of appendix show hypermucinous epithelial cells (tall mucin-rich) with low-grade nuclear atypia. Abundant, dissecting, extracellular mucin (pseudomyxoma ovarii) and irregularly distributed incomplete glands that exhibit retraction from the adjacent basement membrane are seen {24300528; 9575376; 7503361}. Metastases of appendiceal carcinomas more commonly have signet-ring cells and are associated with extracellular mucin collections. Some cases have a goblet cell carcinoid–like appearance and can have cords, trabeculae, and intestinal-type glands {9331286; 17895750; 27338636}. Neuroendocrine markers (chromogranin and synaptophysin) are positive in 20–40% of goblet cell adenocarcinomas {17895750}.

A variety of growth patterns can be encountered with metastatic breast carcinoma. Ductal carcinomas often display glandular, papillary, cribriform, or diffuse patterns. Lobular carcinomas exhibit characteristic single-file linear cords and trabecular and diffuse patterns; signet-ring cell differentiation can be seen {2743281; 29045292; 7237387}.

Metastatic HPV-associated endocervical adenocarcinoma can display mucinous, endometrioid, or usual-type features. Involvement of lower uterine segment and endometrium is not uncommon. The tumour architecture often mimics that of primary ovarian borderline tumour or well-differentiated confluent glandular carcinoma, including villoglandular, papillary, and cribriform growth patterns. The tumours often display hyperchromatic atypical nuclei, conspicuous apical mitoses, and basal apoptotic bodies {18813124; 26166722}. Metastatic gastric-type endocervical adenocarcinoma demonstrates cells with abundant clear and/or pale eosinophilic cytoplasm with distinct cell borders. Cytological atypia can vary from mild to marked {26457350; 30709650}.

The two most common histological types of endometrial carcinoma involving the ovaries are endometrioid and serous, resembling the uterine counterpart. The features that suggest metastasis include bilateral ovarian involvement, small ovarian tumour size, capsular/surface involvement, predominantly hilar distribution, vascular invasion, absence of a precursor lesion (endometriosis, endometrioid adenofibroma), and high tumour grade. Deep myometrial invasion and the presence of lymphovascular invasion in the uterine corpus also suggest metastasis {30550485}.

The immunohistochemical profiles of primary and metastatic ovarian carcinomas are summarized in #13294Table #13294 {12352188; 12454811; 14668551; 16294196; 16931958; 16980943; 17460447; 18724243; 21881489; 25844549; 26551622; 28914716; 29487003}. Additionally, the immunoprofile of primary ovarian mucinous carcinoma is described in section Mucinous carcinoma of the ovary.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: clinical features and macroscopic appearance favouring a metastasis.

Desirable: ovarian surface and superficial cortex involvement; hilar and lymphovascular space involvement; histological features suggestive of a primary tumour other than an ovarian primary; an infiltrative growth pattern with stromal desmoplasia / nodular growth pattern; immunohistochemical profile (see #13294Table #13294).

Staging

For most primary sites, ovarian metastases constitute stage IV disease; endometrial adenocarcinoma is stage IIIA.

Prognosis and prediction

Ovarian metastases qualify as stage IV disease and have a poor outcome. Ovarian involvement by colorectal adenocarcinoma is relatively resistant to chemotherapy compared with other metastatic sites {18455357; 27875625}. Surgical removal of isolated ovarian metastases may be associated with prolonged survival in colorectal and endocervical adenocarcinoma {19820936}. Low-grade mucinous neoplasms involving the ovary are more indolent and have a significantly better prognosis than metastatic appendiceal adenocarcinomas, which are usually high-grade and have a poor outcome {8986525; 11443613; 19641451; 9331286; 17895750}. Integration of genomic studies with clinicopathological interpretation will facilitate more-accurate staging and better management {31846521}. Ovarian metastases of HPV-associated endocervical adenocarcinoma have a favourable prognosis, in contrast to metastases of gastric-type adenocarcinoma, for which the prognosis is poor {18813124; 30709650}.
