4.2.9 Secondary osteosarcoma

Definition

Secondary osteosarcoma is osteosarcoma arising in abnormal bone.

ICD-O coding

9184/3 Secondary osteosarcoma

ICD-11 coding

2B51.Z & XH06W9 Osteosarcoma of bone and articular cartilage of unspecified sites & Osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone (encompasses secondary osteosarcoma)

Related terminology

Acceptable: Paget sarcoma; osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone (PDB); radiation-associated osteosarcoma.

Not recommended: postirradiation sarcoma; radiation-induced sarcoma

Subtype(s)

Osteosarcoma in Paget disease of bone; radiation-associated sarcoma; infarct-related osteosarcoma; osteosarcoma due to chronic osteomyelitis; implant-related osteosarcoma; osteosarcoma secondary to early postzygotic disorders such as fibrous dysplasia

Localization

Paget sarcoma most commonly affects the femur (34%), pelvis (24%), and humerus (24%) {15018182}, reflecting the distribution of uncomplicated PDB, except for a disproportionately high and low incidence in the humerus (24%) and the spine (2%) {15018182}, respectively. Multifocal synchronous tumours may occur (2–17%) {10929418}. Long bone lesions tend to be metaphyseal {6235535}, and tumours have been noted to occur at the site of a previous fracture {2028867}. Radiation-associated osteosarcoma occurs at the site of radiotherapy, most frequently in the flat bones of the chest wall and pelvis {10561354}.

Osteosarcoma related to premalignant bone syndromes generally affects the same locations as non-syndromic osteosarcoma. However, tumours may be multifocal in Rothmund–Thomson syndrome and occur at unusual sites in Werner syndrome (foot and ankle, patella) {22550413}. Bone sarcomas outside the radiation field in hereditary retinoblastoma develop predominantly in the lower limbs {19066271}.

Clinical features

Signs and symptoms

New pain and an enlarging mass in a patient with a history of predisposing bone condition suggest the diagnosis of secondary osteosarcoma; occasionally, the underlying condition is unknown. Paget sarcoma typically develops in a background of polyostotic symptomatic PDB {2028867}. Tumours are frequently lytic, occasionally mineralized {2028867; 11162693}, and may be multifocal {10929418}. Pathological fracture is common {15018182}.

Imaging

Radiographs show lytic (65%) destruction of pagetic bone {6235535}, and there is typically a large extraosseous mass. Lytic tumours appear photopenic on isotope scintigraphy {15018182}; mineralized lesions may be difficult to distinguish from adjacent PDB {2028867}. Replacement of fatty, heterogeneous pagetic marrow, cortical destruction, and an extraosseous mass are useful MRI findings to differentiate secondary osteosarcoma from PDB. Radiation-associated osteosarcoma usually presents some years after treatment, with aggressive bone destruction and an extraosseous mass, superimposed on postradiotherapy bone changes {11162693}. Syndromes predisposing individuals to secondary osteosarcoma may show evidence of a bone dysplasia (e.g. in Rothmund–Thomson syndrome {22550413}) but otherwise show similar presentation and imaging to non-syndromic osteosarcoma {22550413; 29668499; 24924172}. Osteosarcoma at the site of a pre-existing benign bone tumour or chronic osteomyelitis may show imaging evidence of the underlying disease, posttreatment changes, and aggressive bone destruction. Infarct-related osteosarcoma usually occurs at the knee. The tumour usually develops in the margin {26397350} but may obliterate all evidence of the infarct {26397350}.

Epidemiology

Osteosarcoma is the most common sarcoma associated with PDB {17229010}. PDB develops predominantly in populations of English descent: 50% of bone sarcomas in patients aged > 50 years are secondary to PDB in endemic areas {15018182}. In areas where PDB is not found, there is no second, older peak in the bimodal incidence of osteosarcoma {14038496; 17229010}. The incidence of Paget sarcoma in PDB is 0.7–6.3% {15018182}, but as the incidence of PDB is declining, Paget sarcoma also appears to be less frequent {19064007} and is occurring at older ages {19064007}. Most patients are in the sixth to seventh decades of life {15018182} (range: 32–86 years {6571760}).

Radiation-associated osteosarcoma most commonly arises in bone after treatment of tumours with long survival (breast and cervical carcinomas, Hodgkin lymphoma, retinoblastoma) {12057168}. Radiation-associated osteosarcoma is estimated to occur in 0.03% of patients receiving radiotherapy {12057168}, with osteosarcoma accounting for 21–77% {1365680; 11162693}. The median latency period after radiotherapy is 10 years {12057168} (range: 3–55 years {3855683; 6939953}). Most patients are in the fifth decade of life, with an earlier peak in the second decade (children treated for hereditary retinoblastoma and other malignancies) {12057168}. Osteosarcoma in older populations is most frequently due to Paget disease or irradiation {18845233}.

Etiology

Ionizing irradiation is the strongest risk factor for developing secondary osteosarcoma. Development of a radiation-associated osteosarcoma appears to be dose-dependent, with lower, potentially mutagenic doses at the edge of the radiation field {11162693}. Most patients who develop radiation-associated osteosarcoma have received a median of 50 Gy {12057168}. Bone infarction is an acknowledged cause of undifferentiated sarcoma, and an association between caisson disease, sickle cell disease, other biological insults, and osteosarcoma is now recognized {13009398; 13849880; 1423172}. Sarcomas, including osteosarcoma, infrequently develop adjacent to orthopaedic implants {26931145}. A causal link to the constituents of implanted material, which may be carcinogenic in vitro, has been suggested but not proven {11598166}.

Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of secondary osteosarcoma is likely to be variable and determined by its etiology. Comparison of transcriptome modification identified a signature of 135 genes discriminating radiation-associated sarcomas from sporadic sarcomas; the functions of the signature genes suggest that radiation-associated osteosarcomas were subject to chronic oxidative stress, probably as a result of mitochondrial dysfunction {21470956}. Two mutation signatures of ionizing radiation, an excess of small deletions and balanced inversions (both aberration types generate driver mutations), have been identified by paired tumour–normal tissue whole-genome sequencing in radiation-associated malignancies, including radiation-associated osteosarcoma {27615322}. Radiation-associated osteosarcomas feature complex karyotypes {10640139; 28643781; 27615322}. Unlike in primary osteosarcoma, losses are more frequent than gains; in particular, loss at 1p is more common (57% vs 3%).

Macroscopic appearance

The macroscopic features of secondary osteosarcoma are not significantly different from those of primary osteosarcoma. The tumours occur at all sites in bone: in a series of 23 radiation-associated osteosarcomas in children/adolescents (excluding retinoblastomas), 21 were central, 1 was a high-grade surface lesion, and 1 was a periosteal osteosarcoma {10561354}. When an osteosarcoma develops in association with fibrous dysplasia and is diagnosed early, the features of the fibrous dysplastic bone may be apparent, but this is rare.

Histopathology

The histological features of secondary osteosarcoma are not distinguishable from those of primary osteosarcoma.

Cytology

Not clinically relevant

Diagnostic molecular pathology

Not clinically relevant

Essential and desirable diagnostic criteria

Essential: bone tumour with compatible imaging; known history of previous radiation therapy or the presence of an underlying bone abnormality; osteosarcoma histology.

Desirable: histological evidence of underlying abnormal bone.

Staging

Staging is according to bone sarcoma protocols (see <<TNM-Bone>>TNM staging of tumours of bone). See also the information on staging in section Bone tumours: Introduction.

Prognosis and prediction

The prognosis of Paget sarcoma and radiation-associated osteosarcoma, which are typically high-grade tumours unresponsive to chemotherapy, is worse than that of conventional osteosarcoma. In Paget sarcoma, patient age contributes to poor prognosis: the 2-year survival rate is 25%; 29% of patients have pulmonary metastases at presentation {15018182}.

The 5-year survival rate in radiation-associated osteosarcoma is 10–32% {11162693}; 87% of tumours are high-grade {1365680}, and many patients die from metastatic disease within a few months {3280809}. Prognosis is better in operable extremity lesions {11025461}. The use of alkylating agents {3475572} and younger age at treatment {10561354} increase the risk of radiation-associated osteosarcoma and may reduce the latency period {11162693}.

In the setting of cancer predisposition syndromes, the treatment and prognosis of individual tumours are similar to those of non-syndromic lesions; management is complicated and aims to maximize clinical surveillance while minimizing radiation exposure {22550413}.
